Part IX — Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the End of the Qing
57

The 1911 Revolution and the Fall of the Qing

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Why did a dynasty that had spent a decade reforming itself, and that still commanded the largest modern army in the country, lose the allegiance of most of its provinces in a matter of weeks — and how did the settlement that ended two millennia of monarchy come from negotiation as much as from battle?

On the afternoon of October 9, 1911, a man in a rented room in the Russian concession at Hankou, on the north bank of the Yangtze, was handling materials for homemade bombs when something ignited. The explosion injured him and drew the attention of the concession police, who handed the premises and its contents to Qing authorities. Among the papers seized were membership lists, correspondence, and flags and proclamations prepared for an uprising. The revolutionary groups active among soldiers of the New Army in the neighboring city of Wuchang had expected to act later in the autumn. Now their names were in official hands. Several conspirators were arrested and executed within hours. By the evening of October 10, a group of soldiers, fearing that they would be the next to be rounded up, mutinied at an engineering battalion in Wuchang, seized the provincial arsenal, and attacked the offices of the viceroy. By the following morning the city was in their hands. A local accident, a police raid, and the fear of exposure had set off an event that organized revolutionary effort over fifteen years had repeatedly failed to achieve.

This chapter studies the end of the Qing dynasty (1644–1912) and the creation of the Republic of China, an episode traditionally called the Xinhai Revolution after the sexagenary year xinhai (辛亥) in which it began. It asks how a political crisis that had been building for years turned, in the autumn and winter of 1911–1912, into the end of the monarchy. Several developments had to converge: the spread of nationalist and republican ideas among students, soldiers, and overseas communities; the rivalry between revolutionaries and constitutional monarchists; a cabinet appointed in 1911 that disappointed reformers; a railway policy that alienated shareholders and provincial elites; a violent confrontation in Sichuan; a military mutiny in central China; the decisions of provincial assemblies and commanders; and a negotiated bargain between the court, the Beiyang Army commander Yuan Shikai, and the revolutionary leadership.

The chapter matters because 1911–1912 is one of the great political breaks in Chinese history, and because it is easy to misread. The fall of the Qing was not inevitable. The dynasty in 1911 was not the same fragile institution that had faced the Taiping or the Boxers. It had embarked on the New Policies examined in Chapter 56, created provincial assemblies and a national consultative body, trained new armies, and abolished the examination system. Many educated Chinese still hoped for a strengthened constitutional monarchy. The revolution succeeded because the court’s own measures raised expectations that it then failed to satisfy, because armed revolt in one city coincided with a deep crisis of confidence among provincial elites, and because the most powerful military figure in the north chose to bargain rather than to fight to the end.

Much changed. The institution of the emperor, which had anchored political legitimacy for more than two thousand years, ceased to be the foundation of government. New vocabulary — republic, citizen, nation, sovereignty, the people — became part of political argument. Provinces had shown that they could declare independence from the capital and then reassemble into a national government. Much also continued. The soldiers, officers, and regional power-brokers who made the revolution did not disappear, the fiscal weaknesses of the late Qing state were inherited by its successors, foreign powers retained treaty privileges, and the peasants who made up the great majority of the population saw few immediate changes in landholding or taxation. The problem of how to define a Chinese republic that would hold together the territories of a multiethnic empire remained unresolved.

Those affected included people in every part of society: the Manchu bannermen and their families living in garrison districts, many of whom suffered violence; shareholders and merchants who had invested in provincial railway companies; students who had studied in Tokyo and carried new ideas home; soldiers of the New Army, who were often literate, politically aware, and underpaid; secret society members; women who campaigned and fought; overseas Chinese who funded revolutionary organizations; and the peoples of Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang, whose relationship to a fallen dynasty and a new republic was far from settled.

The evidence is unusually varied. Historians draw on the manifestos and journals of revolutionary organizations, the memorials and edicts of the court, the records of provincial assemblies, telegrams exchanged among commanders and politicians, consular reports and newspapers in Chinese and foreign languages, photographs of troops and ruined cities, memoirs written years later (sometimes with a view to claiming credit), and official accounts produced under later governments, each with its own purposes. Because the revolution became the founding event of competing political traditions, in Beijing, in Taipei, and among scholars elsewhere, the sources must be read with particular care.

Finally, the revolution belongs to a wider world. Revolutionaries organized in Japan, Hong Kong, Southeast Asia, Hawaii, and North America; the Qing sought foreign loans from European, American, and Japanese bankers; foreign governments weighed whether to support the dynasty or the new authorities; and Russia and Britain pursued their own interests along the empire’s frontiers. The revolution also took place in a period of upheaval from Russia to Iran to the Ottoman Empire to Mexico, though it should not be reduced to a case of any general pattern. Chapter 58 will follow what happened when a republic existed on paper but had to be built in practice.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Qing dynasty collapsed in 1911–1912 despite a decade of institutional reform, avoiding single-cause or “dynastic cycle” explanations.
  2. Distinguish revolutionary nationalism, constitutional monarchism, and provincial autonomism as separate political positions with different goals.
  3. Trace the development of Sun Yat-sen’s revolutionary activity and the organization of the Tongmenghui, and explain the limits of their influence on the events of 1911.
  4. Analyze the roles of overseas Chinese communities, students in Japan, secret societies, and New Army soldiers in revolutionary networks.
  5. Evaluate the railway nationalization policy and the Sichuan protest movement as causes of revolutionary escalation.
  6. Describe the Wuchang Uprising and explain why a local mutiny became a national political rupture.
  7. Compare the motives of different provincial leaders who abandoned the Qing, and explain why provincial secession was not ideologically uniform.
  8. Assess the nature and scale of anti-Manchu violence and the problem of defining a republic for a multiethnic empire.
  9. Interpret the role of Yuan Shikai and the Beiyang Army in the negotiated end of the dynasty.
  10. Interpret primary sources including the Tongmenghui program and the abdication edict, identifying purpose, audience, and limits.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of how revolutionary the 1911 Revolution was.
  12. Contextualize the revolution within Asian and global history and distinguish it from the early Republic examined in Chapter 58.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did revolutionary nationalists, constitutional monarchists, and provincial elites differ in their aims, and where did their interests overlap?
  3. What was Sun Yat-sen’s actual role in the revolution, and why has his role been remembered more prominently than the roles of many others?
  4. Why did the railway question become a crisis of political legitimacy rather than only an economic dispute?

  7. How did anti-Manchu rhetoric relate to violence against Manchu communities, and how did revolutionaries and the new government try to define a multiethnic nation?

  9. How revolutionary was the 1911 Revolution, and what evidence would a historian use to answer that question?

Historical Context

By 1900 the Qing empire was a vast multiethnic state ruled by a Manchu imperial house that governed Han Chinese provinces through a Han-staffed civil bureaucracy, while ruling Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Manchuria through distinct institutions (Chapter 48). The Banner system had organized Manchu, Mongol, and Han-martial families into hereditary military units, many stationed in garrison districts inside major cities (Chapter 46). The dynasty had survived the Taiping and other mid-century rebellions by relying on regional armies and provincial officials (Chapters 53–54), but the cost was a lasting shift in power toward provincial governors-general. Foreign powers held treaty-port concessions, extraterritorial rights, and spheres of influence, and after the defeat by Japan in 1895 and the Boxer Protocol of 1901 (Chapters 55–56), Qing finances were burdened by indemnities and by loans secured on customs and salt revenues.

The post-Boxer New Policies were the most substantial program of institutional change in the dynasty’s history. The examination system was abolished in 1905, thousands of students went to Japan, new schools and a New Army on Western and Japanese models were created, and in 1908 the court issued an outline for a constitution with a nine-year preparation period. Provincial assemblies met in 1909, and a national Consultative Assembly in 1910. These measures created arenas where educated elites could speak politically and organize. They also made the dynasty’s limits visible. Provincial assemblies petitioned repeatedly for an early parliament, and the court agreed to shorten the timetable to 1913 but refused more.

Geography shaped the revolutionary map. The Yangtze River valley, with its treaty ports such as Hankou, Shanghai, and Nanjing, linked inland provinces to global commerce and to new ideas. The Wuhan cities — Wuchang, Hankou, and Hanyang, on the Yangtze and Han rivers — formed a major administrative, industrial, and commercial hub, home to the Hanyang ironworks and a large garrison. South China, especially Guangdong, had long been a center of overseas migration and of revolutionary organizing among emigrant communities. Sichuan, the great upper-Yangtze basin, had an economy and political culture of its own, and its sense of provincial interest would matter greatly. The north, including the capital region, remained the base of the Beiyang Army, the best-trained and best-equipped modern force. The empire’s frontiers in Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang were also affected by Russian and British ambitions.

The political meaning of “China” was in flux. The word Zhongguo had long referred to a cultural and political center rather than to a bounded nation-state (Chapter 1). By the early twentieth century, intellectuals were debating whether China should be understood as a nation of Han people who had been conquered by Manchus, as a multiethnic nation within Qing borders, or as something else. These were not abstract questions. They would determine whether the new state should be a Han nation-state, a union of “five peoples,” or something else, and whether Mongolia and Tibet were part of it. Students should keep in mind that the Qing empire’s borders were not the borders of an already existing nation, and that the Republic claimed to inherit them in a process that remained contested.

Historical Evidence

Historians reconstruct 1911–1912 from a dense but uneven body of sources, each of which shows the revolution from a particular position. Revolutionary publications and manifestos, such as the journal Minbao and the proclamations issued by the Hubei Military Government, show what activists wanted the public to believe and how they framed enemies, goals, and the nation. They are powerful evidence for political language, but not for what ordinary soldiers or shopkeepers thought. Court documents — edicts, memorials, telegrams between the capital and provincial governors — record the dynasty’s responses and the way officials understood the crisis, but they are shaped by the fear of blame and by formulaic language.

Provincial assembly records and chamber of commerce papers show the interests of local elites and merchants, and in the case of Sichuan and Hunan, the railway companies’ ledgers and shareholder resolutions allow historians to analyze the financial grievances behind the protests. Telegrams were the central medium of 1911: commanders, assemblymen, and negotiators used the telegraph to announce independence, make demands, and sway public opinion. Collections of these telegrams are essential, though many were published selectively and some were intended for public effect rather than as private communication.

Foreign sources, including consular reports, newspapers such as the North-China Herald, missionary accounts, and the diaries of foreign advisers, offer detailed observation of events in treaty ports and capitals. They are valuable but filtered through foreign interests, the assumptions of observers, and limited access to non-treaty-port areas. Photographs, such as those of revolutionary troops at Wuchang and the ruins of Hankou, provide visual evidence of uniforms, flags, and destruction, but are posed, captioned, or selected in ways that require interpretation. Memoirs and oral recollections collected decades later preserve vivid detail about the uprising’s planning and its aftermath, yet they were often written to claim credit within the political struggles of the Republic and thus must be checked against contemporary documents.

Finally, official histories and commemorative traditions have shaped what is remembered. The Nationalist government in Nanjing and later Taiwan, and the People’s Republic, each celebrated the revolution in different terms and emphasized different figures; scholars outside China have read the same records with different questions. Students should ask of every source: who produced it, when, for whom, and what perspective — Manchu or Han, soldier or officer, city or village, male or female — it leaves out.

Section 57.1

The Qing Monarchy and the Crisis of Political Legitimacy

The Qing dynasty entered 1911 as a reforming state, and this is the central puzzle of the chapter. Chapter 56 described how, after the Boxer catastrophe, the court abolished the examination system, built new schools, reorganized the army, restructured ministries, and promised a constitution. Each of these steps was intended to strengthen the dynasty. Each also changed the political landscape in ways the court could not fully control. Students returning from Japan and the West wanted careers in the new institutions. Provincial assemblies, elected on a narrow franchise of men with property or education, claimed a voice in fiscal and railway matters. Newspapers in the treaty ports discussed politics with a freedom that earlier generations had not known. Reform created a public that expected more than the court was prepared to give.

Legitimacy in the Qing context rested on several supports: the dynasty’s claim to rule under Heaven’s sanction, its performance of ritual, its role as protector of Confucian order, its military and fiscal competence, and its ability to balance the interests of Manchu, Mongol, Han, and other subjects. By 1911 several of these supports had weakened. Military defeats since 1840 had damaged claims of competence. The loss of the examination system severed a link between the throne and the scholar-gentry who had been its main social base. The deaths of the Guangxu Emperor and Cixi in 1908 left the throne to a three-year-old, Puyi, with his father Zaifeng as regent. Zaifeng lacked Cixi’s experience and network of loyalties, and his removal of Yuan Shikai from office in 1909 deprived the court of its best-connected military administrator.

Constitutional reform was the most sensitive arena. The 1908 Outline of a Constitution was modeled on the Meiji constitution in Japan and reserved large powers to the emperor. The nine-year preparatory schedule disappointed many reformers. In 1910 provincial delegations traveled to Beijing in three petition campaigns demanding an immediate parliament. The court agreed to shorten the schedule to 1913 and rejected further concessions, dispersing a delegation from the capital and sending some activists back to their provinces. For many constitutionalists this was a decisive experience: the court appeared willing to talk about representation but not to share power.

Money added to the strain. The New Policies were expensive, the indemnity payments continued, and the central government had weak revenue authority; provincial governments collected much of the empire’s taxes and kept much of them. New schools, armies, and police forces required new levies, which provoked local protests, tax riots, and food riots in several provinces in 1910–1911. A poor harvest and floods in the middle and lower Yangtze region in 1910–1911 raised prices and displaced rural people toward the cities. A financial panic in Shanghai in 1910, connected to speculation in rubber shares, damaged some native banks and made merchants more anxious about the state of public finance. None of these conditions caused the revolution by itself, but together they made the political atmosphere in 1911 volatile.

It is useful to distinguish crisis of legitimacy from collapse of capacity. The Qing still had substantial military power, fiscal resources, and officials willing to serve it. Its crisis was above all one of confidence among the educated, propertied, and armed groups who would decide the outcome, and the sections that follow trace how that confidence was lost.

Section 57.2

Revolutionary Nationalism and the Making of a New Political Language

Revolutionary nationalism took shape in the first decade of the twentieth century among students, journalists, and exiles, many in Tokyo and Shanghai. It combined three ideas that had not previously been joined in a single political program. The first was national sovereignty: China was a nation threatened with partition by foreign powers, and its people had a right, and a duty, to defend and govern it. The second was popular citizenship: the government should rest on the participation of citizens rather than on the will of an emperor. The third was anti-Manchu racial or ethnic nationalism: the Qing was a foreign, conquering dynasty whose rule had caused China’s weakness, and the Han people should reclaim the state.

The third element was powerful but was not universal among revolutionaries. Writers such as Zou Rong, whose 1903 pamphlet The Revolutionary Army circulated widely and brought a prison sentence that ended in his death in custody, and Zhang Binglin, a scholar and journalist, drew on memories of the Ming–Qing transition and on Western ideas of race to argue that Manchu rule was alien rule. Some wrote of vengeance. Others, including Sun Yat-sen, emphasized the failures of the dynasty’s government rather than the Manchu ancestry of its rulers, and spoke of the Manchus as obstacles to be removed rather than as enemies to be destroyed. The distinction between a political objection to the Qing government and a racial hatred of Manchu people was blurred in rhetoric and sharpened in practice in 1911 (see 57.13).

Intellectuals also had to define what the nation was. Liang Qichao had argued in the early 1900s for a broad “Chinese nation” (Zhonghua minzu) that would include Manchus, Mongols, and others in a single people. Revolutionaries such as Zhang Binglin thought of the nation primarily as the Han descendants of the Yellow Emperor. After 1905 the argument between the reformist Xinmin congbao, edited by Liang, and the revolutionary Minbao clarified the stakes: did China need a revolution to eject the dynasty, or could the dynasty be reformed? Did a nation require ethnic homogeneity, or could a multiethnic territory hold together as a republic? The debate used terms that had been borrowed from Japanese translations of Western texts, and many modern political words took their meaning in these years.

Historical Vocabulary
Xinhai, Republic, Nation, and Sovereignty

Xinhai (辛亥) is the name of the 48th year in the traditional sixty-year cycle, which corresponded to 1911. Chinese-language writing calls the revolution the “Xinhai Revolution” (Xinhai geming), a name that marks it as the event of that year, with the winter of 1911–1912 included.

Republic (gonghe, 共和) is an old term. It originally referred to the Gonghe regency in the early Zhou period, when, according to later historical tradition, ministers governed after the king fled. Japanese translators used the term for “republic,” and Chinese writers adopted it. In 1911 it meant a political order without a hereditary monarch, but it was understood differently by different actors: some imagined a constitutional government of elected representatives, others an authority of military and provincial leaders, others simply the end of Manchu rule.

Nation (minzu, 民族) is likewise a modern usage, adapted from Japanese. It could mean a people defined by descent and culture or a political community defined by territory and citizenship, and in early twentieth-century writing it was used in both senses. Sovereignty (zhuquan, 主权) expressed the claim that a nation has supreme authority within its territory, a notion that gained force as foreign powers held treaty privileges. When students encounter these words in sources from 1911, they should ask which meaning the writer had in mind, and avoid assuming that a modern definition applies.

Revolutionary nationalism spread through new institutions. Schools, newspapers, student associations, and drama troupes created audiences. Songs, posters, and public speeches reached people who could not read the essays. The political vocabulary of the new schools, the army, and the press became widely shared, although the depth of commitment varied greatly. Understanding the Xinhai Revolution requires recognizing that the people who shared the language of nation and republic did not necessarily share a program.

Section 57.3

Sun Yat-sen and the Early Revolutionary Movement

Sun Yat-sen was born in 1866 in a village in Xiangshan county, Guangdong, near Macao, an area with long ties to overseas migration. At about thirteen he joined his elder brother in Hawaii, where he attended missionary schools and encountered Western ideas and Christianity. He later studied medicine in Hong Kong. This background gave him a mix of experiences unusual among Qing subjects: bilingual education, familiarity with colonial Hong Kong’s administration, and a network among Chinese overseas communities. After the Qing defeat in 1894–1895, he abandoned efforts to petition reformist officials and in Honolulu in 1894 founded the Xingzhonghui (Revive China Society), a small organization of overseas Chinese and Guangdong supporters.

The Xingzhonghui planned an uprising in Guangzhou in 1895, which was discovered and failed. Sun fled, first to Japan and then abroad. In London in 1896, he was detained at the Chinese legation, and released after the intervention of friends and the British government, an episode that brought him international publicity. Over the next fifteen years he traveled across Japan, Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America seeking funds, recruits, and political support. He relied on overseas Chinese merchants and laborers, on Japanese sympathizers, on secret societies such as the Triads in South China, and on members of Hong Kong’s Chinese community. The Huizhou uprising of 1900 and a series of border uprisings between 1907 and 1908 in Guangdong, Guangxi, and Yunnan all failed.

The overseas Chinese were central to this effort. Many came from Guangdong and Fujian, were exposed to discrimination and exclusion abroad, and considered a strong China a protection for themselves. Some joined revolutionary groups for ideological reasons; others gave money because of kinship ties or because they hoped to gain position later. Sun is said to have later called the overseas Chinese “the mother of the revolution,” an acknowledgment of their funding. Overseas contributions were however never sufficient or stable, and rivalries between Sun’s supporters and those of Kang Youwei’s Protect the Emperor Society (Baohuanghui) in many overseas communities meant that communities were divided.

Students should avoid two distortions. One portrays Sun as the sole founder and leader of the revolution, in the manner of later party histories; the other dismisses him as merely a symbol. Sun was an indefatigable organizer, a fundraiser, and a propagandist who held together a fragile network of supporters and gave the movement a program. But he did not command the New Army officers who rose at Wuchang, and his plans centered on borderland uprisings by small forces, a strategy the 1911 events showed to be less effective than organizing within the modern army. His repeated failures make the eventual success of the revolution harder, not easier, to attribute to him personally.

Section 57.4

The Tongmenghui and the Three Principles of the People

In the summer of 1905, Chinese students in Tokyo numbered in the thousands, and revolutionary sentiment was widespread among them. Sun Yat-sen, who arrived in Japan that summer, met Huang Xing, a Hunanese organizer of the Huaxinghui (China Revival Society), and with members of the Guangfuhui (Restoration Society) of Zhejiang, including Zhang Binglin and Cai Yuanpei, helped found the Tongmenghui (Revolutionary Alliance, or United League) on August 20, 1905. Sun was chosen as its leader (zongli). Branches were established in Japan, Southeast Asia, Hong Kong, and several Chinese cities, and by 1911 it had thousands of members, though the numbers are uncertain and many were only loosely connected.

The organization’s journal, Minbao, began publication in November 1905, and its first issue contained Sun’s statement of the Three Principles of the People (sanmin zhuyi): the principle of the nation or people (minzu), usually understood at this stage as the overthrow of Manchu rule and the defense of China against foreign domination; the principle of people’s rights (minquan), the creation of a republican government; and the principle of people’s livelihood (minsheng), a commitment to equalizing land rights (pingjun diquan, 平均地权). In Sun’s early formulation this meant assessing land values and capturing for the public the increases in value that economic and urban growth produced, so that private owners would not appropriate socially created gains, a concern shaped by his view of industrial Europe; it was not a plan to redistribute farms among cultivators. These ideas were developed and revised over the following decades (Chapter 61), and Sun’s later agrarian proposals should not be read back into 1905. In 1905 the principles were presented as a short program rather than as a fully theorized system.

Primary Source Window

The Four-Point Program of the Tongmenghui, 1905

Creators: Sun Yat-sen, Huang Xing, and other founders · Date: 1905 · Type: Political program and membership oath of a revolutionary organization

Context: When the Tongmenghui was founded in Tokyo, members were asked to swear to an oath that summarized the organization’s aims in four short phrases. The oath was repeated in the group’s publications and proclamations, including those issued by revolutionary forces in 1911. It was written for members and potential supporters, mostly students and overseas Chinese, and was intended to be easy to remember.

Paraphrase: The program called on members to drive out the Manchu (“Tartar”) rulers; restore the Chinese nation; found a republic; and equalize land rights (pingjun diquan).

Analysis: The four points combine different ideas. The first is an expression of anti-Manchu nationalism using a historic pejorative term for the Manchus, reflecting the strand of racial politics noted in 57.2. The second invokes a national restoration whose territory and population are not defined. The third states a political form but not how a republic would be organized. The fourth, the most distinctive, indicates concern with the land question. The phrase “equalize land rights” (pingjun diquan) is often misread as a promise to redistribute farmland; in Sun’s early program it referred chiefly to valuing land and taxing or capturing the socially generated rise in land values, and it was open to varied interpretation. It was not implemented by the revolutionary governments of 1912, and it should be distinguished from Sun’s later agrarian proposals. The slogan’s brevity made it effective for mobilization and imprecise as a governing plan. It also shows what the revolutionary coalition shared and what it left unresolved: the memory of Manchu conquest, the aim of a republic, and the question of whether the new nation would be Han or multiethnic.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the program say about the group’s aims, and how many of them concern the Manchus, the form of government, and social policy respectively?
  2. Who was the audience for this oath, and how might that audience have shaped its wording?
  3. What can historians learn from the program about revolutionary goals in 1905?
  4. What can historians not learn from it about the way the group actually organized, and about the revolution of 1911?

The Tongmenghui’s limits were considerable. It was a coalition rather than a disciplined party, and its leaders disagreed about strategy and leadership. In 1907 Zhang Binglin and others, dissatisfied with Sun’s management of funds and his decisions, attacked him publicly, and in 1910 Tao Chengzhang and others reorganized the Restoration Society separately. Huang Xing and Song Jiaoren, a Hunanese organizer, favored building revolutionary organization in the Yangtze valley and within the army, while Sun continued to rely on border uprisings and overseas funds. In 1911 Song Jiaoren and others created a Central China Bureau of the Tongmenghui in Shanghai to coordinate Yangtze activity. The Tongmenghui helped shape ideas and recruited many individuals who took part in 1911, but the Wuchang Uprising was planned and executed by local groups that had their own organization.

Section 57.5

Constitutionalists, Reformers, and Revolutionaries

The revolutionaries were only one political current. After the failure of the Hundred Days Reform in 1898, Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, exiled in Japan and overseas, continued to advocate the preservation of the monarchy under a constitution. Their followers organized the Protect the Emperor Society, later renamed the Constitutional Party, and Liang’s journals reached many Chinese readers inside and outside China. Liang argued that China was not yet ready for a republic, that revolution would invite foreign intervention and disorder, and that gradual change led by an enlightened monarchy was more prudent. Many members of the gentry and merchant class shared these concerns.

Within China, the constitutionalists’ strongest base was in the provincial assemblies created in 1909. Leaders such as Zhang Jian, a prominent industrialist and educator from Jiangsu, Tang Shouqian in Zhejiang, and Tang Hualong in Hubei were elite men with business interests, educational credentials, and a record of local leadership. They formed associations to press for parliament and constitutional government and organized the petition campaigns of 1910. They were not revolutionaries, but they were deeply critical of the court’s reluctance to share power and its centralizing tendencies. In 1911 their frustration with the court would make many willing to cooperate with, or at least not oppose, a revolt.

Historical Debate

Constitutional Reform or Republican Revolution?

The question: Could the Qing have survived as a constitutional monarchy if it had made different decisions, or was revolution the only way to resolve China’s political crisis? Contemporaries disagreed sharply, and historians have continued the argument.

Interpretation A — A reformed monarchy was possible: Many historians argue that the court’s reforms were creating a viable constitutional order, that many elites preferred reform to revolution, and that the decisive failure was the court’s political misjudgments in 1910–1911: refusing an earlier parliament, forming a cabinet dominated by imperial kinsmen, and nationalizing railways without consultation. On this reading, the revolution might have been avoided or delayed had the court adopted the constitutionalists’ proposals. Liang Qichao’s warnings about disorder looked prescient after 1912.
Interpretation B — The dynasty’s structural weaknesses made collapse likely: Others stress that the dynasty faced fiscal weakness, regional militarization, foreign pressure, and a legitimacy deficit rooted in its identification as a Manchu house, and that revolutionary organization, the politicization of the army, and the autonomy of provincial elites would have produced a crisis even with a more skillful court. They note that the constitutional reform was itself conducted on terms that reserved power to the throne, and that many younger educated people had already lost confidence in monarchy.

Evidence: Historians compare the petitions and programs of the constitutionalists with court edicts, study the membership and finances of provincial assemblies, and analyze why assembly leaders changed their positions in autumn 1911. They look at the course of events in provinces where constitutionalists took over revolutionary governments, for example Hunan and Zhejiang, and at the military records on the loyalty of units.

Why it matters: The debate concerns whether the fall of the Qing was contingent on decisions made in 1910–1911 or structurally determined, and thus how much weight to give political choices as against underlying conditions. It also bears on how to evaluate the Republic that followed: if a constitutional monarchy might have worked, the revolution appears more costly; if not, it appears as a necessary rupture.

Student task: Which kinds of evidence would best test whether the monarchy could have been preserved? What does the behavior of provincial assembly leaders in October–November 1911 suggest?

The reformists and the revolutionaries were not always sharply divided in practice. Some individuals moved from one position to the other as events changed. In 1911 many constitutionalists concluded that the dynasty could no longer be reformed, and joined the revolutionary side or accepted a republic as the only way to preserve order and property. This movement of elite opinion, as much as the actions of committed revolutionaries, is the key to the speed of the collapse.

Section 57.6

Revolutionary Networks and Failed Uprisings Before 1911

Between 1900 and 1911, revolutionary groups organized more than a dozen armed attempts, nearly all of which failed. The Huizhou uprising (1900), the Pingliuli uprising in Jiangxi and Hunan (1906), border uprisings from Vietnam into Guangdong, Guangxi, and Yunnan (1907–1908), and mutinies in the New Army in Anhui (1908) and Guangzhou (1910) were suppressed with executions. The Qing government treated the revolutionaries as dangerous, and the outcomes shaped a culture of martyrdom that later commemorations would celebrate.

Revolutionary networks drew on varied social groups. Students in Japan and in the new schools formed the largest intellectual base. Secret societies, such as the Triads in the south and the Gelaohui (Elder Brother Society) along the Yangtze and in Sichuan and the northwest, had members among boatmen, laborers, and soldiers, and had their own traditions of anti-Qing sentiment, though they often fought for local reasons and could be unreliable allies. New Army soldiers and junior officers were increasingly targets of revolutionary recruitment, since they were literate, trained, and had weapons. In Hubei, groups such as the Literary Society (Wenxueshe) and the Progressive Association (Gongjinhui) built cells within the army from 1908 onward.

Women participated actively. Qiu Jin, from Shaoxing in Zhejiang, left her marriage, studied in Japan, joined the Tongmenghui, and returned to run a school that trained revolutionaries and to edit a women’s journal advocating women’s education and the end of foot binding. In July 1907, when her cousin Xu Xilin’s attempted uprising at Anqing failed, Qiu Jin was arrested and executed at Shaoxing without a full trial, a case that provoked outrage. He Zhen, in Tokyo, wrote on feminism and anarchism and criticized male revolutionaries for neglecting women’s liberation. Women also served as couriers, bomb-makers, and fundraisers, and in the winter of 1911–1912 several women’s military units were organized, as discussed in 57.14.

The most famous failed attempt was the Huanghuagang (Yellow Flower Mound) uprising in Guangzhou on April 27, 1911. Huang Xing and about a hundred and fifty to several hundred picked fighters, many of them educated young men from overseas and Guangdong, attacked the offices of the Guangdong–Guangxi viceroy. The plan was disrupted by premature action, weak coordination, and the government’s alertness, and the attackers were defeated in a day of street fighting. Dozens died in combat or were executed; the remains of about seventy-two were later buried at Huanghuagang, and the figure became part of revolutionary commemoration, though the exact number of dead is uncertain. Lin Juemin, a young Fujian writer who died in the attack, left a farewell letter that became widely read.

The uprising was a military failure and a heavy blow, since it cost the Tongmenghui many of its most dedicated members. Yet it also demonstrated that educated young people were ready to die, and it raised public sympathy and funds. Its failure also encouraged Huang Xing, Song Jiaoren, and others to turn to the Yangtze valley and the army, a change that would contribute to Wuchang. The lesson for students is that repeated failure did not eliminate revolutionary activism but changed its methods.

Section 57.7

The Regency, the Imperial Kinsmen Cabinet, and the Constitutional Crisis

Prince Regent Zaifeng governed in the name of his son Puyi from 1908. His position was weak. He lacked the personal authority of Cixi, relied on a circle of Manchu princes and officials, and faced a political landscape in which provincial governors, army commanders, and assemblies had gained influence. Zaifeng and many of his advisers drew a lesson from the late nineteenth century: provincial power had grown at the expense of the throne, and recentralization, particularly of military and financial control, was necessary. They placed Manchu princes in charge of the army and navy ministries, tried to regain control of the New Army, and sought to curb the autonomy of governors-general. Many Han officials and gentry read these measures as an ethnic grab for power, whatever the intentions.

The decisive step came on May 8, 1911, when the court announced the formation of a responsible cabinet, the first in Chinese history, under Prince Yikuang (Prince Qing) as premier. Constitutionalists had long demanded a cabinet, and the announcement was supposed to demonstrate progress toward constitutional government. But of the thirteen ministers, nine were Manchu, and five of those were members of the imperial clan. Critics promptly named it the “Imperial Kinsmen Cabinet.” The appointments contradicted the implication of the constitutional principle that ministers should be accountable to a parliament, since the emperor’s relatives were appointed directly by the throne. Provincial assembly delegates in Beijing petitioned for a change, and the court rebuked them in an edict stating that appointments were the emperor’s prerogative, a statement that closed the door to compromise.

The consequences were serious. Constitutionalists such as Zhang Jian and Tang Shouqian, who had worked to avoid revolution, became convinced that the court was not serious about sharing power. Liang Qichao, in Japan, remained a monarchist but wrote bitterly about the court’s decisions. For the revolutionaries, the cabinet was evidence that reform was a pretense and that the Manchu ruling house placed its own interests above the nation’s. The Manchu–Han tension in politics, which had been muted since the mid-nineteenth century, was now openly acknowledged, though it should be noted that many Manchu officials favored reform and that Han officials also served the court loyally to the end.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Crisis of 1911: Who Wanted What?

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on patterns documented in many sources. They are not quotations from any single historical person, and individuals within each group varied widely.

Constitutionalist assemblymen and gentry: Most wanted a parliament with real powers, protection of provincial economic interests, and orderly change that would preserve property and local social order. They distrusted the court’s centralization, but many also feared that a revolution would bring warlordism, mob violence, and foreign intervention. In autumn 1911 many shifted toward the revolution as the only way to protect their interests.
Revolutionary activists and students: They aimed to end the dynasty and establish a republic and shared a sense of national emergency. They included people committed to social change, others primarily to national strength, and others mostly to expelling the Manchus. Many were young, had studied abroad, and had friends who died in failed uprisings. They had few resources and relied on mobilizing others — soldiers, secret societies, and elites — whose goals differed from theirs.
New Army soldiers and officers: Soldiers were often literate young men from farm and small-town families, paid irregularly in some provinces and subject to political surveillance. Some were revolutionaries; many joined the revolt because of the pressure of events, fear of punishment, loyalty to officers, or hope of promotion. Officers could be pressed into leadership roles they had not sought, as Li Yuanhong was in Wuchang.
Merchants and railway shareholders: Investors in provincial railway companies, who ranged from wealthy gentry to small shareholders, regarded the railways as local property and symbols of local self-strengthening. They were angered by nationalization terms and foreign loans, but responses varied by province and by how much money they stood to lose. Merchants in treaty ports also wanted stability for trade.
Manchu bannermen and their families: Most were not wealthy; many lived on stipends that had eroded, and had few alternative occupations. They found themselves identified with a dynasty they did not control and became targets of hostility. Some supported the court; others hoped for protection from new authorities. In several cities they were victims of violence, as examined in 57.13.

In 1911–1912 some women organized fundraising, nursing, and military units and demanded a role in the new government; their hopes would meet strong resistance in the Republic (Chapters 58 and 60).
Section 57.8

Railway Nationalization, Foreign Loans, and Provincial Opposition

Railways were among the most politically charged subjects in the late Qing. Chinese reformers saw them as the arteries of a strong nation, while foreign powers sought concessions to build and finance lines that would give them economic and strategic influence. Between 1903 and 1908, in what is sometimes called the “rights recovery movement,” provincial elites in Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Hunan, Hubei, Guangdong, and Sichuan organized companies to build lines with domestic capital and to buy back concessions from foreign firms. The movement mixed economic interest and patriotic feeling. Provincial railway companies sold shares to thousands of investors, and shareholders’ meetings became places of political education.

In practice, the companies struggled. Capital was insufficient, management was often poor, and some funds were lost in speculation or misused. Construction on the Sichuan–Hankou line, the Guangdong–Hankou line, and others progressed slowly. Central officials, including Sheng Xuanhuai, argued that only a unified national system financed by foreign loans could build the trunk lines needed. Sheng, who had run the Hanyang ironworks and the China Merchants’ Steam Navigation Company, was an experienced industrialist and a skilled but unpopular official. In 1911 the Ministry of Posts and Communications moved to turn his argument into policy.

On May 9, 1911, the day after the cabinet announcement, an edict declared that trunk railways would be nationalized and that provincial companies would not be allowed to continue building them. On May 20, the government signed the Huguang Railway loan agreement with a four-power banking consortium (Britain, France, Germany, and the United States), borrowing six million pounds sterling to build the Hubei and Hunan sections of the Hankou–Guangzhou line and the section of the Hankou–Sichuan line from Yichang to the Sichuan border; the Sichuan and Guangdong sections were left out of the agreement for domestic political reasons. The loan was an obligation of the Imperial government and was secured as a first charge on provincial revenues of Hubei and Hunan, namely the salt, rice (grain), and likin taxes, together with the earnings of the lines. The government offered to compensate shareholders, but terms varied by province: Hunan and Hubei investors received something close to their money, in other provinces compensation was partial, and in Sichuan, as 57.9 explains, the dispute centered on whether the government would reimburse sums lost to speculation.

The opposition did not arise solely from nationalism. Shareholders feared losses; local elites resented the loss of control over provincial resources and the transfer of authority to Beijing; constitutionalists objected that a major financial decision had been made without consulting the Consultative Assembly or provincial assemblies; and many people believed that foreign loans would give foreign powers a grip on China’s territory and revenue. Memories of earlier loans secured on customs, and the conviction that railways were a matter of sovereignty, fueled these fears. At the same time, nationalization had defenders who argued that a unified system was more efficient and would prevent a patchwork of local lines. Historians still assess the policy differently: some see it as a rational, if clumsily executed, step toward national integration; others as a political blunder at a moment of vulnerability.

In Hunan and Hubei, protests developed in the summer through assembly petitions, shareholder meetings, and public gatherings. In Guangdong, shareholders protested as well. The fiercest and most consequential movement, however, took place in Sichuan.

Section 57.9

The Sichuan Railway Protection Movement

Sichuan’s railway company was a unique institution. Established in 1904 and financed in part by a surtax on rural rents paid by landowners, it gathered contributions from many thousands of small investors across the province, which gave the railway a broader social base than the companies elsewhere. By 1911 it had collected millions of taels but had built little track. Funds had been lost, including a large sum placed with Shanghai financiers and lost in the 1910 speculative crash. The central government’s offer to compensate shareholders only for money actually spent on construction, and not for losses, angered investors who felt they were being forced to absorb the failures of the company’s managers.

In June 1911 the provincial assembly leaders and company directors, among them Pu Dianjun and Luo Lun, formed the Railway Protection Society (Baolu tongzhihui). The movement quickly spread beyond shareholders. Merchants closed shops in a strike, students boycotted classes, and in many counties people refused to pay taxes. Secret societies joined. Public meetings drew large crowds. The movement used the language of loyalty to the throne at first, with demonstrators carrying tablets bearing the emperor’s name and petitioning for justice, but by late summer a radical wing was calling for independence from the court.

Duanfang, a reformist official of the Manchu Plain White Banner (though of Han Chinese descent) who had been a supporter of constitutional reform, was sent from Hubei with troops to take charge of the Sichuan railways. Meanwhile, Zhao Erfeng, a Han Bannerman frontier official with a reputation for severity from his military campaigns in Kham on the Tibetan frontier, was appointed acting governor-general in Chengdu. On September 7, 1911, he invited Pu Dianjun, Luo Lun, and other leaders to the governor-general’s office and had them arrested. A crowd gathered to demand their release, and troops opened fire. Dozens of protesters were killed; the exact number is uncertain. The incident, called the Chengdu Massacre, led to armed uprisings across the province. Secret society members, local militias, and protesters besieged county towns, and the government lost control of much of the countryside.

Cause and Effect

Railway Protests and Revolutionary Escalation

Long-term causes

The rights recovery movement and the idea that railways were a matter of national sovereignty; weak state finances and dependence on foreign loans; fiscal and political decentralization that had given provincial elites control of local resources; the political frustrations of constitutionalists with the court.

Short-term causes

The May 1911 cabinet and nationalization edict; the Huguang loan; shareholder losses and unclear compensation, especially in Sichuan; the appointment of hardline officials and the arrests of September 7.

Immediate effects

Armed unrest across Sichuan; transfer of troops from Hubei to Sichuan, thinning the garrison in Wuhan; further loss of elite confidence in the court; revolutionary groups gained local allies and opportunities.

Long-term effects

Provincial elites were accustomed to acting independently; Sichuan’s politics remained fragmented and militarized in the Republic; nationalization and foreign borrowing became a lasting issue in debates over sovereignty.

Historical complexity: The railway protests did not cause the revolution alone. They turned existing grievances into mass action, but the revolution that followed began with a military mutiny in Hubei by people who had their own organization and aims. Historians therefore speak of the protests as a catalyst, not a sole cause, and note that the protest movement’s goals (provincial control of railways) differed from the revolutionaries’ goal of ending the dynasty.

As Sichuan slipped from control, the Qing ordered more troops to the province, including units of the Hubei New Army. This was a critical decision: it diverted soldiers from Wuhan, where Viceroy Ruicheng and the army’s officers believed that the revolutionary threat was manageable. Revolutionary organizers in Hubei watched the movement of troops and the growing disorder, and interpreted both as an opening.

Section 57.10

The Wuchang Uprising of October 10, 1911

Wuhan in 1911 was a combustible mixture. The Hubei New Army numbered about fifteen thousand men in the Wuchang area, with an additional force stationed elsewhere, and a substantial number of soldiers had come under the influence of revolutionary societies. Two groups, the Literary Society and the Progressive Association, had merged their efforts in 1911, and had links to the Tongmenghui’s Central China Bureau. They made plans for an uprising in the autumn, and discussed a date around mid-October, though disagreements and uncertainty about coordination with outside leaders such as Huang Xing delayed decisions. With the departure of units to Sichuan and the unrest in the province, conditions seemed favorable.

The accidental explosion in Hankou on October 9, described at the opening of this chapter, forced the issue. Qing authorities seized documents, including lists of members, and Viceroy Ruicheng ordered arrests. Three of the conspirators were executed in the early morning of October 10, and troops in barracks feared a purge. That evening, soldiers of the Eighth Engineers Battalion, led by a sergeant named Xiong Bingkun and others, rose in revolt at their barracks, and gunfire signaled other units to join. Rebels seized the Chuwangtai arsenal, which held large stores of weapons and ammunition, and moved on the viceroy’s office. Ruicheng fled to a gunboat on the Yangtze, and the commander of the Eighth Division, Zhang Biao, withdrew. By the morning of October 11, the rebels controlled Wuchang. In the following days they took Hanyang, with its arsenal and ironworks, and Hankou.

Several features of this event deserve emphasis. First, the leadership of the original conspiracy had been largely lost: some of its senior figures had been killed or arrested on October 9–10, and others were absent. The soldiers who began the uprising acted with little direction from above and in the absence of a clear political plan. Second, Sun Yat-sen was far away, in the United States, and learned of the uprising from newspaper reports a few days afterward; Huang Xing was in Hong Kong. The Wuchang revolt was the work of local groups and soldiers, supported by some civilians. Third, the uprising’s success depended on circumstances that could have gone otherwise: Ruicheng’s flight, the absence of the troops sent to Sichuan, and the arsenal’s capture. Many earlier uprisings had failed in similar conditions.

What made Wuchang different from earlier failures was what happened next. The rebels did not retreat, and they recognized that a successful rising needed a leader with prestige and respectability to win over the officer corps, the provincial assembly, and the foreign consuls in Hankou. They turned to a senior officer, Li Yuanhong, whose actions are examined in 57.11. They also issued proclamations that told the public the revolution was directed at the Manchu dynasty and not at foreigners or the people, promised to respect foreign treaty rights and property, and invited sympathetic officers and gentry to join. Foreign consuls in Hankou declared neutrality, a decision that denied the Qing foreign military help against the revolutionaries and allowed the new authorities to claim the status of a belligerent party.

Section 57.11

The Hubei Military Government and the First Revolutionary Coalition

On October 11, the rebels announced the formation of the Military Government of the Republic of China in Hubei, usually called the Hubei Military Government. They needed a leader. Li Yuanhong, commander of the Twenty-First Mixed Brigade, was a respected officer who had studied in Japan and had a reputation for fairness; he had not been a member of the revolutionary groups, and by most accounts he hid at first when the uprising began. Soldiers found him and brought him to the provincial assembly building, where he was urged, and according to some accounts pressured, to serve as military governor (dudu). He refused several times before consenting. His acceptance was crucial: it brought the officer corps into line and made the movement appear a respectable alternative government, but it also put the revolution under the nominal leadership of a man who was not a convinced revolutionary and who had served the dynasty.

Historical Vocabulary
Military Government (junzhengfu) and Military Governor (dudu)

A military government (junzhengfu, 军政府) was an emergency body claiming to exercise civil and military authority in the name of the revolution. The term came from the Tongmenghui’s plans for a three-stage revolution in which an initial period of military government would be followed by tutelage and constitutional rule. In 1911 provinces adopted it as a flexible label: some military governments were led by revolutionaries, others by provincial assembly leaders or defecting officers.

The title dudu (都督) was borrowed from an old term for a regional military commander, and in 1911–1912 it designated the head of a provincial military government. The role mixed civil and military functions and, in practice, gave the holder control of troops. Many dudu held power into the Republic, which shaped the militarization of provincial politics examined in Chapters 58–59.

The coalition was uneasy. Revolutionary activists, New Army officers, and members of the provincial assembly such as Tang Hualong, who had been a constitutionalist leader, all took part. They issued proclamations in the name of the military government that announced the overthrow of Manchu rule, adopted the Han-dominated symbolism of the 18-star flag representing the eighteen provinces of China proper, and proposed a republic. They set up departments for military, civil, and foreign affairs, tried to restore order and markets, and sent messages to other provinces asking them to join. Foreign consuls received assurances that treaties and foreign lives and property would be respected, which helped win de facto recognition as a belligerent authority.

The tensions were evident. Some revolutionaries distrusted Li Yuanhong and wanted to remove him. Li, for his part, allegedly kept contacts open with Qing officials during the early weeks. Constitutionalists and revolutionaries disagreed about the shape of the new government: the former preferred a government of notables with a strong civil element, the latter sought a more radical program. Soldiers demanded pay and promotions. None of these conflicts destroyed the coalition in October, but they foreshadowed difficulties that would plague the new regime. The Hubei Military Government’s significance lay as much in its example as in its achievements: it showed that a government could be formed without the Qing, and that people of different political views could cooperate when faced with a common opponent.

Section 57.12

Provincial Secessions and the Disintegration of Qing Authority

Within three weeks of Wuchang, the revolution had spread beyond Hubei. On October 22, uprisings in Changsha (Hunan) and Xi’an (Shaanxi) brought down Qing governments there. On October 23 Jiujiang in Jiangxi declared independence. Shanxi followed on October 29 under Yan Xishan, a young officer who would dominate its politics for decades, and Yunnan on October 30–31 under officers including Cai E. In early November Shanghai, Guizhou, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu declared independence, and during the rest of the month Guangxi, Anhui, Guangdong, and Fujian did so. By the end of November, most of the provinces south of the Yellow River had cut their ties with the Qing government, and Sichuan, though disorderly, was moving in the same direction. Shandong briefly announced independence and withdrew it under pressure.

Provincial independence was not one phenomenon. In Hunan, revolutionaries in the New Army and secret societies led the uprising, but assembly members and constitutionalists soon took leading positions. In Shaanxi, the Gelaohui and New Army units joined forces, and the uprising had a strong anti-Manchu dimension (see 57.13). In Shanxi, Yan Xishan combined revolution and the preservation of his own power. In Yunnan, officers educated in Japan, many of whom were Tongmenghui members, led an army uprising. In Zhejiang and Jiangsu, assembly leaders and merchants played major roles, and in Shanghai merchants, the local militia, and Chen Qimei, a Tongmenghui activist, took control of the Chinese city and the arsenal. In Guangdong, where revolutionary organization was strongest, the transfer of power was largely negotiated, with the viceroy’s departure and a military government formed by revolutionaries and local elites. In some provinces, Qing governors declared independence to keep their positions.

Why did provinces leave? Motives included conviction, opportunism, fear, and local calculation. Revolutionaries wanted a republic. Section 57.13

Manchu Garrisons, Ethnic Violence, and the Question of National Unity

The anti-Manchu rhetoric of the revolutionary press had been discussed for years. In the autumn of 1911 it met reality in cities where Banner garrisons lived. These communities, descended from the military families that had conquered and then held the empire, occupied walled districts in Xi’an, Fuzhou, Nanjing, Hangzhou, Jingzhou, Taiyuan, Guangzhou, and other places, and in Beijing, where they made up a large proportion of the population. By 1911 many bannermen were poor, assimilated in dress and language to local society, and dependent on stipends that had often been reduced or unpaid. Their relationships with neighboring Han communities were mixed, but the political situation exposed them to danger.

The worst-documented violence occurred in Xi’an. On October 22, 1911, revolutionaries and troops overthrew Qing authority in the city, and in the following days fighting in the Manchu city (the walled garrison district) led to the deaths of a large number of Manchu men, women, and children; some were killed in combat, others in massacres and reprisals, and some died by suicide. Estimates of the dead range from several thousand to far larger numbers, and the figures are disputed because sources are fragmentary and politically charged. Violence against Banner communities also occurred at Taiyuan, Jingzhou, Zhenjiang, Nanjing, and elsewhere, although scale and character varied.

Outcomes elsewhere varied. In Nanjing, anti-Manchu killings followed the revolutionary capture of the city in December 1911, as Edward Rhoads documents in Manchus and Han; the scale is difficult to establish from the surviving sources. In Hangzhou and Guangzhou, by contrast, negotiated arrangements limited bloodshed, and in some other garrisons banner populations survived largely unharmed. Revolutionary leaders, including Sun Yat-sen and Huang Xing, publicly condemned violence against Manchu civilians and issued orders to protect them, partly out of principle and partly to prevent foreign intervention and to preserve the image of the revolution. Local factors counted: the presence of a determined commander on either side, the strength of local revolutionaries, the prior relations between garrison and city, and the timing of events.

Students should keep three things distinct. Anti-Manchu rhetoric was a feature of revolutionary writing, ranging from calls for political change to calls for revenge. Violence against Manchu people was a reality in some places, often carried out by soldiers, secret society members, and civilians in the confusion of revolutionary fighting, and sometimes encouraged by speeches that blurred the dynasty with the people. And efforts to define a multiethnic nation developed in response: as the revolution succeeded, its leaders realized that a purely Han nationalism would leave Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Manchuria without a reason to join the new state. On January 1, 1912, Sun Yat-sen’s inaugural declaration spoke of the union of the lands of the Han, Manchu, Mongol, Hui (Muslim), and Tibetan peoples, and the Provisional Government adopted a five-colored flag representing these five peoples. This formula, the “Five Peoples in One Union” (wuzu gonghe), was a political solution rather than a description of social reality, and it left the status of non-Han communities in practice undefined.

Regional and Frontier Context

The Revolution Beyond the Han Provinces

The revolution took place mainly in the provinces of China proper, but the Qing empire also included large frontier territories whose leaders watched events with their own interests in mind. In Mongolia, Mongol nobles and Buddhist leaders, many of them alarmed by Qing colonization policies introduced after 1901, declared independence from Qing rule in Urga (modern Ulaanbaatar) in December 1911 with Russian diplomatic and material support, and installed the Jebtsundamba Khutughtu as Bogd Khan. In Tibet, Qing garrisons and officials were weakened by mutiny and the loss of authority, and Tibetan leaders, with the Thirteenth Dalai Lama in exile in India after Qing military moves in 1910, moved to expel Qing troops during 1912. In Xinjiang, the Qing governor lost control in some areas, and uprisings occurred in Ili, though the province remained within the Chinese orbit under Yang Zengxin. In Manchuria, where Russian and Japanese interests were strong and Han migration had increased, the Qing authorities remained in place for some months, and revolutionary activity was suppressed or limited.

Frontier leaders were not simply responding to events in the Yangtze valley. They had their own histories with the Qing and their own interests, and their positions differed: some wanted autonomy within a reformed polity, some wanted independence, and some hoped to remain under the throne but not under a republic. The Republic’s claim to inherit Qing territories was therefore a political and diplomatic problem from the start, a question taken up in 57.18 and in Chapter 76. Students should treat modern borders as the result of later negotiation and conflict, not as a continuation of 1911.

Section 57.14

Civil War, Wuhan, and the Struggle for Nanjing

The Qing court responded to Wuchang by calling on its best forces. It assigned the Minister of War, Yinchang, to command and ordered divisions of the Beiyang Army to march south, appointing Feng Guozhang to lead the First Army. By late October, Qing forces were approaching Hankou. Huang Xing arrived at Wuhan on October 28, and Li Yuanhong appointed him commander of revolutionary forces. The revolutionaries had numbers and enthusiasm, and Wuhan’s arsenals gave them rifles, but they lacked training and unified command, and many of their soldiers were recently recruited volunteers and former Qing soldiers.

The battle for Hankou lasted into early November. Beiyang forces, better trained and with stronger artillery, advanced, and in the fighting and its aftermath large parts of the Hankou commercial district burned; foreign residents in the concessions watched from behind their lines. The revolutionaries withdrew to Hanyang, where fighting continued through November. Huang Xing’s attempt to hold the city failed, and Qing forces entered Hanyang on November 27, though they did not press on across the river to Wuchang. Historians debate why. Some argue that Yuan Shikai, who held the political direction of the campaign from Beijing, deliberately limited the offensive in order to keep a revolutionary government alive as a bargaining partner; others point to logistical limits, the difficulty of crossing the Yangtze, and the strength of revolutionary defenses.

Meanwhile, revolutionary forces in the lower Yangtze took the initiative. After Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu declared independence, a coalition of units from these provinces, commanded by Xu Shaozhen, a former Qing general who had switched sides, and with military and civil leaders from the Tongmenghui and the provincial assemblies, attacked Nanjing, which was held by Zhang Xun’s garrison, famous for its queue-wearing loyalty to the dynasty, and by Manchu troops. On December 2, 1911, Nanjing fell, and the capture was followed by anti-Manchu violence against the city’s banner population (see 57.13). The capture of the old Ming capital had symbolic power and practical value: it gave the revolutionaries a major city with a large arsenal, a central location on the Yangtze, and a candidate for the seat of a national government.

Everyday Life and Social History

Revolution, Civilian Displacement, and Social Change

For most people, 1911 was experienced locally and unevenly. In Wuhan, civilians fled the fighting, and the burning of parts of Hankou destroyed shops and homes, leaving thousands homeless; charitable societies, native-place associations, and the Red Cross, active in Shanghai and Hankou, organized relief, hospitals, and burials. In places that changed hands without a fight, such as Shanghai and Guangzhou, the main effects were interrupted tax collection, disrupted shipping, and rumors. Rural districts experienced the revolution through the appearance of troops, the collapse of the magistrate’s authority, requisitions, and in some places bandit activity and local feuds. Grain prices, already high after the 1910–1911 floods, were affected by the disruption of river transport.

Symbols of political allegiance changed quickly. In many places men cut off their queues, the hairstyle imposed on Han men by the Qing, either voluntarily as a statement of allegiance or under pressure from soldiers or neighbors; others refused or kept the queue out of caution, since it was unclear who would prevail. New flags replaced dragon banners, and local governments adopted the Western calendar and republican era names in some places. Students cut classes to join processions, and public meetings, newspapers, and street theater multiplied. For women, the revolution opened new possibilities and new risks: in the winter of 1911–1912 women’s armies and nursing groups were organized in Shanghai and other cities, women’s suffrage was demanded, and educated women gained public visibility. Yet legal and social restrictions on women remained largely intact, as Chapters 58 and 60 show.

Elite and ordinary experiences differed. Merchants and landowners worried about property, and many accepted the revolution if it protected them; laborers and soldiers worried about wages, food, and violence. Evidence for ordinary people’s views is sparse: few kept diaries, and most sources reflect the viewpoint of officials, students, journalists, and foreigners. Historians use petitions, newspaper reports, missionary letters, and local gazetteers to piece together the social impact of the revolution.

Section 57.15

Yuan Shikai’s Return and the Negotiated Revolution

Yuan Shikai’s career before 1911 had made him the most powerful figure in the northern army and bureaucracy. A native of Henan, he rose in the military in Korea and then in the 1890s trained a modern army at Xiaozhan near Tianjin. He became governor of Shandong during the Boxer crisis, successfully shielding the province from the worst of the violence, and then Viceroy of Zhili and Beiyang Commissioner, from which post he controlled the best-equipped forces in China and many of the most capable officials. In January 1909 Zaifeng, who had reasons of personal and political distrust, dismissed him on the pretext of a foot ailment. Yuan retired to Henan, but his officers remained in command of the Beiyang divisions.

Historical Vocabulary
The Beiyang Army (Beiyang jun)

The Beiyang Army (北洋军, “Northern Seas” army) grew from the forces trained by Yuan Shikai after 1895 and later became the core of the New Army in North China. Its name came from the “Beiyang” (northern ocean) commission, a post that supervised trade and defense in the northern coastal provinces. Its officers, such as Duan Qirui, Feng Guozhang, and Cao Kun, had personal ties to Yuan that went beyond bureaucratic loyalty; the army was thus both a state institution and a network of patronage.

This duality matters for the revolution. The court could not command the Beiyang forces without Yuan’s cooperation, and their officers were politically cautious and ambitious. After 1912 the Beiyang commanders and their successors dominated the government in Beijing, giving the name “Beiyang government” to the Republic’s first regime (Chapters 58–59).

When Wuchang rose, the court had few options. On October 14 it appointed Yuan Viceroy of Huguang with command of the campaign, but Yuan declined, citing illness, and set conditions: a parliament, a responsible cabinet, lifting of the ban on political activists, and full authority over the army and finances. Facing deterioration, the court agreed to much of this, and on October 30 issued a self-critical edict promising a constitution; the National Assembly drafted a set of Nineteen Articles on constitutional government limiting the emperor’s powers and strengthening parliament. On November 1 the cabinet of Prince Yikuang resigned and Yuan was appointed premier; he arrived in Beijing on November 13 and formed a new cabinet, with fewer imperial kinsmen.

Yuan’s approach was to combine military pressure with negotiation. He sent forces to retake Hankou and Hanyang to demonstrate his strength, but he did not commit the full resources of the Beiyang Army to destroy the revolution, and he opened lines of communication with Li Yuanhong, with Huang Xing, and with the provincial leaders. He had reasons for caution. Beiyang officers were uncertain about fighting in the south, the court’s finances were failing, with foreign banks reluctant to lend to a dynasty facing revolution, and a prolonged civil war could invite foreign intervention. Yuan also hoped to preserve his own power and the institutions he had helped build. He may have been willing, at different times, to serve a constitutional monarchy or a republic, depending on terms. Historians continue to debate his intentions, but his actions show an independent political actor playing both sides, neither a loyal servant of the dynasty nor an agent of the revolution.

In early December, a ceasefire was arranged through the mediation of foreign consuls and local leaders, and it was repeatedly extended. On December 6, Zaifeng resigned the regency, and Empress Dowager Longyu assumed responsibility for decisions on behalf of the child emperor, with Yuan as the main executive. On December 18, delegates met in Shanghai: Tang Shaoyi, an associate of Yuan, represented the north, and Wu Tingfang, a diplomat and lawyer, represented the revolutionary provinces. They discussed convening a national assembly to decide the form of the state. By then, however, events in Nanjing were moving: the revolutionary side was constructing its own government, and the question became how to turn two rival authorities into one.

China and the Wider World

Foreign Powers and the Revolution

Foreign powers held treaty rights, loans, and concessions in China and watched the revolution with concern. Britain, the largest foreign commercial presence, favored stability and tended to support Yuan Shikai as the person most likely to preserve order and treaty rights; its minister in Beijing, John Jordan, encouraged negotiation. Japan was divided: some officials and army officers wished to preserve a constitutional monarchy under Japanese influence, while private individuals and political groups supported Sun Yat-sen and his colleagues. Russia was most concerned with Mongolia and the north and supported Mongol autonomy. The United States, France, and Germany generally followed a policy of formal neutrality combined with attention to loans and trade.

The consortium of foreign banks that had financed the Huguang loan declined to supply new funds to the Qing, depriving the court of a source of money; at the same time, foreign bankers were wary of the revolutionary government’s credit. The Maritime Customs, administered by foreigners, kept collecting revenue and arranged for debt payments, ensuring foreign creditors were paid and placing a significant source of revenue beyond the control of any Chinese government. In these ways foreign powers did not decide the revolution, but their policies — neutrality, support for negotiation, and control of customs and loans — shaped its limits. The new republic’s leaders, for their part, sought recognition and promised to honor treaties, a promise that reflected their weakness as well as their diplomacy.

Section 57.16

Sun Yat-sen’s Return and the Provisional Republic at Nanjing

When the uprising occurred, Sun Yat-sen was in the United States, on a fundraising trip, and he learned of Wuchang from a newspaper in Denver, Colorado. He did not rush home. Instead he traveled to New York, then to London and Paris, where he discussed possible loans with bankers and sought assurances from the British and French governments that they would not aid the Qing. He believed that foreign diplomacy was essential and that his presence abroad might be more useful than a quick return. He arrived in Shanghai on December 25, 1911, to a hero’s welcome, though revolutionaries in China had by then acted largely without him.

The process of forming a government was already under way. Delegates from independent provinces met first in Shanghai and then in Nanjing in December to coordinate. On December 29, representatives of seventeen provinces elected Sun provisional president, with sixteen delegations in favor. Li Yuanhong was elected vice president. On January 1, 1912, Sun took office at Nanjing and proclaimed the establishment of the Republic of China; the government adopted the Gregorian calendar, with 1912 as the first year of the Republic. The cabinet included Huang Xing as army minister, Wu Tingfang as justice minister, Cai Yuanpei as education minister, and Zhang Jian, the constitutionalist industrialist, as minister of industry, reflecting a coalition of revolutionaries, former officials, and elites. A provisional Senate of provincial representatives began meeting at the end of January.

The Nanjing government had symbolic authority but little power. It lacked revenue, relied on provincial contributions and loans, and commanded an assortment of units whose loyalty was uncertain. Its supporters disagreed about policy: revolutionaries wanted a strong program, while provincial elites sought to preserve their local positions. Sun and his colleagues also knew that Yuan Shikai controlled the strongest army and that their forces could not easily defeat the Beiyang Army in a sustained campaign. Sun had said that he would resign if Yuan could secure the dynasty’s end and support the republic, a position he made public in January. For Sun and some of his colleagues, this was an act of principle and a recognition of reality: the priority was to end the monarchy and avoid a long civil war that foreign powers might exploit. For others, including some close to Huang Xing and Song Jiaoren, it was a risky concession.

This negotiation stage is often described as a compromise between revolutionary legitimacy and military power. Sun’s government offered Yuan the presidency, with conditions: the capital should be at Nanjing, the president would pledge allegiance to the republican constitution, and the Senate would approve. Yuan, who preferred to remain in the north where his base lay, accepted the substance and evaded the conditions, and the question of how binding they were would shape politics in the months that followed (Chapter 58).

Section 57.17

Imperial Abdication and the End of the Qing Dynasty

In January 1912, the court in Beijing was divided. Empress Dowager Longyu, widow of the Guangxu Emperor, presided over discussions with the princes and officials about whether to continue resisting. A group of Manchu princes and military officers, including the Imperial Clansmen Association and the officer Liangbi, favored resistance and the continuation of the monarchy. Others, including many Han ministers and the Beiyang generals, argued that resistance was hopeless. In late January, Liangbi, a prominent opponent of abdication, was killed by a bomb thrown by a revolutionary, a blow to the hardliners. On January 26–27, Duan Qirui and other Beiyang commanders sent a telegram to the court urging the establishment of a republic, a message that signaled that the army would not fight for the throne.

Negotiations between Yuan and the Nanjing side were conducted by telegram and through emissaries. The principal issues were the terms for the emperor and the imperial household, the arrangements for transferring power, and Yuan’s own status. The Nanjing government agreed on a set of “Articles of Favorable Treatment for the Great Qing Emperor after His Abdication,” which guaranteed that the emperor would retain his title and be treated with the respect due a foreign monarch, would receive an annual allowance of four million taels (later converted to dollars), could live temporarily in the Forbidden City and later at the Summer Palace, and that the imperial ancestral temples and tombs would be protected; other articles guaranteed Banner stipends would be maintained until other arrangements could be made, and promised that Manchus, Mongols, Hui, and Tibetans would enjoy equal rights with the Han.

Primary Source Window

The Abdication Edict of the Xuantong Emperor, February 12, 1912

Issuer: Issued in the name of the child emperor Puyi (reign title Xuantong) by Empress Dowager Longyu · Date: February 12, 1912 · Type: Imperial edict

Context: The edict was drafted after weeks of negotiation involving Yuan Shikai, the court, and the Nanjing government. It was published in the official gazette and widely reprinted, and was addressed to the Qing officials, army, and the public. Zhang Jian, a leading constitutionalist, is credited by many accounts with helping draft the text, which suggests the involvement of both sides in its wording.

Paraphrase: The edict states that the people’s hearts incline toward a republic and that the court, in order to spare the people the suffering of civil war, transfers sovereignty to the nation. It authorizes Yuan Shikai to organize a provisional republican government in consultation with the revolutionary side, and declares that the lands of the Manchu, Han, Mongol, Hui, and Tibetan peoples are to remain united as one Republic of China.

Analysis: The edict frames abdication as an act of the court’s benevolence and Heaven’s will as expressed by the people, using traditional language of concern for the people’s welfare to justify a radical change. It does not describe the dynasty’s defeat. By authorizing Yuan to organize a government, it gave him a role that the Nanjing leaders had not wanted him to derive from the court, an ambiguity that mattered: Yuan could claim that his authority came from the throne as well as from the Republic. The text also asserts territorial continuity by naming the five peoples, a formula that the Republic adopted but did not define. Its limits are those of any court document: it records what the court chose to say, not the internal pressures, the financial and military weakness, or the voices of Manchu hardliners and ordinary subjects. It says nothing about how people in Mongolia or Tibet viewed the transfer.

Questions for students:

  1. How does the edict justify the end of the dynasty, and what reasons does it leave out?
  2. Who wrote and issued the edict, and what purposes might they have had in the wording they chose?
  3. What does the edict reveal about the role of Yuan Shikai and about the nature of the settlement?
  4. What can historians not learn from the edict alone about the reasons for abdication or about how different groups received it?

The reaction was mixed. In Beijing and the provinces the abdication was announced with little disturbance; many officials, in the manner of earlier dynastic transitions, accepted a new authority. Some loyalists protested, a few committed suicide, and a small circle of Manchu officials and a number of Han scholars remained attached to the dynasty and its memory, some plotting restoration (Chapter 58). The former emperor remained in the Forbidden City under the arrangements of the Articles; the child Puyi was a symbol more than an actor, and his later life belongs to later chapters. On February 13 Sun Yat-sen submitted his resignation to the Senate and recommended Yuan; on February 15 the Senate elected Yuan Shikai provisional president. The transfer was not yet complete: Yuan was inaugurated at Beijing on March 10, 1912, and Sun’s resignation took effect with that inauguration, an episode belonging to Chapter 58. The revolution’s first phase, the end of dynastic rule, was over, though the questions of where the government would sit, what constitution it would follow, and who would control the army and the provinces were still open.

Section 57.18

The Historical Meaning and Limits of the 1911 Revolution

The achievements of the revolution were real. It ended the monarchy that had defined Chinese political order for more than two thousand years and made republican government, however imperfect, the standard of legitimacy. Even Yuan Shikai’s attempt to become emperor in 1915–1916 (Chapter 58) failed in part because the idea of monarchy no longer commanded support. The revolution established the principle that sovereignty belongs to the people or nation, introduced elections, parliaments, and parties into political life, and opened space for the cultural movements examined in Chapter 60. It also demonstrated that provinces could act collectively and that the Qing’s military and bureaucratic supports could be peeled away.

Its limits were also real. Social structure changed little in the countryside; landholding, tenancy, and local elite power remained largely intact. The army’s politicization and the autonomy of provincial commanders were strengthened, not reduced. The fiscal weakness of the state was inherited by the Republic, which struggled to collect taxes and relied on foreign loans. Foreign treaty privileges continued. The claim that the Republic inherited Qing territories remained contested, as the declarations of Mongolia and the events in Tibet showed. And the revolutionary coalition, a loose alliance of different groups held together by opposition to the dynasty, had no agreed program once it achieved its first goal.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Qing Empire to Republic

What Changed

  • Hereditary monarchy ended as the basis of government; the claim to rule rested on the nation and, in theory, on citizens.
  • New institutions — a provisional senate, provincial military governments, a cabinet — and a new political vocabulary appeared.
  • Manchu privileges were curtailed, and Banner institutions began to dissolve; the status of Manchu people changed sharply.
  • The Western calendar and new national symbols were adopted; queue-cutting and changes in dress marked a visible break.

What Stayed the Same

  • Provincial and military power-holders, many of whom served the Qing, remained influential, and Yuan Shikai and the Beiyang officers moved into the center.
  • Rural social structure, landholding, taxation, and the position of local elites continued with little immediate change.
  • Treaty-port privileges, foreign loans, and the Maritime Customs system persisted.
  • Many officials and clerks continued in their posts, and much administrative practice carried over.

Who experienced change most directly? Manchu bannermen, urban students and activists, and politically engaged elites experienced the sharpest changes; many peasants, whose lives were shaped by local conditions and harvests, often noticed less. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both sides prevents treating 1911 as either a complete rupture or an empty change of names.

Historical Debate

How Revolutionary Was the 1911 Revolution?

The question: Was the Xinhai Revolution a genuine revolution that transformed Chinese politics, or a limited change of regime in which elites and military leaders replaced the dynasty and left society largely unchanged?

Interpretation A — A fundamental political revolution: Scholars in this tradition stress that ending the monarchy, introducing republican legitimacy, and creating new political arenas were revolutionary in themselves. Chinese historical writing under the Nationalist government and in the People’s Republic has often described 1911 as a landmark, though with different emphases: the former as the founding of the Republic under Sun Yat-sen’s leadership, the latter as a “bourgeois-democratic revolution” that was incomplete because it did not transform the social order or end imperialism.
Interpretation B — An elite-led, limited, and regionally varied transformation: Other historians, including those who have studied Hunan and Hubei in depth such as Joseph Esherick, emphasize the role of provincial elites and the army, the continuity of local social structure, and the diversity of motives, and argue that the revolution was less a single movement than a collapse of central authority in which regional power-holders took over. They note that the revolutionary organizations were small and divided and that the outcome depended on elite and military defections.

Evidence: Historians examine the social background of provincial leaders, changes in landholding and taxation, the composition of new governments, the persistence of officials, and the extent of popular participation. They compare provinces and trace individual careers across the 1911 divide.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians explain the problems of the early Republic: if the revolution was thin, the weakness of the new order is easier to understand; if it was deep, the later failure becomes more puzzling. It also influences how political movements claim the revolution’s legacy.

Student task: What definition of “revolution” do you think is most useful for historians, and which evidence from this chapter best supports your answer? What would you want to know about villages and ordinary people to settle the question?

These debates should not obscure the fact that the revolution was a moment of historical contingency. A few differences — Ruicheng’s failure to secure Wuchang, a different decision by the Beiyang Army, a more adept court policy in 1910–1911 — could have produced other outcomes. The period from October 1911 to February 1912 demonstrated how a political order that seemed durable could unravel when its elite supporters concluded it could no longer protect their interests, and how a settlement negotiated in haste could bequeath unresolved problems.

Section 57.19

Chapter Review and Fall of the Qing Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the end of the Qing dynasty as the product of several interacting developments rather than a single cause or a single leader. Revolutionary nationalists, organized in the Tongmenghui and in local groups, built networks of students, soldiers, overseas Chinese, and secret societies but failed repeatedly in armed uprisings. Constitutional monarchists and provincial elites, disappointed by the court’s limited reforms and by the 1911 cabinet and railway policy, lost confidence in the dynasty. The Sichuan railway protests and the transfer of troops weakened the Qing in the central Yangtze; the accidental exposure of conspirators in Wuchang triggered a mutiny that became a revolution because provincial leaders, commanders, and assemblies chose to leave the dynasty. Violence against Manchu communities occurred in some cities, and the new leaders sought a multiethnic formula. Military stalemate and the political strategy of Yuan Shikai produced a negotiated abdication that ended the dynasty and transferred authority to Yuan, leaving the Republic with unresolved problems.

Students should be able to demonstrate several skills at this checkpoint: reconstruct the sequence of events from October 1911 to February 1912 and explain why the order mattered; compare the programs of revolutionaries and constitutionalists; interpret the Tongmenghui program and the abdication edict as sources with particular purposes; explain regional differences in how provinces and frontiers responded; assess competing interpretations of the revolution’s depth; and avoid common errors — attributing the Wuchang Uprising to Sun Yat-sen, treating provincial secessions as ideologically uniform, ignoring ethnic violence, or treating the abdication as a battlefield victory. The review sections that follow provide practice in each of these skills. Chapter 58 begins where this one ends, with the Republic in practice.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Qing dynasty fell in 1911–1912 not because of a single cause, a single leader, or an inevitable decline, but because a decade of reform raised expectations the court could not satisfy while military and political resources shifted to provincial elites, officers, and revolutionary networks. The dynasty in 1911 retained a large army, a functioning bureaucracy, and many supporters. It lost the confidence of the educated, propertied, and armed groups whose cooperation it needed.

Revolutionary nationalism developed in the first decade of the twentieth century among students, journalists, and exiles who combined ideas of national sovereignty, popular citizenship, and anti-Manchu politics. Sun Yat-sen, the most visible organizer, built a movement across overseas Chinese communities, Japan, and South China, and in 1905 helped form the Tongmenghui, whose program called for expelling the Manchu rulers, founding a republic, and equalizing land rights. The alliance was a loose coalition, repeatedly divided, and its uprisings, including the Huanghuagang attempt of April 1911, failed. Women such as Qiu Jin took part, and failure produced martyrs and lessons about the importance of organizing within the army.

Constitutional monarchists, led intellectually by Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao and politically by provincial assembly leaders such as Zhang Jian and Tang Hualong, pursued a different path. They sought an early parliament and a government accountable to elites. The court’s refusal to move faster, and then the formation in May 1911 of a cabinet dominated by Manchu princes, convinced many of them that the dynasty would not share power. The nationalization of trunk railways and the foreign loan, announced in the same month, hit provincial investors and sharpened the sense that the court was acting against provincial and national interests.

The Sichuan railway protest movement, from June to September 1911, turned this grievance into mass action and then armed insurrection, especially after the arrests and shootings in Chengdu on September 7. The court’s decision to send Hubei troops to Sichuan reduced the garrison at Wuhan. When the accidental exposure of conspirators on October 9 prompted a mutiny on October 10, the Wuchang Uprising succeeded in part because of circumstances: the flight of the viceroy, the absence of troops, and the seizure of an arsenal. Sun Yat-sen was in the United States; the immediate leadership came from local groups and New Army soldiers, and Li Yuanhong, a reluctant brigade commander, became military governor.

Within weeks, provinces across the south and center declared independence, for reasons ranging from revolutionary conviction to constitutionalist disillusionment, military calculation, and local politics. The disintegration was a cascade of local decisions rather than the progress of a single movement. In several cities, notably Xi’an, Manchu garrison communities suffered severe violence, while in Nanjing, too, killings followed the city’s capture, and in places such as Hangzhou and Guangzhou negotiated arrangements limited bloodshed. The revolution forced leaders to confront the question of whether a Han nation or a multiethnic polity would succeed the Qing; the formula of five peoples united in one republic answered it in words but not in practice, and Mongol and Tibetan leaders asserted their own positions.

The military situation reached a stalemate. Beiyang forces under Feng Guozhang retook Hankou and Hanyang, but the revolutionaries took Nanjing, and the court turned to Yuan Shikai, whom it had dismissed in 1909. Yuan used his command of the northern army as leverage over both sides, negotiating with the revolutionaries while pressing the court. Foreign powers remained neutral, favored negotiation, and, through the customs and the banks, shaped the financial limits within which both sides operated. Sun Yat-sen returned in December and was chosen provisional president; the Republic was proclaimed on January 1, 1912, at Nanjing, in the south, while Qing authority persisted in the north.

The abdication edict of February 12, 1912, issued in the name of the child emperor by Empress Dowager Longyu, ended the dynasty through a negotiated settlement that included favorable treatment for the imperial house and a transfer of authority to Yuan. It was the product of bargaining among the court, the Beiyang generals, and the revolutionary leadership, with each hoping to secure its interests. On February 15 Yuan was elected provisional president (he was inaugurated on March 10), an outcome that satisfied those who prized order and disappointed those who hoped the revolution would transform the state.

Historians disagree about how revolutionary the events were. The end of the monarchy and the acceptance of republican legitimacy were major changes, while rural social structure, foreign privileges, fiscal weakness, and the power of provincial and military actors continued. The revolution’s coalition lacked a common program beyond the removal of the dynasty, and its unresolved questions — over constitution, territory, army, and the relation of center and province — define the agenda of the next chapters.

Chapter Reflection

The 1911 Revolution ended the imperial political order, but it did not end the problems that had produced it. This chapter shows how a dynasty can lose its capacity to rule not through one catastrophic defeat but through the gradual withdrawal of the support of people whose cooperation was indispensable. It also shows how revolutions are made by many hands: students and soldiers, merchants and assemblymen, women and secret society members, officers and diplomats, as well as by leaders whose names became famous.

The chapter’s themes recur throughout Part X. The weakness of central finance, the autonomy of provincial and military leaders, the uncertain status of frontier regions, and the competing visions of the nation — Han, multiethnic, republican, or monarchical — all shaped the early Republic, the warlord period, and the Nationalist and Communist movements (Chapters 58–65). The language of nation and sovereignty developed in the years before 1911 would remain central to Chinese politics in every later period.

The revolution also connects China to a wider world: to Japan, where revolutionaries studied and organized; to overseas Chinese communities that financed them; to foreign powers whose neutrality and financial influence set limits on the outcome; and to a global moment of constitutional and revolutionary change. Yet the events must be understood in their own context, not as the inevitable prelude to later regimes. At each stage there were alternatives, and the contingency of events — the accident in Hankou, the decisions of commanders, the choices of provincial assemblies — shaped what followed.

For students, the chapter offers a model of historical thinking: distinguishing rhetoric from practice, causes from catalysts, and the claims of commemorative traditions from the evidence of contemporary sources. Understanding 1911 as a negotiated, uneven, and unfinished transformation helps students see Chinese history as diverse, contested, and open-ended, and prepares them to follow, in Chapter 58, the struggle to build the republic that the revolution had proclaimed.
