Religion, Literature, and Intellectual Life Under the Tang

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 845, imperial commissioners across the Tang empire began carrying out an order that, within a few months, closed thousands of Buddhist monasteries and shrines, returned more than a quarter million monks and nuns to lay taxpaying status, confiscated monastic land, melted down bronze images and bells for coinage, and dissolved institutions that in some cases had stood for over two centuries. A religious tradition that had received lavish imperial patronage for generations, that had shaped court ritual, funded translation projects, sponsored some of the empire's most accomplished artists, and structured the religious lives of large numbers of ordinary believers, was suddenly and forcibly stripped of much of its institutional presence. After Wuzong's death in 846, his successor reversed the harshest measures within roughly a year, and Buddhist institutions gradually recovered much of their social presence, though not all of their earlier wealth and privilege. The episode, remembered by its era name as the Huichang persecution, offers an unusually vivid illustration of a truth that runs through this entire chapter: Tang religious and intellectual life was rich, plural, and genuinely creative, but it existed within relationships of unequal and shifting political power, in which imperial favor could be granted, withdrawn, and granted again according to the changing calculations of the court.

Chapters 25 through 27 examined how the Tang state was built, how its government, economy, and society functioned, and how Tang China connected with Central Asia, Korea, Japan, Southeast Asia, the Tibetan Empire, South Asia, and the wider Islamic world. This chapter turns inward, asking a different kind of question. Once goods, travelers, texts, and ideas arrived in Tang China — whether carried by pilgrims returning from India, merchants traveling the Silk Roads, missionaries from Persia and Central Asia, or officials moving between capital and countryside — what did Tang subjects actually do with them? What did people in Tang China believe, study, read, write, paint, carve, print, and argue about? What institutions made this rich cultural and religious life possible, and what institutions constrained it?

The chapter's central argument is that Tang intellectual and cultural life cannot be reduced to any single tradition, court, or genre. Buddhism, in several distinct schools and countless local forms, was thoroughly embedded in Tang society at every social level, from emperors who sponsored monumental translation projects to villagers who supported a local shrine. Daoism, reorganized as both a philosophical inheritance and an increasingly formalized religious institution, received extraordinary imperial favor because the ruling Li family claimed descent from the sage Laozi, even as it coexisted, competed, and blended with Buddhist practice in the daily lives of many Tang subjects. Confucian learning structured the education of officials and the moral vocabulary of the court without functioning as an exclusive belief system, and by the ninth century a small but influential group of scholars had begun to argue that Confucian learning should displace, rather than merely coexist with, Buddhist and Daoist practice. Foreign religious communities — practitioners of the East Syriac form of Christianity that Tang sources called Jingjiao, Manichaean communities linked to Central Asian and Uyghur populations, and Zoroastrian institutions associated chiefly with Sogdian and Persian immigrants — built temples, translated texts, and won periods of court favor, though their institutional security proved considerably more fragile than that of Buddhism or Daoism.

Literary culture flourished alongside this religious diversity. Tang poetry, produced in enormous quantity by court officials, provincial administrators, Buddhist monks, Daoist priests, courtesans, soldiers, and travelers alike, became one of the most celebrated bodies of literature in world history, and it did so not as a detached aesthetic exercise but as a social and political practice woven into examination culture, official careers, friendship, mourning, exile, and protest. Visual and material culture — painting, sculpture, ceramics, tomb furnishings, textiles, and architecture, much of it shaped by the Central Asian and Buddhist influences introduced in Chapter 27 — recorded and reshaped Tang aesthetic sensibilities in ways that survive today chiefly through archaeological recovery rather than continuous transmission. And in the domain of written communication, the Tang period witnessed a gradual, uneven transition from an overwhelmingly manuscript-based textual culture toward the earliest documented uses of woodblock printing, a technological change whose social consequences would only fully unfold in the centuries that followed.

This chapter asks students to hold two ideas together that can seem, at first, to be in tension. The first is that Tang China supported one of the most religiously and intellectually diverse societies in the premodern world, a society in which multiple traditions coexisted, borrowed from one another, and often flourished under the same roof or within the same individual's devotional life. The second is that this diversity was never simply guaranteed. It existed because emperors, empresses, officials, and local elites chose, for reasons of piety, politics, diplomacy, taxation, and personal belief, to permit, fund, regulate, or occasionally suppress particular religious and intellectual communities. Tolerance, patronage, and institutional security were not fixed features of Tang society; they were outcomes of specific historical decisions that could, and did, change.

Evidence for this chapter comes from an unusually wide range of sources. Buddhist sutras and commentaries, monastic account records, and pilgrim travelogues document religious institutions and practice. Daoist scriptures, temple inscriptions, and court edicts document official patronage. The 781 stele erected outside Chang'an documents the history and doctrine of the Church of the East in Tang China in the words of the community itself. Thousands of manuscripts recovered from the sealed library cave at Dunhuang, spanning religious texts, contracts, letters, and school exercises, preserve an extraordinarily direct record of written culture across social classes. Tang poetry collections, compiled both during the dynasty and by later editors, preserve tens of thousands of individual poems. Excavated tombs preserve painted murals, ceramic figurines, and everyday objects that manuscript and literary sources rarely describe. And the anti-Buddhist memorials of officials such as Han Yu, alongside the imperial edicts ordering and later reversing the Huichang suppression, preserve the arguments Tang elites made for and against particular religious communities. Each source type has real value and real limits, and students should practice weighing them against one another throughout this chapter.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain how Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian learning, and smaller foreign religious traditions coexisted and competed within Tang society; describe the institutions — monasteries, temples, schools, academies, and scriptoria — that sustained religious and intellectual life; analyze Tang poetry, art, and material culture as historical evidence with both value and limitation; trace the gradual emergence of printing alongside continued manuscript culture; and explain why Tang religious and intellectual pluralism, however genuine, always operated within a framework of unequal and changing political power, a lesson the chapter's closing sections and the Huichang suppression make unmistakably clear.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the major schools and practices of Tang Buddhism and describe Buddhism's integration into Tang society at multiple social levels.
  2. Describe the institutional functions of Buddhist monasteries and nunneries, including their economic, educational, charitable, and artistic roles.
  3. Trace the development of organized Daoist religion under Tang imperial patronage and its relationship to the ruling house's claimed descent from Laozi.
  4. Analyze the content, institutions, and limits of Confucian learning in Tang society, including its connection to the examination system.
  5. Identify the Church of the East (Jingjiao), Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism as they existed within Tang China, and assess the conditions that shaped their institutional security.
  6. Interpret Tang poetry as both literary achievement and historical evidence, while recognizing its conventions and limitations as a source.
  7. Evaluate Tang art, sculpture, and material culture as evidence for aesthetic change, religious practice, and cross-cultural exchange.
  8. Distinguish manuscript culture from the emergence of woodblock printing and assess the significance and limits of early Tang printing.
  9. Compare the experiences of different religious and social communities within Tang China's plural religious environment.
  10. Explain the causes, events, and consequences of the Huichang suppression of Buddhism and connect it to broader patterns of state regulation of religion.
  11. Assess the concept of "tolerance" as applied to Tang religious life, distinguishing genuine pluralism from unconditional or guaranteed religious freedom.
  12. Connect Tang religious, literary, and intellectual developments to the social and international contexts established in Chapters 26 and 27.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Buddhism become so thoroughly embedded within Tang society, and what forms did that embeddedness take among different social classes?
  2. What did it mean, institutionally, for a monastery to hold land, wealth, and clergy benefiting from significant tax and corvée privileges, and why might a state tolerate this for generations and then reverse course?
  3. Why did the Tang imperial house invest so heavily in Daoism specifically, and how did this patronage shape Daoist institutions?
  4. In what ways did Confucian learning function differently from a religion, and in what ways did it resemble one?
  5. What conditions allowed foreign religious communities such as adherents of the Church of the East, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism to establish institutions in Tang China, and what made their position more precarious than that of Buddhism or Daoism?
  6. What can Tang poetry tell historians about political events, social conditions, and personal experience, and what can it not reliably tell them?
  7. How did cross-cultural artistic influence from Central Asia and the Buddhist world get transformed into distinctly Tang forms of art and material culture?
  8. Why did woodblock printing emerge when and where it did, and why did manuscript culture continue to coexist with print for centuries afterward?
  9. What does the word "tolerance" obscure when applied uncritically to a society in which religious institutions depended on the ongoing favor of an emperor who could, and eventually did, order their dissolution?
  10. How did the Huichang suppression of 845 reveal the underlying relationship between religious institutions and state power that had existed, less visibly, throughout the preceding two centuries?

Historical Context

By the time the developments examined in this chapter unfolded, Buddhism had already been present in China for several centuries, having entered from Central Asia and India along the trade and pilgrimage routes examined in Chapters 16 and 27, and having taken deep root during the political division of the Age of Division discussed in Chapter 23. Northern and southern dynasties alike had patronized Buddhist translation, monument-building, and monastic institutions, so that by the founding of the Tang in 618, Buddhism was not a newly arrived foreign import but a long-established, socially diverse, and institutionally sophisticated presence across Chinese society. What the Tang period added was not the introduction of Buddhism to China but an extraordinary intensification, diversification, and, at the Tang court specifically, formalization of Buddhist institutional life, occurring alongside comparable developments in organized Daoism, Confucian education, and literary culture.

The political framework within which this cultural life unfolded was established in Chapters 25 and 26: a centralized bureaucratic empire governed through a legal code, an examination system that increasingly, though not exclusively, recruited officials on the basis of classical learning, and a capital at Chang'an that had become, by the seventh and eighth centuries, one of the largest and most cosmopolitan cities in the world. Chapter 27 established that this cosmopolitanism was not merely decorative; Chang'an and other Tang cities hosted resident communities of Sogdians, Persians, Central Asian Turks, Koreans, Japanese, Southeast Asians, and other foreign populations, many of whom brought their own religious traditions, languages, and cultural practices with them. This chapter examines what happened to those traditions once established within Tang society — how they were organized, patronized, regulated, and, in some cases, eventually suppressed.

Economically, the institutions examined in this chapter depended heavily on the agricultural and commercial prosperity described in Chapter 26. Buddhist and Daoist monasteries accumulated land, sometimes through direct imperial or aristocratic donation and sometimes through purchase, tenancy arrangements, or the pious donations of ordinary believers, and this landholding, along with the clerical tax and corvée privileges granted to religious institutions and their resident clergy, which varied over time and by legal category, connected the history of religion directly to the history of taxation, landholding, and state finance examined elsewhere in this textbook. When the court later moved to suppress Buddhist institutions in 845, fiscal considerations — the desire to return productive land and able-bodied labor to the tax rolls — operated alongside doctrinal and political objections, a reminder that religious history and economic history cannot be neatly separated.

Socially, this chapter's subject matter touched every level of Tang society, though unevenly. Emperors and empresses sponsored monumental translation projects, imperial temples, and elaborate court rituals; provincial officials and local gentry funded regional monasteries, temples, and schools; ordinary farmers, artisans, and urban residents participated in local religious festivals, made modest donations to nearby shrines, and, in some cases, entered religious life themselves as monks, nuns, or lay devotees. Literary culture, by contrast, remained considerably more concentrated among the literate elite, though the boundaries of that elite were not absolutely fixed, and Buddhist and Daoist religious writing reached far beyond the narrow circle of examination candidates and court officials who produced most surviving secular poetry.

Finally, students should carry forward from Chapter 27 an understanding of how thoroughly Tang China was connected to wider Asian and Eurasian networks of religious and cultural exchange. Buddhism arrived from India by way of Central Asia; Manichaeism arrived from Persia by way of Central Asia and, later, through connections with the Uyghur steppe empire; the Church of the East arrived from Sasanian Persia and the broader Syriac Christian world; Zoroastrian communities followed Sogdian and Persian migration and trade networks. None of these traditions developed in China in isolation from their wider Eurasian contexts, even as each of them changed, adapted, and, in the case of Buddhism especially, developed distinctively Chinese forms over the centuries of settlement examined in this chapter.

Section 28.1

Buddhism in Tang China

By the seventh century, Buddhism in China was not a recently transplanted foreign religion but a mature, deeply rooted, and internally diverse tradition with several centuries of continuous institutional development behind it, much of it examined in Chapter 23's discussion of the Age of Division. What the Tang period contributed was not Buddhism's introduction but its further elaboration, its unprecedented degree of imperial sponsorship at certain points in the dynasty, and the flowering of several distinct schools of thought and practice that would shape Chinese Buddhism, and through it much of East Asian Buddhism, for centuries afterward.

Tang Buddhism should be understood as a family of related but distinct traditions rather than a single doctrine or practice. The Tiantai school, tracing its lineage to teachers of the Sui period, organized the vast and sometimes contradictory body of Buddhist scripture into a systematic hierarchy of teachings, culminating in the Lotus Sutra, and developed sophisticated meditative and philosophical practices around this framework. The Huayan school, drawing especially on the Avatamsaka Sutra, developed an elaborate metaphysics of mutual interpenetration among all phenomena, influential among court-connected scholar-monks and later shaping strands of East Asian Buddhist philosophy well beyond China. Chan Buddhism, whose distinctive teaching lineages developed and diversified across the Tang period, emphasized direct meditative insight and, in some lineages, a suspicion of purely textual or scholastic approaches to enlightenment, developing an influential body of teaching stories, recorded sayings, and lineage narratives that would shape later Chinese, Korean, and Japanese Buddhism profoundly. Pure Land devotional practice, centered on faith in and invocation of the Buddha Amitabha and the aspiration for rebirth in his western paradise, offered a devotional path accessible to laypeople without extensive philosophical training or monastic discipline, and became, over time, one of the most widely practiced forms of Buddhist piety among ordinary believers. Esoteric or tantric Buddhist practices, transmitted from India by traveling teachers during the eighth century, introduced ritual, visualization, and initiatory practices that found particular favor at points within the Tang court, especially in connection with rituals believed to protect the state.

This internal diversity should not obscure the degree to which Buddhist practice, in one form or another, touched nearly every level of Tang society. At the highest level, emperors and empresses sponsored major translation projects, funded the construction and maintenance of imperial temples, and, in some cases, took personal religious instruction from prominent monks; Empress Wu, discussed in Chapter 25 for her political career, was also a notable patron of Buddhist institutions and made explicit use of Buddhist scripture and prophecy to help legitimate her unprecedented assumption of imperial title. At an intermediate level, officials, provincial elites, and wealthy merchants funded regional monasteries, commissioned Buddhist art, and participated in devotional societies. At the level of ordinary people, villagers and urban residents alike supported local shrines, participated in religious festivals tied to the Buddhist calendar, sought the ritual services of monks and nuns at moments of illness, death, or crisis, and, in considerable numbers, entered monastic life themselves, whether from genuine religious vocation, family strategy, economic necessity, or some combination of these motives.

Xuanzang's career after his return from India in 645, examined in Chapter 27 chiefly for the journey itself, deserves attention here for its intellectual and institutional significance. Under substantial imperial sponsorship, Xuanzang led a major translation bureau at Chang'an that produced Chinese versions of a large body of Buddhist philosophical texts, particularly works associated with the Yogacara or "consciousness-only" school of Indian Buddhist philosophy, which analyzed the workings of perception, cognition, and mental construction in exacting philosophical detail. Xuanzang's translations, prized for their precision and fidelity to Sanskrit originals, gave Chinese Buddhist scholars direct access to sophisticated Indian philosophical debates that earlier, sometimes looser translations had only imperfectly conveyed, and his own school of thought, though it did not survive as a major independent tradition for many centuries afterward, exercised significant influence on subsequent Chinese Buddhist philosophy. The scale of this translation project — teams of scholars, scribes, and revisers working under state patronage over years — illustrates how thoroughly Buddhist textual scholarship had become an institutionalized, state-supported enterprise by the mid-Tang period.

Students should resist two common oversimplifications when studying Tang Buddhism. The first is treating Buddhism as a monolithic tradition with a single set of beliefs and practices; the schools described above differed substantially in doctrine, practice, and social base, and individual believers often drew on more than one tradition rather than adhering exclusively to a single school. The second is treating Buddhism as somehow separate from "authentic" Chinese culture, a framing that later anti-Buddhist polemicists, examined in Section 28.4, sometimes encouraged but that does not reflect the actual social and institutional depth Buddhism had achieved by the Tang period. By the seventh and eighth centuries, Buddhism was as thoroughly a part of Tang Chinese society as Confucian learning or Daoist practice, even as debates over its proper place within that society, examined later in this chapter, continued throughout the dynasty.

Section 28.2

Monasteries and Religious Institutions

Understanding Tang Buddhism requires moving from belief and doctrine to institution and organization. Buddhist monasteries and nunneries in Tang China functioned simultaneously as religious communities, economic enterprises, educational centers, charitable institutions, and, in many cases, significant landholders — a combination of roles that made them deeply woven into the fabric of Tang social and economic life, and that also made them, as this section and Section 28.9 will show, subject to close and sometimes hostile state scrutiny.

Entry into monastic life required formal ordination, a process that the Tang state regulated closely, in part because ordained monks and nuns received significant, though not absolute or uniform, exemptions from the labor service and tax obligations that fell on ordinary registered households, examined in Chapter 26; the precise scope of these privileges varied over time and, after the fiscal reforms of 780 discussed in Chapter 26, some categories of temple land became generally taxable except where specific privileges applied. The state issued ordination certificates, periodically conducted censuses of religious personnel, and at various points restricted or expanded the number of individuals permitted to enter religious life, reflecting an ongoing tension between genuine religious sponsorship and concern over the fiscal consequences of a growing tax-privileged clerical population. Monasteries varied enormously in size and wealth, from small rural establishments supported by a handful of nearby households to vast, richly endowed institutions in and around Chang'an and other major cities, some of which held substantial agricultural estates, complete with tenant farmers, mills, oil presses, and other productive assets.

Monastic estates, generally built up through a combination of imperial and aristocratic donation, purchase, and the pious gifts of ordinary believers seeking religious merit, connected monastic institutions directly to the agrarian economy examined in Chapter 26. Because some categories of monastic property and ordained clergy enjoyed significant tax and corvée privileges, the growth of monastic landholding represented a real and, over time, increasingly significant loss of taxable resources to the state — a concern that recurs throughout this chapter and that becomes central to the Huichang suppression examined later. At the same time, monasteries performed genuinely valuable economic and social functions: they operated mills and oil presses that served surrounding communities, extended loans, occasionally functioned as informal banks or pawnshops, provided charitable relief during famine or crisis, and offered lodging to travelers and pilgrims along major routes.

Monasteries also functioned as major centers of education, manuscript production, and artistic patronage. Monastic scriptoria employed and trained scribes who copied sutras, commentaries, and other religious texts by hand, a labor-intensive process that, as Section 28.8 discusses, gradually began to be supplemented by woodblock printing during the Tang period. Monastic workshops commissioned and produced religious art, from monumental cave sculpture, examined further in Chapter 23's discussion of cave temples, to painted murals, portable images, and ritual implements. Some monasteries maintained substantial libraries, and educated monks engaged in sophisticated philosophical and textual scholarship of the kind exemplified by Xuanzang's translation projects.

Historical Vocabulary
Changzhu (常住) — Permanent Monastic Estate

Changzhu refers to the permanent, corporately held property of a Buddhist monastery — land, buildings, mills, and other productive assets — held in the name of the religious institution itself rather than any individual monk or nun. Whether and how fully a given piece of changzhu property was exempt from ordinary taxation and labor obligations varied over time and by category, particularly after the fiscal reforms of 780 discussed in Chapter 26; exemption was a frequently granted privilege attached to religious institutions and personnel rather than an automatic feature of the property category itself. The concept matters historically because it explains how Buddhist monasteries accumulated substantial, self-sustaining economic resources over generations, resources that supported the monasteries' religious, educational, and charitable functions examined in this section, but that also became a central target of state concern when fiscal pressures mounted, culminating in the large-scale confiscation of monastic estates during the Huichang suppression of 845.

Relations between monastic institutions and local communities took many forms. Villagers and urban residents supported local temples and shrines with donations, labor, and participation in religious festivals; wealthy patrons commissioned specific artworks, funded the construction of new halls or pagodas, or sponsored the copying of particular sutras in the hope of accumulating religious merit for themselves or deceased relatives; and monasteries, in turn, provided ritual services — funerary rites, prayers for healing, ceremonies marking important life events — that met needs the state and ordinary family structures could not fully satisfy on their own. This dense web of reciprocal relationships helps explain both why Buddhist institutions grew so extensive during the Tang period and why, when the state eventually moved against them in 845, the consequences were felt not only by monks and nuns themselves but by the wider communities that depended on monastic charity, ritual services, and economic functions.

The tension between religious autonomy and state supervision that runs through this section reached its most dramatic expression in the Huichang suppression of 845, examined in detail in this chapter's Cause and Effect feature and again in Section 28.9. Students should understand this event not as an isolated or inexplicable act of religious hostility but as the culmination of long-standing concerns, present throughout the Tang period and indeed throughout the preceding centuries of Buddhist institutional growth, about the fiscal, economic, and political consequences of an increasingly wealthy religious establishment, substantial portions of which benefited from clerical tax and corvée privileges, operating alongside, and to some degree independent of, the ordinary structures of Tang government and taxation.

Section 28.3

Daoism and Imperial Patronage

Organized Daoist religion under the Tang cannot be understood simply as the philosophical legacy of the classical texts attributed to Laozi and Zhuangzi and examined in Chapter 12. By the Tang period, Daoism had developed, over several preceding centuries, into an institutionally sophisticated religious tradition with its own temples, ordained priesthood, ritual specialists, scriptural canon, sacred geography, and distinctive practices concerning health, longevity, and transcendence — a tradition that received extraordinary and, in certain periods, unparalleled favor from the Tang imperial house.

This favor rested substantially on a specific claim: the ruling Li family asserted descent from Laozi, whose surname was traditionally given as Li, and used this claimed genealogical connection to help legitimate their rule, particularly in the early decades of the dynasty when establishing dynastic legitimacy after the fall of Sui, examined in Chapter 24, remained an urgent political priority. Tang emperors accordingly granted Daoism a formal precedence over Buddhism at several points in official court ceremony, commissioned the construction and renovation of major Daoist temples, sponsored the compilation and copying of Daoist scriptural collections, and, in some cases, personally studied or patronized specific Daoist masters. This patronage should not be mistaken for exclusive devotion; many Tang emperors patronized Buddhist institutions as generously as Daoist ones, and individual rulers moved between, or simultaneously supported, both traditions according to changing political and personal considerations.

Daoist religious practice under the Tang encompassed several interrelated dimensions. Ritual specialists performed ceremonies intended to secure blessings, avert calamity, and, at the level of the state, protect the dynasty and empire itself, paralleling in some respects the protective ritual functions that esoteric Buddhist practice, discussed in Section 28.1, also offered the court. Daoist temples, like Buddhist monasteries, held land, received donations, and functioned as centers of religious and, to a lesser extent, educational activity, though the scale of Daoist institutional landholding generally remained smaller than that of the more extensively developed Buddhist monastic system. Daoist traditions concerning the cultivation of longevity and, in some cases, physical transcendence encouraged practices including controlled breathing, dietary regimens, and alchemical experimentation — the search, through combinations of minerals, herbs, and ritual practice, for substances believed capable of extending life or conferring immortality. This alchemical tradition, whatever its practical results, connected Daoist religious practice to early Chinese traditions of natural philosophy, medicine, and, in an unintended but historically significant way, to early experimentation with substances and combinations that later proved relevant to other fields of practical knowledge.

Daoist sacred geography — networks of sacred mountains, caves, and other numinous sites believed to be inhabited by transcendent beings or connected to particular deities and immortals — provided an important framework for pilgrimage, retreat, and religious practice throughout the Tang period, in some respects paralleling the sacred geography of Buddhist pilgrimage sites and demonstrating that religious mapping of the Tang landscape was not the exclusive province of any single tradition.

Students should avoid framing Tang religious life as a simple binary contest between Buddhism and Daoism. While specific policy decisions, court debates, and the writings of some officials and clergy did at times frame the two traditions as rivals for court favor and social prestige, in practice individual believers, and even individual emperors, frequently participated in both traditions without experiencing this as a fundamental contradiction. Many Tang subjects visited both Buddhist and Daoist temples according to specific needs or occasions, supported both kinds of institutions with donations, and drew on both traditions' ritual and devotional resources. The court's periodic decisions to favor one tradition over the other in specific ceremonial or institutional contexts reflected political calculation and dynastic ideology as much as, or more than, any general religious exclusivity within Tang society at large.

Section 28.4

Confucian Learning

Confucian learning under the Tang occupied a position distinct from both Buddhism and Daoism, functioning less as an organized religion with temples and ordained clergy — though state Confucian ritual and temple veneration of Confucius did exist — and more as the shared educational, ethical, and political vocabulary of the official class examined in Chapter 26. Mastery of the Confucian classics, together with skill in composing formal prose and, increasingly, poetry, formed the core of the education pursued by young men aspiring to official careers through the examination system introduced in that chapter, and the moral and political concepts drawn from Confucian tradition — filial piety, loyalty, ritual propriety, the cultivation of virtue, the responsibilities of rulers and officials toward the governed — permeated official discourse, historical writing, and the self-understanding of the educated elite throughout the dynasty.

Government-sponsored schools and academies, alongside private tutoring and family-based education among literate households, transmitted this classical learning to successive generations of examination candidates. The content of this education centered on close study of texts such as the Analects, the Book of Documents, the Book of Songs, and other works within the Confucian classical canon, together with historical writing, ritual texts, and, for many students, the substantial body of earlier and contemporary poetry that shaped literary composition, examined further in Section 28.6. Confucian learning in this sense functioned as cultural and political capital: mastery of it opened access to official careers, social prestige, and participation in the shared discourse of the governing elite, even for students who ultimately failed to pass the examinations or obtain office.

Throughout most of the Tang period, Confucian learning coexisted with Buddhist and Daoist religious practice without being widely understood as fundamentally incompatible with them; many officials who had built their careers on classical education also patronized Buddhist monasteries, practiced Daoist longevity techniques, or participated in both traditions' festivals and rituals without perceiving deep contradiction. By the early ninth century, however, a current of more explicit criticism had begun to develop, associated especially with the official and essayist Han Yu. In 819, Han Yu submitted a memorial to the throne sharply criticizing the court's ceremonial veneration of a relic believed to be a bone of the Buddha. His argument was principally cultural, ritual, and political: he portrayed Buddhism as a foreign teaching, unknown to the sage-kings of antiquity, that was incompatible with the proper ordering of ruler-subject and father-son relationships central to Chinese governance and family ethics. The memorial nearly cost Han Yu his life and certainly cost him his position at court, as the emperor reacted with anger to what he read as an insult to imperial piety, but Han Yu's broader argument — that Confucian learning, understood as a distinctively Chinese moral and political tradition rooted in the sages of antiquity, deserved a more exclusive claim on the loyalty of the educated elite than a foreign religion such as Buddhism could rightfully hold — represented an influential strand of thought that would resonate strongly with later Song dynasty scholars. Separate from Han Yu's specifically cultural and ritual argument, other Tang officials and, eventually, state policy more broadly also advanced a fiscal critique of Buddhist institutional wealth and tax privilege, a distinct strand of criticism examined further in Section 28.9 that would become central to the justification for the Huichang suppression of 845.

Historical Vocabulary
Guwen (古文) — Ancient-Style Prose

Guwen, literally "ancient-style writing" or "ancient-style prose," refers to a movement, associated especially with Han Yu and like-minded contemporaries, advocating a return to the plainer, more direct prose style associated with classical texts of the Warring States and Han periods, in place of the more ornate, highly parallel prose fashionable in much of medieval Chinese court writing. The movement mattered historically because it was closely tied to the broader intellectual argument, examined in this section, that Confucian learning should be understood as a return to authentic ancient teaching rather than merely one tradition coexisting alongside Buddhism and Daoism. Guwen prose style would exercise significant influence on later Chinese prose writing, including during the Song dynasty examined in Chapter 34.

Students should be careful not to read this ninth-century critique as fully developed Song Neo-Confucianism, examined in Chapter 34, projected backward into the Tang period. Han Yu and like-minded contemporaries articulated an important intellectual position — advocating a return to what they characterized as the authentic ethical and political teachings of antiquity, and criticizing what they saw as the excessive institutional privilege of Buddhist and, to a lesser extent, Daoist establishments — but they did not develop the elaborate metaphysical synthesis of cosmology, ethics, and Confucian classical interpretation that later Song thinkers such as Zhu Xi would construct. Tang Confucian learning should instead be understood as providing important intellectual and institutional groundwork — a renewed emphasis on classical texts, a critique of Buddhist and Daoist institutional wealth, and an argument for the political centrality of Confucian ethical tradition — on which the more systematic Song intellectual developments would later build, rather than as identical to those later developments.

Confucian learning's relationship to historical writing also deserves attention here. The Tang court sponsored the compilation of official histories of preceding dynasties, continuing a tradition examined in earlier chapters concerning the Han historian Sima Qian and the broader practice of dynastic historiography. This historiographical tradition, grounded in Confucian assumptions about the moral lessons history could teach and the responsibility of historians to record events for the instruction of later rulers and officials, connected Confucian learning directly to the political and administrative functions of the Tang state examined in Chapter 26, reinforcing the practical, this-worldly orientation that distinguished Confucian learning from the more explicitly religious and soteriological concerns of Buddhism and organized Daoism.

Section 28.5

Christianity, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism

Chapter 27 traced how Tang China's extensive international connections brought missionaries, merchants, and migrants carrying religious traditions from Persia, Central Asia, and the broader Syriac Christian world into Tang cities. This section examines what became of those traditions once they had taken institutional root within Tang society — how they were organized, patronized, and, ultimately, how precarious their position proved to be compared with the deeply embedded traditions of Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucian learning examined earlier in this chapter.

The East Syriac Christian tradition, referred to in Tang sources by the name Jingjiao, or "luminous teaching," and sometimes described in earlier Western scholarship using the term "Nestorian" Christianity, established a documented institutional presence at Chang'an from the early seventh century. According to the community's own account, preserved in the stele examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, missionaries arrived at the Tang capital in 635 and received permission to translate scriptures and establish places of worship, receiving at various points explicit imperial recognition and support. Jingjiao communities built churches, which Tang authorities initially designated "Persian temples" (Bosi si), reflecting the community's perceived Persian origin, before a 745 imperial edict renamed them "Daqin temples," reflecting a revised official understanding that the religion had ultimately originated still farther west; this shift in official terminology illustrates how the Tang state's categorization of a foreign tradition could change as its own understanding of that tradition's origins developed. The community also undertook the substantial labor of translating Christian scripture and theological concepts into Chinese, a project requiring the same kind of careful terminological innovation that Buddhist translators, examined in Section 28.1, had confronted centuries earlier.

Manichaeism, a dualist religious tradition originating in third-century Persia and combining elements of Zoroastrian, Christian, Gnostic, and Buddhist thought, entered Tang China through Central Asian trade and missionary networks and found a distinctive, if narrow, institutional niche connected especially to the Tang court's diplomatic and military relationship with the Uyghur Khaganate, a steppe power discussed in the context of Tang frontier relations in Chapter 27. Because the Uyghur ruling elite adopted Manichaeism as a court religion during the eighth century, and because Tang China depended heavily on Uyghur military support during and after the disruptions of the An Lushan Rebellion, examined in Chapter 29, Manichaean communities within Tang territory enjoyed a period of qualified official recognition connected directly to this diplomatic relationship — an illustration of how a foreign religious community's institutional fortunes in Tang China could depend as much on geopolitical calculation as on religious conviction or toleration in the abstract.

Zoroastrian religious practice, rooted in ancient Persian tradition, accompanied the substantial communities of Sogdian and Persian merchants, artisans, and other immigrants who settled in Chang'an and other Tang cities as part of the Silk Road connections examined in Chapters 16 and 27. Zoroastrian temples and religious specialists served these immigrant communities, and while Zoroastrianism in Tang China never approached the scale of institutional development achieved by Buddhism or organized Daoism, its continued practice among resident foreign populations illustrates the genuine, if bounded, religious diversity that characterized major Tang cities, particularly Chang'an, throughout much of the dynasty.

These three traditions shared important structural features that distinguished their position from that of Buddhism and Daoism. Each depended heavily on specific, identifiable immigrant or diplomatically connected communities rather than achieving broad conversion among the general Tang population; each depended on continued imperial recognition that could be, and eventually was, withdrawn; and each proved considerably more vulnerable than Buddhism to the general tightening of religious policy that culminated in the Huichang suppression of 845, discussed in Sections 28.2 and 28.9. Official measures associated with that suppression targeted not only Buddhist institutions but also Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Jingjiao Christian establishments, in some cases with even more severe and lasting consequences for these smaller communities, which generally lacked the deep social roots, dispersed lay support, and sheer institutional scale that allowed Buddhism to recover much of its position in the decades following the persecution. The key analytical point for students to take from this section is that Tang religious diversity, while real and in some periods officially encouraged, was neither unconditional nor permanently guaranteed, and smaller foreign traditions in particular remained acutely dependent on the continuing favor of a court whose priorities could, and did, change.

Section 28.6

Tang Poetry

Tang poetry represents one of the most celebrated bodies of literature in world history, but students should approach it first and foremost as a social and political practice deeply woven into the fabric of elite Tang life, rather than as a detached collection of aesthetic masterpieces to be admired in isolation from their historical context. Poetry composition formed part of the standard education pursued by examination candidates discussed in Section 28.4, and skill in composing poetry on set themes and within specific formal constraints was, at various points during the dynasty, directly tested within the examination system itself, examined further in Chapter 26. Beyond its examination role, poetry functioned as the primary medium through which educated Tang subjects marked friendship, mourning, political exile, travel, religious reflection, courtly celebration, and, not infrequently, political criticism delivered through allusion and indirection rather than direct statement.

The scale of Tang poetic production was immense; later imperial compilations preserve tens of thousands of individual poems attributed to more than two thousand named poets, spanning emperors, high officials, provincial administrators, Buddhist monks, Daoist priests, courtesans, soldiers, and travelers. This range of authorship illustrates that poetic composition, while concentrated among the literate elite examined in Section 28.4, was not the exclusive province of the very highest ranks of court society, and that literary skill could serve as a marker of cultivation and social participation across a fairly broad, if still restricted, swath of Tang society.

Several major poets illustrate the range of Tang poetic voices and social positions. Li Bai, active in the eighth century and celebrated for his exuberant, often unconventional style, wrote poetry that frequently drew on Daoist imagery, themes of wandering and transcendence, and a persona of romantic individualism that later Chinese literary tradition would celebrate as emblematic of poetic genius, even as his actual official career remained limited and unstable. Wang Wei, a contemporary devoted to Buddhist, particularly Chan-influenced, meditative themes, combined a successful official career with poetry noted for its quiet, contemplative landscape imagery, and Wang Wei was also an accomplished painter, illustrating the close connection between poetic and visual artistic sensibility examined further in Section 28.7. Du Fu, generally regarded by later Chinese literary tradition as the greatest Tang poet, lived through the catastrophic disruption of the An Lushan Rebellion examined in Chapter 29, and his poetry from this period provides historians with an unusually direct, historically engaged record of warfare, displacement, family separation, government conscription, and social suffering, composed by someone who experienced these conditions rather than merely observing them from a position of security. Bai Juyi, active in the late eighth and early ninth centuries, wrote poetry explicitly concerned with social and political themes, including sharp depictions of the burdens taxation and labor service placed on ordinary people, alongside more personal and reflective verse, and cultivated a direct, accessible style that circulated widely, by Tang accounts, well beyond the narrow circle of court and examination culture.

Du Fu's wartime poetry deserves particular attention as historical evidence. Poems describing conscription officers seizing men for military service, families torn apart by war, and the physical devastation of the countryside during and after the An Lushan Rebellion offer historians a vantage point on Tang society's experience of crisis that official chronicles and administrative records, generally concerned with the perspective and priorities of the court and bureaucracy, do not provide in the same immediate, personal register. At the same time, students should recognize the limits of poetry as historical evidence: poetic convention, formal constraint, rhetorical exaggeration for emotional or political effect, and the individual poet's particular circumstances and literary ambitions all shape what a poem records and how, meaning that poetry can illuminate historical experience powerfully without functioning as a straightforward, literal transcript of events in the way an administrative document might attempt to be.

Tang poetic form itself developed considerable technical sophistication during this period, particularly in the elaboration of regulated verse, a demanding form governed by strict rules of tonal pattern, parallel structure, and line length that examination candidates were expected to master. This technical development illustrates that Tang poetry's cultural prestige rested not only on emotional and imagistic power but also on demonstrated mastery of formal craft, a combination that made poetic skill a genuinely valuable form of social and political capital within the examination-centered elite culture examined throughout this chapter.

Section 28.7

Art and Material Culture

Tang visual and material culture, much of it recovered through archaeological excavation rather than continuous artistic transmission, provides historians with evidence that complements and, in some respects, extends beyond what literary and religious texts alone can offer. Painting, sculpture, ceramics, tomb furnishings, textiles, and architecture together document changing aesthetic sensibilities, religious practice, and the cross-cultural influences that Chapter 27 traced arriving in Tang China from Central Asia, Persia, India, and beyond.

Buddhist sculpture and cave art, building on the traditions established during the Age of Division and examined in Chapter 23, reached new levels of technical sophistication and stylistic confidence during the Tang period, particularly at major cave temple complexes along the routes connecting Chang'an to Central Asia. Tang Buddhist imagery developed a fuller, more naturalistic figural style than earlier periods had generally employed, reflecting both indigenous artistic development and continued absorption of artistic conventions transmitted along the same routes that carried Buddhist teachings and texts. Buddhist painting, surviving today chiefly through cave murals and a smaller number of portable works, depicted scenes from Buddhist scripture, images of Buddhas and bodhisattvas, and, at some sites, donor portraits recording the specific patrons who funded particular artistic commissions, connecting artistic production directly to the patterns of lay devotion and merit-making examined in Section 28.2.

Tomb art and mortuary objects, recovered through archaeological excavation of Tang burials, provide an unusually direct window into elite material culture and, to some degree, popular beliefs about death and the afterlife. Painted tomb murals depicted scenes of court life, hunting, music, and daily activity, while ceramic tomb figurines, known as mingqi, represented a remarkable range of subjects: court officials, foreign merchants and grooms recognizable by their distinctive dress and facial features, camels laden with trade goods, musicians, dancers, and animals, all intended to accompany and serve the deceased in the afterlife. These figurines, alongside the distinctive lead-glazed ceramic ware known as sancai, or "three-color" ware, produced especially during the eighth century, illustrate both the cosmopolitan character of Tang elite society, examined in Chapter 27 in connection with foreign residents and cultural exchange, and the technical sophistication of Tang ceramic production, which combined domestic craft traditions with imported decorative techniques and motifs.

Court and elite painting, though surviving today chiefly through later copies and textual descriptions rather than abundant original works, developed distinctive traditions of figure painting, landscape depiction, and, as illustrated by Wang Wei's dual career as poet and painter discussed in Section 28.6, a close connection between poetic and visual artistic sensibility that later Chinese aesthetic theory would treat as fundamentally intertwined rather than separate art forms. Textile production, including elaborate silk weaving and embroidery, incorporated patterns and techniques drawn from Central Asian and Persian textile traditions alongside indigenous Chinese sericulture examined in earlier chapters, illustrating in yet another medium the pattern of absorption and transformation that runs throughout this chapter's treatment of Tang cultural life.

Students should approach Tang art and material culture with the same interpretive care urged throughout this textbook when working with archaeological evidence. Much of what survives does so because it was buried in tombs or sealed within caves, meaning that the surviving record reflects particular practices — elite burial customs, monastic manuscript and art storage — rather than the full range of Tang visual and material culture, much of which, including most everyday objects, urban architecture, and perishable materials, has not survived at all. Students should therefore treat the surviving material record as a genuinely valuable but partial and unevenly distributed body of evidence, requiring the same critical evaluation applied to literary and documentary sources throughout this chapter.

Section 28.8

Printing and Written Culture

Written culture in Tang China rested overwhelmingly on manuscript production throughout most of the dynasty, and students should understand the gradual emergence of woodblock printing during this period as a significant but slow-developing supplement to, rather than a sudden replacement for, an already sophisticated and extensive manuscript culture.

Manuscript production depended on skilled scribes, many of them working within the monastic scriptoria examined in Section 28.2, copying religious, administrative, literary, and educational texts by hand onto paper, a technology whose earlier development and spread this textbook has traced in preceding chapters. Paper, considerably more affordable and easier to produce in quantity than earlier writing materials, had by the Tang period become the standard medium for the great majority of Chinese textual production, supporting an extensive manuscript culture that included government archives, monastic libraries, private book collections among the literate elite, and the informal, everyday documents — contracts, letters, school exercises, religious texts copied for personal merit — that survive in unusually rich quantity among the Dunhuang manuscripts discussed further below.

Woodblock printing, the technology of carving text and image in relief onto wooden blocks that could then be inked and pressed against paper to produce multiple copies, developed gradually during the Tang period, most plausibly connected initially to the reproduction of Buddhist religious materials — sutras, dharani (ritual incantations), and devotional images — for which producing multiple copies served genuine religious purposes tied to the merit-making practices examined in Section 28.2. The most famous surviving example, a printed scroll of the Diamond Sutra securely dated by its own colophon to 868, demonstrates that by the late ninth century, woodblock printing technology had reached a considerable level of technical sophistication, producing a lengthy, carefully illustrated text through a printing process rather than manual copying. Students should understand, however, that the 868 Diamond Sutra represents a securely dated late example of an established technology rather than printing's point of origin; scattered evidence, including smaller printed items and later textual references, suggests that woodblock printing had already been in more limited use for religious materials for some time before this famous example was produced.

The Dunhuang manuscript cache, sealed within a cave complex on the western frontier and rediscovered only in the early twentieth century, preserves an extraordinarily rich body of both manuscript and printed material spanning several centuries of Tang and post-Tang written culture. Alongside religious texts, the cache preserves everyday documents — contracts for land and property transactions, family letters, school exercises used to teach children literacy and calligraphy, divination texts, and administrative records — that illustrate the coexistence of elite literary culture, examined in Section 28.6, with the more modest, functional written culture of ordinary households and local communities in a frontier region. This coexistence of manuscript and printed material within the same archive, spanning genres from sophisticated religious philosophy to simple household documents, illustrates precisely the point this section emphasizes: printing did not immediately or completely displace manuscript production, and the two technologies coexisted for a very long period, with printing only gradually expanding its social and textual reach over the centuries following the Tang, developments examined more fully in Chapter 33's discussion of Song printing and the broader expansion of literacy that followed.

Students should draw a clear methodological distinction between technological invention and widespread social adoption. The existence of Tang-era printed texts demonstrates that the basic technology of woodblock printing had been developed and applied successfully well before the end of the dynasty, but this technological achievement did not by itself transform Tang written culture into a print-dominated culture; that broader transformation, involving significantly expanded book production, wider circulation of printed texts, and the eventual, though gradual, extension of literacy and access to written material beyond the narrow elite examined throughout this chapter, unfolded chiefly during and after the Song dynasty, examined in Chapter 33. The Tang period's contribution was to establish the essential technology and, particularly within Buddhist institutional contexts, an initial and religiously motivated pattern of use that later centuries would substantially expand.

Section 28.9

Religious and Intellectual Diversity

Having examined Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian learning, and smaller foreign religious traditions largely as separate subjects in the preceding sections, this section asks how these traditions actually coexisted, competed, and interacted within the lived experience of Tang society, and what the limits of that coexistence ultimately proved to be.

Tang subjects did not, in general, experience religious life as a matter of exclusive membership in a single tradition analogous to modern conceptions of religious identity in some other historical and cultural contexts. An individual believer might visit a Buddhist temple to seek merit or ritual assistance at one occasion, consult a Daoist priest for healing or protective ritual at another, and observe Confucian-derived family and ancestral rites as a matter of ordinary social obligation, all without experiencing these practices as fundamentally in conflict with one another. Court ritual calendars accommodated observances connected to multiple traditions; individual officials, as noted in Section 28.4, could pursue Confucian-centered official careers while patronizing Buddhist or Daoist institutions; and popular religious practice at the local level frequently blended elements drawn from multiple traditions in ways that resist neat categorization into separate, bounded religious systems.

This genuine, substantial pluralism, however, coexisted with real competition for court patronage, social prestige, and institutional resources among Buddhist, Daoist, and, in a more limited institutional sense, Confucian establishments, and this competition periodically found expression in explicit court debate, polemical writing such as Han Yu's 819 memorial discussed in Section 28.4, and shifting patterns of official favor. Foreign religious traditions examined in Section 28.5 occupied a still more precarious position within this competitive environment, depending as they did on smaller, more specifically bounded communities and on continued imperial recognition that lacked the deep social and institutional roots Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucian learning had each developed over centuries of Chinese social embedding.

The Huichang suppression of 845, examined already in Section 28.2 and analyzed further in this chapter's Cause and Effect feature, brought these underlying tensions into sudden, dramatic, and unmistakable visibility. Emperor Wuzong, whose personal religious sympathies inclined toward Daoism, ordered the large-scale closure of Buddhist monasteries and shrines across the empire, citing both the economic burden Buddhist institutions placed on state finances through clerical exemptions and the withdrawal or sheltering of land and labor from ordinary fiscal obligations, and cultural and doctrinal objections to Buddhism as a foreign teaching incompatible with proper governance — arguments that echoed, in an official policy context, the critique Han Yu had voiced in 819. The suppression's measures extended beyond Buddhism to affect Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Jingjiao Christian institutions as well, in some cases with even more severe and lasting institutional consequences, since these smaller communities generally lacked the extensive lay support networks and sheer numerical presence that allowed Buddhist institutions to begin recovering within a few years of the emperor's death.

Emperor Wuzong's death in 846 and the accession of a new emperor brought a reversal of the harshest measures within roughly a year, and Buddhist institutions gradually rebuilt much of their monastic infrastructure and social presence over the following decades, though scholars generally agree that Buddhist institutional wealth and privilege never fully returned to their pre-845 scale, and that the episode marked a genuine, lasting shift in the relationship between Buddhist institutions and the Tang state. For the smaller foreign religious communities examined in Section 28.5, recovery proved considerably more limited; the institutional presence of Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and Jingjiao Christianity within China diminished substantially in the decades following the suppression and the subsequent political fragmentation of the late Tang and Five Dynasties periods examined in Chapters 29 and 30.

The central analytical lesson of this section, and in many respects of this chapter as a whole, is that the word "tolerance," while not inaccurate as a general characterization of Tang religious life across much of the dynasty, can obscure an important underlying reality if applied uncritically. Tang religious pluralism was genuine, substantial, and in many respects remarkable by comparative historical standards, but it existed within, rather than outside, a framework of concentrated political authority in which the ultimate security of any religious institution depended on the ongoing favor, or at least tolerance, of the emperor and court. That favor could be granted generously for generations, as it largely was for Buddhism through much of the Tang period, and it could also be withdrawn suddenly and forcefully, as the events of 845 demonstrated with unmistakable clarity. Students should carry this lesson forward into later chapters of this textbook, where similar patterns of religious patronage, competition, and periodic state regulation or suppression will recur in different historical contexts across the long span of Chinese history this textbook examines.

Section 28.10

Chapter Review and Tang Culture Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the religious, literary, artistic, and intellectual life of Tang China, moving from the internal diversity of Tang Buddhism and its deep institutional embedding in Tang society, through the Tang state's extraordinary patronage of organized Daoism, the character and limits of Confucian learning, the more precarious position of foreign religious communities, and on to the celebrated literary and artistic achievements of the period and the gradual, uneven emergence of printing alongside continued manuscript culture. Throughout, the chapter has emphasized two connected themes: first, that religious and intellectual pluralism under the Tang was genuine and remarkable, with multiple traditions coexisting, competing, and frequently blending within the lived experience of Tang subjects at every social level; and second, that this pluralism operated within, rather than outside, a framework of unequal and shifting political power, a reality the Huichang suppression of 845 revealed with unusual clarity but that had shaped religious and intellectual life throughout the dynasty in less dramatic, more continuous ways.

Historical Vocabulary
Jingjiao (景教)

Jingjiao, literally "luminous teaching," is the name Tang sources use for the East Syriac Christian tradition, sometimes called "Nestorian" Christianity in earlier Western scholarship, a term modern scholars increasingly avoid because it can misrepresent the community's own theological self-understanding. Jingjiao missionaries established an institutional presence at Chang'an from the early seventh century, translated Christian scripture and theology into Chinese, and built places of worship under periods of explicit imperial recognition. The term matters historically because it shows how a foreign religious tradition was named, categorized, and partially absorbed into Chinese religious vocabulary, and because the community's own account of its history, preserved in the 781 stele examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, offers a rare instance of a minority religious community describing its own place within Tang society in its own words.

The Xi'an Stele of 781

Creator: Chinese inscription composed by Jingjing, a Church of the East cleric known in Syriac as Adam, with calligraphy attributed to Lü Xiuyan, on behalf of the Jingjiao community at Chang'an · Date: Erected in 781 · Type: Stone stele inscription, combining a historical narrative and a summary of Christian doctrine, written in Chinese with a subsidiary Syriac inscription

Context: The stele was erected near Chang'an to commemorate roughly a century and a half of the Jingjiao community's presence in Tang China, recounting, from the community's own perspective, the arrival of missionaries in 635, the reception the community received from successive Tang emperors, and a summary of core Christian teachings rendered in terms intended to be comprehensible within a Chinese intellectual and religious vocabulary.

Paraphrase of the stele's content (not a translated quotation):

The inscription describes missionaries arriving at the Tang capital during the reign of an early Tang emperor, receiving permission to translate scripture and establish places of worship, and enjoying, at various points over the following century and a half, explicit recognition and material support from the imperial court, alongside a doctrinal summary presenting core Christian concepts in language drawn partly from existing Chinese religious and philosophical vocabulary.

Analysis: The stele is significant as a rare instance of a minority foreign religious community in Tang China describing its own history and position in its own words, rather than being described only through the writings of Chinese officials or later historians. Its combination of historical narrative and doctrinal summary, and its adaptation of Christian concepts into a partly borrowed Chinese religious vocabulary, illustrate the translation and accommodation strategies foreign religious communities used to establish themselves within Tang society, strategies broadly comparable to those Buddhist translators had developed centuries earlier.

Historical Debate

How Tolerant Was Tang Religious Society?

The question: Was Tang China, particularly during its height in the seventh and eighth centuries, a genuinely tolerant, pluralistic religious society, or does that characterization overstate the security and freedom that non-Confucian and non-Chinese religious traditions actually enjoyed?

Interpretation A — Tang China as a genuinely plural religious society: Some historians emphasize the remarkable coexistence of Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian learning, and foreign traditions including Jingjiao Christianity, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism within Tang cities, particularly Chang'an, alongside the willingness of individual believers and even emperors to draw on multiple traditions simultaneously, arguing that this represents a genuinely and unusually plural religious environment by comparative historical standards.
Interpretation B — Pluralism bounded by unequal state power: Other historians emphasize that this coexistence depended entirely on ongoing imperial favor that was never legally guaranteed or institutionally protected, pointing to the sudden and severe consequences of the 845 Huichang suppression, and the even more lasting institutional damage suffered by smaller foreign traditions, as evidence that Tang "tolerance" should be understood as a conditional policy choice rather than a stable structural feature of the society.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — patronage records, temple and church construction, the 781 stele, and the events of 845 — but differ in how much weight to assign to the extended periods of genuine coexistence versus the demonstrated fragility revealed by the suppression and its uneven aftermath.

Why it matters: How historians characterize Tang religious policy shapes broader understanding of the relationship between religious pluralism and state power in premodern societies generally, and cautions against treating any historical society's "tolerance" as permanent or unconditional simply because it persisted for an extended period.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 28.1 through 28.9, evaluate which interpretation better accounts for both the extended periods of coexistence and the events of 845, or explain why a combined view may capture the historical reality more accurately than either interpretation alone.

From the perspective of a Confucian official sympathetic to Han Yu's arguments: Loyalty to classical learning and Han Yu's cultural and ritual objections to Buddhism as a foreign teaching could combine, in an individual official's outlook, with a separate concern, voiced by other officials, over the fiscal burden of extensive clerical tax and corvée privileges, producing a position that was less about personal hostility to Buddhism than about a conviction that Confucian tradition deserved greater institutional priority within Tang governance, reinforced from more than one direction of argument.
From the perspective of a Sogdian Zoroastrian merchant family in Chang'an: Maintaining a family temple and religious practice amid a much larger Buddhist and Daoist religious landscape required careful navigation of legal status, imperial recognition, and the shifting diplomatic relationships between Tang China and the communities' homelands, leaving the family's religious institutions considerably more exposed to policy change than the deeply rooted Buddhist establishments nearby.
From the perspective of a village household near a rural monastery: The nearby monastery's mill, its charitable grain reserves during hard years, and the ritual services its monks performed at times of illness or death mattered more immediately to daily life than any court debate over doctrine, meaning that the 845 suppression's local effects were felt as much through the loss of these practical services as through any specifically religious or doctrinal concern.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Buddhism From the Age of Division to the Huichang Suppression

What Changed

  • Buddhist institutions grew from the regionally varied, politically divided patronage networks of the Age of Division, examined in Chapter 23, into an empire-wide institutional system integrated with unified Tang administration
  • Distinct Buddhist schools such as Chan, Tiantai, Huayan, and Pure Land developed and matured as identifiable traditions during the Tang period
  • Monastic landholding and the scale of clerical tax and corvée privileges expanded to a degree that provoked sustained fiscal and political concern by the ninth century
  • State policy shifted from recurring patronage combined with regulation and periodic criticism to an exceptional empire-wide suppression in 845

What Stayed the Same

  • Buddhism's fundamental dependence on political patronage, already evident under the Northern and Southern Dynasties examined in Chapter 23, continued to shape its institutional fortunes throughout the Tang
  • Lay devotional practice, merit-making donation, and local community support for monasteries remained constant features of Buddhist social life
  • Buddhist textual scholarship and translation, exemplified across centuries from earlier translators through Xuanzang, remained a prestigious and continuous intellectual project

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real institutional growth of Tang Buddhism and its persistent dependence on political favor helps students understand the Huichang suppression not as an unprecedented rupture but as the sharp culmination of a long-standing structural vulnerability inherent in Buddhism's relationship with Chinese political authority since its earliest centuries in China.

Cause and Effect

The Huichang Suppression of 845

Causes

Long-term accumulation of tax-privileged monastic wealth and landholding strained state finances; Confucian critics such as Han Yu had, since at least 819, articulated an influential cultural and ritual objection to Buddhism as a foreign tradition incompatible with proper governance and family ethics, a position distinct from but later reinforced by separate fiscal critiques of Buddhist institutional wealth advanced more broadly among Tang officials; Emperor Wuzong's personal inclination toward Daoism provided a sympathetic ear at court for anti-Buddhist advisers.

Event or transformation

In 845, the court ordered the closure of the great majority of Buddhist monasteries and shrines across the empire, the return of hundreds of thousands of monks and nuns to lay, tax-paying status, the confiscation of monastic land and property, and the melting down of bronze images and bells; the measures also affected Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Jingjiao Christian institutions.

Effects

Immediate loss of Buddhist institutional infrastructure and wealth across the empire; disruption of the charitable, educational, and economic functions monasteries had performed for surrounding communities, discussed in Section 28.2; especially severe and lasting damage to smaller foreign religious communities lacking Buddhism's deep social roots.

Historical complexity

The suppression cannot be reduced to simple religious hostility; it combined genuine doctrinal argument, urgent fiscal calculation, court politics, and the personal religious inclinations of a specific emperor, and its reversal within roughly a year after Wuzong's death shows that the underlying policy was contested and reversible rather than a fixed ideological program.
Everyday Life and Social History

Religion in a Tang Urban Community

Evidence for everyday religious life comes from different kinds of Tang communities and should not be blended into a single composite without care. Excavated remains and documentary sources concerning Chang'an itself suggest that a resident of the capital might live within walking distance of a Buddhist neighborhood temple, a Daoist shrine, and, in certain wards of the city, a Zoroastrian or Jingjiao Christian place of worship, without necessarily experiencing exclusive attachment to any single one of these institutions. The Dunhuang manuscripts, by contrast, come from a frontier oasis city rather than the capital, and while they illuminate a genuinely comparable pattern of everyday religious practice, contract-making, and basic literacy in a Tang urban setting, they document conditions specific to a western garrison and trade town and should be treated as a distinct, complementary body of evidence rather than a direct stand-in for life in Chang'an.

Households in both kinds of communities marked important life events — birth, illness, marriage, death — with religious observances that might draw on Buddhist funerary ritual, Daoist protective practice, and Confucian-derived ancestral rites within the same family across different occasions. Local temple festivals, tied to the Buddhist or Daoist ritual calendar, provided occasions for community gathering, commerce, and entertainment alongside religious observance, blurring any sharp line between "religious" and "social" life in the way modern categories might suggest.

Literacy and education, concentrated among households with the resources to pursue examination preparation discussed in Section 28.4, nonetheless touched a wider population than the narrow examination-bound elite alone, as school exercises and simple documents recovered from Dunhuang illustrate; basic literacy for practical purposes such as contracts and correspondence extended, unevenly, beyond the small circle of accomplished poets and classical scholars examined in Section 28.6. Students should recognize that many residents of major Tang cities, especially Chang'an, experienced the plural religious and intellectual environment examined throughout this chapter as an ordinary, largely unremarkable feature of daily life, at least until the events of 845 made the underlying fragility of that pluralism suddenly and painfully visible.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the religious, literary, artistic, and intellectual life of Tang China as a domain of genuine and remarkable diversity that nonetheless operated within a framework of concentrated and, at critical moments, decisively exercised political power. Building on the political, economic, and international foundations established in Chapters 25 through 27, this chapter has shown that Tang subjects at every social level participated in a religious and cultural environment shaped by multiple, coexisting traditions rather than any single dominant belief system or artistic school.

Buddhism, examined in Sections 28.1 and 28.2, emerged as the most thoroughly embedded of these traditions, encompassing several distinct schools of thought and practice, supported by an extensive institutional infrastructure of monasteries and nunneries that combined religious, economic, educational, and charitable functions, and patronized at the highest levels of the Tang court through projects such as Xuanzang's translation bureau. Daoism, examined in Section 28.3, received extraordinary imperial favor connected to the ruling house's claimed descent from Laozi, developing its own sophisticated institutional and ritual traditions that coexisted, rather than simply competed, with Buddhist practice in the lives of most Tang subjects. Confucian learning, examined in Section 28.4, structured official education and political discourse without functioning as an exclusive religious system, even as ninth-century critics such as Han Yu began articulating arguments for its greater institutional priority that would resonate strongly with later Song intellectual developments.

Smaller foreign religious traditions, examined in Section 28.5, illustrated both the genuine reach of Tang cosmopolitanism and the more precarious institutional position such traditions occupied compared with the deeply rooted establishments of Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucian learning. Tang poetry, examined in Section 28.6 through the contrasting careers of Li Bai, Wang Wei, Du Fu, and Bai Juyi, emerged as both a celebrated literary achievement and a valuable, if interpretively complex, body of historical evidence for social and political experience. Tang art and material culture, examined in Section 28.7, documented cross-cultural artistic exchange and religious devotion through evidence recovered largely from tombs and sealed archives, while the gradual emergence of woodblock printing, examined in Section 28.8, established an important technological foundation without yet transforming Tang written culture away from its overwhelmingly manuscript-based character.

The chapter's closing sections brought these threads together around the central lesson of Tang religious and intellectual pluralism's genuine but conditional character. Section 28.9 and this chapter's Cause and Effect feature examined the Huichang suppression of 845 as the dramatic culmination of long-building fiscal, political, and doctrinal tensions, an event that abruptly curtailed Buddhist institutional wealth and inflicted even more lasting damage on smaller foreign religious communities, before a partial reversal within roughly a year demonstrated that state religious policy remained contested and changeable rather than fixed.

Students completing this chapter should carry forward a clear understanding that Tang cultural and religious achievement, however impressive, cannot be separated from the political structures that made it possible and that, at critical moments, constrained or reversed it. This lesson prepares students directly for Chapter 29's examination of the An Lushan Rebellion, which this chapter has deliberately avoided narrating in military and political detail but which nonetheless forms an essential backdrop to Du Fu's wartime poetry and to the broader mid-Tang transformation of court patronage, religious institutions, and literary culture that the rebellion set in motion.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Sutra
A Buddhist scripture, typically recording the teachings of the Buddha, central to Buddhist textual study, translation, and copying.
Chan Buddhism
A school of Chinese Buddhism emphasizing direct meditative insight, developing distinctive lineages and teaching traditions during the Tang period.
Tiantai school
A Buddhist school organizing scripture into a systematic hierarchy of teachings centered on the Lotus Sutra.
Huayan school
A Buddhist school developing an elaborate metaphysics of mutual interpenetration among phenomena, drawing on the Avatamsaka Sutra.
Pure Land practice
A devotional Buddhist tradition centered on faith in the Buddha Amitabha and aspiration for rebirth in his paradise, widely accessible to lay believers.
Esoteric Buddhism
Ritual and initiatory Buddhist practices transmitted from India during the eighth century, favored at points within the Tang court.
Yogacara ("consciousness-only")
An Indian Buddhist philosophical tradition analyzing perception and cognition, translated and promoted by Xuanzang.
Monastic estate (changzhu)
The permanent, corporately held property of a Buddhist monastery, portions of which benefited from clerical tax and corvée privileges that varied over time and by legal category.
Ordination
The formal process by which an individual became a recognized monk or nun, regulated by the Tang state through certificates and censuses.
Organized Daoist religion
The institutionally developed form of Daoism under the Tang, including temples, ordained priests, ritual specialists, and a scriptural canon.
Daoist alchemy
Practices seeking to extend life or confer transcendence through combinations of minerals, herbs, and ritual, connected to Daoist longevity traditions.
Guwen (ancient-style prose)
A prose style movement advocating a return to plainer classical writing, associated with Han Yu and later influential in Song prose.
Jingjiao
The Tang-era term for the East Syriac Christian tradition ("Church of the East") present in China from the early seventh century.
Manichaeism
A dualist Persian-originated religious tradition present in Tang China, connected institutionally to relations with the Uyghur Khaganate.
Zoroastrianism
An ancient Persian religious tradition practiced chiefly among Sogdian and Persian immigrant communities in Tang cities.
Regulated verse
A demanding Tang poetic form governed by strict rules of tonal pattern, parallel structure, and line length.
Examination poetry
Poetry composed under set themes and formal constraints, at points directly tested within the Tang examination system.
Mingqi
Ceramic tomb figurines representing officials, foreigners, animals, and other subjects, placed in Tang burials.
Sancai ware
Lead-glazed "three-color" Tang ceramic ware, especially produced during the eighth century.
Woodblock printing
A printing technology carving text and image in relief onto wooden blocks for reproduction, developed gradually during the Tang period.
Diamond Sutra (868)
The earliest surviving complete printed book bearing an explicit date, though woodblock printing technology had already been in more limited use for religious materials before it was produced.
Dunhuang manuscripts
A sealed cache of manuscripts and printed materials from a western frontier cave complex, preserving religious, administrative, and everyday Tang documents.
Huichang suppression
The 845 empire-wide suppression of Buddhist institutions ordered by Emperor Wuzong, also affecting Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Jingjiao communities.
Syncretism
The blending of elements from multiple religious traditions within the same practice, community, or individual belief.
Relic veneration
Buddhist devotional practice centered on physical remains or objects associated with the Buddha, a focus of Han Yu's 819 critique.
Stele
An inscribed stone monument, such as the 781 Xi'an stele documenting Jingjiao history and doctrine.
Court patronage
Financial and institutional support granted by the emperor or court to a religious, artistic, or intellectual institution or individual.
Lay devotion
Religious practice and support undertaken by non-ordained believers, including donation, pilgrimage, and merit-making.
Scriptorium
A monastic workshop for the copying and production of manuscripts.
Merit-making
Religious practice, especially in Buddhism, intended to accumulate spiritual benefit through donation, copying texts, or other pious acts.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a period of extraordinary religious and intellectual creativity, in which Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian learning, foreign religious traditions, poetry, art, and the technologies of written communication coexisted, competed, and interacted within one of the premodern world's most cosmopolitan societies. Understanding this period matters because it demonstrates, with unusual clarity, how a society can sustain genuine pluralism across many generations while that pluralism remains, at its foundation, dependent on the decisions of concentrated political authority.

The traditions and institutions introduced in this chapter did not remain confined to the Tang period in their historical significance. Buddhist schools that matured during this era shaped Chinese, Korean, and Japanese religious history for centuries afterward; the Confucian critique articulated by Han Yu anticipated the far more systematic Neo-Confucian synthesis examined in Chapter 34; Tang poetry established models of literary achievement that later Chinese literati would study, imitate, and measure themselves against for the rest of imperial history; and the printing technology that first appeared in Tang Buddhist contexts would, in the centuries following the dynasty's fall, transform the scale and reach of Chinese written culture far beyond what this chapter has been able to document within the Tang period itself.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill: resisting the temptation to describe a plural, tolerant-seeming society in purely celebratory terms without examining the underlying structures of power that made that pluralism possible, and that could, when circumstances changed, curtail or dismantle it. The Huichang suppression of 845 should remain in students' minds not as an isolated aberration but as a moment that revealed, with unusual sharpness, a condition present throughout the preceding two centuries of religious and cultural flourishing examined in this chapter.

As students move forward into Chapter 29's examination of the An Lushan Rebellion, they should carry with them a clear sense of what stood to be disrupted: a religiously and intellectually plural society, sustained by court patronage, monastic institutions, examination-centered education, and flourishing literary and artistic production, all of which the mid-eighth-century crisis and its long aftermath would reshape in ways the following chapters will examine in detail.
