Part XII — Reform and Contemporary China
74

Market Transformation and Global Integration

China: A History of a Civilization · Principal chronology: 1992–2012, with a brief 1989–1991 transition

Chapter Opening

In January 1992 an eighty-seven-year-old man who no longer held any senior state or Party office travelled south by train from Beijing, toured factories and the Shenzhen special economic zone, and told the officials who met him that China must be bolder in reform. Within a decade China had joined the World Trade Organization. Within two decades it was the world's largest exporter and its second-largest economy.

The traveller was Deng Xiaoping. He relinquished the chairmanship of the Communist Party Central Military Commission in November 1989 and the parallel state Central Military Commission chairmanship in March 1990, following the crackdown examined in Chapter 73. By the time of the 1992 tour he held neither office. His authority in 1992 rested on his revolutionary record, his personal networks within the Party and army, and his standing as the architect of the reforms begun in 1978. What he said on the tour, and the way provincial leaders, newspapers, and ministries took it up, helped end a period of economic retrenchment and political caution. The tour is among the best-known episodes in modern Chinese history, but it did not by itself create a market economy. Markets, private traders, and foreign investors had been present since the 1980s (Chapter 72). What the 1990s added was an institutional framework: new tax rules, a unified exchange rate, company law, reformed banks, restructured state enterprises, a housing market, and a place in the world trading system.

This chapter studies that transformation from the early 1990s to about 2012. Its guiding question is how China changed its economic institutions, industrial structure, labor force, and international commercial relationships over those two decades, and what the economic gains, social costs, and structural contradictions of the change were. It follows several lines at once: the policy decisions of leaders such as Deng Xiaoping, Jiang Zemin, Zhu Rongji, Hu Jintao, and Wen Jiabao; the institutional design of the tax, banking, and legal systems; the experience of workers laid off from state factories and of young migrants entering export factories; the business strategies of entrepreneurs, foreign investors, and local officials; and the position of Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan in China's commercial geography.

The chapter argues that two distinctions must be kept in view. The first is between marketization and privatization. China greatly expanded markets, competition, and private business, but it did not sell off its state sector wholesale. Many small state firms were sold, closed, or transformed, while large enterprises in finance, energy, telecommunications, and heavy industry were reorganized and kept under state control. The state also retained ownership of urban land, strong influence over bank lending, and the power to direct investment. The second distinction is between global economic integration and political liberalization. China's trade, investment, and financial links with the world grew enormously, yet the Communist Party kept its monopoly on political power, and it presented market reform as a way of strengthening Party rule, not weakening it.

What changed was large and measurable. Real output grew at rates that were extraordinary by international standards. Exports rose from tens of billions of dollars to about two trillion dollars a year. A rural population that in 1992 was still the majority became, around 2011, a minority of the total, as more than a hundred million people moved to cities and factories. Poverty, as measured by international standards, fell sharply. What continued was equally important: Party leadership of the state and the economy, the household registration system that limited migrants' access to urban public services, the fiscal and administrative importance of local governments, and a policy tradition of experimenting locally before generalizing nationally.

The people affected were very different from one another. Millions of employees in state factories lost the job security that had defined urban life since the 1950s (Chapter 67). Young rural women and men built electronics, garments, toys, and machinery in coastal factories, often far from their families. Entrepreneurs created firms that the 1980s would have classed as illegal or ideologically suspect. Local officials competed to attract investors. Farmers lost land to roads, factories, and housing developments. Urban households became homeowners almost overnight, when housing stopped being allocated by employers. Consumers abroad bought Chinese goods, while workers in some importing countries faced new competition.

The evidence available to historians is richer for this period than for almost any earlier one, and it needs careful handling. It includes Party Congress reports and Central Committee decisions, State Council notices, statutes, the Basic Laws of Hong Kong and Macao, the WTO accession protocol, statistical yearbooks, firm-level datasets, bank and company reports, journalism, ethnographies of factories, memoirs, and interviews. Policy statements record what leaders intended and announced, not necessarily what was implemented. Official statistics are indispensable but are subject to changes in definitions, to local reporting incentives, and to scholarly dispute. Each source type is evaluated where it is used.

The chapter belongs to Asian and world history as well as Chinese history. China's growth was tied to Japanese, Korean, Taiwanese, Hong Kong, Southeast Asian, European, and American firms, to the rules of the WTO, and to global patterns of demand and finance. The 2008 financial crisis showed how closely China was now linked to the world economy and also how much capacity its state institutions had to mobilize resources in response. The chapter ends around 2012, when the economic position reached by that date was clear and a new leadership generation was about to take office. Later political, territorial, and strategic developments belong to Chapter 76.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Deng Xiaoping's 1992 Southern Tour is best understood as an acceleration of an existing reform process rather than the beginning of reform and opening.
  2. Distinguish marketization from privatization, and global economic integration from political liberalization, using Chinese examples.
  3. Analyze how the 1994 tax-sharing reform and exchange-rate unification changed the fiscal and international position of the Chinese state.
  4. Compare state-owned, collective, private, publicly listed, and foreign-invested enterprises, and explain how ownership patterns changed between 1992 and 2012.
  5. Evaluate the social consequences of state-enterprise restructuring, especially for laid-off workers and their families.
  6. Describe the institutional arrangements under which Hong Kong and Macao returned to Chinese sovereignty, and assess their commercial roles.
  7. Explain the significance of China's 2001 WTO accession while avoiding the claim that it alone caused China's industrial growth.
  8. Interpret processing trade, value added, and global supply chains, and distinguish gross export value from domestic value added.
  9. Assess the experiences of rural migrants and the extent to which labor laws were enforced.
  10. Analyze the roles of infrastructure, bank credit, urban housing reform, and state investment in sustaining growth.
  11. Evaluate China's response to the 2008 global financial crisis, including the structure and consequences of the stimulus program.
  12. Interpret economic statistics carefully, recognizing differences in definitions, price adjustment, and measurement.

Historical Context

The reforms of the 1990s followed more than a decade of experiment. After 1978 the Party leadership shifted its priority from class struggle to economic modernization (Chapter 71). Agriculture was decollectivized through the Household Responsibility System, rural industries known as township and village enterprises grew rapidly, special economic zones in Guangdong and Fujian welcomed foreign capital, and prices for many goods were allowed to float alongside planned prices in a "dual-track" system (Chapter 72). These measures created a mixed economy in which plan and market coexisted, but they left the core of the urban industrial sector largely unchanged: state-owned factories still employed most urban workers, supplied housing, schools, clinics, and pensions through the work unit (danwei), and received credit through state banks on political as well as commercial criteria.

By the late 1980s the strains of this halfway system were visible. Inflation reached high levels in 1988, officials with access to both planned and market prices were accused of profiting from the gap, and arguments over the pace and direction of reform divided the leadership. The protest movement of 1989 and its suppression (Chapter 73) intensified political caution. The government imposed austerity, credit was tightened, and conservative voices argued that market reform risked weakening socialism. Growth slowed sharply in 1989–1990. International sanctions imposed after June 1989, and the collapse of communist governments in Eastern Europe and then the Soviet Union, added to the leadership's sense of vulnerability.

The geography of the reform era matters. The southeastern coast, especially Guangdong, Fujian, and later the Yangtze River Delta around Shanghai, had the strongest connections to overseas Chinese networks, to Hong Kong and Taiwan, and to maritime trade, a regional advantage with deep roots (Chapters 37 and 42). The northeast, by contrast, had been the great base of heavy industry in the planned economy, with large state enterprises in steel, machinery, coal, and oil. Its position was reversed as markets expanded. Inland provinces, Sichuan and Chongqing in the southwest, Henan and Hubei in the center, and the vast western regions, had large rural populations and less access to export markets. Restructuring therefore affected different regions at different times and in different ways.

Students should also keep in mind that "China" in this chapter means the People's Republic of China, whose territory, population, and political system differ from earlier "Chinas" examined in this textbook (see Chapter 1). Hong Kong and Macao, until 1997 and 1999, were under British and Portuguese administration respectively, and Taiwan was governed by the Republic of China (Chapter 65). Their commercial relationships with the mainland, explored in Division IV, were therefore also relationships across distinct legal and political systems.

Finally, this chapter treats economic transformation as a set of distinct mechanisms, not a single rise. A rise in GDP, a reform of tax collection, a change in factory ownership, and an expansion of exports each had separate causes, beneficiaries, and costs. The chapter's task is to separate them, to show how they interacted, and to avoid explaining all of them with a single word such as "reform," "globalization," or "state capitalism."

The Southern Tour and the Renewal of Reform

Section 74.1

Economic Retrenchment and Uncertainty After 1989

The retrenchment that followed 1989 did not begin with the protests. In September 1988, after retail inflation had risen sharply and households had rushed to buy goods and withdraw savings, the leadership had already launched a program of "improving the economic environment and rectifying the economic order." Credit was restricted, investment projects were cancelled or delayed, and the government sought to reassert control over prices and over the allocation of scarce materials. The events of June 1989 then made the political atmosphere far more cautious. Senior figures who emphasized planning and stability, among them Premier Li Peng and the veteran economic planner Chen Yun, gained influence. Chen's well-known image of the economy as a bird that must be kept within the cage of the plan expressed a view that markets could supplement but should not displace central planning.

The economic effects were immediate and uneven. Official real growth fell to around four percent in 1989 and 1990, a sharp drop from the double-digit pace of the mid-1980s. Many state enterprises found themselves with unsold stocks and unpaid bills, and chains of mutual indebtedness, often called "triangular debts," spread among firms that could not collect payment from one another. Township and village enterprises, which depended heavily on local bank credit and on informal supply arrangements, were hit by tighter lending. The number of individually owned businesses fell. Foreign direct investment stagnated at about three and a half billion dollars a year, as sanctions and political uncertainty deterred some investors, although others from Hong Kong and Taiwan continued to place capital in the southern provinces.

Ideological argument shifted as well. Critics of reform asked whether particular policies were "surnamed capitalist or surnamed socialist" (xing zi xing she), and warned that special economic zones and foreign investment could open the way to "peaceful evolution," a term the Party used for the gradual undermining of socialism through Western influence. Reform-minded officials were reluctant to propose bold steps. At the same time, important building blocks of later change were put in place. In 1990 the government announced the development of the Pudong district east of Shanghai, and stock exchanges opened in Shanghai (December 1990) and Shenzhen (1991). In 1991 growth recovered to about nine percent. The direction of policy was therefore genuinely unsettled: investment and growth were recovering, but the leadership had not decided how far marketization could go.

Historians draw on several kinds of evidence for this period: Party documents and speeches (many published later in selected collections), official statistics, newspaper commentary, and retrospective accounts by officials and economists. Because internal Party debates were conducted in closed meetings, many details come from memoirs and interviews that may be shaped by later political positions. Students should therefore treat a precise reconstruction of who argued what, and when, as provisional.

Section 74.2

The Collapse of Soviet Communism and China's Reform Debate

Between 1989 and 1991 the communist governments of Eastern Europe fell, and in August 1991 an attempted coup against Mikhail Gorbachev failed. On 25 December 1991 the Soviet Union ceased to exist. Chinese leaders watched with great attention, and many drew the lesson that the Soviet Party had lost control through a combination of economic stagnation, political liberalization without institutional safeguards, ethnic division, and a weakening of ideological discipline. For conservatives, the collapse confirmed the dangers of abandoning socialist principles and relaxing political vigilance. For reform advocates, the same events demonstrated that a government that failed to raise living standards could lose public support.

These two readings were not entirely opposed. Almost all leaders agreed that the Party must retain political control and must keep itself united. The argument was about economic means. Reformers argued that only faster growth and rising incomes could secure the Party's legitimacy, and they pointed to China's own history since 1978 as evidence. Cautious leaders worried that rapid marketization, speculation, and inflation would produce social unrest of the kind that had helped trigger the 1989 crisis. China's leaders also observed the experience of Russia and Eastern Europe after 1991, where rapid price liberalization and privatization were accompanied by deep output declines and political turmoil. Chinese officials and many economists cited this as an argument for gradual, experimental change, although later scholars debate how fully such comparisons explain the very different Chinese outcomes, which also reflected China's lower share of industrial employment, its large rural labor surplus, and its different political structure.

The debate over reform in 1991 was therefore less a choice between market and plan than a dispute about how reform would be authorized and who would define socialism. Deng Xiaoping's frustration with the cautious climate, and his belief that the Party could not survive without continued improvement in living standards, made him willing to intervene in the debate directly, which he did in early 1992.

Section 74.3

Deng Xiaoping's Southern Tour of 1992

Deng Xiaoping's journey lasted from mid-January to late February 1992. He visited Wuchang (Wuhan) on 18 January, spent several days in Shenzhen, travelled to Zhuhai, and ended in Shanghai. He held no title that gave him formal authority to direct policy, and this distinction matters: his influence came from his revolutionary standing, the loyalty of senior officials and military figures, and the fact that local leaders in Guangdong, Shanghai, and elsewhere wanted political cover to move faster. The tour was less a command than a signal that was amplified by officials who found it useful.

The substance of the remarks, as later published, was a defense of reform in practical terms. Deng argued that the central question was development, that those who feared too much "capitalism" should recognize that markets and planning were both economic tools, and that policies should be judged by whether they raised productive capacity, strengthened the country, and improved living standards. He warned that anyone who would not reform risked losing power, and he told officials to avoid fruitless debates about labels. He did not announce a new program in detail. The effect was to shift the burden of proof: officials who wished to proceed with market-oriented projects could now invoke the leader's support, and those who hesitated risked being seen as obstructing reform.

The way the message spread shows how the political system worked. Deng's remarks were not immediately printed in the national press. They circulated first within Party circles and then, in March 1992, as a Central Committee document. A widely read newspaper account, published in the Shenzhen Special Zone Daily on 26 March under a title that spoke of a spring wind blowing from the east, brought the story to a national audience. In the following months provincial governments announced new development zones, enterprises raised capital, foreign investors renewed plans, and investment spending rose steeply. In 1992 real growth reached about fourteen percent, and fixed-asset investment surged in both 1992 and 1993, leading to renewed inflation and the need for another round of stabilization (Section 74.5).

Primary Source Window

Deng Xiaoping, Talks in Wuchang, Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Shanghai (January–February 1992)

Creator: Deng Xiaoping, as recorded and edited by Party officials · Date: January–February 1992; circulated March 1992; published in Selected Works of Deng Xiaoping, vol. 3 (English edition, Foreign Languages Press, 1994) · Type: Edited record of informal speeches and conversations

Context: After three years of retrenchment, Deng spoke to local officials and managers during a tour of the south. The published text is a compilation prepared by Party editors. It is not a verbatim transcript of a single speech.

Paraphrase: Deng argued that the amount of planning or market involved does not separate socialism from capitalism, and that the three tests of a policy are whether it advances productive forces, builds national strength, and raises living standards. In the standard English translation he describes planning and market forces as both "means of controlling economic activity."

Analysis: The text is valuable because it reveals how reform was justified in the Party's own vocabulary: through criteria of results ("benefits") rather than through a theory of property rights. It also shows the style of Chinese policy communication, in which a leader's remarks, rather than a legal text, set the terms. Its limits are considerable. It reflects the version that Party editors chose to publish, it says little about implementation, and it does not record the objections of conservatives or the views of workers, farmers, or foreign observers. Translations differ in wording between editions.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about how Deng defines the test of a successful policy?
  2. Who created the published text, and how might editing have shaped it?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the way the Party authorized policy change?
  4. What can historians not learn from it alone about what happened after 1992?
Historical Debate

Did the Southern Tour Redirect or Accelerate Reform?

The question: Many accounts present 1992 as the moment when Deng rescued reform from conservative resistance. Others stress that market-oriented changes were already advancing and that the tour sped up what was in motion. The disagreement bears on how historians understand the role of individual leaders in economic change.

Interpretation A — The tour redirected a stalled process: Scholars of elite politics, including biographers of Deng and historians of the Party leadership, argue that the retrenchment of 1989–1991 had put reform at real risk. Conservative leaders influenced policy, and without Deng's intervention the leadership might have settled on a more cautious, plan-oriented course. On this view, the tour was a decisive political act.
Interpretation B — The tour accelerated existing momentum: Economists and historians who emphasize institutional and local dynamics point out that growth was already recovering in 1991, that Pudong and the stock exchanges had been launched, that local governments and enterprises had strong incentives to expand, and that Jiang Zemin and others were moving toward greater market orientation. On this view, the tour gave political permission to forces that were already building.

Evidence: Both sides use Party documents, memoirs, the timing of the 1991 recovery, and the investment surge of 1992–1993. They differ on how heavily to weigh elite choices against structural pressures, and they face the common difficulty that internal deliberations are poorly documented.

Why it matters: The question concerns whether Chinese economic reform depended on a single leader's decisive action or on broader institutional and regional pressures. It also shapes how students should read later "turning points."

Student task: What additional evidence, for example about regional investment decisions before January 1992 or about the content of internal Party meetings, would help decide between the interpretations?

Section 74.4

The Fourteenth Party Congress and the Socialist Market Economy

The Fourteenth National Congress of the Communist Party met in Beijing in October 1992. In his political report, General Secretary Jiang Zemin affirmed Deng Xiaoping's theory of "building socialism with Chinese characteristics" and declared that the aim of economic restructuring was to establish a "socialist market economy." The report said the market should play the basic role in allocating resources under the state's macroeconomic regulation. The formulation was deliberately broad. It rejected the older idea that planning was the defining feature of socialism, yet it kept the adjective "socialist," which signaled continued Party leadership, a dominant place for public ownership, and a commitment to distributional goals.

The Congress also reshaped the leadership. The Standing Committee of the Politburo now included Jiang Zemin, Li Peng, Qiao Shi, Li Ruihuan, Zhu Rongji, Liu Huaqing, and Hu Jintao, a group that combined older and younger officials and gave Zhu Rongji a central role in economic management. The older generation of revolutionary veterans retreated further from formal power, and the Central Advisory Commission was abolished. In March 1993 the National People's Congress amended the Constitution to replace the reference to a planned economy based on public ownership with a statement that the state practiced a "socialist market economy," and to change the term for state-run enterprises from "state-run" (guoying) to "state-owned" (guoyou). The change in wording expressed a policy shift: the state would act as owner, and enterprises would be expected to make their own operational decisions within the law.

The ideological construction should not be mistaken for a plan. The report did not specify how prices, ownership, taxes, or banks would be reorganized. It provided authorization, a vocabulary, and a goal that later policy documents, discussed in the following sections, would make concrete.

Primary Source Window

Jiang Zemin, Report to the Fourteenth National Congress of the Communist Party of China (October 1992)

Creator: Jiang Zemin, on behalf of the Thirteenth Central Committee · Date: 12 October 1992 · Type: Party Congress political report (official, published in English by Foreign Languages Press and by Chinese official media)

Context: Delivered at the opening of the Congress after Deng's tour, the report set the Party's overall line for the following five years.

Paraphrase: The report states that the goal of economic restructuring is to set up a socialist market economic system, in which the market has a "basic" role in allocating resources subject to state macroeconomic control, while public ownership remains the main body alongside other forms of ownership.

Analysis: The report is a statement of political line, not a description of existing practice. It shows how the leadership reconciled markets with Party doctrine by redefining socialism around ownership and leadership rather than central planning. Its limits are that Congress reports are drafted by consensus, avoid specifics, and do not reveal disagreements. They are also addressed to the Party and its cadres, so they say little about how ordinary people understood or experienced the shift.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the report say about the relationship between markets and the state?
  2. Who wrote it, for whom, and what purpose did it serve?
  3. What can historians learn about the legitimizing function of ideology?
  4. What can they not learn about actual implementation?
Section 74.5

Jiang Zemin, Zhu Rongji, and the 1993 Framework for Economic Reform

The investment boom that followed the Southern Tour soon produced overheating. Banks lent heavily, local governments launched hundreds of "development zones," real estate speculation flourished in coastal cities, and consumer price inflation climbed, reaching roughly twenty-four percent in 1994 by the official consumer index. Zhu Rongji, who became executive vice premier in 1993 and also served as governor of the People's Bank of China, led a stabilization program in mid-1993 that restricted credit, curbed illegal fundraising, reined in speculative zones, and reasserted central control over bank lending. The program, which also involved reforms of taxes and exchange rates, produced what economists call a "soft landing": by 1996 inflation had fallen to single digits while growth remained near ten percent.

In November 1993 the Third Plenum of the Fourteenth Central Committee adopted a lengthy decision on establishing a socialist market economic structure. It set out priorities that would organize policy through the decade: a modern enterprise system with clear property rights and separation of enterprises from administrative authorities; a unified national market for goods, capital, labor, and technology; indirect macroeconomic regulation in place of administrative commands; an income distribution system that gave priority to efficiency while paying attention to fairness; and a new social security system. The decision marked the shift from piecemeal experimentation to an effort to design a coherent institutional framework.

Jiang Zemin served as General Secretary from 1989, as head of the Central Military Commission, and as state President from March 1993, combining the three main offices. He built his authority gradually and relied heavily on technocrats and on Zhu Rongji's administrative drive. Their partnership produced the major changes of the 1990s, but implementation varied. Local governments sometimes slowed or adapted central instructions, and some of the 1993 aims, such as a full social security system, took many years to materialize.

Cause and Effect

Reform Ideology, Political Authorization, and Institutional Change

Causes

Long-term: a decade of partial reforms and a mixed plan-market economy. Short-term: post-1989 retrenchment, the Soviet collapse, Deng's intervention, and local officials' wish for freedom to expand.

Event or transformation

The Southern Tour, the Fourteenth Congress, and the 1993 Third Plenum moved reform from experiment to a declared institutional program.

Effects

Immediate: an investment boom and inflation. Long-term: new fiscal, banking, enterprise, and legal institutions, discussed in Divisions II and III.

Historical complexity

Ideological authorization was necessary but not sufficient. The institutions that resulted depended on bureaucratic bargaining, local interests, and technical design.

Note: A political declaration did not by itself create a tax system or a bank. Students should ask who built each institution, with what resources, and against what resistance.

Division II · 1993–2001

Building the Institutions of a Market Economy

A market economy requires fiscal rules, banks, currency arrangements, and legal frameworks, not only private businesses.

Section 74.6

The Tax-Sharing Reform of 1994

By the early 1990s the central government's fiscal position had weakened. Under the "fiscal contracting" arrangements of the 1980s, provinces negotiated how much revenue to remit to Beijing, and many kept most of what they collected. Total government budget revenue as a share of national output fell over the 1980s and early 1990s to levels low by international standards, and the central government's share of that revenue shrank. The center struggled to finance national projects, to redistribute resources among rich and poor provinces, and to respond to macroeconomic shocks. Rapid growth in the coastal provinces also meant that central dependence on local goodwill had grown.

The reform introduced on 1 January 1994 replaced contracting with a "tax-sharing system" (fenshuizhi). Taxes were divided into central taxes, local taxes, and shared taxes. A value-added tax with a standard rate of seventeen percent became the main shared tax, with the central government taking seventy-five percent and localities twenty-five percent; the consumption tax and some other levies went to the center; business tax and enterprise income tax went largely to local governments (details changed in later years). Separate national and local tax bureaus were created, so that the central authority collected its own taxes instead of relying on provincial agencies. The center also promised "tax returns" and transfers to localities, drawing on a formula that left earlier revenues mostly in place at first.

The consequences were large. The central share of budget revenue roughly doubled, from about a fifth in 1993 to over half in 1994, and the government gradually built the revenue base for large national spending programs. Yet the reform left local governments with most responsibility for spending on education, health, infrastructure, and administration. Localities therefore looked for other sources of revenue: extra-budgetary fees, enterprise profits, land-lease sales, and borrowing through investment companies. The fiscal arrangement thus shaped the incentives behind urban development, land use, and local debt discussed in Sections 74.36 to 74.40.

Historical Vocabulary
Tax Sharing, Value-Added Tax, and Fiscal Decentralization

Tax sharing (fenshuizhi) is the system, introduced in 1994, in which taxes are assigned by type to the central government, to local governments, or to both. A value-added tax (VAT) is levied on the increase in value at each stage of production, so that a firm pays tax on its sales minus the tax already paid on its inputs. Fiscal decentralization refers to the assignment of spending responsibilities and revenue powers to lower levels of government.

Historians use these terms to explain a central feature of reform-era politics: the central government reasserted control over revenue, but local governments kept most spending duties and substantial discretion over land and investment. A tax reform in 1994 therefore helps explain behavior in the 2000s, such as local competition for factories and the growth of local debt.

Section 74.7

Exchange-Rate Reform and the Integration of China's Currency System

Before 1994 China operated a complicated system of multiple exchange rates. An official rate applied to most government transactions and was set well below the rate at which foreign currency traded in local "swap centers," where enterprises could exchange yuan for dollars. Exporters, importers, and foreign investors faced different prices depending on the channel. A separate currency, Foreign Exchange Certificates, circulated for use by visitors and in certain stores. Multiple rates distorted trade, encouraged arbitrage, and complicated China's attempts to rejoin the world trading system.

On 1 January 1994 the government unified the official rate and the swap-center rate. The renminbi was devalued on the official market to approximately 8.7 per US dollar, in effect adopting the market rate, and the authorities announced a "managed floating" regime. In practice, after 1995 the yuan was held very close to 8.3 per dollar for the following decade. Enterprises were required to sell foreign exchange earnings to designated banks, and a national interbank foreign exchange market was established. In December 1996 China accepted the obligations of Article VIII of the International Monetary Fund's Articles of Agreement, which committed it to current-account convertibility: residents and firms could freely obtain foreign currency for trade and certain other current transactions.

Current-account convertibility is not the same as capital-account convertibility. China continued to limit cross-border flows of capital, including portfolio investment, short-term borrowing, and the conversion of domestic assets into foreign currency. Controls were not absolute and were sometimes evaded, but they allowed authorities to manage the exchange rate and to insulate the banking system from sudden capital movements. The unification and the lower, more competitive rate made exports more profitable, and the later accumulation of reserves discussed in Section 74.29 followed from this combination of an undervalued or stable currency and capital controls. In July 2005, after growing external pressure, China allowed a small revaluation and moved to a managed system referenced to a basket of currencies.

Section 74.8

Price Liberalization and the Changing Role of Planning

The "dual-track" price system of the 1980s allowed state firms to sell a planned quota at fixed prices and any surplus at market prices. It permitted growth without immediate shock, but it also created large opportunities for profit from the difference between the two prices, which was one of the sources of popular anger about corruption in 1989. During the 1990s the plan track shrank. By the mid-1990s the large majority of retail goods and a growing share of industrial inputs and farm products were sold at market-determined prices. The government gradually abolished grain and cotton procurement quotas, although it kept a system of price support and state purchase for some staple crops.

Planning did not vanish. The central government continued to issue five-year plans, now increasingly framed as indicative strategies and priorities for investment, and it kept administrative control over prices and entry in sectors it regarded as strategic or politically sensitive: electricity, oil, telecommunications, railways, tobacco, banking, and certain basic commodities. Land remained publicly owned: urban land belonged to the state and rural land to rural collectives, with use rights allocated by lease or contract. The state therefore retained a distinctive set of powers over economic activity even as ordinary goods markets became competitive.

For ordinary people, price liberalization meant that shortages declined and consumer choices expanded, but it also meant that costs of housing, schooling, and health care rose as subsidies were removed. The price reforms are a good example of why the chapter distinguishes marketization from privatization: the prices of most goods were set by the market, yet a large part of the economy's capital, land, and strategic sectors remained under state control.

Section 74.9

Company Law, Property Rights, and Commercial Institutions

Firms need legal forms in which to organize, raise capital, and limit liability, and they need courts and rules to enforce contracts. In the early reform years these were largely absent or improvised. The Company Law adopted in December 1993 and effective from July 1994 created two main corporate forms, limited liability companies and joint-stock companies, specified the rights of shareholders, directors, and managers, and provided a template for converting state enterprises into companies. Other major laws followed: the Contract Law (1999), the Securities Law (1998), laws on commercial banks and the central bank (1995), the Enterprise Bankruptcy Law (2006), and the Property Law (2007). The Constitution was amended in 1999 to recognize the non-public sector as an important part of the socialist market economy and to commit the state to governing according to law, and in 2004 to state that lawfully obtained private property is inviolable.

The effectiveness of these institutions was uneven. Courts were part of the state apparatus, local governments often influenced judges, and enforcement of judgments could be difficult, particularly against well-connected parties. Entrepreneurs relied on personal networks, local relationships, and reputations (the practice often described as guanxi) as supplements or substitutes for formal legal enforcement. At the same time, legal reform made practical differences in areas such as corporate registration, foreign investment, intellectual property, and contract enforcement, and it was in part a response to commitments China would make in WTO negotiations (Section 74.24).

Land and property rights were particularly complex. Urban land-use rights could be leased from the state for fixed terms, usually seventy years for housing, and transferred in secondary markets, but ownership of land remained with the state. Rural land was collectively owned, and village and township authorities had strong powers over its conversion to other uses, a situation that fueled disputes when farmers' land was requisitioned for development (Section 74.40). The legal framework thus created strong property rights in some areas and weak or ambiguous ones in others.

Historical Debate

Markets, State Planning, and Chinese Institutional Development

The question: Did China's rapid growth occur because of its marketizing reforms, because of the continuing strong role of the state, or because of a distinctive combination? And did weak formal institutions hinder or help?

Interpretation A — Market opening and private initiative drove growth: Economists such as Nicholas Lardy have stressed that the most dynamic parts of the economy were market-oriented firms, private entrepreneurs, and foreign-invested enterprises, and that growth accelerated when the state retreated from direct production. On this view the persistent state sector represented a drag, and reforms that expanded competition were decisive.
Interpretation B — State capacity and strategic intervention were central: Other scholars, among them researchers of industrial policy and political economy, emphasize that the state financed infrastructure, directed credit, shaped industrial clusters, and used local government competition to promote growth. Barry Naughton and others describe a hybrid in which state and market actors interacted, and in which the strategic sectors remained state-led.

Evidence: Sources include firm-level data on productivity by ownership type, studies of local government behavior, comparative case studies, and analysis of credit allocation. Sources differ on measurement: for example, productivity comparisons depend on how capital, land subsidies, and credit are priced.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians interpret reform: as a gradual retreat of the state, or as the restructuring of state power. Yasheng Huang has argued, for instance, that the 1980s rural entrepreneurial phase may have been more market-friendly than the 1990s urban state-led phase, a claim that other scholars dispute.

Student task: Which kinds of evidence would best test each interpretation? Why might the two views both contain part of the truth?

Section 74.10

Banking Reform, Nonperforming Loans, and Financial Supervision

In the planned economy, banks functioned largely as cashiers of the state: they distributed funds according to plan. In the 1980s the People's Bank of China became a central bank, and four specialized banks, later called the "Big Four" (the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China, the Agricultural Bank of China, the Bank of China, and the China Construction Bank) took over commercial lending. In 1994 three "policy banks" (the State Development Bank, the Export-Import Bank, and the Agricultural Development Bank) were created to finance projects of national priority, so that the commercial banks could in principle lend on commercial criteria. Laws on the central bank and commercial banks followed in 1995.

In practice, the large banks continued to lend heavily to state-owned enterprises, often for political and social reasons, including keeping factories open and employees paid. Many of these loans could not be repaid. By the late 1990s the proportion of nonperforming loans in the banking system was very large, with estimates ranging widely depending on definition, from over a fifth of loans to considerably more under stricter standards. The government responded in stages. In 1998 it issued special bonds worth about 270 billion yuan to recapitalize the four state banks. In 1999 it established four asset management companies (Cinda, Great Wall, Orient, and Huarong) to take over roughly 1.4 trillion yuan of bad loans, usually at book value, with funding from the central bank and bonds. A second round followed in 2003–2005, in which the state injected tens of billions of dollars from foreign exchange reserves into major banks through the holding company Central Huijin, and several banks sold shares to strategic foreign investors and listed on the Hong Kong and Shanghai stock markets, among them the China Construction Bank (2005) and the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (2006).

New supervisory institutions accompanied this clean-up: the China Securities Regulatory Commission (1992), the China Insurance Regulatory Commission (1998), and the China Banking Regulatory Commission (2003). The reforms improved the reported health of the banks and the transparency of their accounts, but they did not end the state's role in credit allocation. Large banks remained state-controlled, and lending continued to favor large state enterprises, local governments, and politically supported projects. This pattern became important again in 2009 (Section 74.44).

Section 74.11

Macroeconomic Stabilization and the Asian Financial Crisis of 1997–1998

The financial crisis that began in Thailand in July 1997 spread rapidly to Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, and other economies in East and Southeast Asia, causing sharp currency falls, bank failures, and recession. China was affected but avoided a financial collapse. Several features helped: the renminbi was not freely convertible for capital transactions, private short-term foreign borrowing was limited, the economy had large foreign exchange reserves relative to its short-term debt, and most bank lending was domestic and state-guaranteed in practice. Authorities kept the yuan's value stable rather than devaluing, a decision that Chinese officials presented as a contribution to regional stability and that critics elsewhere in Asia received with mixed views, since competing exporters had seen their currencies fall sharply.

Yet China was not untouched. Export growth slowed in 1998, weakening demand from crisis economies compounded pressure on coastal factories, and official growth fell to about 7.8 percent. Deflationary pressure appeared in 1998–1999. The government responded with fiscal expansion, issuing long-term treasury bonds for infrastructure, increasing investment in roads, railways, and water projects, and cutting interest rates. Hong Kong was hit directly: speculators attacked the Hong Kong dollar's peg to the US dollar, and in August 1998 the Hong Kong government intervened to buy large quantities of local shares and futures to defend the market and the peg. Beijing supported Hong Kong politically and signaled that it would not devalue.

The episode strengthened the argument, advanced by Zhu Rongji and others, that China needed stronger banks, tighter supervision, and gradual rather than abrupt financial opening. It also supported the case for joining the WTO, since leaders judged that domestic reform would be spurred by external commitments. Historians nevertheless caution against attributing China's resilience only to capital controls: its structure of lending, high savings, and low foreign debt were also important.

Division III · 1993–2005

State-Enterprise Restructuring and the Expansion of Private Capital

How the ownership structure of the economy changed without a wholesale withdrawal of the state from production.

Section 74.12

The Problems Facing State-Owned Enterprises

State-owned enterprises (SOEs) were the core of the urban economy in 1992. They produced most industrial output, employed the majority of urban workers, and were the channel through which the state delivered housing, medical care, schooling, and pensions. Reforms in the 1980s had given managers more autonomy and allowed them to retain part of their profits, but they had not forced firms to compete or to face bankruptcy. By the early 1990s, a large share of SOEs, especially those in older industries, were losing money. The "iron rice bowl" (tiefanwan), the guarantee of lifelong employment, now appeared as a liability: firms carried far more workers than they needed, often including retirees and dependents, and bore social-service obligations that new private or foreign-invested competitors did not.

Several factors compounded the problem. Equipment was frequently outdated. Firms depended on bank credit and were rarely closed. New competitors, including township and village enterprises, private firms, and foreign-invested manufacturers, entered the same markets with lower costs and fewer obligations. Price liberalization removed protective margins. Firms in the northeast, such as the heavy industrial enterprises of Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang, faced these pressures particularly acutely, since they had been built for the planned economy and were concentrated in single-industry cities.

The dilemma for the leadership was both economic and political. Closing or selling large numbers of loss-making firms would improve efficiency but would displace millions of workers whose welfare the Party had promised to protect. Leaving them unreformed would drain the banking system and fiscal resources. For several years in the early and mid-1990s policy oscillated between enterprise-level experiments (such as the contract responsibility system and, from 1994, the "modern enterprise system" pilot programs) and cautious steps toward restructuring.

Section 74.13

Grasping the Large and Letting Go of the Small

The strategy known as zhuada fangxiao, "grasp the large, let go of the small," was endorsed in the mid-1990s and given authoritative form at the Fifteenth Party Congress in September 1997. It distinguished between a relatively small number of large SOEs in strategic sectors, which the state would retain, consolidate, and strengthen, and a much larger number of small and medium enterprises, which local governments were permitted to restructure through sale, merger, leasing, conversion to cooperatives or shareholding companies, or closure. Premier Zhu Rongji set a goal of helping most large and medium-sized loss-making SOEs recover within three years (1998–2000).

The policy did not mean uniform privatization. Local practice varied widely. In some coastal localities, particularly in Zhejiang and Guangdong, small SOEs and collectives were sold to managers or outside investors in the late 1990s. In inland and northeastern regions, where few buyers or alternative employers existed, more firms were merged or closed, and local governments kept more control. Many managers acquired shares in the firms they ran, sometimes at favorable prices, which generated accusations of insider dealing and the loss of state assets. Large firms in sectors such as oil, power, telecommunications, steel, aviation, and banking were reorganized into groups that remained under state control. In 2003 the government created the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC) to act as the owner of central enterprises.

Research by economists using enterprise data has documented the scale of the restructuring. Between the late 1990s and the mid-2000s, the number of state-owned industrial firms fell dramatically and the share of state firms in industrial employment dropped sharply, while the surviving large state-controlled firms grew larger and, in many cases, more profitable. The result was a state sector that was smaller, more concentrated, and more capital-intensive, not one that had disappeared.

Section 74.14

State-Enterprise Layoffs and the End of Lifetime Employment

The restructuring of state firms was the largest displacement of urban workers in the history of the People's Republic. Employment in urban state-owned units, which stood at about 110 million around 1995, fell by tens of millions over the following decade, and urban collective firms shrank even more sharply in proportion. Estimates of the number of workers laid off between the mid-1990s and the early 2000s are on the order of thirty million or more, depending on definitions and on how retirements, transfers, and informal re-employment are counted. Some workers were retired early, some took buy-out payments, some found jobs in the private sector or services, and many entered long-term unemployment or irregular work.

An institution peculiar to this period was the category of xiagang, "stepping down from one's post." A xiagang worker was formally still attached to the enterprise, which was expected to pay a basic living allowance, continue to pay social-insurance contributions, and run a "re-employment service center" that helped find new work. From 1998 policy provided that the enterprise, the government, and social insurance would each bear a share of costs, and that after about three years workers would be shifted onto the unemployment insurance system and then, if needed, onto the urban minimum living guarantee (dibao) created in 1999. By the early 2000s the re-employment centers were wound down and the category phased out. The device softened the immediate impact of mass layoffs but also obscured their scale in official unemployment statistics, which captured only workers registered as unemployed.

Loss of a position in a state factory meant much more than loss of wages. For decades the work unit had provided housing, a clinic, a school for children, and a pension. In many cases these benefits were withdrawn or reduced as firms were closed or sold, and pension and medical arrears accumulated in distressed regions. Older workers, who had least chance of re-employment, and women, who were often laid off first, were especially vulnerable. Protests occurred in several cities, particularly in the northeast, over unpaid wages, severance, and pensions, including large demonstrations by oilfield and factory workers in Daqing and Liaoyang in 2002. Authorities responded with a mixture of payments, arrests of leaders, and, in later years, expanded benefits and the 2003 plan to revitalize the northeast's old industrial bases.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Worker Laid Off from a State Enterprise

This account is a composite based on patterns reported in surveys, ethnographies, and journalism. It describes no single named individual.

Consider a woman in her mid-forties who has worked since she was eighteen in a state-owned textile mill in a medium-sized industrial city in the northeast. Her husband works in the same enterprise. They live in a mill apartment that the work unit assigned them in the 1980s; their child attended the mill's school; the mill's clinic provided medical care. In 1997 the mill's output falls and the bank refuses to extend credit. In 1998 her section is closed and she becomes xiagang. For three years she receives a small allowance, much less than her former wage, and attends occasional meetings at the re-employment center, which offers training in domestic service or small-trade skills. She cleans offices and sells snacks at a stall, earning irregular income.

The purchase of the apartment (Section 74.36) gives the family an asset, but the lump sum they must pay strains their savings. When the mill is sold to a private investor in 2001, she is offered a buy-out payment, and the family's pension contributions are settled only partially. Her experience varies from that of a younger colleague who finds work in a new electronics plant, or of a manager who obtains shares in the restructured firm. Historians use survey data, memoirs, local archives, and ethnographic studies to reconstruct such trajectories, but they note that those who suffered most may be least represented in official records.

Section 74.15

Corporatization, Stock Markets, and Changing State Ownership

Corporatization transformed many state enterprises from administratively managed factories into companies with boards of directors, shareholders, and formal accounts. The stock exchanges in Shanghai and Shenzhen, established in 1990–1991, offered a venue to raise capital. Two classes of shares appeared on the mainland: A-shares, traded in renminbi by domestic investors, and B-shares, traded in foreign currency for overseas investors, and from 1993 selected state firms listed in Hong Kong as "H-shares." In 2000 the large oil companies PetroChina and Sinopec raised significant capital in international markets.

Listing did not mean that the state surrendered control. In most cases the state or a state-owned parent held a controlling stake, and a large portion of shares remained non-tradable until a reform of the "split-share structure" in 2005–2006. The effect was to introduce partial market discipline, with disclosure requirements, analysts, and investors, while leaving strategic control in the hands of the Party-state, which also appointed top managers. SASAC, established in 2003, supervised central enterprises, and the number of centrally controlled firms was reduced through mergers to well under 150 by the late 2000s, with many of the largest being among the world's largest companies by revenue.

Historical Vocabulary
State Ownership, Corporatization, and Private Ownership

State ownership means that the state, at central or local level, holds the property rights over an enterprise's assets. Corporatization means converting such an enterprise into a company with legal personality and a governance structure of shareholders, board, and management, which can occur without changing who controls it. Private ownership means that individuals or non-state organizations hold the rights. The reform-era economy also included collective firms, owned by village, township, or urban collectives, foreign-invested firms, and mixed firms with several types of owners.

Students should distinguish these categories carefully. A listed company with the state as majority shareholder is neither a traditional planned-economy enterprise nor a private firm, and many firms that were "collective" in name were in practice controlled by managers or local governments.

Section 74.16

The Expansion of Private Enterprise and New Economic Elites

Private business had been legalized in limited form in the 1980s, and the constitutional amendment of 1988 recognized a private sector as a supplement to the public economy. Early private businesses were mostly small: street vendors, restaurant owners, tailors, and repairers, registered as "individual households" (getihu). After 1992 the scope widened. Companies owned by private entrepreneurs, some of them former officials, engineers, or factory managers, appeared in manufacturing, construction, trade, and services. Many were family-run firms. Others, like Huawei in Shenzhen, founded in 1987, grew into large corporations; Haier, Lenovo, and later internet firms such as Alibaba (1999) and Tencent (1998) illustrate different organizational forms and relationships with the state, with some having roots in collective or state institutions.

The legal and political status of the private sector improved gradually. The 1999 constitutional amendment described the non-public sector as an important component of the economy, and the 2004 amendment protected lawfully obtained private property. In practice, private firms still faced obstacles. They had limited access to bank loans compared with state firms and often relied on informal finance, retained earnings, or family networks; they faced entry barriers in sectors reserved for state firms; and they depended on local officials for permits and land. Yet private enterprise accounted for a growing share of industrial output, employment, exports, and innovation, and the number of registered private firms rose from a few hundred thousand in the early 1990s to millions in the early 2000s.

New economic elites emerged. Wealthy entrepreneurs, real estate developers, and executives became visible members of urban society, and lists of China's richest individuals became a staple of business media from the late 1990s. Their relationships with officials varied: some succeeded through competitive innovation, others through connections, and some through both, and surveys show that a considerable proportion of owners had Party ties. Concerns about corruption, land deals, and the transfer of state assets to insiders contributed to public skepticism, a theme that returns in Section 74.34.

Section 74.17

Jiang Zemin's Three Represents and the Political Accommodation of Entrepreneurs

The expansion of the private sector raised a political problem: the Communist Party described itself as the vanguard of workers and peasants, yet private entrepreneurs were becoming a significant social group. Jiang Zemin's response, first articulated in early 2000 and elaborated in a speech on 1 July 2001, the eightieth anniversary of the Party's founding, was the formula of the "Three Represents." The Party, he said, must represent the development requirements of advanced productive forces, the orientation of advanced culture, and the fundamental interests of the overwhelming majority of the people. The speech also opened the way for private entrepreneurs to join the Party.

The Three Represents were written into the Party Constitution at the Sixteenth Congress in November 2002 and into the state Constitution preamble in 2004. Some Party members, especially on the ideological left, objected that the Party was abandoning its class basis. Others argued that it was a pragmatic way to incorporate rising social forces, to maintain Party leadership, and to prevent entrepreneurs from becoming an independent political force. Scholars debate whether it represented a transformation of the Party from a class-based organization into a broad governing party or merely a method of co-opting economic elites. Whatever interpretation is preferred, the doctrine illustrates a theme of this chapter: economic transformation did not lead the Party to cede political power; it adapted its membership and doctrine in order to keep it.

Section 74.18

Hong Kong's Return to Chinese Sovereignty in 1997

Hong Kong Island had been ceded to Britain after the First Opium War (Chapter 52), Kowloon was added in 1860, and the New Territories were leased for ninety-nine years from 1898. As the lease neared its end, the British and Chinese governments negotiated, and in December 1984 Margaret Thatcher and Zhao Ziyang signed the Sino-British Joint Declaration. China agreed to resume the exercise of sovereignty over the whole territory on 1 July 1997, and to establish a Special Administrative Region (SAR) in which Hong Kong's existing social and economic system and way of life would be preserved for fifty years. The National People's Congress adopted the Basic Law, the SAR's constitutional document, in April 1990.

The formula known as "One Country, Two Systems" had been conceived by Deng Xiaoping initially with Taiwan in mind. It gave Hong Kong an executive-led government headed by a Chief Executive, a legislature, an independent judiciary with final adjudication, its own currency and monetary authority, a separate customs territory and tax system, and the retention of the common-law legal tradition. Foreign affairs and defense were responsibilities of the central government. The Basic Law also stated an eventual aim of electing the Chief Executive and the legislature by universal suffrage, under conditions to be determined later, an aim that became a subject of political dispute in later years (Chapter 76).

The transfer took place at midnight on 30 June–1 July 1997. Tung Chee-hwa became the first Chief Executive. The economic arrangements were reassuring to businesses, but the handover coincided almost immediately with the Asian financial crisis (Section 74.11), which brought sharp falls in property and share prices. The commercial significance of the transition is the focus of this chapter; later political contests over autonomy and electoral arrangements belong to Chapter 76.

Section 74.19

Hong Kong as a Financial, Commercial, and Investment Gateway

Hong Kong's importance to the mainland economy began before the handover. After 1978 many Hong Kong manufacturers, facing rising land and labor costs at home, moved production to Guangdong, particularly the Pearl River Delta, where they leased land, hired large numbers of workers, and exported through Hong Kong's port. Hong Kong supplied capital, management, marketing contacts, design, and shipping services, while the mainland supplied low-cost labor. Estimates of the number of mainland workers employed by Hong Kong-owned firms in Guangdong in the 1990s range in the millions. This "front shop, back factory" division of labor made Hong Kong the main entrepôt for China's exports and imports and a leading source of reported foreign direct investment.

The financial side was equally important. The Hong Kong stock exchange became the principal venue for mainland companies seeking international capital, through H-shares and through "red chip" companies controlled from the mainland but incorporated elsewhere. Hong Kong banks arranged syndicated loans, and Hong Kong's courts and accounting standards gave foreign investors confidence. Reported Hong Kong investment in China, however, overstates the territory's own contribution. A significant portion of the recorded flows was "round-tripping," in which mainland entities sent capital to Hong Kong and returned it as foreign investment to benefit from tax and other incentives, and Hong Kong also served as a conduit for investors from Taiwan, other parts of Asia, and the West. Historians therefore use the term "Hong Kong investment" with care.

After 1997 the relationship deepened and changed. A Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement signed in 2003 gave Hong Kong goods and services preferential access to the mainland. Shenzhen and Guangzhou developed their own ports, finance sectors, and service industries, reducing the extent to which mainland trade needed to pass through Hong Kong, while Hong Kong shifted towards finance, professional services, and logistics. The territory's role as a place where mainland and international capital markets meet persisted, but its share of mainland GDP, once very large, declined as the mainland grew.

Section 74.20

Macao's Return to Chinese Sovereignty in 1999

Portuguese traders had settled at Macao in the sixteenth century with the permission of Ming officials, and the settlement long operated as a trading post and a Catholic mission center (Chapters 42 and 44). After a revolution in Portugal in 1974, Lisbon recognized Macao as Chinese territory under temporary Portuguese administration, and in April 1987 Portugal and the PRC signed a Joint Declaration on the Macao question. The Macao Basic Law, adopted in 1993, provided for a high degree of autonomy for fifty years. The transfer took place on 20 December 1999, and Edmund Ho became the first Chief Executive.

Macao's economy was dominated by casino gaming, which had been operated under a long-standing monopoly. In 2002 the new government ended the monopoly and awarded concessions to additional operators, including American and Hong Kong-based companies, bringing large investment in resorts and casinos. Visitor numbers, especially from mainland China, rose rapidly after mainland tourism policies were relaxed in 2003, and by 2006 Macao's gaming revenue surpassed that of the Las Vegas Strip. The economy became extremely specialized and dependent on gaming and tourism, and it also became a center of regional connections with Guangdong, particularly Zhuhai across the border. Macao also signed a Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement with the mainland in 2003.

Historians point out that Macao's experience illustrates how territorial transition and economic opening interacted: a small territory with a distinctive legal system used its status to attract international capital, while the mainland's growth and policy on visitor travel determined much of its prosperity. Questions about labor, inequality, and regulation arose, but later developments are beyond this chapter's scope.

Section 74.21

Taiwan, Cross-Strait Investment, and Economic Integration

The Republic of China government in Taiwan permitted its residents to visit the mainland from 1987, and Taiwanese businesses soon began investing there, first in labor-intensive light industry such as footwear, toys, and garments, then in electronics and computer hardware. Investment was often channeled through Hong Kong or third countries because direct links were restricted and Taipei limited official contact. Beijing offered preferential treatment, including a 1994 law on the protection of Taiwanese investment. Taiwanese firms concentrated in Guangdong, Fujian, and later Jiangsu, especially around Kunshan and Suzhou, and Taiwanese managers brought production methods and connections to global brand customers. The electronics manufacturer Foxconn (Hon Hai), for example, built very large factory complexes in Shenzhen and later in other cities.

Political relations were tense. Taiwan's government at times sought to slow or direct investment (for instance through the "go slowly, be patient" policy announced in 1996), and the Taiwan Strait crisis of 1995–1996 showed the danger of military escalation (Chapter 65). Yet commercial integration continued, supported by business networks, and cross-strait trade grew rapidly. Direct flights, shipping, and postal links, the "three links," were established in 2008, and in 2010 the two sides signed the Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement. Taiwanese capital thus both reinforced mainland China's position in East Asian production networks and became a point of potential vulnerability for Taiwan's economy and politics. The contested political questions, including sovereignty and competing interpretations of earlier understandings, are examined in Chapter 76.

Section 74.22

The Expansion of Foreign Direct Investment

After 1992 foreign direct investment (FDI) in China rose dramatically. Utilized FDI, which stood at roughly four billion dollars in 1991, reached about eleven billion in 1992 and more than twenty-seven billion in 1993, and exceeded forty billion dollars annually in the second half of the 1990s. In 2003 China became the largest recipient of FDI among developing countries, and by the late 2000s annual inflows were close to or above one hundred billion dollars. Investors came from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, the United States, and Europe, and used several organizational forms: equity and contractual joint ventures with Chinese partners, wholly foreign-owned enterprises, and, later, majority holdings in listed companies.

Local governments competed intensely to attract foreign investors, offering tax holidays, cheap land, infrastructure in industrial parks, and administrative support. Chinese regulators initially required joint ventures in many sectors, such as automobiles, as a way to transfer technology and management to Chinese partners; results varied, and Chinese firms often learned most in sectors with intense competition. Foreign investment contributed to employment, exports, and tax revenue, and foreign-invested firms accounted for a majority of China's exports by the early 2000s. It also faced limits: restrictions in sectors the government regarded as sensitive, disputes over intellectual property, difficulties with local enforcement, and, later, concern among some Chinese officials that dependence on foreign technology constrained domestic innovation.

China and the Wider World

Foreign Investment and East Asian Production Networks

China's industrial rise took place within a regional system. From the 1980s, Japanese, then South Korean, Taiwanese, and Singaporean firms had shifted labor-intensive production to lower-cost locations, a process supported by rising currency values at home and by trade liberalization in Asia. Chinese factories became the final assembly point for components produced across the region, and the resulting trade patterns meant that China ran trade deficits with its Asian neighbors, who supplied parts, while running surpluses with the United States and Europe, where many finished goods were sold. Investment was therefore not simply something "done to" China: Chinese authorities selected terms, regions, and sectors, and Chinese suppliers, workers, and managers built capabilities that allowed the economy to move up from simple assembly. Later chapters return to the political and strategic consequences of these ties.

Section 74.23

Regional Industrial Clusters and the New Geography of Growth

Manufacturing developed in concentrated clusters. The Pearl River Delta in Guangdong specialized in electronics, toys, garments, shoes, and consumer goods, with cities such as Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Foshan each developing distinctive industries and supplier networks. The Yangtze River Delta around Shanghai, Suzhou, Wuxi, Hangzhou, and Ningbo combined heavy industry, electronics, and textiles, with a strong role for local governments and foreign investors, as well as many private firms in Zhejiang. The Bohai Rim around Beijing, Tianjin, and Shandong combined government, heavy industry, and ports. Smaller specialized towns produced a single product in large quantities, such as socks, buttons, or lighters, with hundreds of small firms in networks of suppliers and traders.

Local government competition was a driving force. County and city officials were evaluated and promoted in part on growth, investment, and revenue, so they sought projects and sometimes shielded favored firms. This arrangement stimulated development but also encouraged duplication of capacity, protection of local firms, environmental neglect, and the accumulation of local debt (Sections 74.38 and 74.40). Logistics improvements, including expressways, ports, and rail, lowered transport costs and enlarged the markets of each cluster. Inland provinces, including Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Sichuan, and Chongqing, increased their manufacturing share later, as costs rose on the coast and as government policy encouraged inland investment (Section 74.35).

Regional and Frontier Context

Coast, Interior, and Frontier in the Reform-Era Economy

The coast attracted most export manufacturing and foreign capital, and its cities grew fastest. The northeast, formerly the planned economy's industrial heartland, experienced layoffs and decline in the 1990s. The inland provinces supplied much of the migrant labor to the coast. The western and frontier regions, including Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, Qinghai, and Gansu, had smaller populations, relied more on resource extraction, state investment, and transfers, and had distinct ethnic, linguistic, and political histories (Chapter 48). Regional comparison prevents the mistake of treating the coastal export boom as the experience of the whole country.

Section 74.24

China's Long Negotiations to Join the World Trading System

The Republic of China had been one of the original contracting parties of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1947, but the government in Taipei announced its withdrawal in 1950, and the People's Republic did not take up the seat. In July 1986 the PRC applied to resume its status as a contracting party. The negotiations lasted fifteen years. They were prolonged by the pause after 1989, by disagreements over whether China should be treated as a developing country with lighter obligations or as a major trading economy, and by the complexity of China's still-evolving institutions. When the World Trade Organization replaced GATT on 1 January 1995, China's application was converted into a request for WTO membership.

The talks combined multilateral and bilateral components. China had to negotiate market-access terms separately with major trading partners, above all the United States and the European Union, and to present a schedule of tariff reductions, commitments for services and agriculture, and rules on trade-related matters such as intellectual property and investment. Disputes involved textile quotas, agricultural tariffs, banking and insurance access, telecommunications, auto-industry protection, subsidies, and the treatment of state enterprises. Political events, including the 1999 bombing of the Chinese embassy in Belgrade by NATO aircraft, interrupted progress, but Premier Zhu Rongji's visit to Washington in April 1999 and the November 1999 US–China bilateral agreement revived it. The European Union concluded its own agreement in May 2000, and the US Congress granted China permanent normal trade relations in October 2000.

For the Chinese leadership, the arguments for accession were domestic as well as international. Zhu Rongji and others hoped that external commitments would discipline state enterprises, open sectors such as banking and telecommunications to competition, and strengthen the hand of reformers against vested interests. Opponents, including some officials and academics, worried that agriculture, autos, and finance could be damaged. The debate shows that WTO membership was a deliberate decision, supported by a coalition inside the government, and not a passive response to foreign pressure.

Section 74.25

China's Accession to the WTO in 2001

The accession package was completed in September 2001 in the Working Party and approved at the WTO's Ministerial Conference in Doha on 10 November 2001. China completed its domestic ratification procedures, and it became the WTO's 143rd member on 11 December 2001. Taiwan, under the name "Separate Customs Territory of Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen and Matsu," joined shortly afterward, on 1 January 2002. The agreement required China to reduce tariffs substantially (the average tariff on industrial goods was to fall to under ten percent over several years), to lower or remove non-tariff barriers such as quotas and licenses, to grant foreign and domestic firms the right to import and export, to open services including banking, insurance, telecommunications, and distribution in stages, and to apply laws in a uniform, transparent manner with provision for judicial review.

The protocol also contained provisions that gave China's trading partners special protections. A "transitional product-specific safeguard" allowed members to restrict imports of Chinese goods causing market disruption for twelve years, and the protocol also allowed special price-comparability methodologies in anti-dumping investigations subject to specified conditions. Under Section 15(d), the particular methodology authorized by Section 15(a)(ii) was scheduled to expire fifteen years after accession, in December 2016; this did not automatically remove every trade remedy or settle all questions about market-economy treatment. Chinese negotiators accepted these transitional provisions as part of the accession bargain, and disputes about their subsequent operation continued.

The chapter's governing interpretation is that accession accelerated and locked in changes that were already underway. China had been lowering tariffs unilaterally since the 1990s, foreign investors were already building export bases, and domestic enterprises were already restructured. Membership increased the security of access to foreign markets, in particular by removing the annual uncertainty in the United States over China's trading status, and it reinforced domestic reformers' ability to cut regulatory barriers. Its effect on specific industries differed, and some, such as automobiles and agriculture, adjusted over several years.

Section 74.26

Export Manufacturing and the Emergence of China's Global Factory

China's exports grew from about eighty-five billion dollars in 1992 to over two hundred sixty billion in 2001 and about 2.05 trillion in 2012. The composition changed. In the early 1990s, textiles, garments, footwear, and toys dominated. By the 2000s, electronics, computers, telecommunications equipment, machinery, and household appliances accounted for most of the value. Factories in the coastal clusters assembled goods for global brands and retailers, sometimes under contract manufacturing arrangements in which Chinese or foreign-invested firms produced to the design and specification of customers elsewhere, and sometimes under their own brands. Large retailers, such as Walmart, and brand companies in the United States, Europe, and Japan sourced increasing shares of their products from Chinese suppliers.

The sources of competitiveness included low labor costs, large pools of rural migrant workers, dense networks of suppliers, rapidly improving logistics, and policies that favored exports, such as export tax rebates, special customs arrangements, and access to land and infrastructure in industrial parks. A less visible factor was scale: large factories and clusters allowed fast turnaround, specialization, and quick adaptation to orders. The end of global textile quotas under the Multifibre Arrangement on 1 January 2005 produced a surge of Chinese textile and garment exports and triggered temporary safeguard measures in the United States and Europe, which illustrate how the shift affected production elsewhere.

Export manufacturing was also socially costly and uneven. Factories relied on workers who were frequently young, often women, working long hours for wages that were low relative to the value of what they produced (Section 74.31). Not all production was low-technology, and over time firms in some clusters moved into higher-value goods, but the earlier dependence on labor-intensive assembly shaped the development of the economy for decades.

Section 74.27

Global Supply Chains, Ports, and International Production Networks

A single product could be made from components produced in many countries. In a typical electronic device, advanced chips might be designed in the United States, fabricated in Taiwan or South Korea, combined with displays from Japan or South Korea and parts from Southeast Asia, and assembled in a Chinese factory before being shipped to consumers in North America or Europe. China's role in many such chains was assembly, which employed millions of workers but captured a modest share of the final value. Customs and trade statistics record the entire value of the finished product as a Chinese export, though most of that value originated elsewhere.

Much of this trade took the form of processing trade, in which firms imported components duty-free, assembled them, and re-exported the finished goods. Processing trade accounted for more than half of China's exports in the late 1990s and declined to roughly two-fifths by 2012, as more domestic suppliers developed and as a growing share of exports used domestically made inputs. Researchers who have attempted to separate domestic from foreign content estimate that the domestic value added in Chinese exports was often substantially lower than the gross export value, especially in electronics, though it rose over time. Case studies of consumer electronics have found that China's assembly work accounted for only a small fraction of the retail price.

Ports, shipping, and logistics were essential. Container ports at Shanghai, Shenzhen, Ningbo-Zhoushan, Guangzhou, Qingdao, and Tianjin expanded rapidly; the deepwater Yangshan port off Shanghai opened in 2005, and Shanghai became the world's busiest container port around 2010. Standardized containers, computerized customs clearance, and new roads and railways lowered costs and made it possible for firms to integrate Chinese production into just-in-time international supply systems.

Historical Vocabulary
Processing Trade, Value Added, and Global Supply Chains

Processing trade is the import of materials or components for assembly or processing and re-export, often under special tariff and tax treatment. Value added is the difference between the value of a firm's output and the value of the inputs it purchased; it measures how much value was created in a particular stage or country. A global supply chain is a production network whose stages are located in several countries.

The distinction between gross export value and domestic value added is central. If a factory in China imports parts worth eighty dollars and exports a finished product priced at one hundred, the recorded export is one hundred dollars, but the value added in China is at most twenty dollars, out of which wages, profit, and taxes are paid. Historians and economists use value-added data to avoid overstating China's role as a producer, and also to see how the share of domestic content rose as Chinese suppliers developed.

Section 74.28

Trade Relations with the United States, Europe, Japan, and Asia

The United States and the European Union became China's largest export markets, while Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and ASEAN countries were the largest suppliers of components and equipment. China's trade surplus with the United States grew to more than two hundred billion dollars a year in the late 2000s by US measurements, a figure that is sensitive to how re-exports through Hong Kong and the origin of components are counted. Consumers in importing countries benefited from low-priced goods, and retail and manufacturing firms gained from lower costs. Some local manufacturing industries and communities suffered job losses, a point documented by economists studying the "China shock" in the United States and Europe, although scholars disagree on the exact size and on the extent to which technology and other trade also contributed.

Trade disputes became frequent. They included safeguard measures on textiles in 2005, anti-dumping cases on products such as footwear and tires, disputes over intellectual property enforcement, complaints about export restrictions on raw materials such as rare earths, and a long argument over the exchange rate: critics in the United States and Europe argued that China kept its currency undervalued, while Chinese officials emphasized exchange-rate stability and the dangers of rapid change. After 2005 the yuan rose gradually against the dollar, with a pause during the 2008–2010 crisis.

Relations with Japan combined economic interdependence with political tension, and relations with South Korea and Southeast Asia deepened as supply chains integrated. China and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations agreed to a framework for a free trade area in 2002, which entered into force in stages from 2005 and 2010. The pattern was of an Asian production network feeding Chinese assembly and global consumption, with China's own domestic demand growing in importance only gradually.

Section 74.29

Foreign Exchange Reserves, External Imbalances, and International Financial Integration

Large trade surpluses and capital inflows produced a vast accumulation of foreign exchange. Reserves stood at about 19 billion dollars in 1992, passed 100 billion in 1996, exceeded 800 billion by the end of 2005, and surpassed a trillion dollars in 2006, when China overtook Japan as the largest holder. By 2011 reserves were over three trillion dollars. The current-account surplus reached a peak of roughly nine to ten percent of GDP in 2007. Because the central bank bought foreign currency to restrain the yuan's rise, it had to manage the resulting increase in domestic money supply, using reserve requirements and the issue of central-bank bills, and it invested much of the reserves in foreign government bonds, particularly US Treasuries. In 2007 the government created a sovereign wealth fund, the China Investment Corporation, with initial capital of about two hundred billion dollars.

Economists link the imbalance to several domestic factors: very high household and corporate savings, low consumption relative to output, an undervalued or tightly managed exchange rate, and high investment in export capacity. Other economies, including the United States, with low saving and large borrowing, were the counterpart. Economists differ on how far the imbalances reflected Chinese policy and how far they reflected global conditions, but there is wide agreement that they posed risks. They exposed China to the fluctuating value of reserves held in foreign assets, to inflationary pressures, and to trade friction, and they left the economy dependent on external demand.

International financial integration remained selective. Capital controls were relaxed in some areas, with the Qualified Foreign Institutional Investor program (2002) permitting limited foreign portfolio investment and Qualified Domestic Institutional Investor schemes (2006) allowing limited outward investment, and Chinese firms were encouraged under the "going out" policy to acquire foreign assets, as when Lenovo bought IBM's personal-computer business in 2005. Other attempted acquisitions, such as the bid by CNOOC for the American company Unocal in 2005, met political opposition abroad.

Section 74.30

Rural-to-Urban Migration and the Changing Labor Force

Under the planned economy, the household registration (hukou) system tied people to their place of registration and to the agricultural or non-agricultural status they inherited, and it controlled access to grain rations, housing, and employment in cities. After decollectivization, agricultural productivity rose, and a large surplus of rural labor became visible. In the 1980s the state loosened restrictions, allowing rural residents to work in towns and, with temporary permits, in cities, and the movement gathered pace after 1992 as coastal factories and construction sites recruited labor. The population living outside their place of registration, often called the "floating population," rose from perhaps 20 to 30 million around 1990 to about 121 million in the 2000 census and about 221 million in the 2010 census. By 2012, the National Bureau of Statistics counted more than 160 million rural workers employed outside their home townships.

Migrants moved along established channels. Kinship, village, and county networks informed workers about jobs, and recruiters, labor brokers, and relatives helped arrange transport and housing. Migration was seasonal or cyclical for many, who returned for the harvest or the Lunar New Year, a mass movement that became the largest annual human migration in the world. Migrants usually kept their rural registration, which meant that they had land rights at home but often limited access in cities to schooling for children, public housing, and many social benefits. The system contributed to a labor force that was mobile but legally precarious, with cities relying on workers whom they did not fully include.

Policy toward migrants shifted over time. Early in the decade local authorities often treated migrants as a threat to order, requiring temporary residence permits and operating a system of "custody and repatriation" that could detain people without permits. In 2003, after the death in police custody of a young worker named Sun Zhigang, public outrage prompted the State Council to abolish custody and repatriation. In 2006 a State Council document set out principles for protecting migrant workers' rights, including payment of wages, and from 2006 the agricultural tax was abolished nationwide, easing burdens on those who remained in the countryside. The fuller story of hukou reform is told in Chapter 75.

Section 74.31

Factory Workers, Wages, and Industrial Labor Conditions

The workforce in export factories was dominated by young migrants, many in their late teens and twenties, and in the labor-intensive industries a large proportion were women. Workers often lived in factory dormitories, ate in company canteens, and worked long shifts, six days a week or more, with overtime during peak seasons. Pay systems combined basic wages, piece rates, and overtime premiums, and a significant portion of earnings could be withheld as deposits or delayed. Managers imposed strict discipline, fines for lateness, and limits on movement. In larger plants, assembly lines were highly standardized, and workers' tasks were repetitive and precisely timed.

Conditions varied widely. Some factories, especially large foreign-invested ones supplying global brands, adopted codes of conduct and submitted to audits, while many small subcontractors operated with few safeguards. Industrial accidents, occupational diseases, and fires occurred; a fire at the Zhili toy factory in Shenzhen in November 1993 killed 87 workers, many of whom were trapped behind locked or blocked exits. Later investigations of poisoning by solvents in electronics assembly, injuries in machine-pressing, and dust-related lung disease in mining and stone-cutting documented hazards. Workers' wages did rise over time, and from the mid-2000s labor shortages in coastal regions, rural land and tax policy, and rising expectations led to higher pay and, in some cases, better conditions.

Historians draw on ethnographic studies such as those by Pun Ngai and Leslie T. Chang, on surveys, on journalism, and on factory audits to reconstruct these experiences, while recognizing that each source has limits. Ethnographies capture depth but cover a few sites; surveys cover more but may not reach the most vulnerable; company audits may reflect management's presentation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Migrant Factory Worker in Coastal China

This account is a composite based on patterns reported in ethnographies, surveys, and journalism. It describes no single named individual.

Imagine a young woman of nineteen from a village in an inland province such as Henan or Sichuan. Her family farms a small plot, and the income from farming alone does not cover school fees for her younger brother. A cousin who already works in a Guangdong electronics factory tells her of an opening, and she travels by train and bus for more than a day with a cloth bag and the address of a labor broker. She obtains a temporary residence permit, pays a placement fee, and is assigned a bed in a factory dormitory shared with seven other women.

Her days follow a routine of twelve-hour shifts, with a short lunch in the canteen and few days off. She is paid a basic wage and piece-rate bonuses, from which a portion is held back until the end of the year. She sends a share of her earnings home, keeps a little for herself, and uses a mobile phone to keep in touch with friends and family. Her aims are to save money, to learn skills, and perhaps to change jobs, which she does after a year for slightly better pay. She faces risks: delayed wages, no medical insurance, and the possibility of being laid off when orders fall. After a few years, she may marry, return to the village, or settle in a smaller city. Historians note that experiences differed by gender, age, region, and employer, and that many migrants reported both hardship and a sense of independence unavailable in the village.

Section 74.32

Labor Law, Collective Representation, and Workplace Conflict

The Labor Law, adopted in July 1994 and effective in January 1995, established a framework for written employment contracts, maximum working hours, minimum wages, safety standards, social insurance, and dispute resolution. It was followed by regulations and, in 2007, by the Labor Contract Law, effective 1 January 2008, which required written contracts, limited the use of repeated short-term contracts, and imposed severance obligations, and by the Labor Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Law of 2008, which simplified procedures. These laws reflected both official concern about disputes and pressure from scholars, lawyers, and activists, and they prompted vigorous argument in 2006–2007, with some business groups, including foreign chambers of commerce, objecting that they would raise costs.

Enforcement was a persistent problem. Local governments, eager to attract investment, often tolerated violations, labor inspection agencies were short-staffed, and workers who sought redress faced lengthy arbitration and costs. The only legally recognized national organization, the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, was an organ of the Party-state with a mandate to maintain stability and to represent workers' interests, and enterprise-level unions were often controlled by management. Workers therefore relied on informal strategies: collective petitions, work stoppages, protests, and appeals to higher authorities. Strikes and protests were widely reported in the 2000s, among them a stoppage at a Honda parts plant in Guangdong in 2010 that led to wage increases, and Foxconn's Shenzhen facilities were scrutinized after a cluster of worker suicides in 2010. Government responses combined concessions, mediation, and, in some cases, repression of organizers.

The key analytical point for students is the distinction between legal rights and effective rights. A statute that guaranteed written contracts and wage payment did not by itself ensure that employers complied, nor that workers could afford to pursue claims. Research by sociologists such as Ching Kwan Lee has examined how workers used the law and how local officials mediated conflicts.

Section 74.33

Social Security, Unemployment, and the Changing Welfare System

The dismantling of work-unit welfare required the building of new systems. A basic pension scheme for urban employees, combining a pooled fund with individual accounts, was introduced in 1997; an urban employee medical insurance scheme followed in 1998; unemployment insurance regulations were issued in 1999; and the urban minimum living guarantee covered the poorest households from 1999. Later additions included a rural cooperative medical scheme revived and subsidized from 2003, a pilot rural pension program from 2009, and resident medical insurance for the urban non-employed from 2007. Financing relied heavily on local governments and on employer and employee contributions, which led to large disparities between regions and between groups.

Coverage was uneven. State employees and civil servants kept relatively generous benefits, workers in formal urban employment obtained partial coverage, and many private-sector employees, informal workers, and migrants remained uninsured or covered in ways that were hard to transfer when they changed locations. The cost of medical care and schooling, formerly subsidized, became a significant burden, and surveys in the late 1990s and early 2000s showed that medical expenses were a major cause of household impoverishment. Official policy under Hu Jintao and Wen Jiabao increased attention to social programs, but the system remained fragmented.

Multiple Perspectives

State Managers, Entrepreneurs, Migrants, and Local Officials

State-enterprise managers: Those running large state firms often welcomed corporatization and listing as sources of capital and independence, while managers of small, loss-making firms faced closure or sale. Some acquired shares or positions in successor companies; others lost their posts. Many also bore responsibility for explaining layoffs to workers who had been colleagues for decades.
Private entrepreneurs: They gained from legal recognition, from access to labor and land, and from export markets, but often lacked bank credit and depended on officials' goodwill. Their incentives favored fast expansion, and some owners cut costs by ignoring regulations.
Rural migrants: Migrants gained wages higher than farm income but faced insecure contracts, limited access to urban services, and exclusion from decision-making. They had less bargaining power than employers and local officials, and their ability to organize collectively was legally restricted.
Local officials: Promoted partly on growth and revenue, they sought investment and often tolerated violations, yet they were also responsible for maintaining order and could be penalized for large protests. Their positions varied between wealthy coastal cities with large revenues and poorer inland counties with heavy obligations.
Section 74.34

Unequal Gains: Rural-Urban, Regional, and Occupational Inequality

Incomes rose across the population, but unevenly. The ratio of average urban to rural household income, which had narrowed in the early 1980s as agriculture was decollectivized, widened again after the mid-1990s and reached roughly three to one by the late 2000s by official measures, higher if urban benefits are counted. Coastal provinces had much higher average incomes than inland and western ones. Wage differences by occupation widened, with managers, professionals, and employees in monopoly sectors earning far more than workers in manufacturing or agriculture. The official Gini coefficient for household income, a standard measure of inequality, was reported at about 0.48 in the early 2000s and about 0.49 in 2008, before falling slightly to roughly 0.47 in 2012; independent estimates are sometimes higher. The distribution of wealth, driven by housing and business ownership, became increasingly unequal.

Scholars explain these trends by multiple factors: the urban-rural gap in education and services, hukou restrictions, differences in access to credit and land, regional location, the legacies of state-sector privilege, corruption and rent-seeking, and returns to skills. The shift from egalitarian rhetoric to the priority of efficiency, expressed in the 1993 decision, was reflected in policy, but later Party documents emphasized fairness and harmony. Perceptions of inequality, in particular concern about officials and wealthy families benefiting from connections, shaped public attitudes.

Historical Debate

Did Market Reform Reduce Poverty at the Cost of Greater Inequality?

The question: The proportion of the population living in extreme poverty fell greatly between 1990 and 2012, while income and wealth inequality rose. Historians and economists disagree about how these trends are related.

Interpretation A — Growth lifted large numbers out of poverty, and inequality was a by-product of that growth: Using World Bank poverty lines, economists estimate that the share of people living on less than the international extreme-poverty line fell from roughly two-thirds in 1990 to roughly one-tenth by 2010. On this view, rapid growth driven by market reform and trade created employment and raised rural incomes, and rising inequality, while a concern, was less significant than absolute gains.
Interpretation B — Inequality reflected institutional choices that limited the gains of some groups and could undermine long-term development: Other scholars argue that hukou rules, land policy, unequal public services, state-sector privilege, and corruption shaped the distribution of gains, that official poverty lines were low, and that insecurity and costs of health and education offset some income gains. They point out that the largest poverty reduction occurred in the early reform years, when inequality was lower.

Evidence: Household surveys, official and international statistics, and studies of migrant and rural welfare. Measurement difficulties are significant: poverty lines, price indices, and survey coverage differ, and top incomes are poorly captured.

Why it matters: The debate affects whether reform is evaluated chiefly by absolute improvements or by distributional fairness, and which policies are seen as responsible for each.

Section 74.35

Regional Development Programs and the Expansion of Inland Investment

By the late 1990s the gap between the coast and the interior was a political concern. In 1999–2000 the leadership launched the Western Development strategy (xibu da kaifa), covering twelve provincial-level units: Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Guangxi, and Inner Mongolia. It emphasized infrastructure, such as highways, railways, airports, pipelines, and electricity transmission; ecological programs, including the conversion of cropland on steep slopes to forest and grassland; resource development; and fiscal transfers. Major projects included the West-East natural gas pipeline, which began operating in 2004, power transmission lines from western hydroelectric plants to the coast, and the Qinghai-Tibet Railway, which opened in July 2006. Complementary programs were announced to revitalize the old industrial bases of the northeast (2003) and to promote the "rise" of the central provinces (2004).

The effects were mixed. Public investment improved connections and raised growth rates in several western and central provinces, and from the mid-2000s some manufacturing relocated inland, especially to cities such as Chongqing, Chengdu, Zhengzhou, and Wuhan. Yet differences in per-capita income between coastal and inland provinces remained large, and the programs also generated debate. Supporters saw them as correcting regional imbalance. Critics pointed to the dependence of western areas on transfers and extraction, the environmental costs of mining and dams, and the fact that many large projects benefited coastal markets. In frontier regions such as Xinjiang and Tibet, development programs interacted with questions of migration, ethnic identity, and political autonomy, which are discussed in Chapter 76.

Section 74.36

Urban Housing Reform and the Commercialization of Property

Before the reforms, most urban residents lived in housing allocated by their work unit or by city housing bureaus at nominal rents, in small apartments that the state had underinvested in for decades. Experiments in the 1980s and early 1990s sold some public housing to tenants at subsidized prices and created housing provident funds, but allocation in kind remained dominant. In July 1998 the State Council issued a notice that announced the end of the welfare distribution of housing by work units, to be replaced by a system in which employees would receive housing subsidies in cash and buy or rent homes. The notice called for a multi-level supply, with "economical and comfortable" housing for low- and middle-income families, and market-priced housing for higher incomes, and for the development of mortgage lending and housing finance.

The reform interacted with the land system. Urban land remained state-owned, but its use rights could be leased to developers for decades. Local governments, which after 1994 had limited tax revenue, discovered that selling land-use rights generated large sums, and rules introduced in 2002 required commercial and residential land to be sold through public bidding, auction, or listing. Banks began to provide mortgages. In 2003 a State Council circular described real estate as a pillar industry of the economy. Home ownership rates rose rapidly, with the great majority of urban households owning their homes by the 2000s, and housing became the main store of household wealth. Property prices in large cities rose sharply, especially from the mid-2000s, and governments introduced purchase restrictions and tightening measures at various times, including in 2010–2011.

The consequences were large and mixed. Many families gained substantial assets, especially those who had bought public housing early at low prices or purchased before price booms. Construction employed millions and generated demand for steel, cement, and appliances. But rising prices made it hard for young people and migrants to afford homes, local government finances became dependent on land sales, and the combination of lending, developers' debt, and investment in housing made the property sector a source of financial risk. Because rural land was collectively owned and could be converted to urban use only through requisition, disputes between farmers and local governments multiplied.

Section 74.37

Roads, Railways, Ports, Energy, and Large Infrastructure Projects

Infrastructure expanded at a pace without precedent in China. The first expressway, in Shanghai, had opened in 1988, and the national expressway network grew from under 5,000 kilometers in the late 1990s to nearly 100,000 kilometers by 2012, one of the longest in the world. The railway system was modernized through successive "speed-up" campaigns, and a high-speed rail network began with the Beijing–Tianjin line in August 2008, followed by Wuhan–Guangzhou in 2009 and Beijing–Shanghai in June 2011. By the end of 2012 China had by far the longest high-speed rail network in the world. A fatal collision near Wenzhou in July 2011, which killed forty people, led to investigations and slower speeds, and the railways minister had been dismissed for corruption earlier that year. Ports and airports expanded, and electricity generating capacity rose more than sixfold between 1992 and 2012, with coal supplying the large majority of power.

The Three Gorges Project on the Yangtze was the most visible symbol. The National People's Congress approved it in 1992, though with an unusually large number of abstentions and votes against. Construction began in 1994, the dam was completed in 2006, and the last generators were installed in 2012, making it the largest hydropower station in the world. Its supporters pointed to flood control, electricity, and improved navigation. Critics pointed to the displacement of about 1.3 million people, the flooding of historic and archaeological sites, sedimentation and ecological change, and concern over engineering safety. Resettlement involved compensation and relocation but also complaints of corruption and inadequate livelihoods.

Infrastructure investment was financed by tax revenue, bonds, bank loans, and local land revenues, and it served several purposes: raising productivity, linking regions, creating employment, and stimulating demand during downturns. Evaluating its returns is difficult, since some projects had large economic benefits and others yielded low returns, and the long-run financial effects are explained in Sections 74.38 and 74.44. Resettlement and land requisition remained points of conflict.

Section 74.38

Banking Expansion, Credit Allocation, and State Investment

After the recapitalization of state banks, credit grew rapidly in the 2000s. Bank loans financed manufacturers, property developers, and infrastructure, and large state-owned enterprises, which were viewed by bankers as safe borrowers, obtained a disproportionate share. Private firms, particularly small and medium ones, found it harder to obtain loans and relied on trade credit, retained earnings, informal lenders, and wealth within families. A parallel system of "policy lending" directed credit to government priorities, and interest rates were controlled by the central bank, with deposit rates held low, which kept the cost of capital cheap for borrowers and meant that savers earned low returns. Economists describe this as a form of financial repression that subsidized investment.

Local governments played a growing role. Because the Budget Law of 1994 generally forbade them from running deficits or issuing bonds, many created "local government financing vehicles," companies that could borrow from banks using land and future revenue as collateral and that invested in roads, industrial parks, and urban projects. The pattern allowed rapid construction but concentrated financial risk in opaque corporate structures whose obligations were implicitly backed by public authorities. The share of investment in GDP reached extraordinary levels, nearly half by 2011 according to national accounts, and household consumption fell to about a third of GDP.

Continuity and Change Over Time

State Economic Authority Before and After Market Reform

What changed?
  • Most prices were set by markets, and private and foreign-invested firms accounted for most exports and a large share of employment.
  • State enterprises were smaller in number, corporatized, and in many cases publicly listed.
  • Housing, once allocated by employers, became a market commodity, and work-unit welfare largely ended.
  • The central government rebuilt its fiscal base and used regulatory agencies in place of direct plan commands.
What stayed the same?
  • Communist Party leadership of the state, with Party committees in major enterprises and banks.
  • State ownership of urban land and of large firms in strategic industries, and strong influence over credit.
  • Reliance on local government initiative and on investment-led growth.
  • Restrictions on independent labor organization and on capital flows.

Who experienced change most directly? State-enterprise workers, rural migrants, and urban households facing the housing market. Who experienced continuity? Senior officials and managers of large central enterprises. Why does it matter? The pattern shows that market reform changed the instruments of state power more than it removed state power.

Section 74.39

Economic Growth, Productivity, Poverty Reduction, and Industrial Upgrading

Official figures show real GDP growing at about ten percent per year on average between 1992 and 2012, with a peak above fourteen percent in 1992 and again in 2007, a dip to about 7.8 percent in 1998, and a decline to 7.7 percent in 2012. Per-capita output rose several-fold. In 2010 China's nominal GDP surpassed that of Japan, making it the world's second-largest economy. Growth came from capital accumulation, a large and mobile labor force, and increases in productivity as workers moved from low-productivity agriculture into industry, as firms learned from foreign partners, and as resources were reallocated from inefficient state firms. Some economists, such as Alwyn Young, argued that official growth figures overstated true productivity growth, while others stressed that reallocation and technology transfer were real sources of improvement.

Industrial upgrading occurred in stages. Steel, cement, coal, shipbuilding, and chemicals expanded dramatically, automobiles became a mass-market industry (China became the world's largest auto market in 2009), and exports shifted toward machinery and electronics. Chinese companies in telecommunications, high-speed rail, and power equipment began to compete internationally, though dependence on imported high-end components, such as advanced semiconductors, remained. Living standards improved: households acquired appliances, mobile phones, and, increasingly, cars, and life expectancy and literacy rose.

Measurement requires care. Growth rates are official and subject to revision. Poverty rates depend on the poverty line and on price adjustments. GDP does not measure environmental costs or distribution, and growth in output is not identical to improvements in well-being. The benefits and burdens varied by region, occupation, and gender, as the previous sections show.

Section 74.40

The Environmental, Fiscal, and Distributional Costs of Growth

Rapid industrialization and urbanization placed heavy demands on resources and the environment. Coal supplied roughly seventy percent of primary energy, and emissions of sulfur dioxide, particulates, and carbon dioxide rose. Around 2006–2007 China's total carbon dioxide emissions surpassed those of the United States, although per-capita emissions remained much lower. Many rivers and lakes suffered industrial and agricultural pollution, with incidents such as the benzene spill into the Songhua River in 2005 and the algal bloom in Lake Tai in 2007 prompting public alarm. Cropland was converted to construction, aquifers in the north fell, and air quality in many cities was poor. Environmental protection laws and agencies existed, but local officials prioritized growth, and enforcement was inconsistent. Chapter 75 treats environment and energy more fully.

Fiscal and distributional costs were also significant. Local governments' reliance on land sales and borrowing encouraged the requisition of farmland, often at compensation levels far below the prices at which the land was later leased to developers, which led to numerous local protests, including a prolonged dispute at Wukan village in Guangdong in 2011. Local debt accumulated: China's National Audit Office reported that local governments' debts stood at about 10.7 trillion yuan at the end of 2010. The costs of growth fell unevenly on farmers who lost land, on workers in polluted or hazardous workplaces, on residents of polluted regions, and on future generations who would inherit environmental and fiscal obligations.

These costs complicate any simple account of achievement. They also show that the policy debates of the late 2000s, over "scientific development" and a more "harmonious society," responded to problems created by the pattern of growth itself.

The Global Financial Crisis and China's Economic Position by 2012

Section 74.41

The Hu Jintao–Wen Jiabao Leadership and Adjustments to Development Policy

At the Sixteenth Party Congress in November 2002, Hu Jintao succeeded Jiang Zemin as Party General Secretary, and in March 2003 he became state President, with Wen Jiabao as Premier. Jiang retained the chairmanship of the Central Military Commission until 2004. The new leadership inherited a rapidly growing economy and a set of social strains. Its slogans reflected an effort to respond: the "Scientific Outlook on Development," which called for growth that was coordinated across regions and sectors and mindful of resources and environment, and the "harmonious society," which highlighted social equity and stability. The outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) in 2002–2003 exposed weaknesses in the public health system and strengthened calls for greater social investment.

Policy changes included the abolition of the agricultural tax in 2006 and the introduction of subsidies to farmers, reforms of rural health insurance, free compulsory education in rural areas, expanded social security, an emphasis on energy efficiency in the Eleventh Five-Year Plan (2006–2010), and the 2008 Labor Contract Law. The leadership also continued marketization: it brought bank reform to fruition, held the 2005 revaluation of the yuan, and kept expanding trade. Many scholars see the Hu–Wen decade as one of both rapid growth and strengthened state economic power, with large central enterprises growing and state banks leading investment. Others stress that attempts to rebalance toward consumption and social welfare met resistance from local governments, state firms, and interests that benefited from existing patterns.

Section 74.42

The 2008 Beijing Olympics and China's Changing Global Economic Image

The International Olympic Committee awarded the 2008 Summer Games to Beijing in 2001, the year of China's WTO accession. The preparations involved investment on a scale of tens of billions of dollars in stadiums, a new airport terminal, subway lines, roads, and environmental measures such as relocating factories and restricting vehicles. The Games, held from 8 to 24 August 2008, were presented by Chinese officials as a demonstration of the country's modernization and openness. The opening ceremony and the large athletic delegation attracted worldwide attention, and the 2010 Shanghai World Expo extended the display.

The Olympics were also occasions for international criticism, over human rights, Tibet (where protests and a security crackdown in March 2008 preceded the Games), air pollution, and press freedom, and for domestic nationalism. Historians treat them as significant as symbols and as a mirror of how the Chinese state wished to be seen, rather than as an economic turning point, since the investment spent was a small part of national spending. Their timing, just before the global financial crisis, meant that the image of confident growth was soon tested.

Section 74.43

The Global Financial Crisis and China's Exposure to External Demand

The failure of the US investment bank Lehman Brothers in September 2008 deepened a crisis that had begun with the collapse of the American housing and mortgage markets, and it caused credit to freeze and demand to fall in the United States, Europe, and Japan. China's banks held relatively little of the toxic assets that damaged Western banks, and the financial system was not directly threatened. The shock came through trade. Chinese exports, which had been growing by twenty percent or more a year, fell sharply in the last months of 2008, and for the whole of 2009 exports declined by about sixteen percent. Orders for toys, garments, furniture, and electronics dried up, and factories, particularly smaller exporters in the Pearl River Delta, closed or cut staff. Growth in the fourth quarter of 2008 slowed to well below the rate of preceding years, and official figures indicated that tens of millions of migrant workers lost jobs or had their working hours reduced, with an official estimate of roughly twenty million returning home.

The shock demonstrated the degree to which China's growth had come to rely on external demand, and the way global production networks transmitted shocks. It also showed the importance of the countryside as a reserve: many workers returned to family farms, and the government provided incentives for employment and training. The social concern about unrest, particularly among returning migrants and unemployed graduates, was an important motive for the response in Section 74.44.

Section 74.44

The 2008–2010 Stimulus, Credit Expansion, and State-Led Investment

On 9 November 2008, following a State Council executive meeting, the government announced a program of about four trillion yuan, to be spent by the end of 2010, to expand domestic demand. The package listed ten broad areas: affordable housing, rural infrastructure, railways, roads, and airports, health and education, environmental projects, innovation and industrial restructuring, post-earthquake reconstruction in Sichuan (after the earthquake of May 2008 that killed about seventy thousand people), increased household incomes through tax and subsidy measures, and tax reform, including a change in the value-added tax that lowered business costs. Additional measures included lower interest rates, a looser credit policy, and subsidies and tax cuts to encourage purchases of cars and appliances, particularly in rural areas.

The financing structure is easy to misunderstand. The four trillion yuan was a headline figure for a multi-year program, not a single outlay by the central government. Only a minority of it, roughly 1.2 trillion yuan according to official statements, came from the central budget. The rest was expected from local governments, state-owned enterprises, and bank loans. In practice, lending rose enormously: new yuan loans exceeded 9 trillion yuan in 2009, almost double the 2008 figure, and local government financing vehicles borrowed heavily to fund projects. Official GDP growth recovered to 9.4 percent in 2009 and 10.6 percent in 2010, and China's recovery helped sustain global demand for commodities and machinery, so that many observers credited it with supporting the world economy.

The longer-term effects were debated. The program supported employment and growth at a critical moment and delivered infrastructure, but it also contributed to rising local government debt, overcapacity in sectors such as steel and cement, inflationary pressure and property price increases in 2009–2010, and an increase in the dependence of growth on credit and investment. It reinforced the position of state banks and enterprises relative to private firms. The sequel, in the form of efforts to control local debt and to rebalance the economy, belongs to the story at the end of this chapter and to later chapters.

Section 74.45

China by 2012: Global Economic Power and Unresolved Structural Problems

By 2012 China was the world's largest exporter of goods, the second-largest economy by nominal GDP, the largest holder of foreign exchange reserves, a leading destination for foreign investment, and an increasingly important investor abroad. Its manufacturing output was the largest in the world, its ports were among the busiest, and its companies, many state-controlled, appeared among the largest in the world. Its role in global commodity markets, as a buyer of oil, iron ore, copper, and soybeans, affected producers from Australia and Brazil to Africa and the Middle East. Per-capita income was still well below that of wealthy countries, and large parts of the population remained rural and poor, so that the country was simultaneously a major economic power and a middle-income developing economy.

Unresolved problems were widely recognized in China's own policy discussions. The economy depended heavily on investment and exports; household consumption was a small share of GDP; local governments carried large debts and relied on land sales; property markets were prone to bubbles; state banks lent preferentially to large borrowers; labor and land costs were rising; the working-age population was about to begin to shrink; income inequality was high; environmental degradation was serious; and productivity growth was slowing. Policymakers spoke of "changing the growth model," and the Twelfth Five-Year Plan (2011–2015) emphasized rebalancing, services, and technological upgrading. In 2012 official growth slowed to 7.7 percent, the lowest in thirteen years. The leadership transition that began at the Eighteenth Party Congress in November 2012 opens the story told in Chapter 76.

Historical Debate

The Sustainability of China's Growth Model

The question: Was the investment- and export-led pattern of 1992–2012 sustainable, or did it contain contradictions that would require fundamental change?

Interpretation A — The model had reached limits and required rebalancing: Some economists argue that high investment, low consumption, rising debt, overcapacity, demographic change, and environmental limits indicated declining returns to capital and the need for reform of finance, land, state enterprises, and the hukou system. They cite the declining productivity of investment after 2008 and the growth of local debt.
Interpretation B — The model was adaptable, and its stresses were manageable: Others point to the state's record of adjusting policy, the large reserves of labor and savings, the potential for urbanization and technological catch-up, and the continuing availability of high-return infrastructure and industrial investment, arguing that pessimistic predictions repeatedly underestimated China's capacity to adapt.

Evidence: National accounts, debt and credit data, productivity estimates, demographic projections, and comparative studies of other East Asian economies. Critics note that official statistics may not capture hidden debts, and that forecasts depend on assumptions about policy.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians interpret the period: as the successful execution of a development strategy or as the accumulation of imbalances that later policy would have to address. As a historian, one should avoid reading the later outcomes back into earlier decisions.

Section 74.46

Chapter Review and Global Integration Checkpoint

The central question of this chapter is how China combined market-oriented reforms, large-scale state investment, private entrepreneurship, and international economic integration to become a major global economic power, and what institutional and social tensions that development created. The evidence reviewed above supports several linked conclusions.

First, the Southern Tour of 1992 and the Fourteenth Party Congress accelerated and authorized a reform process that was already underway; they did not begin it. Second, the 1990s were a decade of institution building: tax sharing and exchange-rate unification (1994), the Company Law, bank recapitalization and supervision, and later legal changes created conditions for markets. Third, ownership changed but the state did not leave: small and medium state firms were sold, merged, or closed, large state enterprises were corporatized and consolidated, the private sector grew, and the Party adapted its doctrine to include entrepreneurs. Fourth, integration into the world economy occurred through foreign investment, processing trade, and supply chains, with the WTO accession of 2001 locking in and accelerating changes, and with Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan-linked capital as important intermediaries. Fifth, the gains were large, in output, trade, incomes, and poverty reduction, but the costs, which included layoffs, insecure migrant labor, inequality, environmental damage, and local debt, were also large and were distributed unevenly. Sixth, the 2008 crisis showed both dependence on external demand and the state's capacity to mobilize credit and investment.

Students should be able to distinguish what policy documents announced from what was implemented, to compare state, collective, private, listed, and foreign-invested firms, to explain why gross export figures overstate domestic value added, and to evaluate both achievements and costs without reducing the period to celebration or condemnation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transformation of China's economic institutions, industrial structure, labor force, and international position between the early 1990s and about 2012. It began with the retrenchment that followed 1989 and the uncertainty about the future of reform, deepened by the collapse of Soviet communism. Deng Xiaoping's Southern Tour of 1992, supported by local officials and amplified by the media and by the Fourteenth Party Congress, accelerated a reform process that had begun in 1978 and gave it ideological authorization as the building of a socialist market economy.

The 1990s were a decade of institution building. The 1994 tax-sharing reform restored central fiscal capacity while leaving local governments with large spending duties, a legacy that shaped local behavior in the following decades. The unification of exchange rates and the move to current-account convertibility changed the basis of foreign trade, while capital controls remained. Price liberalization reduced the plan, though the state retained control over strategic sectors, land, and credit. The Company Law and later legislation created legal forms for firms, and banks were recapitalized and supervised more closely, though they remained state-controlled and favored state borrowers. China's handling of the Asian financial crisis showed both the value of those controls and the vulnerability of an export-dependent economy.

The ownership structure of the economy changed, but not through wholesale privatization. Under the policy of grasping the large and letting go of the small, many small state firms were sold, merged, or closed, while large enterprises were corporatized, listed, and consolidated under state control, supervised after 2003 by SASAC. The private sector expanded and gained legal and political recognition, culminating in the constitutional amendments of 1999 and 2004 and the Three Represents. The restructuring caused the displacement of tens of millions of workers, who lost not only wages but housing, health care, and pensions that had been provided through the work unit. Its costs were concentrated among older workers, women, and regions such as the northeast.

Hong Kong's return in 1997 and Macao's in 1999 integrated two territories with distinct legal and economic systems under the formula of One Country, Two Systems. Hong Kong served as a financial and commercial gateway for investment in the mainland, though recorded Hong Kong investment included capital that originated elsewhere, and Taiwanese firms built large production networks on the mainland even as political tension persisted. Foreign direct investment, joint ventures, wholly foreign-owned firms, and local government competition shaped industrial clusters in the Pearl and Yangtze River Deltas and later inland.

China's accession to the WTO in December 2001, after fifteen years of negotiation, locked in market-access commitments and accelerated integration into global production. It did not create China's export industry, which rested on earlier investment, infrastructure, labor supply, and policy. Processing trade and supply chains made China the final assembly point for products whose components came from across Asia, which means that gross export figures overstate domestic value added, although domestic content rose over time. The resulting trade surpluses and capital inflows produced vast foreign exchange reserves and global imbalances.

Rural-to-urban migration supplied the labor for the export boom and the construction of cities. Migrants earned more than they could on farms but faced insecure contracts, limited access to urban services because of hukou rules, long hours, and workplace hazards. Labor laws, such as the 1994 Labor Law and the 2008 Labor Contract Law, gave workers rights on paper, but enforcement was uneven, and independent organization was restricted. The welfare system was rebuilt in fragmented fashion after work-unit benefits disappeared. Incomes rose and poverty fell sharply, but inequality, between rural and urban areas, between regions, and among occupations, widened.

Investment in infrastructure, housing reform, and bank credit supported growth. The Western Development strategy and other regional programs aimed to reduce disparities. The 1998 housing reform created urban property markets and made housing the main household asset, while land sales became a major source of local revenue. High-speed rail, expressways, ports, power plants, and the Three Gorges Project transformed the physical landscape, with benefits in productivity and connectivity and costs in resettlement, environmental damage, and financial risk. Rapid growth brought severe pollution, resource demands, and disputes over land, and local debt accumulated through financing vehicles.

The Hu Jintao–Wen Jiabao leadership emphasized balanced development and social policy while continuing marketization and strengthening state firms. The Beijing Olympics of 2008 symbolized China's new visibility. The global financial crisis of 2008 cut export orders, led to factory closures and job losses among migrant workers, and showed China's dependence on external demand. The stimulus announced in November 2008 was a multi-year program of about four trillion yuan, of which only a minority came from the central budget, with the rest drawn from local governments and bank credit. It sustained growth and employment but increased debt and overcapacity and reinforced state-led investment.

By 2012 China was a leading exporter, the world's second-largest economy, and a major force in global trade, but it also faced structural problems: dependence on investment and exports, low household consumption, local debt, inequality, environmental stress, and an approaching demographic shift. The period shows that marketization did not mean the withdrawal of the state, and that integration into the world economy did not imply political liberalization. It also shows that economic transformation was driven by many mechanisms and actors, and that its gains and costs were unevenly distributed. These themes carry into the social, demographic, and technological changes explored in Chapter 75 and the governance and international questions examined in Chapter 76.

Key Terms

Socialist market economy
The official goal adopted in 1992: an economy in which markets play the basic role in allocating resources under state macroeconomic regulation and Party leadership, with public ownership as the main body.
Southern Tour (nanxun)
Deng Xiaoping's January–February 1992 visits to Wuchang, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, and Shanghai, which renewed momentum for reform.
Marketization
The expansion of market-determined prices, competition, and exchange, which is not identical to privatization.
Privatization
The transfer of assets or enterprises from public to private ownership, which in China occurred partially, mainly among small and medium firms.
Dual-track pricing
A 1980s system in which planned quotas were sold at fixed prices and surplus output at market prices.
Tax-sharing system (fenshuizhi)
The 1994 fiscal reform that assigned taxes among central, local, and shared categories.
Value-added tax (VAT)
A tax on the increase in value at each stage of production; the main shared tax after 1994.
Fiscal decentralization
The assignment of revenue and spending powers to lower levels of government.
Managed floating exchange rate
The system announced in 1994 in which the yuan was officially allowed to float within controlled limits, in practice held near 8.3 per dollar for about a decade.
Current-account convertibility
Freedom to exchange currency for trade and related payments, accepted by China in 1996; distinct from capital-account convertibility.
Capital controls
Restrictions on cross-border financial flows, which China retained after 1996.
Company Law (1993)
The law establishing limited liability and joint-stock companies, effective July 1994.
Nonperforming loan
A bank loan on which the borrower is unlikely to repay, widespread among state-bank loans to SOEs in the 1990s.
Asset management company
One of four institutions created in 1999 to take over bad loans from the big state banks.
State-owned enterprise (SOE)
An enterprise in which the central or local state holds ownership.
Zhuada fangxiao
"Grasp the large, let go of the small," the policy of keeping and strengthening large SOEs while relaxing control over small ones.
Xiagang
The status of a laid-off state-enterprise worker nominally still attached to the firm, used mainly in 1990s policy.
Danwei (work unit)
The urban workplace through which the planned economy distributed jobs, housing, and welfare.
Corporatization
Conversion of an enterprise into a company with formal governance, possible without change in who controls it.
SASAC
The State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, created in 2003 to act as owner of central SOEs.
Three Represents
Jiang Zemin's doctrine that the Party represents advanced productive forces, advanced culture, and the interests of the vast majority, used to admit entrepreneurs to the Party.
One Country, Two Systems
The constitutional formula under which Hong Kong and Macao retained their legal and economic systems within the PRC.
Special Administrative Region (SAR)
The status of Hong Kong (1997) and Macao (1999) under their Basic Laws.
Foreign direct investment (FDI)
Investment by foreign firms or individuals that gives lasting control over an enterprise in another country.
Processing trade
Import of components for assembly and re-export, often under tariff exemptions.
Value added
The value created at a stage of production, equal to output minus purchased inputs.
Global supply chain
A production network whose stages are located in several countries.
WTO accession
China's entry into the World Trade Organization on 11 December 2001 under a negotiated protocol.
Hukou
The household registration system that tied residents to place and status and limited migrants' access to urban services.
Nongmingong
Rural migrant workers employed in cities and factories.
Labor Contract Law
The 2007 law, effective 2008, requiring written contracts and limiting repeated short-term contracts.
Western Development
The policy launched in 1999–2000 to promote infrastructure and growth in western provinces.
Local government financing vehicle
A company set up by a local government to borrow and invest in projects, collateralized by land and expected revenue.
Stimulus program (2008)
The announced four trillion yuan multi-year package financed by central, local, and bank sources.
Current-account surplus
The excess of a country's exports of goods and services (and related income) over its imports; peaked around 2007 in China.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the economic transformation of the 1990s and 2000s reshaped the lives of hundreds of millions of people and changed the world's economic geography. It shows how a state that retained a Leninist party structure adopted markets, foreign investment, and global trade as instruments of national development, and how that choice created institutions, such as the fiscal system, state-controlled banks, and local government competition, that still frame Chinese economic life.

The period also reveals larger themes of this textbook: the repeated interaction of central power and local initiative, the importance of regional variation between coast, interior, and frontier, the movement of people from countryside to city, and the ways in which Chinese economic history is connected to Asia and the world. The coastal ports and trading networks of earlier centuries (Chapters 37 and 42), the commercial economies of the Song and Ming (Chapters 32 and 43), and the treaty-port economies of the nineteenth century (Chapter 52) provide historical background for understanding why the southeast became the center of the export boom, though the institutions of the reform era were new and should not be seen as the inevitable result of earlier patterns.

The chapter has asked students to separate marketization from privatization and economic integration from political liberalization, to read policy documents as statements of intent rather than proof of implementation, and to weigh the achievements of growth and poverty reduction against the costs of layoffs, insecurity, inequality, and environmental damage. These habits of mind prepare students for Chapter 75, which examines urbanization, demography, technology, and environment, and Chapter 76, which considers governance, territory, and China's role in the twenty-first century.

China's economic transformation was diverse, interconnected, contested, and unfinished. By 2012 it had produced a country that was both a leading global economy and a developing society with unresolved problems, and the history of that transformation remains an active subject of research and debate.
