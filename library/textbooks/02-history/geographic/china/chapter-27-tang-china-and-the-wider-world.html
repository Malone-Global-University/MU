Tang China and the Wider World

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Three centuries before the Tang dynasty was founded, a Sogdian woman named Miwnay, stranded in a Chinese frontier town amid war and famine, wrote an anguished letter to her mother in distant Samarkand, while a merchant named Nanai-vandak wrote to a partner named Varzakk about debts, goods, and the political chaos overtaking the towns along the Gansu Corridor. These letters, dated to 313–314 CE and now known to historians as the Sogdian Ancient Letters, never reached their destinations. They were found, sixteen centuries later, sealed in a Chinese watchtower along the desert route west of Dunhuang, part of a small bundle of stranded correspondence that predates the Tang dynasty by roughly three hundred years. By the time the Tang court rose to power in 618, the Sogdian commercial network these letters document had already been moving people, goods, debts, and family anxieties across this same frontier for centuries — a reminder that the Eurasian exchange this chapter examines was not a Tang invention but a deep, preexisting structure into which Tang China inserted itself as an unusually large, wealthy, and institutionally sophisticated participant. Tang China was never a sealed, self-contained civilization radiating culture outward to passive neighbors. It was one major node within a much larger, much older world of interacting states, mobile peoples, and long-distance exchange.

Chapter 26 examined how the Tang state organized government, law, land, taxation, and society within its own recognized territory, and how officials, examination candidates, landholders, and ordinary rural households experienced that internal order. This chapter turns outward. It asks how the Tang dynasty, at the height of its power during the seventh and eighth centuries, related to the states, peoples, and commercial networks that surrounded it: the Turkic and other nomadic confederations of the steppe, the oasis kingdoms and Sogdian merchant communities of Central Asia, the expanding Tibetan Empire on the high plateau to the southwest, the Korean kingdoms and their successor state of Silla, the Japanese court eagerly importing and adapting continental institutions, the trading polities of maritime Southeast Asia, the Buddhist and commercial worlds of India, and the Persian and Arab merchants and, eventually, the armies of the expanding Islamic caliphates.

It is tempting, and historically inaccurate, to describe all of this simply as "Tang influence" flowing outward from a superior Chinese center to grateful or subordinate neighbors. That framing gets the history backward as often as it gets it right. Silla defeated Tang forces and expelled them from most of the Korean peninsula after the two states had jointly destroyed Goguryeo and Baekje. The Tibetan Empire inflicted serious military defeats on Tang armies, married Tang and Chinese princesses into its own royal house in relationships in which Tibetan rulers at times possessed substantial diplomatic and military leverage, and at times occupied the Tang capital itself. Sogdian merchants, not Chinese officials, organized and profited from much of the long-distance trade that moved along the routes historians call the Silk Roads. Japanese rulers studied Tang institutions closely and then adapted, modified, or discarded them according to their own political needs, producing a Japanese state that resembled Tang China in some respects and diverged from it sharply in others. This chapter asks students to hold two facts together without collapsing one into the other: Tang China was genuinely one of the most powerful, wealthy, and administratively sophisticated states in the world during this period, and Tang power was embedded in, dependent upon, and repeatedly checked by other independent states and peoples who were pursuing their own interests rather than simply receiving Chinese civilization.

Mobility is the second great theme of this chapter. Soldiers of Turkic, Sogdian, Korean, and other backgrounds served in Tang armies and sometimes rose to high military command. Buddhist monks such as Xuanzang crossed Central Asia and the Himalayan passes to study in India and returned with texts, relics, and firsthand knowledge of lands the Tang court had never directly ruled. Korean and Japanese students and monks sailed and traveled overland to study at Chang'an's academies and monasteries, then carried back administrative models, religious teachings, and technical knowledge that they reshaped for their own societies. Sogdian, Persian, and Arab merchants settled in Chinese port and frontier cities, married locally, and sometimes remained for generations. Captives, refugees, hostages, and diplomatic envoys moved in all directions across contested frontiers. None of this movement was fully controlled by any single government, however powerful; the Tang state, like its neighbors, operated within an Asian and Eurasian world it could shape but never fully command.

Geography structured this world of interaction without determining it. To the north and northwest lay the steppe, home to shifting confederations of Turkic and other pastoral peoples whose cavalry power the Tang state needed as allies and feared as enemies, often within the same decade. To the west and southwest, the string of oasis cities across the Tarim Basin — Kucha, Kashgar, Khotan, Turfan — connected China to Central Asia and, beyond it, to Persia and the Mediterranean world, while the high Tibetan plateau to the south of these oases became, over the course of the seventh century, the base for an entirely new imperial power that the Tang could neither ignore nor easily defeat. To the northeast, the Korean peninsula's competing kingdoms offered the Tang both alliance and, ultimately, humiliation. Across the sea to the east, the Japanese archipelago absorbed continental models at its own pace and on its own terms. To the south, the maritime world connected Guangzhou and other Chinese ports to Southeast Asian trading states and, through them, to the Indian Ocean and the expanding commercial and religious networks of the Islamic world.

Evidence for this chapter is unusually rich and unusually international. It includes Tang dynastic histories and court records, but also Sogdian merchant letters recovered from a Chinese watchtower, Tibetan treaty inscriptions carved in stone, Korean and Japanese court chronicles describing the same events from different vantage points, the travel account of the pilgrim-monk Xuanzang, Arabic and Persian geographical writing describing Tang China from the outside, and the material remains — coins, ceramics, glass, textiles, musical instruments, tomb figurines of foreign merchants and camels — recovered from Chang'an, Dunhuang, and other sites along these networks. Reading these sources together, rather than relying on Chinese court records alone, is essential to understanding this chapter's central argument, and it models a broader historical skill: understanding any international relationship requires evidence from more than one side of it.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to describe the major states and peoples with which Tang China interacted, explain how these relationships differed from one frontier to another, trace the major networks along which people, goods, and ideas moved, and understand why the era's cultural and commercial exchange should be understood as a process of mutual adaptation rather than one-directional transmission from a Chinese center to passive recipients beyond it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Tang China is best understood as one major participant within a wider Eurasian and Asian world of states, rather than as an isolated civilization projecting influence outward.
  2. Describe the diplomatic, military, and commercial mechanisms — including marriage alliance, protectorate administration, frontier garrisons, and tribute-linked exchange — through which the Tang state managed relations with neighboring powers.
  3. Trace the political and commercial significance of Central Asia, including Sogdian merchant networks, Turkic confederations, and the oasis kingdoms of the Tarim Basin.
  4. Analyze the Silk Roads as a network of overland routes carrying goods, technologies, artistic motifs, and religious ideas, rather than as a single fixed road.
  5. Evaluate the Tibetan Empire as an independent imperial power capable of defeating Tang forces and exercising substantial diplomatic leverage, rather than as a peripheral dependency of China.
  6. Compare Tang relations with Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla, and explain how Silla shifted from ally to rival following the destruction of the other Korean kingdoms.
  7. Assess Japanese adoption and adaptation of continental institutions as a process of selective, locally driven choice rather than passive imitation.
  8. Explain the significance of maritime Southeast Asian trading states in connecting Tang China to the Indian Ocean world.
  9. Describe Tang connections to India, Buddhist pilgrimage networks, and the Persian, Arab, and early Islamic commercial and political world, including the Battle of Talas.
  10. Analyze the cosmopolitan character of Tang cities such as Chang'an while distinguishing genuine cultural openness from the social boundaries and periodic hostility foreign communities also encountered.
  11. Distinguish relationships in which the Tang held the diplomatic and military advantage from relationships in which neighboring states held equal or superior leverage.
  12. Connect the international relationships examined in this chapter to the internal institutions examined in Chapter 26 and to the frontier military crisis examined in Chapter 29.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

Chapter 25 examined the Tang founding, the consolidation of imperial authority under Emperor Taizong, and the dynasty's initial expansion into Inner Asia; Chapter 26 examined the internal institutions — government, law, land, taxation, and social organization — that sustained the Tang state within its recognized territory. This chapter assumes that internal picture and turns to the wider world in which the Tang state operated, a world the Tang inherited in part from earlier periods examined in prior chapters. The Han dynasty, discussed in Chapters 15 through 17, had already established diplomatic and commercial contact with Central Asia, sent Zhang Qian on his famous missions, and fought extended wars with the Xiongnu confederation; the routes and relationships historians now call the Silk Roads were centuries old by the time the Tang dynasty was founded in 618. The Age of Division, examined in Chapters 19 through 23, had seen extensive migration of Inner Asian peoples into North China, the spread of Buddhism along Central Asian transmission routes, and the formation of a genuinely multiethnic northern Chinese society from which the Tang imperial house itself, with its partly Xianbei ancestry and marriage connections, directly descended. Sui unification, examined in Chapter 24, had briefly reasserted Chinese military power in Inner Asia and fought costly wars against Goguryeo in Korea before its own collapse.

The Tang dynasty therefore did not create its international position from nothing. It inherited long-established trade routes, diplomatic precedents, and a northern Chinese elite culture already shaped by centuries of interaction with Inner Asian peoples. What changed under the Tang, especially during the seventh and early eighth centuries, was the scale and intensity of this interaction. Tang military campaigns extended Chinese political and military presence deep into the Tarim Basin and toward the borders of Persia; Tang courts received and dispatched diplomatic missions across an unusually wide geographic range; Tang cities, especially the capital Chang'an, grew into some of the largest and most cosmopolitan urban centers in the world, hosting merchant, religious, and diplomatic communities from across Asia; and Tang cultural and institutional models were studied, borrowed, and adapted by neighboring states, most notably Silla and Japan, on a scale without close precedent in earlier Chinese history.

Students should bear in mind several cautions established in earlier chapters and directly relevant here. First, as Chapter 1 emphasized, the geographic and political meaning of "China" changed over time; the Tang state's actual military and administrative reach, especially in Inner Asia, fluctuated considerably across the dynasty's nearly three centuries, expanding dramatically under Taizong and Gaozong in the seventh century, reaching its greatest extent under Xuanzong in the early eighth century, and contracting sharply after the An Lushan Rebellion examined in Chapter 29. Second, as Chapter 8 discussed in connection with the Zhou dynasty and Chapter 16 discussed in connection with Han frontier relations, historians increasingly avoid describing all forms of Chinese diplomatic and commercial relations with neighboring peoples through the single, simplified label of a "tribute system." Tang relationships with the Turks, Tibet, Silla, Japan, and Southeast Asian states differed substantially from one another in form, in the balance of power involved, and in the practical motivations of the parties concerned; some relationships involved genuine subordination, others involved formal ritual gestures masking practical equality or even Tang disadvantage, and still others involved primarily commercial motivations dressed in diplomatic language on both sides.

Third, students should understand that Inner Asian steppe confederations, Central Asian oasis kingdoms, the Tibetan Empire, the Korean kingdoms, Japan, and Southeast Asian polities were independent historical actors with their own internal politics, their own strategic interests, and their own perspectives on their relationships with Tang China, even where the surviving documentary record is disproportionately Chinese. Where possible, this chapter draws on non-Chinese evidence, including Sogdian, Tibetan, Korean, and Japanese sources, precisely to avoid reconstructing this international world solely through a Tang lens.

Finally, this chapter's geographic and thematic boundaries connect directly to neighboring chapters. Chapter 28 will examine Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian learning, foreign religions, poetry, art, and printing as subjects in their own right; this chapter mentions religious transmission, artistic influence, and intellectual exchange only insofar as they moved through the international networks under discussion here, leaving detailed treatment of doctrine, institutions, and cultural achievement to Chapter 28. Chapter 29 will examine the An Lushan Rebellion and the resulting transformation of Tang frontier military organization, fiscal structure, and central authority; this chapter's account of frontier armies, Central Asian garrisons, and the military figures who commanded them provides necessary background for understanding why that later crisis, when it came, proved so destructive.

Section 27.1

Tang China in Eurasia

The Tang dynasty, founded in 618 and consolidated under Emperor Taizong in the following decades, governed one of the largest, wealthiest, and most institutionally elaborate states in the world during the seventh and eighth centuries. But the Tang state did not exist in isolation. Its northern and western borders opened directly onto the Eurasian steppe and the string of oasis cities that historians call Central Asia; its southwestern border faced the rapidly expanding Tibetan Empire; its northeastern border touched the contested Korean peninsula; its eastern coastline faced the Japanese archipelago across open water; and its southern ports connected by sea to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and, through those waters, to the Islamic world emerging in the Middle East. Understanding Tang China therefore requires understanding this surrounding world, not as a passive backdrop to Chinese history but as an arena of interaction among independent, often competing political powers.

Historians increasingly describe borders in this period not as fixed lines on a map but as zones of interaction — areas where authority was contested, overlapping, negotiated, and often exercised indirectly rather than through direct territorial administration. This was especially true along the Tang state's Inner Asian frontiers, where the practical reach of Tang government extended along garrisoned routes and oasis cities rather than across continuous, uniformly governed territory. A useful example is the administrative mechanism historians call jimi, or "loose-rein" administration, discussed in more detail in the Historical Vocabulary feature below: rather than governing conquered or allied steppe and Central Asian peoples through the same prefectural and county institutions used in the Chinese interior, examined in Chapter 26, the Tang state frequently recognized local rulers' continued authority in exchange for nominal submission, tribute, and military cooperation, retaining Tang titles and a degree of oversight without attempting direct administrative control.

Diplomacy in this period operated through several overlapping mechanisms that historians must distinguish carefully rather than collapsing into a single "tribute system." Formal tributary missions, in which foreign rulers or their representatives presented gifts to the Tang emperor and received gifts, titles, and trading privileges in return, were genuinely important and did convey, in many cases, real elements of political subordination or at least acknowledged hierarchy. But the same ritual vocabulary of tribute could also describe relationships that were, in practical terms, closer to equality, alliance, or even Tang disadvantage — as this chapter's discussion of Tibet in Section 27.4 will make especially clear. Marriage alliances, in which a Tang princess (often an adopted or collateral relative of the imperial house rather than a direct daughter of the reigning emperor) married a foreign ruler, served diplomatic purposes that varied by relationship: sometimes signaling Tang superiority, sometimes serving as a costly concession Tang rulers made to secure peace with a stronger neighbor. Frontier garrisons, protectorates, and military alliances added further layers of interaction, as did the simple, ongoing movement of merchants, monks, and migrants across borders that no government fully controlled.

This chapter's central historical challenge, taken up section by section, is to describe each of these relationships on its own terms rather than fitting all of them into a single template. The Turks and other steppe peoples related to Tang China primarily through a shifting mixture of warfare, alliance, and loose-rein administration. Central Asian oasis kingdoms and Sogdian merchant communities related to Tang China primarily, though not exclusively, through trade, made possible by garrisoned routes and protectorate administration. Tibet related to Tang China as a genuine imperial rival, at times a diplomatic partner secured through marriage alliance, and at times a military threat capable of striking the Tang capital itself. Korea's Silla related to Tang China first as a military ally against common enemies and then as a political rival that successfully resisted Tang territorial ambitions. Japan related to Tang China primarily through voluntary, selective study and adaptation of continental institutions, conducted at a physical and political distance that allowed considerable independence. Southeast Asian polities related to Tang China primarily through maritime commerce. And the Islamic world, still relatively new to the international stage in the seventh and eighth centuries, related to Tang China through a mixture of distant commercial contact and, on one notable occasion at Talas, direct military confrontation.

Section 27.2

Central Asian Connections

Central Asia in the Tang period was not a mere corridor connecting "China" to "the West." It was itself a region of independent, economically sophisticated, and culturally distinctive societies — Sogdian city-states, Turkic confederations, and Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms — whose own histories, interests, and internal politics shaped the character of Tang engagement with the wider Eurasian world as much as Tang policy did.

The oasis kingdoms of the Tarim Basin, including Kucha, Kashgar, Khotan, Turfan (the site of the kingdom of Gaochang, conquered by Tang forces in 640), and Karashahr, occupied a chain of fertile, irrigated settlements strung along the northern and southern edges of the Taklamakan Desert, each supported by meltwater from the surrounding mountains and each positioned along one of the branching routes that made up the Silk Roads. These kingdoms possessed their own languages (including Tocharian in some oases and various Iranian and Turkic languages elsewhere), their own Buddhist monastic institutions, often richly endowed through their control of trade, and their own local rulers, who navigated a difficult position between larger neighboring powers — Tang China to the east, the Western Turks and later the Tibetan Empire to the west and south, and various steppe confederations to the north. Tang policy toward these kingdoms combined military conquest, as at Gaochang, with the "loose-rein" administrative techniques discussed in Section 27.1, formalized through the Anxi Protectorate and its associated "Four Garrisons," a military-administrative structure intended to secure the trade routes and project Tang authority westward without attempting the kind of direct, intensive administration used within China proper.

Sogdian merchants, originating from city-states such as Samarkand and Bukhara in what is now Uzbekistan, organized much of the long-distance overland commerce that moved along these routes. Sogdian trading communities, often organized around kinship and native-place ties, established permanent settlements in cities across the Tarim Basin, at Dunhuang, and deep within China proper, including at Chang'an itself, where Sogdian merchants, entertainers, and religious specialists (practicing Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and other religions discussed further in Chapter 28) formed a recognizable and economically significant community. Sogdian merchants dealt in silk, of course, but also in horses, furs, metalwork, glassware, musical instruments, and a wide range of luxury and everyday goods, extending credit, organizing caravans, and maintaining correspondence networks across enormous distances, as the Sogdian Ancient Letters examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window vividly illustrate.

Turkic-speaking steppe confederations, meanwhile, alternated between conflict and alliance with the Tang state throughout this period. The defeat of the Eastern Turks by Tang forces in 630, discussed in Chapter 25, led to a period of Tang dominance over much of the eastern steppe, formalized through the loose-rein system and symbolized by Taizong's acceptance of the title "Heavenly Qaghan" from assembled steppe leaders — a title that acknowledged Tang authority in terms steppe rulers themselves would recognize, rather than imposing a purely Chinese framework of legitimacy on non-Chinese peoples. This dominance was neither permanent nor uncontested; Turkic power revived at various points across the seventh and eighth centuries, and Tang relations with steppe peoples continued to shift between conflict, tribute-linked alliance, and periods of Turkic military pressure on Tang frontiers.

Students should resist treating Central Asia simply as a passageway between two more "important" civilizations. Sogdian merchants, Tarim Basin rulers, and Turkic leaders pursued their own strategic and commercial interests, allied with or resisted Tang power according to their own calculations, and shaped the character, volume, and direction of Eurasian exchange as much as Tang policy did. Central Asia's societies were active historical participants, not simply a corridor through which "Chinese" and "Western" influence passed.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorate (都護府, duhufu)

A duhufu, usually translated "protectorate" or "protectorate general," was a Tang military-administrative institution established to secure and oversee frontier regions beyond the reach of ordinary prefectural and county government. A protectorate combined military command with a degree of diplomatic and administrative oversight of subordinate or allied local rulers, functioning as an intermediate structure between direct Tang provincial government and the fully indirect "loose-rein" (jimi) relationships discussed above.

The Anxi Protectorate, discussed in this section, was the most significant protectorate for Tang engagement with Central Asia, overseeing the Four Garrisons and the wider Tarim Basin. Other Tang protectorates existed along other frontiers, including in the northeast and, briefly, in former Korean territory following the conquests examined in Section 27.5. Students should understand protectorates as flexible military-administrative tools whose actual reach and effectiveness varied considerably depending on the military balance of power in a given frontier region at a given time, rather than as fixed or permanent territorial possessions.

Historical Vocabulary
Jimi (羈縻) — "Loose-Rein" Administration

The term jimi, sometimes translated as "loose-rein" or "bridle and halter" administration, describes a Tang (and, in modified forms, earlier and later Chinese) approach to governing frontier peoples and territories without direct administrative incorporation. Under this system, the Tang court granted local rulers — of Turkic, Tibetan-adjacent, Tarim Basin, or other backgrounds — nominal Tang titles, formal recognition, and a symbolic place within the Tang political order, while allowing them to retain their own systems of local rule, succession, and, in most respects, internal governance. In exchange, these rulers were expected to acknowledge Tang overlordship, provide tribute and, when called upon, military support, and refrain from independent hostile action.

This arrangement should not be mistaken for full political control. Loose-rein relationships varied enormously in how much real authority the Tang court exercised, ranging from close cooperation and effective alliance to relationships that were, in practice, closer to formal recognition of independent power. The system also proved unstable over time: loose-rein rulers who calculated that Tang power had weakened, or that a rival power such as Tibet or a resurgent Turkic confederation offered better terms, could and did shift allegiance. Students should understand jimi administration as one tool among several — alongside direct conquest, marriage alliance, and simple commercial engagement — through which the Tang state managed an Inner Asian world it could influence but never fully command.

Section 27.3

The Silk Roads

The term "Silk Roads," coined by a nineteenth-century European geographer and now used throughout this textbook for convenience and because it is widely recognized, describes not a single fixed road but a shifting, branching network of overland caravan routes connecting China, through Central Asia, to Persia, the Middle East, and, ultimately, the Mediterranean world. Students should understand this network as it functioned during the Tang period: a set of alternative paths across difficult terrain, whose specific routes shifted according to political conditions, water availability, and the fortunes of the oasis cities that served as essential waystations.

Caravans moving west from Chang'an typically passed through the Gansu (Hexi) Corridor, introduced in Chapter 2, then split into northern and southern routes skirting the Taklamakan Desert through the string of Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms discussed in Section 27.2, before converging again in the region of Kashgar and continuing westward through the mountain passes of the Pamirs into the Ferghana Valley, Sogdiana, and beyond, toward Persia and, eventually, the Byzantine and, later, Islamic Mediterranean world. Goods moved in both directions along these routes. From China came silk — the trade good that gave the network its modern name, though far from the only commodity carried — along with paper, ceramics in more limited quantities given their fragility over overland transport, tea in later periods, and other manufactured goods. Moving eastward into China came horses (critically important to Tang military power and discussed further in Chapter 25's treatment of Tang military organization), furs, precious metals and gemstones, glassware (a technology in which Persian and Roman/Byzantine glassmakers held a considerable technical advantage over Chinese producers during this period), musical instruments, exotic animals, medicines, and aromatic substances used in religious ritual, medicine, and elite consumption.

Alongside material goods, the Silk Roads carried technologies, artistic styles, and religious ideas. Buddhist teachings, images, and monastic institutions moved along these routes from India through Central Asia into China over the centuries examined in earlier chapters, and continued to do so during the Tang period, as Xuanzang's journey, discussed in Section 27.8, illustrates directly. Artistic motifs — including specific decorative patterns, musical instruments and performance styles, and elements of dress and material culture associated with "Western" or Central Asian fashion — became fashionable within Tang elite culture, a phenomenon examined further in Section 27.9. Technical knowledge, including certain aspects of textile production, metalworking, and, according to later tradition connected with the Battle of Talas discussed in Section 27.8, papermaking, moved westward from China into the Islamic world over the following centuries.

It is important for students to understand that no single government controlled this network end to end. Tang authority, exercised through the Anxi Protectorate and associated garrisons discussed in Section 27.2, secured and facilitated trade through the portions of the route within reach of Tang military power, particularly during the periods of greatest Tang strength in the seventh and early eighth centuries. But long stretches of the network passed through territory controlled by Sogdian city-states, Turkic confederations, the Tibetan Empire, and, farther west, Persian and, increasingly, Islamic polities, none of which were simply extensions of Tang authority. Trade continued, moreover, through periods of considerable political instability and conflict, organized by merchant communities — Sogdians especially, but also others — whose commercial networks proved remarkably resilient to shifting political fortunes. Students should therefore understand the Silk Roads not as a Tang Chinese achievement or possession, but as a genuinely multi-polity, multi-ethnic commercial and cultural system in which Tang China was an important, wealthy, and powerful participant rather than a sole author or controller.

Section 27.4

The Tibetan Empire and Tang Relations

No relationship examined in this chapter illustrates the limits of Tang power more clearly than the Tang state's long, difficult, and repeatedly humbling engagement with the Tibetan Empire. Students who have absorbed a simplified image of Tang China as an unrivaled regional hegemon need this section to correct that picture directly: for much of the seventh through ninth centuries, Tibet was a genuine imperial rival, capable of defeating Tang armies in open battle, contesting Tang control of Central Asia for generations, and, in 763, briefly occupying the Tang capital itself.

The Tibetan Empire's rapid rise in the seventh century, traditionally associated with the reign of Songtsen Gampo, transformed a set of previously fragmented plateau polities into a unified, militarily formidable state capable of projecting power well beyond the Tibetan plateau itself, into the Tarim Basin to the north, the Qinghai region and Gansu Corridor to the northeast, and parts of the Himalayan foothills and South Asia to the south. The Tibetan state developed its own script, adapted from an Indian writing system, its own administrative institutions, and, over the following century, extensive patronage of Buddhism transmitted both from India and, to a lesser extent, from China, a religious development within Tibet's own imperial and cultural history that lies beyond this chapter's scope.

Tang-Tibetan diplomacy in this period combined marriage alliance, warfare, and shifting territorial competition. The marriage of the Tang princess Wencheng to Songtsen Gampo in 641, often presented in later Chinese and Tibetan tradition as evidence of close and harmonious relations, should be understood cautiously: marriage alliances of this kind were a standard diplomatic tool used by many states in this period, including the Tang court itself with other neighbors, and their political meaning varied by circumstance. In some tellings emphasized by later Tibetan Buddhist tradition, the marriage is remembered as part of Tibet's spiritual and cultural elevation through connection with Buddhist civilizations to its south and east; in Tang court records, the marriage was more often framed as a conventional instrument of frontier diplomacy. Neither framing should be taken as a simple, neutral statement of the underlying balance of power, which in fact favored Tibet considerably more, at various points in this period, than either framing alone might suggest.

Over the following century, Tang and Tibetan forces fought repeatedly over control of the Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms and the strategically vital Qinghai region, with control of specific garrisons and cities passing back and forth between the two powers on multiple occasions. Tibetan forces inflicted serious defeats on Tang armies at various points across the later seventh and early eighth centuries, and the Tibetan Empire's sustained military pressure was a constant strategic concern for Tang frontier policy throughout this period, not an occasional or minor threat. The most dramatic illustration of Tibetan strength came in 763, in the immediate aftermath of the devastating An Lushan Rebellion examined in Chapter 29, when Tibetan forces, taking advantage of Tang military exhaustion and internal disorder, briefly occupied Chang'an itself before withdrawing — an event without close parallel among any of the other relationships examined in this chapter, and one that should permanently correct any assumption that Tang China's neighbors were uniformly weaker or subordinate powers.

Students should draw two conclusions from this section. First, the Tibetan Empire must be studied as an independent, historically significant imperial power in its own right, with its own institutions, religious developments, and strategic interests, rather than as a peripheral or dependent extension of Chinese history. Second, the Tang-Tibetan relationship demonstrates that even at the height of Tang power, Chinese military and diplomatic dominance over neighboring Inner Asian states was neither automatic nor permanent, but had to be actively contested, and was sometimes lost, against genuinely capable rival powers.

Section 27.5

Korea and the Tang World

The Korean peninsula in the early seventh century was divided among three competing kingdoms — Goguryeo in the north, extending into what is now Manchuria; Baekje in the southwest; and Silla in the southeast — each with its own royal house, its own relationship to Buddhism and Chinese administrative models, and its own shifting alliances with neighboring powers, including one another, Tang China, and, in Baekje's case, Japan. Sui forces, discussed in Chapter 24, had already fought costly and ultimately unsuccessful wars against Goguryeo, campaigns that contributed significantly to the Sui dynasty's collapse; Tang rulers inherited both this military rivalry and a cautious awareness of its dangers.

Tang policy toward the peninsula shifted decisively when the Tang court allied with Silla against the other two kingdoms. Combined Tang-Silla forces destroyed Baekje in 660; a Baekje-restoration movement, supported by a Japanese expeditionary fleet, was decisively defeated by Tang naval forces at the Battle of Baekgang in 663, an engagement discussed further in Section 27.6 for its significance in shaping subsequent Japanese foreign policy. Tang-Silla forces then turned against Goguryeo, destroying it in 668 after a long and difficult campaign.

The destruction of Baekje and Goguryeo did not, however, produce the outcome Tang planners had likely hoped for. Rather than accepting Tang administrative incorporation of the conquered territories, Silla, having achieved its own strategic goal of eliminating its Korean rivals, turned against its former Tang ally. Through sustained military resistance across the 670s, Silla succeeded in expelling Tang forces and administrators from most of the Korean peninsula, establishing Silla control over a unified (though not total) Korean political space south of a rough frontier with the former northern Goguryeo territory. Tang China, despite its role in destroying two Korean kingdoms, ultimately failed to translate that military success into lasting territorial control of the peninsula — a clear illustration of this chapter's recurring theme that military victory and durable political dominance were not the same thing, even for as powerful a state as Tang China.

The former northern territories of Goguryeo, along with adjacent regions of Manchuria, gave rise over the following decades to a new state, Balhae, founded by a mixture of former Goguryeo elites and neighboring Mohe peoples. Balhae developed its own considerable political and cultural sophistication, maintained diplomatic and commercial relations with Tang China, Silla, and Japan, and should be understood by students as a significant actor in its own right rather than a mere afterthought to the Goguryeo-Silla-Tang story; treating the post-Goguryeo Korean and Manchurian world as reducible to Silla alone would understate its genuine political complexity.

Following the conflicts of the 660s and 670s, Tang-Silla relations gradually normalized into a more conventional diplomatic and commercial relationship, with Silla sending regular missions to the Tang court, adopting selected elements of Tang administrative and legal models (a process of adaptation comparable in some respects, though not identical, to the Japanese case examined in Section 27.6), and participating in the broader cultural and religious exchange networks of the Tang world, including Buddhist pilgrimage and study at Chang'an's monasteries. Students should carry forward from this section a clear sense of Silla's trajectory: partner in conquest, successful rival in war, and, subsequently, an independent East Asian state engaged in sustained but selective cultural and diplomatic exchange with Tang China on its own terms.

Section 27.6

Japan and Chinese Cultural Influence

Japan's relationship with Tang China differed fundamentally from every other relationship examined in this chapter in one crucial respect: it involved no shared land border, no sustained Tang military presence, and, after the Japanese defeat at the Battle of Baekgang in 663 discussed in Section 27.5, no further direct Japanese military engagement on the Korean peninsula or against Tang forces. Japan's engagement with Tang China was instead conducted primarily through voluntary diplomatic missions, study, and the deliberate, selective importation of continental institutions and knowledge — a process historians describe carefully as adaptation rather than simple imitation, because Japanese rulers and elites consistently modified, reinterpreted, or rejected continental models according to Japan's own political circumstances and preexisting social structures.

Official Japanese embassies to Sui and, especially, Tang China, known as kentoshi, carried officials, students, and Buddhist monks across the sometimes dangerous sea crossing to study at Chang'an and elsewhere in China, then return with texts, administrative knowledge, religious teachings, and technical expertise. These missions, dispatched at intervals across the seventh through ninth centuries, represented substantial investments of resources and risk — sea crossings of this period were genuinely hazardous, and several missions suffered shipwreck or other serious losses — reflecting the high value Japanese rulers placed on direct access to Tang institutions and learning.

The results of this sustained engagement are visible across multiple dimensions of early Japanese state-building, conventionally associated with the Japanese Ritsuryo state of the late seventh and eighth centuries. Japanese rulers adopted, with substantial modification, a Tang-influenced law code structure (the term ritsuryo itself derives from Chinese legal and administrative terminology examined in Chapter 26's discussion of Tang law), a system of central ministries and provincial administration loosely modeled on Tang bureaucratic organization, a grid-based capital city plan directly inspired by the layout of Chang'an (visible in the design of the Japanese capitals at Fujiwara-kyo, Heijo-kyo/Nara, and, later, Heian-kyo/Kyoto), the use of Chinese characters for writing (subsequently adapted into Japan's own syllabic writing systems), Chinese-derived calendrical and astronomical knowledge, and extensive Buddhist institutions and teachings transmitted via Korea and directly from China.

Students must avoid, however, the misleading conclusion that Japan simply copied Tang China wholesale, or that this process represented Chinese cultural superiority imposing itself on a passive Japanese periphery. Japanese elites adopted continental models selectively and adapted them substantially to fit existing Japanese social structures, particularly the continued importance of powerful aristocratic lineages whose influence the Tang-style bureaucratic and examination systems, examined in Chapter 26, were never fully permitted to displace in the way they gradually did within China itself. Japan never adopted anything resembling the full Tang civil service examination system as a primary mechanism of elite recruitment; aristocratic birth continued to matter far more in the Japanese case. Japanese rulers also selectively rejected or modified specific continental institutions that did not suit Japanese political circumstances, and Japanese Buddhism, while deeply influenced by Chinese Buddhist schools and translations, developed its own distinctive institutions, sects, and relationships to the imperial court over subsequent centuries, a development touched on further in Chapter 28.

The Battle of Baekgang in 663, in which Tang naval forces decisively defeated a Japanese fleet supporting Baekje-restoration forces on the Korean peninsula, deserves particular attention as a turning point in Japanese foreign policy. Students should note that the kentoshi embassies described above were not created in response to this defeat; the practice of sending official missions to the continental court had already begun by 630, under the Sui and early Tang, and reflected a preexisting Japanese interest in continental institutions and Buddhism. What Baekgang changed was narrower but still significant: after this defeat, Japanese rulers withdrew from direct military intervention on the Korean peninsula and did not attempt it again, while continuing and, in subsequent decades, expanding the already-established pattern of diplomatic missions and institutional study described above — an illustration of how military defeat could redirect the military dimension of a state's engagement with a more powerful neighbor without needing to create its cultural and diplomatic dimension from nothing.

Section 27.7

Southeast Asian Relations

While the overland Silk Roads examined in Section 27.3 dominate popular imagination of Tang international connection, maritime trade connecting southern Chinese ports, above all Guangzhou, to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and beyond was of comparable and, in some respects, growing commercial significance during the Tang period, a significance that would only increase further during the Song period examined in Chapter 37.

Southeast Asian polities of this period, both on the mainland (including states in the regions that would later become Vietnam, Cambodia, and Thailand) and in the maritime archipelago (including Srivijaya, a major trading state based in Sumatra with substantial influence over maritime routes and ports in the Malacca-Sumatra region), were themselves sophisticated commercial and political powers, not mere intermediaries passively transmitting goods between China and India. Srivijaya, in particular, built its considerable wealth and regional influence substantially on its position along key maritime corridors connecting the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea — though historians caution that the precise routes ships used and the extent of Srivijaya's control over them varied over time, and that later, more Malacca-centered patterns of shipping should not simply be projected backward onto this earlier period. Srivijaya taxed and facilitated passing trade while also participating in the exchange of locally and regionally sourced products such as camphor, benzoin, and other forest resins, and it maintained active diplomatic relations with Tang China, sending missions and receiving recognition in terms that, once again, should not be read as straightforward political subordination.

Guangzhou, positioned at the mouth of the Pearl River and reachable by seagoing ships arriving from Southeast Asia, India, and the Persian Gulf, developed during the Tang period into a major international port city, home to substantial foreign merchant communities, including Persian, Arab, Indian, and Southeast Asian traders, some of whom settled for extended periods or permanently. Tang authorities established specific administrative offices to manage maritime trade and foreign merchant communities at Guangzhou, reflecting both the commercial importance of this trade to state revenue and the practical challenges of governing a genuinely international port population.

Goods moving through this maritime network included aromatic woods and resins (frankincense, myrrh, and similar substances valued for religious and medicinal use), spices, ivory, exotic animals, precious stones, and, moving outward from China, silk, ceramics (increasingly significant as a maritime export in this and later periods, since ceramics traveled far better by ship than by overland caravan), metalwork, and other manufactured goods. This maritime trade also carried religious and cultural influence: Buddhist monks traveled by sea as well as overland between China, Southeast Asia, and India, and Southeast Asian polities absorbed both Indian and, to a lesser degree, Chinese religious and political models according to their own particular historical circumstances and preferences, producing distinctive Southeast Asian syntheses rather than simple replication of either source.

Students should conclude from this section that the maritime world connecting Tang China to Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean deserves treatment as a major, independent dimension of Tang international engagement, not a minor supplement to the overland Silk Roads, and that Southeast Asian polities such as Srivijaya were themselves powerful, wealthy commercial actors shaping the terms of this exchange rather than passive conduits between larger neighbors.

Section 27.8

India, Buddhism, and the Islamic World

This section connects Tang China to South Asia and to the rapidly expanding Islamic world of the Middle East, two regions whose religious, intellectual, and commercial significance for Tang China was considerable, while leaving detailed treatment of Buddhist doctrine, institutions, and other religious traditions to Chapter 28, which examines religion in Tang China as a subject in its own right.

India's connection to Tang China during this period operated primarily, though not exclusively, through Buddhist pilgrimage, translation, and intellectual exchange, building on centuries of transmission examined in earlier chapters. The monk Xuanzang's journey, briefly noted in this chapter's chronology and opening context, exemplifies this connection directly. Departing China in 629 (in defiance, according to later tradition, of an imperial prohibition on unauthorized travel beyond the frontier, though the prohibition's precise enforcement remains a matter of some historical uncertainty), Xuanzang traveled overland through the Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms and Central Asia, crossed into India through mountain passes, and spent more than a decade studying, traveling, and collecting Buddhist texts, eventually studying at the great monastic university of Nalanda in northeastern India before returning to China in 645 with a substantial collection of texts, relics, and firsthand knowledge of the numerous Central Asian and Indian kingdoms he had passed through. Xuanzang's account of his travels, along with the extensive translation work he subsequently undertook at Tang court sponsorship, provides invaluable evidence for the political geography, religious institutions, and everyday conditions of the societies along this route during the seventh century; his significance as a translator and the specific content of Buddhist teaching he transmitted are treated further in Chapter 28.

Beyond Buddhist pilgrimage, direct Tang-Indian political contact remained relatively limited compared to Tang relations with closer neighbors, mediated substantially through Central Asian intermediary states and through the broader network of maritime trade discussed in Section 27.7, which connected Chinese ports to Indian ports as part of the same Indian Ocean commercial world that linked Southeast Asia, India, Persia, and the Arab world together.

The rise of Islam and the rapid seventh-century expansion of the Arab caliphates transformed the political landscape of the Middle East and, over succeeding decades, extended Islamic political and military influence into Persia and toward the western edges of Central Asia — developments that eventually brought Islamic power into direct contact, and occasional conflict, with the sphere of Tang influence in Central Asia. Persian and, subsequently, Arab merchants had long participated in the overland and maritime trade networks connecting the Middle East to China, and this participation continued and, in some respects, intensified as Islamic commercial networks expanded across the seventh and eighth centuries; Persian and Arab merchant communities, alongside Sogdian and other Central Asian traders, were a recognized presence in Tang port and frontier cities, discussed further in Section 27.9.

Direct military confrontation between Tang and expanding Islamic (specifically, by this point, Abbasid) power occurred at the Battle of Talas in 751, fought in the Talas River valley in present-day Kyrgyzstan/Kazakhstan between a Tang army, commanded by the Korean-born Tang general Gao Xianzhi, and a combined force of Abbasid troops and their Central Asian allies. The battle resulted in a decisive defeat for Tang forces and is often cited, in both scholarly and popular accounts, as a major turning point checking further Tang westward military expansion into Central Asia. Students should approach this battle's broader historical significance with considerable caution, however: Tang administrative presence in parts of Central Asia, including at Khotan, continued in some form even after 751 and, in isolated pockets, after 763, showing that Talas did not instantly or completely end Tang engagement in the region. It should not be treated as a grand "clash of civilizations" between a unified Chinese East and a unified Islamic West; Tang forces at Talas were fighting primarily over regional influence in Central Asia amid a complex, shifting web of local alliances, not engaged in a religiously or civilizationally framed confrontation as later observers have sometimes imagined it. Nor should the popular tradition connecting Talas to the transmission of papermaking technology to the Islamic world, based on later accounts describing Chinese papermakers captured after the battle, be treated as a fully secure or simple explanation for that technology's spread; papermaking knowledge likely moved westward through multiple channels and over an extended period, of which any transmission connected to Talas prisoners was, at most, one contributing episode among others. Furthermore, the more immediate and significant cause of the Tang retreat from an active Central Asian military role was not Talas itself but the far more devastating An Lushan Rebellion, which erupted only a few years later and which Chapter 29 examines in detail; Talas is best understood as one contributing factor within a broader pattern of Tang overextension and gathering internal crisis, rather than as a single, war-deciding turning point in isolation.

Section 27.9

Cosmopolitanism and Cultural Exchange

The relationships and networks examined throughout this chapter converged, in concentrated form, within Tang China's great cities, above all the capital Chang'an, which during the seventh and eighth centuries was among the largest and, by many measures, most genuinely cosmopolitan cities in the world. Understanding daily life within this cosmopolitan urban world helps students connect the large-scale diplomatic, military, and commercial relationships examined in previous sections to concrete, everyday experience.

Chang'an's population during the Tang dynasty's height included substantial communities of Sogdian, Persian, Arab, Turkic, Indian, Korean, and Japanese residents, alongside merchants, diplomatic envoys, hostages (a recognized diplomatic institution in which foreign rulers' relatives resided at the Tang court, partly as guarantee of good relations and partly to receive a Tang-influenced education), students, and Buddhist, Zoroastrian, Manichaean, Nestorian Christian, and, eventually, Muslim religious specialists and their congregations, whose specific religious institutions are examined further in Chapter 28. The city's markets offered goods from across this entire network: Central Asian and Persian horses, glassware, and textiles; Southeast Asian aromatics and exotic goods arriving via the maritime routes discussed in Section 27.7; and a wide range of imported foods, musical instruments, and fashions.

This cosmopolitan exchange left a visible mark on elite Tang culture. Central Asian ("Western," referred to in Chinese sources using terms discussed in the Historical Vocabulary feature below) music, dance, clothing styles, and material goods became genuinely fashionable within the Tang court and among the urban elite during certain periods, particularly the reign of Xuanzong in the early eighth century; Tang poetry, examined further in Chapter 28, frequently references Central Asian wine, music, and material culture as markers of sophisticated urban taste. Polo, a sport of Central Asian and Persian origin, became a popular elite pastime. Tomb figurines recovered from Tang burials — including the famous glazed ceramic figures of foreign merchants, grooms, and entertainers, and of heavily laden Bactrian camels — provide vivid material evidence of how visible and culturally significant this foreign presence was within elite Tang consciousness.

Students must be careful, however, not to romanticize this cosmopolitanism into an image of a uniformly tolerant, borderless multicultural society, which would misrepresent the actual historical evidence. Foreign merchant and religious communities within Tang cities were subject to specific administrative regulation, including designated residential quarters in some cases and specific legal and taxation arrangements distinct from those applied to Chinese subjects. Periods of enthusiastic fashion for "Western" goods and culture coexisted with periods of official suspicion or hostility toward specific foreign communities, most dramatically illustrated by events beyond this chapter's immediate scope — including later Tang-period persecutions of Buddhist institutions (discussed in Chapter 28) that also affected Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Nestorian Christian communities associated with foreign merchant populations. Attitudes toward specific foreign groups could also shift sharply in response to political and military events; the trust placed in frontier generals and soldiers of non-Han background, a pattern directly relevant to the career of An Lushan himself, discussed in Chapter 29, illustrates both the genuine opportunities cosmopolitan Tang society offered to non-Han individuals and the potential for suspicion and backlash when political circumstances changed.

The balanced conclusion students should draw is that Tang cosmopolitanism was real, consequential, and historically significant — visible in commerce, religion, art, fashion, cuisine, and the demographic composition of major cities — while also existing alongside social boundaries, administrative regulation, and periodic hostility that prevented this openness from constituting anything like full social or legal equality between Chinese subjects and resident foreign communities. Both halves of this picture are necessary for an accurate understanding of cosmopolitan life in the Tang world.

Historical Vocabulary
Hu (胡) — "Foreign" or "Western" Identity in Tang Sources

Tang-period Chinese sources frequently use the character hu to refer, often generically, to peoples and goods associated with Central Asia and, more broadly, with non-Chinese peoples to the north and west — a usage with roots stretching back to earlier periods of Chinese history examined in previous chapters. Tang sources describe "hu" merchants, "hu" music, "hu" dance, "hu" clothing, and "hu" wine, among other categories, generally without distinguishing precisely among the many specific peoples — Sogdians, Turks, Persians, and others — that this broad label could encompass.

Students should use this term cautiously and critically. On one hand, its frequent, largely neutral or even admiring use in Tang poetry, art, and commercial vocabulary demonstrates the genuine cultural visibility and prestige that Central Asian and "Western" goods and fashions held within elite Tang society, supporting this section's account of cosmopolitan exchange. On the other hand, the term's generic, undifferentiated character illustrates a historical limitation: Tang sources often did not carefully distinguish among the specific, quite different peoples and cultures this label grouped together, meaning that historians must draw on non-Chinese sources — Sogdian, Turkic, Persian, and others where they survive — to recover the more precise ethnic, linguistic, and cultural distinctions that a single Chinese term like hu can obscure.

Section 27.10

Chapter Review and Tang World Map Study

This chapter has examined Tang China not as an isolated civilization but as one major participant within a wider, interconnected Eurasian and Asian world of independent states, mobile peoples, and long-distance commercial and cultural networks. Having surveyed the steppe and Central Asia, the Tibetan Empire, Korea, Japan, Southeast Asia, India, and the Islamic world in turn, students should now be able to identify the major states and regions surrounding Tang China, explain the distinct character of each relationship, trace the major overland and maritime networks connecting them, and explain why the era's exchange of goods, technologies, and ideas should be understood as a genuinely reciprocal and adaptive process rather than a one-directional transmission of Chinese civilization outward to passive recipients.

A recurring analytical thread across this chapter has been the question of leverage: who, in any given relationship, actually held the diplomatic, military, or commercial advantage, and how did that balance shift over time? The Tang state held clear military advantage over the Eastern Turks following 630 and over Baekje and Goguryeo in the 660s, but Tibet held real and repeated military advantage over Tang forces across much of this period, and Silla successfully resisted Tang territorial ambitions on the Korean peninsula despite having fought as a Tang ally. Japan, protected by distance and shaped by a decisive naval defeat that ended its military ambitions on the peninsula, engaged with Tang China almost entirely on its own chosen terms of voluntary study and selective adaptation. Central Asian oasis kingdoms and Sogdian merchant communities operated within a Tang-influenced security framework while retaining substantial independent commercial and cultural agency of their own. Southeast Asian polities such as Srivijaya built independent wealth and power by controlling the maritime routes connecting Tang China to the wider Indian Ocean world. No single formula captures all of these relationships; each must be understood on its own historical terms.

Background Source: The Sogdian Ancient Letters (Pre-Tang)

Creators: Miwnay, writing to her mother in Samarkand, and Nanai-vandak, writing to a business partner named Varzakk; part of a small bundle of letters (the "Sogdian Ancient Letters") recovered by archaeologists in a Chinese watchtower west of Dunhuang · Date: 313–314 CE, roughly three centuries before the founding of the Tang dynasty in 618 · Type: Private commercial and family correspondence, never delivered

Context: These letters predate the Tang period covered in this chapter and are included here as background evidence, not as Tang-era documentation. They demonstrate that the Sogdian commercial and family network discussed in Section 27.2 was already well established along the Gansu Corridor and Dunhuang region centuries before the Tang dynasty existed. The letters were apparently abandoned or lost in transit and preserved by chance in the dry desert conditions of a Chinese frontier watchtower, where archaeologists recovered them in the twentieth century.

Paraphrase of the letters' content (not a translated quotation):

Miwnay's letter describes desperate conditions at a Chinese frontier settlement amid war and apparent famine, and pleads for help reaching her family in Samarkand. Nanai-vandak's letter to Varzakk records specific commercial transactions, debts, and the disruption of trade caused by regional conflict, showing the ordinary, continuing business concerns of Sogdian merchants operating across a long-distance network even amid political upheaval.

Analysis: These early fourth-century letters matter for this chapter precisely because of their age: they show that the Sogdian trading world Tang China entered in the seventh century was not a Tang creation but a long-established network with its own deep history, its own named individuals, and its own prior experience of both prosperity and crisis along these same frontier routes.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the three-century gap between these letters and the Tang dynasty historically significant rather than a minor detail?
  2. What does the letters' survival suggest about the depth and age of the commercial network Tang China later engaged with?
  3. What are the limits of using letters from the fourth century to make claims about Tang-period conditions?
  4. Why is the existence of named, non-Chinese-language sources like these important for historians studying the broader Sogdian world discussed in Section 27.2?

A Tang-Era Source: Xuanzang's Record of the Western Regions

Creator: Xuanzang's observations and reports, compiled by the monk Bianji under imperial order · Date: Completed 646, describing travels undertaken between 629 and 645 · Type: Geographical and travel record

Context: As discussed in Section 27.8, Xuanzang traveled overland through the Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms and Central Asia before crossing into India. Following his return, his reports and observations were compiled by the monk Bianji under imperial order into a written record describing the kingdoms, cities, monasteries, and peoples he encountered along the way, offering a genuinely Tang-period, firsthand record of the Central Asian and Indian world this chapter examines.

Paraphrase of the account's content (not a translated quotation):

The record describes the customs, ruling authorities, Buddhist institutions, and commercial life of numerous oasis kingdoms along Xuanzang's route, noting differences in language, dress, and religious practice from one kingdom to the next, and commenting on the relative prosperity, security, and political allegiance of each place he passed through during his journey west and his return east.

Analysis: Xuanzang's account is valuable precisely because it records firsthand observation of Central Asian and Indian societies during the Tang period itself, offering a view of the oasis kingdoms and the wider Buddhist world that complements, and sometimes complicates, the more official, state-centered perspective found in Tang court records.

Historical Debate

Chinese Dynasty or Eurasian Empire?

The question: Should the Tang dynasty, especially in its seventh- and eighth-century height, be understood primarily as a Chinese dynasty that also engaged in foreign relations, or as a genuinely Eurasian empire whose ruling house, military institutions, and cultural life were themselves substantially shaped by Inner Asian, non-Han elements?

Interpretation A — The Tang as a Chinese dynasty with extensive foreign relations: This interpretation emphasizes that the Tang state's core institutions — its bureaucracy, examination system, law codes, and agrarian tax base, examined in Chapter 26 — were fundamentally continuous with earlier Chinese imperial traditions traced from the Qin and Han dynasties onward, and that the Tang imperial house, despite its partly Xianbei ancestry, governed and identified itself primarily within a Chinese dynastic and cultural framework, using Chinese-language court records, Confucian ritual and political ideology, and Chinese historiographical self-understanding.
Interpretation B — The Tang as a Eurasian or Inner Asian-inflected empire: This interpretation, associated with a body of recent scholarship, emphasizes the Tang imperial house's mixed Xianbei and Chinese ancestry, the adoption of steppe-derived titles such as "Heavenly Qaghan," the extensive reliance on non-Han generals and frontier troops (a pattern directly relevant to Chapter 29's discussion of An Lushan), the deeply cosmopolitan character of Tang court and urban culture documented in Section 27.9, and the Tang state's active, sustained military and administrative engagement across Inner Asia, to argue that the Tang is better understood as a multiethnic Eurasian empire that happened to be centered in China proper, rather than as a straightforwardly "Chinese" dynasty in the same sense as, for example, the Han or the later Ming.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same broad body of evidence — court records, administrative institutions, frontier military organization, and cultural production — but weigh this evidence differently, with Interpretation A emphasizing institutional and ideological continuity with earlier Chinese dynasties and Interpretation B emphasizing the significance of Inner Asian ancestry, titulature, military reliance, and cosmopolitan cultural practice.

Why it matters: This debate shapes how historians periodize Chinese history, how they explain the Tang dynasty's particular strengths (including its unusually effective cavalry-based military power and its unusually successful Inner Asian diplomacy) and particular vulnerabilities (including its reliance on non-Han frontier generals, directly relevant to the crisis examined in Chapter 29), and how they understand the broader relationship between "Chinese" and "Inner Asian" history across the long span of this textbook.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, particularly the Tang relationship with the Turks, the adoption of the Heavenly Qaghan title, and the cosmopolitan character of Chang'an described in Section 27.9, evaluate which interpretation better accounts for the evidence, or explain why elements of both may be necessary for a complete picture.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Views of the Tang World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified from surviving evidence, not quotations from specific named individuals.

From the perspective of a Tang frontier official at an Anxi Protectorate garrison: Securing the oasis routes required constant, exhausting negotiation among competing local rulers, watchfulness against Tibetan and Turkic military pressure, and dependence on supply lines stretched perilously far from the agricultural heartland described in Chapter 26, making Central Asian administration feel less like triumphant expansion and more like a costly, continuous effort to hold a contested and distant frontier.
From the perspective of a Sogdian merchant based in Chang'an: Commercial success depended on maintaining trust and credit across a network of relatives and trading partners stretched from Samarkand to the Chinese interior, navigating Tang commercial regulations and periodic official suspicion toward foreign residents, while also enjoying the genuine opportunities a wealthy, demand-rich Tang market offered for luxury goods, entertainment, and specialized craft production.
From the perspective of a Silla court official following the 670s conflict with Tang: Having fought alongside Tang forces to eliminate Goguryeo and Baekje, only to then resist Tang efforts at direct administrative control, the experience likely reinforced a strong sense of independent Silla political identity even as Silla continued to draw selectively on Tang administrative and legal models for its own state-building purposes in the following decades.
From the perspective of a Japanese student monk traveling with a kentoshi embassy: Years of arduous sea travel and study at Chang'an's monasteries and academies offered access to texts, teachings, and administrative knowledge unavailable at home, but returning to Japan meant translating and adapting what had been learned to a very different social and political landscape, in which powerful aristocratic lineages, not an examination-based bureaucracy, remained the primary basis of elite status.
From the perspective of a Tibetan court minister during the empire's height: Tibet's rapid rise gave its rulers real leverage in dealings with Tang China, expressed through marriage alliance on terms Tibet itself substantially shaped, sustained military pressure in the Tarim Basin and Qinghai region, and, eventually, the demonstrated capacity to strike directly at the Tang capital when Tang weakness allowed it — a record of independent imperial achievement that later, Tang-centered narratives have not always represented fairly.
From the perspective of a Southeast Asian merchant sailing between Srivijaya and Guangzhou: Profit depended on navigating the seasonal monsoon winds, paying appropriate duties and tolls to the authorities influential along the Malacca-Sumatra corridor and the port facilities at Guangzhou, and managing relationships with commercial partners from many different linguistic and religious backgrounds, a routine but demanding form of expertise that made maritime Southeast Asia's trading states indispensable rather than incidental to this entire exchange system.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Tang Position on Its Frontiers

What Changed

  • Tang military and administrative reach in Central Asia expanded dramatically after 630 and 640, reached its greatest extent under Xuanzong in the early eighth century, and then contracted sharply after Talas (751) and, more decisively, after the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763)
  • Tang-Korean relations shifted from alliance (through 668) to open conflict (670s) to normalized, selective diplomatic and cultural exchange in subsequent decades
  • Japanese engagement shifted from direct military involvement on the Korean peninsula (through 663) to sustained peaceful study missions afterward
  • Tibetan power grew from a newly unified plateau state in the early seventh century to an empire capable of occupying the Tang capital by 763

What Stayed the Same

  • Sogdian-organized overland commerce and Southeast Asian-organized maritime commerce continued throughout the period, largely independent of specific political and military fluctuations
  • Buddhist pilgrimage, translation, and institutional connections between China, Central Asia, and India persisted across the entire period examined in this chapter
  • Chang'an's role as a cosmopolitan hub attracting merchants, envoys, students, and religious specialists from across this network remained a constant feature of the Tang world, even as the specific political relationships surrounding the city shifted
  • No single Tang framework — neither straightforward dominance nor straightforward equality — adequately described every frontier relationship at any single point in time; multiplicity and variation across frontiers remained a constant

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic shifts and the underlying continuities in Tang frontier relationships helps students avoid treating "the Tang dynasty" as though it maintained one fixed, unchanging relationship to its neighbors across nearly three centuries, and prepares students to understand why the crisis examined in Chapter 29 struck a state whose frontier position had already grown considerably more precarious than at its seventh-century height.

Cause and Effect

The Tang-Silla Conflict

Causes

Tang and Silla shared a common strategic interest in eliminating Goguryeo and Baekje but had never fully agreed on what would happen to the conquered territory afterward. Following the destruction of Baekje (660) and Goguryeo (668), Tang authorities attempted to establish direct administrative control over former Korean territory, including areas Silla considered its own sphere of influence, leaving the postwar settlement ambiguous and contested from the outset.

Event or transformation

Silla mounted sustained military resistance across the 670s, fighting on familiar terrain with strong local support, and successfully expelled Tang military and administrative presence from most of the Korean peninsula by the end of the decade.

Effects

Silla achieved effective, though not total, control over a unified Korean political space; Tang China abandoned direct territorial ambitions on the peninsula in favor of a more conventional diplomatic relationship; and the northern former Goguryeo territory gave rise to the independent state of Balhae rather than falling under either Silla or Tang control.

Historical complexity

The conflict should not be reduced to simple ingratitude or betrayal on either side; it reflects a structural problem common to many wartime alliances, in which allied partners with different postwar goals for the same contested territory find their cooperation breaking down once the shared enemy has been defeated.
Everyday Life and Social History

Foreign Communities in Chang'an

Life for a foreign merchant, entertainer, or religious specialist resident in Chang'an during the Tang dynasty's height combined genuine commercial and cultural opportunity with specific administrative regulation and social boundaries. Foreign merchants, particularly Sogdian, Persian, and Arab traders, often lived within recognized commercial districts, conducted business under specific Tang legal and taxation arrangements distinct from those applied to Chinese subjects, and, in many documented cases, married into local families, raised children within Chinese society, and, over generations, became substantially integrated into the fabric of urban Tang life while often retaining distinctive religious practices, business networks, and, in some cases, elements of their ancestral language and identity.

Religious life for these communities included Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Nestorian Christian institutions serving Central Asian, Persian, and Sogdian residents, alongside the far more numerous Buddhist institutions serving the broader population, all discussed in greater religious and institutional detail in Chapter 28. Entertainers, musicians, and dancers of Central Asian background found ready demand for their skills among the Tang elite, whose fashionable enthusiasm for "Western" music and performance style is well documented in surviving Tang poetry and visual art, even as such performers typically occupied a socially marginal, if economically viable, position within the broader Tang social hierarchy examined in Chapter 26.

Students should recognize that the experience of foreign residents in Tang cities varied considerably by wealth, occupation, and the specific political climate of a given period. A wealthy Sogdian merchant family with generations of established Chang'an residence experienced urban life very differently from a newly arrived trader, a temporary diplomatic hostage, or a musician dependent on elite patronage, and the general climate of official tolerance or suspicion toward foreign communities shifted over time in ways that could affect all of these groups, particularly during periods of political and military crisis such as the one examined in Chapter 29.

Regional and Frontier Context

A World of Zones, Not Lines

This chapter's treatment of Tang frontiers should leave students with a clear, geographically grounded sense of regional variation. The northwestern frontier, running through the Gansu Corridor into the Tarim Basin, was a zone of garrisoned oasis administration, sustained commercial traffic, and recurring military contest with Tibet and Turkic powers. The southwestern frontier, facing the Tibetan plateau, was a zone of high-altitude military confrontation punctuated by marriage diplomacy, where Tang forces operated at a significant logistical disadvantage against a well-adapted highland power. The northeastern frontier, facing the Korean peninsula and Manchuria, was a zone of naval and amphibious military campaigning, followed by a transition to conventional diplomatic relations once Silla had secured effective control of most of the peninsula. The eastern maritime approach, facing Japan, was a zone of voluntary, distance-mediated cultural and intellectual exchange largely free of direct military confrontation after 663. The southern maritime approach, centered on Guangzhou, was a zone of sustained commercial integration into a wider Indian Ocean trading world dominated by non-Chinese maritime powers such as Srivijaya.

Each of these zones involved different mixtures of military force, administrative technique, commercial incentive, and cultural exchange, and each involved different, independently motivated non-Chinese actors whose own interests shaped the character of the relationship as much as Tang policy did. Students should resist any single, uniform mental model of "the Tang frontier" and instead think in terms of this variety of frontier zones, each with its own history, logic, and balance of power.

China and the Wider World

Reading This Chapter Forward

The relationships examined in this chapter did not end with the Tang dynasty. The overland and maritime trade networks discussed in Sections 27.3 and 27.7 would continue, with shifting participants and routes, through the post-Tang transition examined in Chapter 30 and into the Song period, when maritime connections to Southeast Asia, India, and the Islamic world, examined in Chapter 37, would become, if anything, more economically significant than they had been under the Tang. Japanese selective adaptation of continental institutions, examined in Section 27.6, would continue to evolve on its own trajectory across the following centuries of Japanese history. Buddhist connections among China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, and India, touched on throughout this chapter, would remain a defining feature of East and Inner Asian religious life for centuries afterward, examined further in Chapter 28 and in later chapters concerning Tibetan Buddhism under the Yuan and Qing. And the pattern of reliance on non-Han frontier military power, evident throughout this chapter's discussion of Turkic, Sogdian, and other frontier soldiers and commanders, connects directly to the career of An Lushan and the catastrophic rebellion examined in Chapter 29, which would permanently transform the Tang state's internal structure and, with it, its capacity to sustain the kind of expansive Eurasian engagement this chapter has described at its height.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Tang China's place within a wide, interconnected world of Eurasian and Asian states, peoples, and commercial and religious networks during the seventh and eighth centuries, building on the internal institutional foundations traced in Chapter 26. Rather than presenting Tang international relations as a single, uniform pattern of Chinese influence radiating outward, this chapter has emphasized that each frontier relationship examined here — with the steppe and Central Asian oasis kingdoms, with the Tibetan Empire, with Korea, with Japan, with Southeast Asia, with India, and with the expanding Islamic world — had its own distinct character, its own balance of power, and its own historical trajectory.

Students have learned that Tang engagement with Inner Asia operated through a mixture of direct conquest, "loose-rein" (jimi) administration, protectorate structures such as the Anxi Protectorate and its Four Garrisons, and sustained military contest with rival powers, especially the Tibetan Empire and various Turkic confederations. The chapter emphasized that Sogdian merchants and Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms were independent, economically sophisticated historical actors whose own commercial networks and political calculations shaped the character of Silk Road exchange as much as Tang policy did, and that the Silk Roads themselves should be understood as a shifting network of routes carrying goods, technologies, and religious ideas in multiple directions, not a single fixed road or a Chinese possession.

The chapter gave particular attention to the Tibetan Empire as a genuine imperial rival, capable of defeating Tang armies, exercising substantial diplomatic leverage, and, in 763, briefly occupying the Tang capital itself — evidence that should permanently correct any assumption of automatic or uncontested Tang superiority over its Inner Asian neighbors. The chapter traced Korea's shift from a three-kingdom political landscape through joint Tang-Silla conquest of Baekje and Goguryeo to a subsequent Tang-Silla conflict in which Silla successfully resisted Tang territorial administration, ultimately securing effective control over most of the peninsula while the northern former Goguryeo territory gave rise to the independent state of Balhae.

Japan's relationship with Tang China was examined as a case of voluntary, selective adaptation rather than passive imitation. The Japanese naval defeat at Baekgang in 663 ended direct Japanese military involvement on the Korean peninsula, while the already-established tradition of official study missions, dating from around 630, continued and expanded afterward, bringing Tang-influenced law, administration, city planning, writing, and Buddhism into a Japanese political and social context that consistently modified continental models to fit its own preexisting aristocratic order.

The chapter examined maritime Southeast Asia, especially the trading state of Srivijaya and the port city of Guangzhou, as a major, independent dimension of Tang international connection, linking China to the wider Indian Ocean world of India, Persia, and the expanding Islamic caliphates. It examined India primarily through the lens of Buddhist pilgrimage and translation, exemplified by Xuanzang's journey, while directing detailed religious analysis to Chapter 28, and it examined the Islamic world through the lens of expanding commercial contact and the Battle of Talas in 751, cautioning students against treating that battle as either a civilizational clash or a simple, singular explanation for later technological transmission.

Finally, the chapter turned to the cosmopolitan character of Tang cities, especially Chang'an, showing how the international relationships and networks examined throughout the chapter converged in concentrated, everyday form within a genuinely multiethnic urban population, while cautioning students against romanticizing this cosmopolitanism into a fully tolerant, borderless society; foreign communities in Tang China experienced real opportunity alongside real administrative regulation, social boundaries, and periodic hostility.

Across all of these sections, two analytical themes have recurred: the question of leverage, meaning who actually held the advantage in any given relationship and how that balance shifted over time, and the theme of mobility, meaning how soldiers, merchants, monks, students, envoys, and migrants moved across political boundaries that no single state fully controlled, driving much of the exchange this chapter has documented. Together, these themes should leave students with a picture of Tang China as an unusually powerful and wealthy participant within, rather than the sole author of, a dynamic and genuinely multi-polity Eurasian and Asian world.

This chapter's account of extensive frontier military engagement, reliance on non-Han soldiers and commanders, and sustained Central Asian overextension provides essential background for Chapter 29's examination of the An Lushan Rebellion, which arose directly from the frontier military structures this chapter has described and which would permanently transform the balance between central Tang authority and regional military power. Students should carry this chapter's international, multi-polity framework forward into that discussion, understanding the coming crisis not as an isolated internal Chinese political failure but as one consequence of the extensive, demanding, and ultimately overextended Eurasian engagement examined here.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined Tang China at what many historians regard as the height of its international power and cultural prestige, and it has done so by insisting throughout on a single organizing principle: that this power and prestige existed within, and depended upon, a wider world of independent states and mobile peoples rather than standing above or apart from it. Understanding this matters because it corrects a common and misleading picture of Chinese history as a story of one civilization's steady, self-contained development, interrupted only occasionally by contact with the outside world.

The relationships examined in this chapter — with Central Asian oasis kingdoms and Sogdian merchants, with the Tibetan Empire, with Korea's competing kingdoms, with Japan, with Southeast Asian trading states, with India, and with the expanding Islamic world — did not end with the Tang dynasty itself. The commercial networks, religious connections, and patterns of selective institutional adaptation examined here would continue, in modified form, through the post-Tang transition and deep into the Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing periods examined in later parts of this textbook, even as the specific states, routes, and balances of power involved continued to shift.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill directly relevant to the textbook's overall approach: resisting the temptation to write international history from only one side of a relationship. By drawing on Sogdian letters, Tibetan and Korean political context, and evidence of Japanese selective adaptation alongside Tang court records, this chapter has tried to recover something closer to a genuinely multi-sided account of the Tang world — a method students should carry forward into their evaluation of every subsequent chapter examining China's relations with its neighbors.

As students move forward into Chapter 28's examination of religion, literature, and intellectual life under the Tang, and then into Chapter 29's account of the An Lushan Rebellion, they should carry with them this chapter's central understanding: that Tang China's remarkable wealth, cultural achievement, and cosmopolitan urban life were inseparable from its extensive, demanding, and ultimately precarious engagement with a wider Eurasian and Asian world — an engagement that brought Tang China extraordinary prestige and connection, and that would also, through the frontier military structures this chapter has described, help produce the crisis that Chapter 29 examines in full.
