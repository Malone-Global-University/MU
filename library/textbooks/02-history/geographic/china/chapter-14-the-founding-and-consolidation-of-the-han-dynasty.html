Part III — The Han Empire
14

The Founding and Consolidation of the Han Dynasty

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the tomb of a minor local official at Zhangjiashan, in what is now Hubei province, a set of bamboo strips was sealed away around the second century BCE. Among them was a collection of statutes and ordinances generally identified as dating to 186 BCE, the second year of Empress Lü's rule, some sixteen years after the Han dynasty was proclaimed. A reader who expected these texts to reveal a new legal world, built in reaction against the Qin empire that Han had replaced, would be surprised. Much of what the strips record would have looked familiar to a Qin clerk.

This chapter studies how a dynasty came to endure after the collapse of the first imperial state. Chapter 13 ended with an important distinction: the Qin dynasty failed within roughly fourteen years of unifying the Warring States world, yet much of its administrative framework survived the dynasty that created it. Chapter 14 asks the natural next question. How did the Han turn the wreckage of Qin and years of civil war into an imperial order that could actually last? The Han state, in its two phases known as Western and Eastern Han, would endure for roughly four centuries in total, and its name would become attached to the largest population group in later Chinese history and to the Chinese writing system itself. That long legacy makes it tempting to assume that Han durability was inevitable, or that it flowed from a single wise decision. This chapter argues instead that consolidation was cumulative, contingent, and negotiated.

The chapter's central argument is that Han durability did not result from simply rejecting Qin. Liu Bang and his successors preserved and adapted much of the Qin imperial framework, while changing how power, taxation, labor, regional authority, and political coalitions were managed. The early Han accepted a more decentralized and negotiated political order than Qin had attempted, granting large kingdoms to military allies and to members of the imperial Liu family. Only gradually, as warfare subsided, agriculture recovered, rival power centers were reduced, and dynastic institutions became more secure, did the imperial center grow stronger. Where Chapter 13 showed that conquest was not the same as consolidation, this chapter shows that durable consolidation required adaptation, compromise, recovery, and time.

The people affected by these developments were many. They included rebel leaders who revived the names of Warring States kingdoms, former Qin clerks who kept the paperwork of government running through the civil war, soldiers who had to be demobilized, farming families who had to be brought back to registered land, and kings who ruled territories almost as large as some Warring States. They also included women of the imperial household, above all Empress Lü, who held real power in the decades after Liu Bang's death. Beyond the central plains, they included peoples of the south and northeast, such as the rulers of Nanyue, and the steppe confederation of the Xiongnu, whose military strength shaped early Han foreign policy.

The evidence for this period is unusually varied, and it changes how historians ask questions. Traditional narratives come chiefly from Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji) and from Ban Gu's Book of Han (Hanshu), both composed under a dynasty that had a stake in how its own founding was remembered. Since the later twentieth century, however, excavated legal and administrative manuscripts, above all those from Zhangjiashan tomb no. 247, have allowed scholars to test the received narrative against documents produced by people living inside the early Han legal system. The tomb's contents were not written to flatter the dynasty, and they were not composed centuries later. That makes them an unusually valuable check on the literary tradition.

Several things changed between 209 and 141 BCE. A revolt against Qin turned into a multipolar rebellion and then into a civil war. A wartime coalition became a dynasty. A political system that combined centrally governed commanderies with large semi-autonomous kingdoms became one in which the kingdoms were sharply reduced. Fiscal and labor demands were moderated relative to the Qin peak, though never abandoned. Other things continued: the commandery-and-county framework, the written culture of administration, the legal apparatus, the system of ranks and marquises, and the underlying dependence of the state on registered farming households. Understanding which was which is the main task of this chapter.

This chapter also connects China to a wider world. The early Han was not an isolated agrarian state. To its north, the Xiongnu confederation created an imperial power on the steppe at almost exactly the moment Han was being founded, and early Han diplomacy with it was a matter of vulnerability rather than dominance. To the south, the state of Nanyue, founded by a former Qin official, ruled a large territory that the Han recognized rather than conquered. To the northeast, refugees and new regimes were forming on the Korean peninsula. Chapter 16 will examine these relationships in greater depth. Here, they serve as a reminder that early Han consolidation happened in a crowded and unsettled political landscape.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the rebellions against Qin produced a multipolar political contest rather than a single unified revolution.
  2. Distinguish the stages by which the Han dynasty was founded, from Liu Bang's acceptance of Qin's surrender in 207 BCE to his proclamation as emperor in 202 BCE.
  3. Analyze why Liu Bang defeated Xiang Yu, emphasizing coalition-building, logistics, and rewards rather than personal character alone.
  4. Describe how the Han reconstructed government after 202 BCE, including its capital, its administration, and its treatment of Qin institutions.
  5. Assess the evidence of the Zhangjiashan statutes for continuity between Qin and early Han law.
  6. Evaluate the early Han commandery-kingdom system and explain why it was both politically necessary and politically dangerous.
  7. Contextualize the political role of Empress Lü and evaluate how later sources shaped her reputation.
  8. Trace the reduction of regional kingdoms under Emperors Wen and Jing, including the Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BCE.
  9. Interpret early Han fiscal restraint and agricultural recovery as state-building strategies rather than simple kindness toward the population.
  10. Compare interpretations of how different early Han government was from Qin government, using both excavated and literary evidence.
  11. Connect early Han consolidation to regional and Eurasian contexts, including Nanyue and the Xiongnu.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the skills required to win a civil war differ from those required to govern an empire afterward?
  2. Why did Liu Bang, a man from a modest background with no claim to royal descent, defeat rivals who were better born and militarily more celebrated?
  3. What does excavated evidence reveal about the relationship between Qin law and early Han law that the literary tradition tends to obscure?
  4. How much regional autonomy could the early Han permit without recreating the political fragmentation of the Warring States?
  5. Why should historians treat later praise of the reigns of Emperors Wen and Jing as both description and historical memory?
  6. In what ways could restraint in taxation and labor demands serve the interests of the state rather than merely relieve the population?
  7. How did the early Han court function as a partnership among the emperor, founding ministers, imperial relatives, and regional kings?
  8. Why is the claim that Han rulers succeeded because they learned that Qin had been too harsh too simple to serve as an explanation?

Historical Context

To understand the Han founding, students need to recall what Qin had actually done. Between 230 and 221 BCE, the state of Qin conquered the other Warring States powers, as Chapter 13 explained, and Qin Shi Huang proclaimed a new imperial title. The empire that resulted was governed through a system of commanderies and counties staffed by appointed officials, supported by a body of written law, standardized weights, measures, and script, and financed by heavy demands on labor and grain. Those demands built roads, walls, and palaces, and supplied armies on distant frontiers. Qin did not eliminate the memory of the former states. Elite families, local communities, and officials in the eastern territories continued to think of themselves in terms of older political loyalties.

When Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BCE, the empire was governed by a court in which succession was contested and manipulated. A year later, in 209 BCE, groups of conscripts in the Huai region, led by Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, rose in revolt, and the uprising spread. Qin armies initially suppressed several rebel forces, notably under the general Zhang Han, but court crisis and proliferating uprisings eventually overwhelmed the regime. Chapter 13 has already examined the causes of that failure, and this chapter does not repeat that discussion. What matters here is the political geography of what followed: for several years there was no imperial authority capable of enforcing its will across the former Warring States territories, and a number of leaders claimed kingship.

The geography of these events matters. The old Qin heartland lay in Guanzhong, the fertile basin of the Wei River in the west, protected by mountain passes and river gorges. The former eastern states, including Chu in the middle Yangtze and Huai regions, Qi in Shandong, Zhao and Yan in the north, and Wei and the Warring States state of Han (韓) in the central plains, were reservoirs of regional loyalty and military manpower. The Sichuan basin and the Hanzhong basin to its north offered a secure base that was relatively distant from the main theatre of war. Whoever could combine these resources, control the grain supply, and command the loyalty of experienced commanders had the best chance of ruling.

Students should also avoid assuming that the political meaning of "China" was stable in this period. The Qin and Han states ruled territory that included the Yellow River and Yangtze basins and extended into parts of the northwest, the southwest, and the far south, but they did not rule everything that later governments would claim, and the boundaries were fluid. The Xiongnu were building a steppe empire to the north. The territories of Lingnan in the far south were governed by a former Qin official who declared an independent kingdom, Nanyue. Even within the settled core, the early Han state was uncertain about how much of the former Qin territory it could actually administer directly. The dynastic name Han (漢) itself began as the name of a kingdom, granted to Liu Bang in 206 BCE, before it became a dynastic name and, much later, an ethnic label. It is not the same name or character as the Warring States state of Han (韓) mentioned above; the dynastic name is associated with the Han River region and Hanzhong.

Finally, students should be aware of the intellectual setting. The early Han was not a Confucian state. Multiple traditions of thought, including those associated with law and administration, with Huang-Lao ideas about non-interference, with ritual specialists, and with persuaders and strategists, circulated at court and in the regions. Confucian learning would become far more prominent in later Han political culture, as Chapter 17 explains. To describe early Han government as Confucian would project later developments backward, and the chapter's central institutional story is instead one of Qin-Han continuity, gradual adaptation, and political bargaining.

Section 14.1

Rebellion Against Qin

This section serves as a bridge. Chapter 13 (Section 13.9) has already examined the causes of Qin's collapse, so the aim here is narrower: to explain how the rebellions created the political world from which Han emerged. The key point is that there was no single, unified "Han revolution" against Qin. The revolt that began in 209 BCE under Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, both of modest background, quickly spread, but it was followed not by one movement but by many. Local officials, former aristocrats, military commanders, and village leaders proclaimed themselves kings, revived the names of older states, and fought both Qin forces and each other.

Regional identities mattered in this process. In the former territory of Chu, a large and culturally distinctive southern state before Qin's conquest, leaders drew on memories of Chu kingship. A descendant of the old Chu royal house was found and installed as a nominal king, Huai, to give an emerging military coalition a claim to legitimacy. In the north and east, leaders revived Zhao, Yan, Qi, and Wei. These revivals did not mean that the Warring States world had simply been restored. The new kings often depended on commanders whose ambitions exceeded their own, and the political map was constantly shifting. It is best understood as a competition among many claimants operating in a space where Qin authority had collapsed.

Two of the most important commanders came from Chu military families and the surrounding region. Xiang Liang and his nephew Xiang Yu led a large force, and after Xiang Liang's death in 208 BCE, Xiang Yu emerged as the preeminent battlefield commander, winning a decisive victory over Qin's main army at Julu in 207 BCE. Liu Bang, a former local official from the Pei area, operated alongside these forces and was for a time subordinate to the Chu leadership. Both were pursuing a common enemy, but they were also competitors for the prize that Qin's collapse would leave behind. Early in the rebellion, no one could have known that Liu Bang, whose forces were smaller, would become the eventual victor.

At the Qin court, the situation deteriorated rapidly. The eunuch official Zhao Gao, who had manipulated the succession after the death of Qin Shi Huang, forced the Second Emperor to take his own life in 207 BCE, and installed Ziying, described in sources as a member of the Qin ruling house, as ruler with a lesser title. Ziying, according to the received accounts, had Zhao Gao killed and then, when Liu Bang's forces reached the capital region in late 207 BCE, surrendered to him. With that act, Qin dynastic authority effectively ended. The surrender was not a peaceful transition. It removed a symbol rather than solving a problem, because the central question now was who would control the political space that Qin had unified.

That question makes the rebellion against Qin a useful case for historians of state formation. The rebels shared an opposition to Qin but had no shared program for what should replace it. Their resources, loyalties, and ambitions differed. For readers who study the sources, there is another lesson: the surviving narratives were written after Han had won, and they tend to organize the confusion of 209–207 BCE into a story in which Liu Bang's success looks foreordained. Students should keep the contingency in view. In 207 BCE, the most likely outcome was not a single Han empire but a set of competing kingdoms, and it was Xiang Yu, not Liu Bang, who set the terms of the first postwar settlement.

Section 14.2

Liu Bang and the Founding of Han

Liu Bang is best introduced carefully. Later tradition, particularly the accounts by Sima Qian and Ban Gu, includes legends about his birth and destiny that were intended to establish the dynasty's legitimacy, and historians treat these as part of dynastic memory rather than as evidence of what actually happened. What can be said with more confidence is that Liu Bang came from the region around Pei, in the former territory of Chu, that his family was not aristocratic, and that before the rebellion he had served as a low-ranking local Qin official, often rendered "ting chief" or "pavilion/post chief" (tingzhang), under the Qin administration. He was therefore not an illiterate outsider to government. He had direct experience of how Qin local administration operated, of its paperwork and its demands, and of the pressures it placed on ordinary people.

Students should avoid the shorthand that Liu Bang was a "peasant who became emperor." His origins were not aristocratic, and he lacked the ancestral standing of figures like Xiang Yu. But a local official occupied a position of some authority and social contact, and the trajectory from Qin local official to rebel leader to emperor fits a broader pattern in which people who had gained administrative experience under Qin were among the most effective political actors after its fall. Many of Liu Bang's key associates, including Xiao He, had also been minor officials, and this background helps explain why the Han state kept so much of the Qin governing apparatus.

The chronology of the founding is a matter of stages, and it is worth setting out carefully because it is often compressed. In 207 BCE, Liu Bang accepted Ziying's surrender. In 206 BCE, Xiang Yu, the dominant commander, divided the former empire into a series of kingdoms, assigned many to his own allies, took for himself the title Hegemon-King of Western Chu, and gave Liu Bang the kingdom of Han, comprising the Hanzhong basin and the territories of Ba and Shu in the southwest, a region that was defensible but comparatively remote from the central plains. The name "Han" thus first appears as a kingdom in an arrangement designed, at least in part, to contain its holder. It was only in 202 BCE, after defeating Xiang Yu, that Liu Bang was proclaimed emperor and is conventionally known as Emperor Gaozu, "High Ancestor." The founding of Han was therefore a process, not a single date.

The choice of imperial title deserves comment. Qin Shi Huang had invented the title huangdi, emperor, and the Han adopted it. Liu Bang did not abolish the imperial office that Qin had created; he inherited and redefined it. He was enthroned by a group of kings and marquises who urged him to take the title, a detail that the sources emphasize because it presents his rule as sanctioned by the political community rather than seized by force. Whatever the truth of the ceremony, it reflects a real dependence: the new emperor owed his victory to allies who expected to be rewarded and who commanded large forces of their own. As Sections 14.4 and 14.5 explain, this dependence shaped the early Han political settlement more than any ideological program.

The name that Liu Bang chose for his dynasty is a reminder of how the Han inherited more than it created. He kept "Han" (漢), the name of the kingdom he had received from Xiang Yu, rather than inventing a new dynastic label. This Han is not the same name or character as the Warring States state of Han (韓); it derives from the Han River region of his kingdom. The people who would later be called the Han people, and the script called the Han script, took their names from this dynasty, though these usages are much later than the period discussed here. Chapter 15 and Appendix E discuss how "Han" changed in meaning from a dynastic name to a broader cultural and ethnic label. For now, it is enough to note that in 202 BCE, "Han" designated a ruling house and its state, not an ethnic category.

Section 14.3

The Chu-Han Contention

The civil war between Xiang Yu and Liu Bang, conventionally called the Chu-Han Contention and dated to 206–202 BCE, needs to be understood as more than a sequence of battles. The more instructive question is why Liu Bang won. Popular and traditional accounts often treat the contest as a moral drama in which the flexible and wise Liu Bang triumphed over the brutal and arrogant Xiang Yu. Sima Qian's narrative, which is brilliant literature, helped establish that reading. But it is also the reading of the victorious side's historians, and it obscures the structural factors that shaped the outcome.

Xiang Yu began the war in a stronger position. He commanded the largest and most experienced army, had won the decisive battle against Qin's main force, and was the arbiter of the postwar settlement. His partition of 206 BCE, however, created resentments. Some commanders received smaller or less desirable territories than they thought their services merited, and rebellions in Qi and elsewhere soon drew him away from the main theatre. Xiang Yu also placed his own capital at Pengcheng in the east, far from the strategic heartland of Guanzhong, where Qin's former territory was left divided among former Qin generals. That decision gave Liu Bang an opening.

Liu Bang, from his base in Hanzhong, moved back into Guanzhong in 206 BCE and quickly took control of it. This was strategically decisive. Guanzhong was a fertile, defensible region with a large agricultural surplus, protected by mountain passes, and its resources could support a sustained war. The Han side also benefited from the administrative work of Xiao He, who organized supplies, grain, and manpower from the rear and kept records of households and resources. This is a case where logistics and administrative capacity, supplied largely by men with Qin bureaucratic experience, mattered as much as battlefield skill.

The war was not a simple march to victory. In 205 BCE, Liu Bang led a large coalition force into the east and captured Pengcheng, only to be routed by a much smaller counterattack led by Xiang Yu. The Han side recovered, however, in part because its supply base in Guanzhong remained intact and in part because of skilled generalship by Han Xin, who conducted campaigns in the north and east that brought new territories, and their resources, into the coalition. The war centered on positions such as Xingyang and Chenggao, on the routes between Guanzhong and the central plains, where Liu Bang could exploit interior lines and his logistical advantage while Xiang Yu's forces were stretched.

A crucial feature of the Han success was the management of allies. Liu Bang won over or retained the loyalty of commanders and regional leaders, including former enemies, by promising and distributing rewards in the form of titles, kingdoms, and territory. Han Xin, for example, was made King of Qi during the conflict; other commanders such as Ying Bu and Peng Yue received kingdoms as well. This strategy had a cost. Each kingdom granted in wartime was a potential rival after victory, and a victor who had made such promises would face the problem of how to keep them, or break them, once the war ended. It is one of the central ironies of the Han founding that the same practices that helped win the war created the political dilemma of the early empire.

The end came in 203 and 202 BCE. A truce in 203 BCE divided the territory between the two sides, with the boundary drawn at a canal, but Liu Bang, urged by advisers, broke the agreement and pursued Xiang Yu. His allies converged on Xiang Yu's forces, who were surrounded at Gaixia in 202 BCE. Xiang Yu, according to the traditional narrative, broke out with a small force and died at the Wu River after a final stand. Sima Qian's account of the death of Xiang Yu is among the most famous passages in Chinese historical writing, and its sympathy for the defeated hero is a reminder that the sources are not simply hostile to the losing side.

For students, the important analytical question is: how did the skills required to win a civil war differ from those required to govern an empire afterward? Winning required military talent, mobility, and the willingness to make promises to many people. Governing required routine administration, reliable taxation, adjudication of disputes, management of expectations, and the ability to reduce the power of former allies without provoking their revolt. Liu Bang's success at the first task did not guarantee success at the second, and much of the rest of this chapter concerns how the Han dynasty acquired the second set of capacities.

Section 14.4

Early Han Political Reconstruction

After 202 BCE, the Han faced an extraordinary problem: a victorious military coalition had to become a functioning government. The new emperor commanded an army of veterans and allies but ruled a population exhausted by nearly a decade of warfare, and the previous imperial system had collapsed. Reconstruction meant a series of practical decisions: where to locate the capital, how to demobilize soldiers, how to reward supporters, how to restore routine administration, and how to bring displaced people back to farming.

The choice of capital was itself a political decision. Liu Bang initially established his court at Luoyang in the central plains, but advisers, including Lou Jing and Zhang Liang, argued for a site in Guanzhong, and the court soon moved to Chang'an, in the Wei River valley near the former Qin heartland. Their reasoning combined strategic and economic considerations: Guanzhong was defensible, its agricultural base was strong, and it could draw on the resources of the Yellow River region and of the southwest. Palace construction, supervised by Xiao He, began there, and the Changle and Weiyang palaces became the core of the imperial court. Chang'an's walls were built later, under Emperor Hui, and the Hanshu reports that laborers, including women, were mobilized from the surrounding region, a reminder that the Han state continued to rely on corvée labor from the beginning.

Demobilization was an urgent problem. Edicts reported in the Han sources describe measures to send soldiers home, to grant them exemptions or privileges, and to encourage people who had fled to the mountains and marshes to return to their registered places and resume farming. Such measures were not merely humanitarian. A state could not tax or conscript people it could not count, and one of the main tasks of the early Han administration was to reconstruct the household registers on which government depended. The sources indicate that population had declined in the preceding wars, though they do not permit reliable numerical estimates, and students should be wary of precise figures cited without a clear source.

The Han also had to reward supporters. A system of noble ranks, which Qin had used to reward military service, was retained and adapted, and a class of marquises (lie hou) received titles and income from households assigned to them. Some senior supporters were also given kingdoms, as Section 14.5 explains. Such rewards created a group of people with a stake in the new dynasty, but they also created a problem of scale, because the more territory and revenue was assigned to allies, the less remained to support the central government.

Above all, students should not conclude that the Han abolished Qin government and replaced it with something new. The best modern scholarship, drawing on excavated legal and administrative documents, finds extensive institutional and legal inheritance from Qin. The commandery-and-county framework was retained in the territories the emperor directly governed. Written records, standardized procedures, and the bureaucratic paperwork of government continued. The same is true of law. The traditional account, in the histories, credits Xiao He with drawing up a body of Han law that built on Qin models. Modern scholars treat the precise details of this tradition cautiously, but the excavated material confirms its main thrust: the early Han inherited most Qin legal rules while modifying some of them and adding others.

This is the best place to introduce the Zhangjiashan tomb no. 247, excavated in the 1980s in Hubei province. Among its contents was a set of bamboo strips titled Statutes and Ordinances of the Second Year (Ernian lüling), generally identified as 186 BCE, the second year of Empress Lü's rule. This is contemporary early Han legal material, not a later reconstruction. It is a different kind of evidence from the Qin administrative documents from Liye, examined in Chapter 13: Liye is an archive produced through the everyday operation of a Qin county government, while Zhangjiashan is a tomb manuscript that preserves legal knowledge, apparently compiled for burial with a person connected to official or legal expertise. Because the two are different kinds of evidence, they complement each other, and together they allow students to compare Qin administrative practice with early Han legal texts. The primary source feature in Section 14.10 discusses this evidence in detail.

Section 14.5

Central and Regional Authority

This section dismantles another common textbook cliché: that the Han simply continued Qin-style absolute centralization. It did not. The early Han was a hybrid imperial order. Some territory, especially in the west and around the capital, remained under centrally administered commanderies and counties, while large parts of the east and south were assigned as kingdoms to allies and to members of the imperial Liu family. These kings had their own courts, officials, armies, and tax revenues, though the emperor retained ultimate authority and the right to appoint certain key officials. Historians call this arrangement the commandery-kingdom system.

The compromise made political sense. Liu Bang had won the war with the help of powerful allies who commanded troops and controlled territory, and he could not simply dismiss them. In the early years, he granted kingdoms to a number of non-Liu allies, including Han Xin, Ying Bu, Peng Yue, and others. This arrangement acknowledged the reality of the coalition that had won the war. It also risked recreating a political landscape not unlike that of the Warring States, with a number of autonomous regional powers of which the emperor was only the most prominent.

The emperor soon acted to reduce this risk. Within a few years, most of the non-Liu kings were eliminated, replaced, or driven to rebellion and defeat. Han Xin, initially King of Qi and later King of Chu, was demoted to marquis and later executed, in 196 BCE, after being accused of plotting rebellion, an episode in which the accuracy of the accusation is debated in the historical sources. Ying Bu and Peng Yue met similar ends. Liu Bang replaced many of these kings with relatives from the imperial house, on the reasoning that kinsmen would be more loyal. A tradition, preserved in the sources, held that Liu Bang had sworn an oath, sometimes called the covenant of the white horse, that only members of the Liu family could be kings and only men with merit could be marquises. Historians debate the historicity of the oath, but it reflects a widely held theory of how the dynasty should be structured.

Students should note one important exception to the pattern. The kingdom of Changsha in the south, held by a non-Liu family, survived for several decades, a case that shows the early Han system was not as uniform as later summaries suggest. Excavated tombs at Mawangdui near modern Changsha, associated with the Changsha kingdom's elite, have yielded silk manuscripts and rich material goods that illustrate the wealth and culture of regional courts. They show that the kingdoms were not simply administrative units but centers of culture and patronage in their own right.

The central question of the period was therefore: how much regional autonomy could the Han permit without recreating political fragmentation? The kingdoms were large and were ruled by men of the imperial lineage who, over time, became more distant relatives of the ruling emperor. Each successive generation increased the distance between the emperor and the kings, and reduced the sentimental force of kinship. Meanwhile, the kingdoms continued to control significant land, population, and revenue. The tension between central authority and regional power was not a temporary problem to be solved once, but a structural feature of the early empire that would occupy the court for decades. Section 14.6 explains how it was gradually resolved.

The commandery-kingdom system also affected ordinary people. A resident of a kingdom lived under the laws and taxes set by the king's administration, within the framework of imperial law, and might be subject to labor service organized by the kingdom. Officials in the kingdoms could be tied by loyalty to the king rather than to the emperor. Merchants and artisans could benefit from the resources of regional courts. And peasants in a kingdom that rebelled, as several did, could find themselves mobilized for a war that was fought in the name of a king they had never met. The system was not simply an abstract question of constitutional design. It shaped the lives of the people who lived within its territories.

The kingdoms also interacted with the northern frontier. In 201 BCE a northern king, also named Han Xin but not to be confused with the general, went over to the Xiongnu, and Gaozu campaigned against them, only to have his force encircled near Pingcheng in 200 BCE. The settlement that followed helped establish the practice of heqin, marriage alliance and gifts, discussed in Chapter 16. The episode shows that questions of regional authority and frontier security were entangled from the start.

Section 14.6

Emperor Wen and Emperor Jing

It would be a mistake to move directly from Gaozu to Emperor Wen, because the period between them was essential for understanding how fragile the new dynasty still was. Gaozu died in 195 BCE, and his son Liu Ying succeeded as Emperor Hui. Hui was young and, according to the sources, physically and politically weak, and real power came to be exercised by his mother, Empress Lü. After Hui's death in 188 BCE, she placed infant emperors on the throne and ruled through them until her own death in 180 BCE. For roughly fifteen years, then, the empire was governed by a woman who held no formal title equivalent to emperor but who directed policy, appointed officials, and shaped the succession.

Empress Lü should be treated as a political ruler and power broker, not through the traditional caricature of an exceptionally wicked woman. Later sources, particularly those written by the men who overthrew her family and by historians serving the dynasty that followed, are often hostile to her and to her relatives. They emphasize her cruelty to rivals, most famously in a story about her treatment of a concubine of Gaozu, and they stress her promotion of Lü family members to kingdoms and offices. These accounts require the same source criticism that students apply to hostile narratives about Qin. The Zhangjiashan statutes, generally identified as dating to 186 BCE, preserve early Han legal material from her period and point to a legal system that was being maintained in a routine way, which suggests that her government was not merely a period of intrigue, though the manuscript's limits as evidence are discussed in Section 14.10.

Nevertheless, her rule strained the political settlement. By granting kingdoms to Lü relatives, she violated the principle, associated with the oath of the white horse, that only Liu family members should be kings. When she died in 180 BCE, senior officials and generals who had served since the founding, including Chen Ping and Zhou Bo, moved against the Lü family, supported by members of the Liu lineage, and eliminated it. This episode is a reminder that in the early Han, the dynasty's survival depended on the loyalty of a small circle of founding ministers and relatives who were prepared to act. It also shows that the imperial house was not yet secure enough for succession to be automatic.

After the removal of the Lü family, the ministers selected Liu Heng, a son of Gaozu who had been ruling the northern kingdom of Dai, as emperor. He is known as Emperor Wen and reigned from 180 to 157 BCE. Choosing a ruler from a distant, relatively modest kingdom, without a powerful maternal family, was a deliberate strategy to avoid concentrating power in the hands of another dominant in-law clan. Wen's reign is remembered in the tradition as a model of restraint: he is credited with reducing certain burdens on the population, limiting extravagance at court, and, in 167 BCE, according to the sources, abolishing some mutilating punishments. The details of the latter reform, and of its practical effect, are debated, since some substitute punishments may have been harsh. The point for students is that the tradition associated Wen with moderation, and that this reputation itself became part of the dynasty's political memory.

Wen's reign was not without difficulty. Kings within the empire remained powerful, and some acted independently. A rebellion by a king of Huainan, a son of Gaozu, was suppressed in the 170s BCE. Officials such as Jia Yi wrote memorials urging the court to reduce the size of the kingdoms by dividing them among the heirs of each king, so that no single kingdom would be large enough to challenge the center. Wen did not carry out the full program, but the arguments were now on the table. The Xiongnu also posed continuing pressure on the northern frontier, and the court's approach to them was cautious and defensive, as Chapter 16 explains.

Emperor Jing succeeded in 157 BCE and reigned until 141 BCE. Under his advisers, especially Chao Cuo, the court began to pursue a more aggressive policy toward the kingdoms, seeking to reduce their territories and authority. This policy provoked a crisis. In 154 BCE, a coalition of seven kingdoms, led by the King of Wu, Liu Pi, and including Chu, Zhao, and several kingdoms in the Shandong region, rose in revolt against the court. The Rebellion of the Seven States is a pivotal event in early Han history. The rebels claimed that their aim was to remove a dangerous adviser, Chao Cuo, but the underlying issue was whether the kings would accept the loss of territory and power.

The court responded with military force, led by the general Zhou Yafu, and the rebellion was crushed within a few months. Chao Cuo was executed in a failed attempt to appease the rebels. The consequences were significant, though they unfolded over time. Military suppression in 154 BCE was followed by administrative reorganization: in the following years, including a major reorganization confirmed in 144 BCE, the court restricted the kings' powers, took over the appointment of the senior administrators of the kingdoms, and reduced some kingdoms in size. Suppression and bureaucratic subordination were related but not identical events, and the political weight of the kingdoms as a whole declined sharply as a result. By the end of Jing's reign, the relationship between the emperor and the kingdoms was considerably different from that under Gaozu.

It would be misleading to describe the rebellion simply as feudalism versus centralization. The kingdoms were not relics of a pre-Qin order; they were products of the early Han political settlement itself. Their kings were members of the ruling dynasty. The immediate conflict involved attempts by the court to reduce their territories and authority, and the rebels' response was to defend privileges they believed had been guaranteed by the founding settlement. The outcome did not abolish the kingdoms, and later Han rulers continued to appoint relatives as kings, but it changed their character. They became closer to noble estates with limited administrative autonomy than to semi-independent states.

Students should also treat the later praise of the reigns of Wen and Jing as a subject of historical analysis. Later writers, including Sima Qian and Ban Gu, spoke of these decades as a period of prosperity and good government, contrasting them with the more expansionist reign of Emperor Wu. That contrast serves the interests of later moralizing writers, and the actual record is more complicated: the court still used corvée, still faced Xiongnu raids, still dealt with rebellion, and still applied harsh punishments. The "golden age" of Wen and Jing is both a description of real recovery and a piece of historical memory.

Section 14.7

Fiscal Policy and Agricultural Recovery

This section explains why restraint could itself be a form of state-building. After decades of Warring States warfare, Qin mobilization, rebellion, and the Chu-Han civil war, the land needed farming households producing grain, rebuilding communities, and remaining on registered land. A state that demanded too much from the population risked driving it away, into hiding, or into rebellion, as the fall of Qin had demonstrated. The early Han court therefore had an interest in moderating its demands, at least for a time.

The sources describe reductions in some taxes and labor demands under the early emperors, along with policies intended to encourage the return of displaced people to their farms, the settlement of veterans, and the restoration of households. The land tax was reduced at various points, particularly under Wen and Jing, according to the received tradition, though the exact rates and their application are difficult to reconstruct from the surviving sources, and students should be wary of tidy percentages. The state also seems to have limited the scale of state construction and of military campaigns for several decades, in part because it lacked the resources for Qin-scale mobilization and in part because it recognized the risk of overextension.

The Han state's concern with smallholders was practical. Independent farming households were the basic unit of taxation, military recruitment, and labor service. They produced the grain that fed the capital, the officials, and the armies. A state with many stable smallholder households was better placed than one whose population was concentrated in the hands of great landlords or had fled its jurisdiction. The court's sympathy for the small farmer, which appears in memorials by officials such as Chao Cuo, was therefore partly a matter of fiscal and military interest. Chapter 15 examines how the emergence of large landholdings later complicated this picture.

The economic policies of this period also involved money and commerce. The court at times permitted private minting of coins, a policy that later governments reversed, and it did not attempt at first to monopolize major industries. Merchants and manufacturers were able to accumulate wealth in this period, a development that Sima Qian describes and that later officials would criticize. The early Han state's relatively limited intervention in commerce contrasts with the more extensive state economic activity of the reign of Emperor Wu, which Chapter 15 will discuss.

Students should avoid turning this into a simple cliché: Qin equals harsh taxation and Han equals low taxes and happy peasants. The better point is that early Han rulers moderated state demands while preserving the fiscal and administrative machinery needed to govern. Registers were maintained, taxes were collected, corvée and military service were still owed, and laws were still enforced. Scholars characterize the early Han as preserving much of the Qin imperial order while supporting a smallholder agrarian economy and adapting the fiscal state to conditions of recovery. Restraint was a policy choice within an inherited system, not a repudiation of it.

The effects of this policy are hard to measure precisely, but the tradition and the archaeological evidence suggest a gradual economic recovery. By the middle of the second century BCE, sources describe granaries filling and the accumulation of wealth in the capital and provinces. Archaeological evidence of tombs with rich goods in various regions also indicates growing prosperity among the elite. Ordinary farming households are much harder to see in the record, and it would be a mistake to assume that recovery benefited everyone equally. Flooding, drought, and locust plagues were recurring problems, and the surviving sources note that in years of natural disaster the court remitted taxes or distributed grain, a form of state response that would recur throughout Han history.

Section 14.8

Imperial Court and Bureaucracy

This section is not yet the full account of Han government and administration that Chapter 15 will provide. Instead, it explains how the founding coalition became a governing elite. The early Han government was not simply a matter of the emperor giving orders and bureaucrats obeying. It involved a network of relationships among the emperor and imperial household, founding ministers, military commanders, Liu relatives, regional kings, central officials, and local administrators.

The founding generation dominated the early court. Xiao He, who had organized logistics during the war, served as chancellor and was credited with establishing the basic legal and administrative order of the new state. When he died in 193 BCE, he was succeeded by Cao Shen, whom later tradition remembered as having followed the arrangements Xiao He had made rather than inventing new ones, a reputation that reflects an important theme: continuity in government was itself valued. Chen Ping and Zhou Bo, two of the ministers who helped remove the Lü family in 180 BCE, held senior positions in the court, and men from the founding military and administrative circle often served as marquises with significant influence.

Below this elite, the administration functioned largely through officials who were recruited and trained in the traditions of Qin administration. Clerks, scribes, and local officials kept records, administered laws, and collected taxes, and a person of literate skill could rise through service. Recruitment was mainly by recommendation, by hereditary privilege, by purchase in some cases, and by promotion through the ranks, rather than by any examination system, which would develop much later, as Chapter 26 and Appendix H explain. The early Han bureaucracy was thus more open than an aristocracy but less regularized than the later examination order.

The imperial household itself was an institution of political importance. The emperor's mother, wife, and their families could exercise considerable influence, as the career of Empress Lü showed. Later dynasties would develop mechanisms to control the power of consort families, but in the early Han this was an open problem. The mother of Emperor Jing, Empress Dowager Dou, remained an influential figure into the reign of Emperor Wu, and she is associated in the sources with a preference for Huang-Lao thought, a body of ideas that stressed non-interference and restraint. That link between the imperial household and political philosophy is a reminder that ideas at court were shaped by personal relationships as well as by doctrine.

Succession was a persistent problem. The Han did not have a fixed law of succession beyond the general preference for the eldest son of the empress, and the deaths of emperors often triggered contests among relatives and ministers. The events of 195 to 180 BCE show that the dynasty could survive a regency and a coup, and by 141 BCE, the transfer of power from Jing to his son was smooth. The gradual regularization of succession, together with the reduction in the power of the kingdoms, was one of the achievements of the period.

Students should note carefully what this section does not claim. Early Han government was not a Confucian bureaucracy. Confucian learning existed, and Confucian scholars such as Shusun Tong, who helped design court ritual for Gaozu, served at court, but the early Han intellectual and political environment remained plural. Legalist-influenced administrators, ritual specialists, Huang-Lao advocates, strategists, and others all had roles. The institutional story here is Qin-Han continuity plus gradual adaptation. The growing importance of Confucian learning belongs mainly to the reign of Emperor Wu and after, and Chapter 17 discusses it.

Section 14.9

Foundations of a Long-Lived Empire

This section synthesizes rather than introducing new events. By 141 BCE, the question to ask is: what had changed since 202 BCE? The Han dynasty now had a recognized ruling lineage; a stable imperial capital and court; a functioning inherited-and-modified bureaucracy; greater control over the regional kingdoms; a recovering fiscal and agricultural base; more established succession practices; and several generations separating the state from the founding civil war. Each of these was a product of choices and circumstances, and none was guaranteed.

The important conclusion is not that the Han succeeded because it was kinder than Qin. The explanation is closer to this: the Han transformed a wartime coalition into a dynasty by retaining useful Qin institutions, moderating some of the extraction and mobilization associated with the Qin imperial experiment, tolerating regional power when necessary, gradually reducing that autonomy when possible, and allowing economic and political recovery to occur over several generations. That is a story of accumulation rather than a single decision.

Several features distinguish this from a simple story of rejection of Qin. First, the Han inherited an enormous amount from Qin, including its imperial title, its commandery-and-county framework, much of its law, its clerical culture, and its practice of registering households. Second, the Han operated through elite coalitions of a kind that Qin had not faced in the same form, including military allies and imperial kin who could not be treated as ordinary officials. Third, the Han benefited from timing: the population, worn down by war, was receptive to a period of peace. The dynasty did not escape external pressure, however. The Xiongnu crisis of 201–200 BCE, in which Gaozu's army was encircled near Pingcheng, imposed a major constraint, and early Han rulers managed it largely through diplomacy and limited warfare rather than offensive campaigns. Fourth, the Han managed dangerous moments of succession politics, including the crisis of 180 BCE. Finally, the Han gradually reduced competing regional power, in 154 BCE most decisively.

The consolidation also had limits. The Han state in 141 BCE was still relatively modest in its reach. It faced the Xiongnu with a defensive and diplomatic posture rather than an offensive one. The great expansion of the empire, and the great expansion of its fiscal apparatus, lay in the future, under Emperor Wu. Consolidation created the conditions for later expansion but did not itself constitute it. Students should therefore treat the period 202–141 BCE as a foundation, not as the full development of the Han imperial system.

This section also prepares the way for Chapter 15. This chapter has asked how the Han state became durable. Chapter 15 will ask how that durable state actually governed its economy and society: how the bureaucracy worked, how taxes were collected, how land was held, how merchants and markets operated, and how families and social hierarchies were organized. The questions belong together. The state that emerged from the consolidation described here would rest on a particular relationship between officials and local society, and understanding that relationship requires the detailed study that Chapter 15 provides.

Section 14.10

Chapter Review and Early Han Checkpoint

This chapter has examined how the Han dynasty emerged from the collapse of Qin and a civil war, and how, over roughly sixty years, it became a durable imperial order. The rebellions of 209–207 BCE created a multipolar political contest, from which Liu Bang emerged as the eventual victor in a war decided by coalition-building, logistics, and the management of allies. The founding of Han was a process, running from Liu Bang's acceptance of Qin's surrender in 207 BCE, through the grant of the kingdom of Han in 206 BCE, to his proclamation as emperor in 202 BCE.

After 202 BCE, the Han reconstructed government by inheriting and adapting Qin institutions, especially the commandery-and-county framework, law, and clerical administration, while accommodating powerful allies through the commandery-kingdom system. The dynasty survived the death of its founder, the regency of Empress Lü, and the crisis of 180 BCE, and under Emperors Wen and Jing it moderated fiscal and labor demands, supported a recovering smallholder economy, and gradually reduced the power of the kingdoms, most decisively after the Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BCE. Durable consolidation was the product of adaptation, compromise, recovery, and time.

Historical Evidence — What Sources Tell Us About the Early Han

Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji, c. 100 BCE)
The principal narrative source for the rebellion, the Chu-Han Contention, and the early Han court. Sima Qian wrote within a century of these events, drew on earlier records and oral traditions, and was a gifted narrator, but he wrote under a dynasty with a stake in its own founding, and his portraits are shaped by literary and moral purposes.
Ban Gu, Book of Han (Hanshu, first century CE)
A dynastic history of Western Han, compiled later than the Shiji and often drawing on it. It preserves edicts, memorials, and treatises on law, economy, and geography, and is invaluable for institutional history, but it too reflects the outlook of a later Han court.
Zhangjiashan tomb no. 247 (Hubei; texts c. 186 BCE and after)
Bamboo strips including the Statutes and Ordinances of the Second Year, a text known as the Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases, and mathematical and medical texts. The statutes preserve contemporary early Han legal material and were buried with a person associated with official or legal expertise. The archaeological context does not prove that the surviving copy was an office working document; scholarship has argued that it was specially compiled for funerary use, perhaps to display the tomb owner's status and legal knowledge. It is therefore exceptionally important evidence for early Han law, but it is not a county-office archive like the Liye documents.
Mawangdui tombs (near Changsha, Hunan; second century BCE)
Tombs associated with the elite of the Changsha kingdom, containing silk manuscripts, textiles, lacquerware, and maps. They illustrate the wealth and culture of regional courts, though they reflect elite rather than ordinary life.
Coins, seals, and administrative artifacts
Coins show the state's monetary policy and the tolerance of private minting at times; seals and sealings identify officials and offices. They complement texts but require interpretation.
Limits of the evidence
Most sources reflect the perspectives of elite men, and many were shaped by dynastic memory. Ordinary farmers, women outside the court, and people in frontier regions are seen mostly through officials' eyes. The excavated documents help correct the bias of the literary tradition but cannot fully replace it, and each tomb represents a particular person, place, and date.
Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Commandery-Kingdom System (junguo zhi, 郡國制)

The term describes the early Han arrangement in which part of the empire was governed directly by the central government through commanderies (jun) and counties (xian), while other parts were assigned as kingdoms (guo) to kings, mostly members of the imperial Liu family. The kings had their own administrations and revenues, though they owed allegiance to the emperor. The name combines jun and guo.

The term matters because it names a genuine political compromise, not simply a continuation of Qin centralization or a return to Zhou-style feudalism. Historians sometimes contrast fengjian (enfeoffment) with junxian (commanderies and counties), but the early Han combined elements of both. When students read that "the early Han was a hybrid order," they should understand that hybridity means a deliberate, negotiated balance between the emperor's need for central control and his dependence on powerful allies and relatives.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Marquis (hou) and Merit Ranks (jue, 爵)

Under Qin and Han, a system of ranks rewarded service to the state, especially in war. Lower ranks could be granted widely to commoners and carried privileges such as reductions in punishments or service obligations, while the highest ranks were held by marquises (lie hou), who received income from assigned households. The Han inherited this system from Qin and adapted it.

The system matters because it shows how the state rewarded loyalty and tied people to it. A rank was not just an honor; it was a legal status with concrete consequences. The distribution of marquisates to founding allies gave them a stake in the dynasty and represented a lesser form of reward than a kingdom. The rank system is an example of institutional continuity across the Qin-Han transition, and it reappears in Chapter 15's discussion of social status.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: The Zhangjiashan Early Han Statutes, c. 186 BCE

Creator: Anonymous copyists; buried with a local official in tomb no. 247 at Zhangjiashan (Jingzhou, Hubei) · Date: The Statutes and Ordinances of the Second Year are generally identified as dating to 186 BCE, the second year of Empress Lü (High Empress); the interpretation of "Second Year" has itself been discussed by scholars · Type: Bamboo-strip legal manuscript, buried with the tomb owner

Context: The tomb was excavated in the early 1980s and yielded a large group of bamboo strips, including a collection of statutes, a casebook of legal cases, and technical texts. The statutes cover a broad range of administrative subjects, including ranks, household registration, land, movement through passes, and official duties. They were buried with a person associated with official or legal expertise. Scholarship has argued that the statutes manuscript was compiled for burial, probably to display the tomb owner's status and legal knowledge, rather than being an office working copy placed in the grave. It still preserves contemporary early Han legal material.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

The statutes set out rules for the granting of ranks, the registration of households and land, the regulation of travel through checkpoints, and the duties of officials, in the form of numbered clauses. Comparison with Qin legal manuscripts shows that many rules are the same or closely similar, and that others were modified or added by the early Han.

Analysis: The Zhangjiashan statutes are important because they allow students to test a familiar claim, that the Han rejected the Qin legal order and started afresh. The literary tradition includes a story that Liu Bang, on entering Guanzhong in 207 BCE, promised to replace Qin's harsh laws with only a few simple provisions. The excavated statutes indicate that in practice, the early Han legal system inherited a large body of Qin law. That does not mean nothing changed. Scholars identify modifications, and the punishments, procedures, and priorities of the Han state evolved over time. But it does mean that continuity was extensive.

The source's limits are equally important. It is one collection buried with one person for funerary purposes, and it may not include all Han statutes. Its date, while generally accepted, rests on internal evidence and interpretation of the phrase "second year." It shows the letter of the law, not how it was applied, and does not tell us how ordinary people experienced it. The casebook found in the same tomb, the Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases, should not be read as a straightforward window onto courtroom practice: scholars caution that it is not an official docket, and that it mixes material based on actual cases with shaped or constructed narratives. Nor is Zhangjiashan a county-office archive like Liye, which was produced by the routine operation of government. Finally, the source represents an administrative perspective: it tells us what officials were expected to do, and much less about what people who were subject to those rules thought.

  1. What does this source say or show about the subjects that early Han law regulated?
  2. Who created it, and what might their purpose have been in preserving these texts in a tomb?
  3. What can historians learn from it about continuity between Qin and Han?
  4. What can historians not learn from it alone about how law worked in practice?
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Different Was Early Han from Qin?

The question: Did the early Han represent a genuine break from the Qin imperial system, or was it essentially Qin government under a new dynasty?

Interpretation A — Han as a reaction against Qin: Some scholars, following the traditional narrative, emphasize that the Han consciously moderated Qin practices. They point to the reduction in taxes and labor demands, the grant of kingdoms, the more restrained approach to war, and the memory, preserved in the literary sources, that Liu Bang abolished Qin's harshest laws. On this view, Han's success came from responding to the failures of Qin.
Interpretation B — Han as an adaptation of the Qin order: Other scholars emphasize the extensive continuity in institutions, law, and personnel. They point to excavated legal manuscripts showing that the Han inherited most Qin legal rules, to the retention of the commandery-and-county system, and to the fact that many Han officials had Qin backgrounds. On this view, the Han did not so much reject Qin as adapt and stabilize it.

Evidence: Interpretation A relies heavily on literary sources such as the Shiji and Hanshu, and on speeches and memorials from the period that criticize Qin. Interpretation B relies more on excavated documents like the Zhangjiashan statutes and on comparison with Qin administrative records from Liye. Many current scholars adopt a position combining elements of both, recognizing that Han moderated some Qin demands and accepted a more decentralized political order, while preserving the underlying institutional framework.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they explain imperial durability. If the Han succeeded by rejecting Qin, then the lesson is about the importance of moral government. If it succeeded by adapting Qin, then the lesson is about institutional inheritance, political bargaining, and time.

Multiple Perspectives: Living Through the Han Consolidation

Note: these are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals.

A founding military veteran: A man who had fought for Liu Bang could expect rewards in the form of rank, exemptions, or land, and might hope to gain a marquisate. Yet many veterans also needed to return to farming, and their status depended on the state's willingness to honor its promises. Those who served kings might find their loyalty tested when the kings rebelled.
A regional king: A king of the Liu lineage ruled a kingdom with its own court and revenues, and viewed the founding settlement as a guarantee of his standing. From this perspective, court efforts to reduce his territory looked like a breach of trust. From the emperor's perspective, the same kings were a threat. Neither view can be dismissed, but the kings who rebelled in 154 BCE paid a heavy price.
A central administrator: An official such as Chao Cuo saw the kingdoms as a danger to the empire's unity and argued for reducing them. Officials in this position had a professional stake in central authority, but also faced personal risk, as Chao Cuo's execution shows.
A farming household: For farming families, the main concerns were harvests, taxes, and labor service. Reduced demands could ease a household's burden, but they did not remove it, and natural disasters or local officials' demands could still cause hardship. Their voices survive mostly indirectly, through the records of those who governed them.
A woman of the imperial court: Empress Lü held real power as a mother and regent, but her authority depended on her relationship to the emperor and on the support of officials. After her death, her family was destroyed and her reputation was shaped by those who survived her. Her experience shows both the opportunities and the vulnerabilities of women in the early Han court.

These perspectives are not equally powerful or equally documented. The king and the official had voices in the sources, the farmer largely did not. Recognizing that imbalance is part of historical method.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Qin Administration to Early Han Government

What Changed

  • The political structure became a hybrid of commanderies and kingdoms rather than a fully commandery-based empire
  • Fiscal and labor demands were moderated for a time, and the state avoided Qin-scale mobilization
  • A dynasty's legitimacy came to rest partly on a ruling lineage and its allies, and the succession became more institutionalized
  • The capital moved to Chang'an, the Han court developed its own ritual and political culture, and consort families became major political actors

What Stayed the Same

  • The imperial title, the commandery-and-county framework, and clerical administration
  • Much of the legal code, as excavated statutes show
  • The system of ranks and the practice of registering households
  • Dependence on smallholding farmers as the basis of taxation, labor, and military service

Who Experienced Change Most Directly?

Kings, marquises, and court officials experienced the most direct change, because their status depended on shifting political arrangements. Kings saw their powers curtailed, founding allies were removed or rewarded, and consort families rose and fell within a few decades.

Who Experienced Greater Continuity?

Local clerks and county officials experienced more continuity, because they kept doing much of the same work under the same legal and administrative framework. Farming households experienced a mix: moderation in some demands but continuity in the basic obligations of registration, taxation, labor service, and military service.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that dynasties can change while institutions persist, and that the meaning of a political transition depends on where one looks.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Civil-War Coalition to Durable Dynasty

Long-Term Causes

The Warring States legacy of bureaucratic administration and written law; the Qin unification and its institutional inheritance; and the devastation and dislocation of Qin's collapse, which left the population receptive to peace and much of the elite willing to accept a workable settlement.

Short-Term Causes

The multipolar rebellion of 209–207 BCE; the need to reward the allies whose forces had won the war; and the practical tasks of demobilization and restoring household registration.

Immediate Trigger / Context

The Chu-Han Contention of 206–202 BCE, decided by control of Guanzhong, logistics, and coalition management, left a victorious coalition that had to become a government while facing pressure from the Xiongnu on the northern frontier.

Event / Transformation

A wartime coalition became a dynasty through a series of crises: the elimination of non-Liu kings, the regency of Empress Lü, the coup of 180 BCE, the restraint of Emperor Wen, and the suppression and subsequent reorganization of the kingdoms under Emperor Jing.

Immediate Effects

A recognized ruling lineage, a stable capital at Chang'an, a more regular succession, and kingdoms whose political power was sharply reduced after 154 BCE.

Long-Term Consequences

A recovering fiscal and agricultural base and a durable institutional foundation on which later expansion, and the more elaborate government and society examined in Chapter 15, could be built.

Historical Complexity

No single cause explains Han durability. It depended on institutional inheritance from Qin, elite coalitions, regional compromise, fiscal moderation, recovery from war, the management of frontier pressure, and skillful and lucky handling of succession crises. Attributing it to one enlightened policy or to Liu Bang's supposed lessons from Qin's failure oversimplifies a process that unfolded over generations.
Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life: Recovery After Decades of War

For most people in the early Han, the effects of political change were felt through the rhythms of farming, taxation, and labor service. A typical household in the Yellow River plain grew millet and other dry crops, while households in the Huai and Yangtze regions grew rice, as earlier chapters described. Families had to rebuild after years of disruption: replanting fields, repairing houses, and, for some, returning from flight or displacement. Registration in the household records was both a burden and a form of protection, since it defined a family's obligations and also its claim to land.

Men were liable for labor service and military service, and this continued under the Han. Work on palaces, walls, roads, and canals relied on corvée, as the building of Chang'an's walls shows. Women's work in textile production, food preparation, and farm labor was fundamental to household survival and to the tax system, since cloth and grain were both forms of tax. Women also appear in the sources as laborers mobilized for state projects. The household was the basic unit of both production and taxation.

The experience varied by region and class. Families in the capital region were closest to the court and to state demands; those in the kingdoms were subject to the rules of their kings; those in remote areas might see the state mainly through occasional officials. The wealthy families of the regional courts, whose tombs at Mawangdui and elsewhere contain silk, lacquer, and books, lived very differently from tenants and poor smallholders. Health, disease, and natural disaster affected everyone, but the resources to cope with them differed. Students should avoid assuming that the elite culture visible in tombs and texts was shared by all.

Regional and Frontier Context

Guanzhong and the northwest
The Qin heartland and the site of Chang'an. Its agricultural surplus, defensible terrain, and administrative continuity made it the base of the Han state.
The eastern kingdoms (Qi, Chu, Wu, Zhao)
Regions with strong local identities and large populations, held as kingdoms. Their rulers' interests often diverged from the court's.
The Yangtze region and Changsha
A southern region of growing importance. The Changsha kingdom, ruled by a non-Liu family, illustrates that regional courts developed their own wealth and culture.
Nanyue and Lingnan
After the collapse of Qin, Zhao Tuo, a Qin official, established the kingdom of Nanyue in the far south. The Han court under Gaozu recognized him as king, and relations continued to fluctuate. Nanyue shows that the political meaning of the south was not fixed.
The northern frontier and the Xiongnu
The Xiongnu confederation, ruled by a supreme leader called the chanyu, was a formidable military power. After Han forces attacked the Xiongnu in 201–200 BCE and Gaozu's army was encircled near Pingcheng, the resulting settlement helped establish a policy of marriage alliance (heqin) and gifts, discussed further in Chapter 16. This was a diplomatic relationship between powers, not a simple case of "tribute."
The northeast and Korea
Refugees from the Yan region and the political upheaval of the period contributed to the formation of new regimes on the Korean peninsula, traditionally associated with Wiman. The dating and details are debated.

China and the Wider World

The early Han state emerged at the same time as the Xiongnu empire on the steppe, and the two powers were closely linked. The Xiongnu's rise created a powerful neighbor that the early Han could not easily defeat, and shaped Han frontier policy for decades. Trade and diplomacy accompanied conflict: silk, grain, and other goods were exchanged through frontier markets and gifts. In the south, Nanyue connected the Han world with maritime Southeast Asia, and the reach of the Han court into the Lingnan region was limited. Students should not treat these relationships as something done to China; the Han court actively negotiated, fought, and traded with its neighbors, and its choices, from marriage alliances to military restraint, were part of how the early empire consolidated. The broader Eurasian consequences of these relationships are explored in Chapter 16.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked how the Han turned the wreckage of Qin and years of civil war into an imperial order that could endure. Its central argument is that Han durability did not result from simply rejecting Qin. Instead, Liu Bang and his successors preserved and adapted much of the Qin imperial framework while changing how power, taxation, labor, regional authority, and political coalitions were managed. Chapter 13 showed that conquest was not the same as consolidation; this chapter has shown that durable consolidation required adaptation, compromise, recovery, and time.

The rebellions that began in 209 BCE were not a single revolution but a multipolar contest. Chen Sheng and Wu Guang were followed by regional kings, revived state identities, former elites, and military commanders. Liu Bang was initially one participant among many, and it was Xiang Yu who set the terms of the first postwar settlement in 206 BCE. The surrender of Ziying in 207 BCE ended Qin authority but did not resolve the question of who would control the political space Qin had unified.

The founding of the Han was a process. Liu Bang, a former local Qin official of modest but not peasant origins, accepted Qin's surrender in 207 BCE, received the kingdom of Han from Xiang Yu in 206 BCE, and became emperor in 202 BCE after defeating him. His victory in the Chu-Han Contention owed much to control of Guanzhong, the logistical work of Xiao He, the generalship of Han Xin, and the distribution of rewards to allies. These same rewards created the political problem of the early empire.

After 202 BCE, the Han reconstructed government by demobilizing soldiers, restoring household registration, establishing the court at Chang'an, and retaining and adapting Qin institutions. The Zhangjiashan statutes of about 186 BCE demonstrate extensive continuity in law across the dynastic boundary, and they allow students to test the claim that the Han rejected Qin. The imperial title, the commandery-and-county framework, the system of ranks, and clerical administration all survived.

The early Han was a hybrid order. Some territory was governed directly through commanderies, while large kingdoms were assigned to allies and to members of the Liu family. This was a political compromise made necessary by the coalition that had won the war, and it created a structural tension. Most non-Liu kings were removed within a few years, but Liu kingdoms remained, and the central question was how much regional autonomy could be allowed without recreating fragmentation.

The period after Gaozu's death in 195 BCE tested the dynasty. Empress Lü dominated the court for about fifteen years, and although later sources portray her hostilely, the evidence suggests an administration that maintained law and order while straining the settlement by promoting her relatives. After the removal of her family in 180 BCE, Emperor Wen's reign was associated with restraint and recovery, and under Emperor Jing the court moved against the kingdoms. The suppression of the Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BCE sharply reduced their political power and strengthened the imperial center.

Fiscal restraint was a form of state-building. Reduced taxes and labor demands, support for smallholders, and limited military mobilization helped a population devastated by war recover and stay on registered land. But the state maintained its records, taxes, laws, and obligations. Early Han policy moderated Qin's demands without dismantling its machinery. The later reputation of Wen and Jing as a golden age is both description and historical memory.

By 141 BCE, the Han had a recognized ruling lineage, a stable capital, an inherited and modified bureaucracy, reduced regional kingdoms, a recovering economy, and a more regular succession. The explanation for this durability is cumulative and cannot be reduced to the claim that Han rulers learned that Qin was too harsh. The dynasty operated in a crowded world that included the Xiongnu to the north and Nanyue to the south, and its relations with them shaped its choices.

Chapter 14 thus explains how the Han state became durable. Chapter 15 will examine how that durable state governed: its bureaucracy, taxation, agriculture, landholding, markets, social hierarchy, and family organization. Together the two chapters show that imperial durability depends on institutions, negotiation, and time, as well as on rulers.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an empire becomes durable. The Qin unified a vast territory quickly but failed to consolidate it. The Han inherited the same territory and much of the same machinery, and over several generations converted it into a lasting state. The difference lay less in the machinery than in how it was used, who was included in the governing coalition, how much regional power was tolerated, and how much time was allowed for recovery.

The consolidation described here shaped later Chinese history in several ways. It established Chang'an as an imperial capital and set precedents for court politics, including the role of consort families and regional kings. It showed that institutional continuity could survive dynastic change, a pattern that would recur across later transitions. And it created the stable base from which Emperor Wu would launch the expansion discussed in Chapter 16, and on which the government and society examined in Chapter 15 rested.

The chapter also connects China to wider histories. The early Han emerged alongside the Xiongnu empire, and its frontier policy was shaped by that relationship. Its southern neighbor Nanyue linked it to the wider southern world. Understanding the consolidation of the Han requires seeing it as one state among several in a shifting Asian landscape, not as the sole center of the world.

Finally, the chapter models a way of thinking about Chinese history as diverse, contested, and regionally varied. The early Han was a negotiated order, not a preordained outcome. Its institutions were inherited and adapted. Its sources are shaped by dynastic memory and can be tested against excavated evidence. Its people, from kings to farmers to empresses, experienced it very differently. Recognizing this complexity prepares students for the rest of the Han story and for the many later transitions in this textbook, none of which should be read as a simple cycle or a foregone conclusion.
