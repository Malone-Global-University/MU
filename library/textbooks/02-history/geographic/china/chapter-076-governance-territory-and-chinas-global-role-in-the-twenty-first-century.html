Part XII — Reform and Contemporary China

Governance, Territory, and China’s Global Role in the Twenty-First Century

China: A History of a Civilization · Principal chronology 2000–2026 · Contemporary cutoff: November 1, 2026

Chapter Opening

The People's Republic of China of the twenty-first century is neither a revived empire nor a simple continuation of its own Maoist past. It is a modern state whose institutions, territorial claims, and historical self-understanding were built from many earlier layers, and whose future is not written in any of them.

Each March, nearly three thousand deputies gather in the Great Hall of the People in Beijing for the annual session of the National People's Congress. Beside it meets the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference. Premier and ministers deliver reports, deputies vote, and the proceedings are broadcast. In March 2026 the session adopted the Outline of the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan for 2026-2030 on March 12, and the government set a growth target for that year of 4.5 to 5 percent, a little below the roughly 5 percent of recent years. A student watching might conclude that the legislature governs China. A student who knew only that the Communist Party leads the state might conclude that the legislature is a stage set. Neither conclusion is adequate. The scene is a doorway into the central problem of this chapter: how political authority in the People's Republic is organized, exercised, and justified, and how that authority is bound up with claims about territory, history, and national identity.

This chapter studies the People's Republic of China (PRC) from about 2000 to November 1, 2026, with the background needed to understand it. It examines the institutions of Party and state; the centralization of authority under Xi Jinping, who became General Secretary of the Communist Party in November 2012 and state president in March 2013; the role of historical memory in political legitimacy; the changing governance of Hong Kong; the unresolved question of Taiwan's status; the political and cultural questions surrounding Tibet and Xinjiang; the Belt and Road Initiative; and China's relations with its neighbors, the United States, Russia, and the wider world. It ends with the structural challenges facing the country and with the limits of using history to predict.

It matters because the questions of this chapter are questions that the whole textbook has been preparing students to ask. What is "China"? Chapter 1 argued that the term has referred to different territories, states, and communities over time. Chapters on the Qin, Han, Tang, Yuan, Ming, and Qing showed how large empires governed diverse regions through varied institutions. Chapters 51 to 57 traced foreign imperialism, rebellion, and the end of the imperial order; Chapters 58 to 65 the Republic, war, and revolution; Chapters 66 to 75 the Mao era, reform, and social transformation. Chapter 76 asks how the modern state that emerged from that sequence governs itself and relates to peoples and places that have, in different periods, been inside, outside, and at the edge of Chinese political authority.

Much has changed since 2000. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and became the second-largest national economy. Its leaders moved from the collective, term-limited style of the Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao eras toward a more centralized leadership around Xi. Hong Kong's promised autonomy was reshaped by confrontation and legislation. Taiwan, a democracy with its own government, became one of the most closely watched flashpoints in world politics. Xinjiang and Tibet became the subjects of intense domestic security policy and international controversy. The Belt and Road Initiative, announced in 2013, linked Chinese finance and construction to dozens of countries. Relations with the United States shifted from deepening interdependence toward competition, punctuated by trade truces. Other things continued: Party leadership of the state, the importance of the bureaucracy and local government, the claim to represent a historically continuous civilization, and the tension between central direction and local reality.

The people affected include some 1.4 billion citizens of the PRC, the residents of Hong Kong and Macao, the twenty-three million people of Taiwan, Tibetans, Uyghurs and other Muslim communities in Xinjiang, migrant workers, officials, entrepreneurs, students, and the populations of dozens of countries where Chinese companies and banks operate. They did not experience these decades in the same way, and their views of them differ.

The evidence is unusually varied and unusually contested. There are constitutions and statutes, Party congress reports, white papers, official statistics, and speeches. There are court rulings, such as the 2016 arbitral award on the South China Sea; assessments by United Nations bodies; leaked documents; satellite imagery; interviews with exiles and residents; scholarly field research where access allowed it; and the records of other governments. These sources have unequal evidentiary status. A government statement establishes what that government asserted, not automatically that its claims are true; an allegation is not a judicial finding; a projection is not an outcome. Throughout the chapter, the same source-critical standards are applied to Chinese, Taiwanese, American, European, and international actors alike.

Finally, this chapter closes the textbook. It does not argue that the PRC is a dynasty in modern dress, nor that the past dictates the future. It asks students to distinguish real continuities, such as a tradition of bureaucratic government and a claim to historical depth, from institutions that are distinctly modern: a Leninist party-state, a constitution, a nation-state system of sovereignty, and a global economy. The last section returns to the textbook as a whole and asks what historical patterns can, and cannot, help us understand about China today.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Communist Party, the formal institutions of the Chinese state, and the People's Liberation Army, and explain how they overlap in practice.
  2. Trace the development of Chinese leadership from the Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao periods to the centralization of authority under Xi Jinping, and describe precisely what the 2018 constitutional amendment changed.
  3. Analyze how historical memory, patriotic education, and narratives of national rejuvenation have been used in contemporary political legitimacy.
  4. Distinguish the 1997 Basic Law framework, the 2014 Umbrella Movement, the 2019 protests, the 2020 National Security Law, and the 2024 Article 23 ordinance in Hong Kong.
  5. Explain the historical foundations of the Taiwan question, distinguishing sovereignty claims, governing institutions, and the differing one-China formulations.
  6. Compare the distinct histories of Tibet and Xinjiang and evaluate official statements against independent assessments, recognizing different evidentiary status.
  7. Explain the origins, objectives, financing, and contested outcomes of the Belt and Road Initiative.
  8. Assess China's relations with Asia, the United States, Russia, and global institutions as combinations of cooperation, interdependence, and competition.
  9. Interpret the economic, demographic, environmental, and technological challenges of the 2020s and the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan as plans, not outcomes.
  10. Evaluate claims that Chinese history reveals patterns predicting the future, and contextualize the modern PRC within the longer history of Chinese states.

Historical Context

By 2000 the People's Republic had passed through a succession of political eras described in earlier chapters. The Maoist state (Chapters 66 to 70) created a Leninist party-state, collectivized agriculture and industry, and endured the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. After 1978 (Chapters 71 and 72) Deng Xiaoping and his colleagues reoriented policy toward growth, markets, and foreign investment while retaining Party rule. The events of 1989 (Chapter 73) shaped how later leaders thought about stability, and Deng's Southern Tour in 1992 (Chapter 74) accelerated market reform. By the turn of the century, China had absorbed Hong Kong (1997) and Macao (1999), was preparing to enter the World Trade Organization, and was urbanizing and aging at a pace described in Chapter 75.

The geography of the state is itself a historical product. The PRC's territory corresponds closely to that of the late Qing empire (Chapters 46 to 48), with major exceptions: Mongolia, which became an independent state with Soviet backing in the twentieth century, and Taiwan, which Japan ruled from 1895 to 1945 and which the Republic of China has governed since the late 1940s. The Qing had governed its Inner Asian regions through institutions distinct from those of the provinces. The Republic and then the PRC reorganized those relationships under the language of the modern nation-state, citizenship, and sovereignty. Whether the incorporation of each frontier region is better described as inheritance, integration, or imposition is not a single historical question; it is a different question for Tibet, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and Taiwan, and the answers are contested.

The state's institutions have two roots. One is the Leninist model adopted by the Chinese Communist Party in the 1920s: a disciplined party that leads the state, the army, and mass organizations. The other is a long administrative tradition: a bureaucracy recruited by merit, hierarchies from center to county, and a practice of governing a continental society through local officials with considerable discretion. These roots should not be fused into a claim that the PRC is "traditional." Nonetheless, students who have followed this book will recognize recurring problems: how a center controls its agents, how fiscal resources are shared, how diverse regions are integrated, and how rulers legitimate themselves.

The international setting changed profoundly. The Soviet Union disappeared in 1991, leaving the United States as the dominant power and China as a rising economy. China's entry into global supply chains, described in Chapter 74, produced prosperity and new vulnerabilities. After the global financial crisis of 2008, Chinese policymakers and many observers concluded that China's relative position had improved; after 2012 foreign policy became more assertive and more ambitious. By the 2020s, a world of tariffs, export controls, regional security disputes, and competing visions of international order had replaced the assumptions of the 1990s.

This chapter relies on public sources up to November 1, 2026. Events after that date, including the outcomes of scheduled elections and negotiations, are outside its scope. Where recent developments are reported, they are dated and attributed, because contemporary history is revised as archives open and scholarship matures.

Division I — Communist Party Governance and the Structure of the State

This division explains how the political system operates before turning to the changes of the Xi era. The central analytic distinction is between the Communist Party and the government of the People's Republic. They are different organizations with different charters and personnel systems, yet they are interwoven at every level.

76.1 Governance from Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao to the Twenty-First Century

The leadership that guided China into the new century was shaped by the reform era and the aftermath of 1989. Jiang Zemin served as General Secretary from 1989 and state president from 1993; the premier, Zhu Rongji (1998–2003), presided over restructuring of state enterprises and the negotiations for WTO entry. Jiang's "Three Represents" formula, promoted around 2000–2002, opened Party membership to private entrepreneurs, redefining the Party as representative of advanced productive forces and not solely of workers and peasants. Hu Jintao succeeded him as General Secretary in November 2002 and as president in March 2003, with Wen Jiabao as premier. Their slogans, "Scientific Outlook on Development" and "harmonious society," reflected concern about inequality, regional gaps, and environmental costs.

The Jiang–Hu years are often characterized as an era of collective leadership: a Politburo Standing Committee of seven to nine members, informal retirement norms, and a leadership transition after two five-year terms. Scholars debate how far this was ever an institutionalized system, and how far it reflected the particular balance among leaders who owed their positions to Deng Xiaoping. What is clear is that, during these years, the government achieved extraordinary growth, joined the global economy, and dealt with crises such as SARS in 2003, the Wenchuan earthquake of May 2008, and the global financial crisis, which prompted a large stimulus program. It also left unresolved problems: local debt, corruption, environmental damage, and weak social insurance.

Governance in this period was not politically static. The state tightened and loosened control over media and civil society in cycles. Legal reform progressed in commercial law while political activism faced limits, as in the case of Liu Xiaobo, a writer sentenced in 2009 after co-authoring the manifesto Charter 08 and awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2010. The inheritance that Xi received in 2012 therefore included both a successful growth model and widely shared official concern that corruption, factionalism, and fiscal and ecological strain threatened the Party's authority.

Leadership alone does not explain the period. The Jiang–Zhu years of the late 1990s restructured the state sector: tens of millions of workers were laid off or moved off enterprise payrolls, housing was privatized, and the government negotiated entry into the WTO partly to lock in domestic reform against opposition. The Western Development campaign (launched in 2000) and the Northeast Revitalization program (2003) tried to address regional gaps; the abolition of the agricultural tax in 2006, the expansion of rural medical insurance, and the Labor Contract Law (effective 2008) responded to rural and labor grievances. These measures were uneven in effect, but they show that the "harmonious society" language reflected real policy concern and not only rhetoric.

Citizens' experiences of the state changed in this period in ways that mattered for later politics. In 2003 the death in custody of a young migrant, Sun Zhigang, detained for lacking a temporary residence permit, provoked public outrage online and in the press and led the State Council to abolish the custody-and-repatriation system. The episode is often cited as an example of how public opinion, lawyers, and journalists could influence policy within limits. The same decade saw a rise in local protests over land seizures, wages, and pollution, which official sources counted in the tens of thousands per year until the government stopped publishing the figures.

Scholars interpreted the Jiang–Hu system differently. Some, following Andrew Nathan's concept of "authoritarian resilience," argued that institutionalized succession, meritocratic recruitment, and responsive policy made the Party adaptable. Others pointed to the weakness of the rule of law, corruption, and the inability to reform entrenched interests. The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence available, mostly official documents, elite memoirs, and field studies, supports both partial adaptation and persistent weaknesses. This chapter treats the period as the immediate institutional background for the Xi era, not as a golden age or a failure.

76.2 The Communist Party: Congresses, Committees, and Leadership Hierarchies

The Party's constitution describes a pyramid. The National Congress, nominally the highest body, meets once every five years for about a week; delegates number in the thousands and endorse a political report, amendments to the Party constitution, and a new Central Committee. The Central Committee, elected in 2022 with 205 full members and 171 alternates (376 in all), meets in plenary sessions, usually annually, and elects the Politburo of twenty-four members, within which the Politburo Standing Committee (seven members since 2012) is the inner core. The General Secretary presides over the Politburo and its Standing Committee and heads the Secretariat. Beneath this structure, Party committees exist at provincial, municipal, county, township, and village levels, and Party organizations are established in state agencies, universities, state enterprises, and increasingly in private firms. Party membership approached one hundred million in the mid-2020s according to Party statistics, though members' activism and influence vary enormously.

The governing principle is "democratic centralism": discussion followed by binding decisions that subordinate lower organs to higher ones. In practice, the relationship between formal rules and political practice has varied with the leaders and the period. Retirement conventions, such as an informal age limit for the Standing Committee, were widely observed in some periods; the Party's formal rules also contain provisions on collective leadership and prohibitions on personality cults. Evidence about how decisions are actually made is limited, because deliberations are secret. Students should therefore treat accounts of internal "factions" or "power struggles" as inferences from published outcomes, leaks, and interviews, not as documented facts.

Historical Evidence

Reading the Party-State Structure

The formal structure is documented in the Party and state constitutions, statutes, official organizational charts, and personnel announcements. Other kinds of evidence include official media, speeches, internal Party regulations published after adoption, memoirs, and scholarly interviews. These sources show titles and rules reasonably well but reveal little of informal bargaining. Researchers also use personnel data, such as career paths of provincial leaders, to test hypotheses about promotion. Each approach has limits: published rules may not describe practice, memoirs are selective, and data on appointments cannot reveal motives.

Several Party organs matter more than their titles suggest. The Organization Department manages personnel; the Propaganda Department supervises media, education, and cultural policy; the United Front Work Department manages relations with non-Party groups, religious organizations, ethnic minorities, and overseas Chinese; the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI) enforces Party discipline; and the Political-Legal Commission coordinates the courts, procuratorates, and police. The General Office manages the flow of documents to the top leadership. The National Congress meets every five years: the Nineteenth in 2017, the Twentieth in 2022, and the Twenty-First is scheduled, according to the Fifth Plenum communique reported in October 2026, for the second half of 2027.

The Central Committee is not a fixed body. Members elected in 2022 have since been removed, have died, or have been expelled, and under Party rules alternate members fill vacancies in order of votes received. At the Fifth Plenum (October 26–29, 2026), the official communiqué recorded 162 full members and 117 alternate members in attendance, 279 attendees in those categories, and listed ten alternates promoted to full membership. The attendance figure is lower than the original 376 full and alternate members elected in 2022, but the difference cannot by itself be treated as a count of expulsions or vacancies: absence, changes in membership, and discipline cases are distinct matters. The plenum confirmed 33 expulsions and removed another official from his Party position. Students should treat any statement about the Central Committee, Politburo, or Central Military Commission as dated and distinguish the official record from outside analysis.

Local Party organization is where most citizens meet the Party. A county Party secretary generally outranks the county head; a village Party branch secretary may also serve as the village committee head, a practice that official policy has encouraged in many places. Party cells in universities, hospitals, and state firms shape appointments and decisions, and since the 2010s regulations have strengthened Party groups in private companies and foreign-invested firms, though how much they influence business decisions varies. Party membership itself is a mix of motives: some join for ideological commitment, others for career advantage, and surveys of members show varied levels of engagement. This variety is a reason to avoid describing the Party as a monolith, even while recognizing its disciplined structure.

76.3 State Institutions: The National People's Congress, State Council, and Courts

The state has its own architecture. Under the constitution, the National People's Congress, with nearly three thousand deputies indirectly elected, is the highest organ of state power. It meets annually, usually in March; its Standing Committee, of a couple of hundred members, meets roughly every two months and enacts most legislation. The NPC elects the president and vice-president, the chairman of the state Central Military Commission, and the heads of the Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate; under Article 62 it decides on the choice of the premier upon the president's nomination, and then approves the other State Council members whom the premier nominates. The State Council, led by the premier, is the executive: ministries and commissions administer finance, education, industry, public security, and other fields. Local people's congresses and governments exist at each level below.

The courts operate within a system in which the Party's Political-Legal Commission supervises judicial, prosecutorial, and security agencies. The legal system expanded greatly after 1978: a Civil Code took effect on January 1, 2021, and administrative litigation, which allows citizens to sue officials, was expanded by the Administrative Litigation Law, amended in 2014. At the same time, the courts do not exercise independent constitutional review, and the constitution is not directly applied by courts to invalidate laws. The Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference brings together representatives of the eight non-Communist "democratic parties," social groups, and others; it consults but does not legislate.

Historical Vocabulary
Party, State, and Constitution

Party-state describes a system in which the ruling party leads the institutions of the state and the armed forces. In the PRC the Party and the state have separate institutions, with the Party holding leading political authority. The constitution is the state's fundamental law, adopted in 1982 and amended several times, including in 1988, 1993, 1999, 2004, and 2018. Its text describes institutions and rights; the 2018 amendment inserted a statement of Party leadership into Article 1. Historians therefore read it for formal structure, for the ideological claims of each period, and for what it reveals about institutional change, while noting that constitutional provisions are enforced through political and administrative channels more than through courts.

Primary Source Window

The PRC Constitution and Its 2018 Amendments

Context: The constitution was adopted in December 1982 and amended in 2018 at the first session of the Thirteenth NPC, in March. Source type: Constitutional text, official English translation published by the Ministry of Justice.

Paraphrase and brief excerpt: Article 1 describes the PRC as a socialist state under the people's democratic dictatorship. The 2018 amendment added a sentence stating that Party leadership is the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics, and the preamble incorporated Xi Jinping Thought. Article 79 had stipulated that the president and vice-president serve no more than two consecutive terms; that restriction was deleted. The amendments also created the National Supervisory Commission as a state organ.

Analysis: The text shows what leaders chose to formalize and when. It does not show how decisions are actually made, who drafted the amendments, or how citizens understood them. The amended Article 79 changed a state-office restriction; the constitution does not contain a two-term limit on the Party General Secretary, who is selected under Party rules.

The legislature's role is real but specific. Deputies to the NPC, who serve part-time and are mostly Party members, include officials, business people, entertainers, workers, and representatives of ethnic groups; the Standing Committee and its Legislative Affairs Commission draft much legislation. The NPC passes the budget and approves the government work report, usually by large majorities with a small number of dissenting votes, which has led many observers to call it a "rubber stamp." Scholars who study it argue that this undersells the extent of bargaining before bills reach the floor: draft laws are circulated for public comment, ministries and local governments lobby, and controversial provisions are sometimes withdrawn or delayed. At the same time, the NPC does not check the Party leadership. The accurate description is that it is a legislature inside a party-led system, with influence at the level of detail and implementation, not at the level of fundamental political direction.

The state executive is large. The State Council directs ministries such as Finance, Commerce, and Education, commissions such as the National Development and Reform Commission, and bodies including the People's Bank of China, which operate under the premier. The Party's 2018 and 2023 reforms changed the arrangement: some functions moved to Party organs or merged into new agencies, narrowing the autonomy of the State Council. Premiers have also changed in prominence: Zhu Rongji and Wen Jiabao were closely identified with economic policy, while Li Qiang, premier since March 2023, operates in a system where the Party's Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, led by Xi, exercises leading direction.

The courts have four levels, with the Supreme People's Court at the top, and decide very large numbers of cases, mostly civil and commercial disputes among individuals and firms. Reforms after 2014 aimed to reduce local interference by establishing circuit courts, changing case-registration procedures so that courts must accept eligible cases, and requiring judges to answer for their decisions. In politically sensitive cases, courts operate under Party leadership, and in the 1982 constitution judicial independence is defined as independence from administrative organs, not from the Party. Since 2018 the National Supervisory Commission, an organ with status equal to the courts, has investigated corruption and abuse of power by public employees, sometimes described as a fourth branch. The president's constitutional position is that of head of state; since 1993 the same person has usually held the posts of General Secretary, president, and Central Military Commission chairman, a "three-in-one" arrangement that Xi has continued.

76.4 The Party, the People's Liberation Army, and the Central Military Commission

The PLA is the armed force of the Party and the state, not a neutral national army in the Western sense. Since the Gutian Conference of 1929 the Party has insisted on "the Party commands the gun," exercised through political commissars, Party committees in units, and the Party's Central Military Commission. A state Central Military Commission exists under the constitution, but its membership is in practice identical to the Party body, a formula often summarized as "one institution, two names." The CMC chairman is therefore the commander-in-chief, and the same person has held the offices of General Secretary and CMC chairman since 2012.

Military modernization since the 1990s, funded by economic growth, has included a larger navy, an expanded missile force, space and cyber capabilities, and in 2015–2016 a major reorganization into five theater commands and new services. Discipline and anti-corruption inside the armed forces became a defining feature of the Xi era, and the purges of 2023–2026, described below, reached the highest ranks.

The PLA's modernization has several strands. A 2015–2016 reorganization created five theater commands in place of the earlier military regions, a Joint Staff Department, and new service branches; in April 2024 the Strategic Support Force was reorganized into separate Aerospace, Cyberspace, and Information Support Forces. Official defense budgets have risen in most years by single-digit percentages, and outside analysts argue that actual spending is higher than the published figure. A policy of "military-civil fusion" links defense and civilian industry. The Party has named 2027, the centenary of the PLA's founding on August 1, as a milestone for building capabilities; analysts differ over whether this date signals a specific plan for operations, since public evidence is limited to statements about institutional goals and to reporting about internal instructions.

Party control of the army has been sharpened by recurring discipline campaigns. From 2023, the leadership of the Rocket Force was removed; Defense Minister Li Shangfu was dismissed in October 2023 and later expelled from the Party. In 2025, He Weidong, a vice chairman of the Central Military Commission, and the political work director Miao Hua were expelled. In January 2026, reports indicated that Zhang Youxia, the senior vice chairman and a veteran with combat experience, was under investigation; the Politburo expelled him in September 2026, and the Fifth Plenum confirmed the decision on October 29 along with that of Liu Zhenli, the chief of the Joint Staff, and other officials, as part of a package of 33 expulsions. The Party said that these officials had committed serious violations of discipline and law. Zhang Shengmin, who had led the military's discipline inspection commission and became a CMC vice chairman in October 2025, was elected to the Politburo. As reported through the cutoff, the CMC was reduced to Xi and Zhang Shengmin.

How to interpret this is an open question for historians. The official explanation emphasizes corruption in procurement and promotions and a need to strengthen Party discipline. Analysts have also pointed to the purges as an expression of the leader's concern over loyalty and over the reliability of the Rocket Force and procurement systems; some argue that the turnover weakens near-term command effectiveness, others that it removes obstacles to readiness. The evidence is thin: the investigations are secret, and information comes through official announcements and leaks. This textbook therefore records the events and the official rationale and does not extrapolate confidently about readiness or intentions.

76.5 Cadre Management, Local Government, and Central–Local Relations

The Party controls personnel through the nomenklatura system, under which the Organization Department of the Central Committee manages appointments to senior posts, and corresponding departments manage lower levels. Officials, called cadres, are promoted according to evaluations that, from the 1980s, increasingly stressed economic growth, and in later periods added targets for social stability, poverty reduction, environmental protection, and Party discipline. Local officials thus had strong incentives to meet targets, sometimes by mobilizing land sales, borrowing through local government financing vehicles, or reporting favorable statistics.

China is formally a unitary state, but it governs through a multilevel hierarchy of provinces, prefecture-level cities, counties, and townships. Fiscal reforms of 1994 centralized revenue while leaving local governments responsible for large expenditures. This produced the gap between obligations and resources that local governments filled with land finance and debt, a theme in Section 76.49. The result is that central policy is implemented unevenly: sometimes vigorously, sometimes symbolically, sometimes with local adaptation, and sometimes with evasion. This should remind students not to imagine that all local decisions are made in Beijing.

Cause and Effect

Party Leadership and Central–Local Implementation

Causes

Central leaders set priorities; local officials are promoted on measurable targets; fiscal arrangements leave local governments with heavy expenditure responsibilities; information flows upward through the same hierarchy that is evaluated.

Event or transformation

Policies are announced centrally, then interpreted and implemented by provincial, city, county, and township governments with differing resources and incentives.

Effects

Rapid mobilization is possible where goals are clear and resources available. Overreach, selective implementation, statistical distortion, and local debt are also documented. Campaigns can correct some problems and create others.

Historical complexity

The same system produced high-speed infrastructure and costly misallocation. It cannot be reduced to either efficiency or dysfunction; evidence differs by sector, province, and period.

The scale of the system is large. The mainland has thirty-one provincial-level units (twenty-two provinces, five autonomous regions, and four directly governed municipalities), around three hundred prefecture-level units, roughly two thousand eight hundred counties and county-level cities, tens of thousands of townships, and several hundred thousand villages, with Hong Kong and Macao governed as special administrative regions. Each level has a Party committee and a government. This many-layered system means that most citizens' interactions with the state are with township and county officials.

Observers have described the incentives in several ways. In a "target responsibility system," superiors set quantitative goals, and some are "veto" targets that cancel an official's other achievements if missed, such as maintaining social stability, enforcing family planning (in the past), or meeting environmental goals. Researchers such as Li-An Zhou have argued that promotion "tournaments" based on growth rates encouraged competition; later studies qualify this, finding that personal ties and other factors also matter. Sebastian Heilmann described a pattern of "experimentation under hierarchy," in which local pilots, such as the Special Economic Zones, are tested and, if successful, scaled up nationally. Together these accounts suggest a system that combines central goal-setting with local discretion.

The 1994 tax-sharing reform gave the central government a larger share of revenue while leaving local governments responsible for education, health, and infrastructure. To bridge the gap, local governments relied on land-lease sales and on borrowing through local government financing vehicles, which became a source of risk in the 2010s. Local administrations also vary in capacity: coastal Guangdong and Zhejiang have large tax bases, while western and northeastern provinces depend heavily on central transfers. The result is that the same central policy, whether on environmental inspection, poverty reduction, or Covid control, produced different outcomes in different places. Ordinary people experience this variation directly, through the quality of schools, hospitals, and local officials, which is why scholars of China stress that "the government" is many governments.

76.6 Law, Political Participation, Consultation, and Institutional Accountability

Citizens engage with the state through channels that are limited but real. Villagers elect village committees under a 1998 organic law, although Party branches dominate and the quality of elections varies. People's congress elections at the lowest levels are direct, with higher levels chosen indirectly; candidates are vetted. Citizens petition through the letters-and-visits system (xinfang), file administrative lawsuits, post online complaints that sometimes provoke official responses, and take part in local protests over land, wages, pollution, and housing. Scholars have documented that officials sometimes respond to such pressure, including through local policy change, while the right to organize independent parties, unions, and large-scale civil society organizations is restricted.

The Party also created supervisory mechanisms, including discipline inspection commissions and, since 2018, the National Supervisory Commission, which investigates public officials. These bodies are accountable to the Party and not to independent courts or competitive elections. Lawyers who advocated for rights faced detention in the "709" crackdown that began in July 2015. Understanding accountability in the PRC thus means attention to several channels at once: hierarchical discipline, legal procedures, consultation, public pressure, and the limits on political competition.

Courts and lawyers have become more prominent as the economy has become more complex. Chinese citizens file very large numbers of civil cases; contracts, property rights, and consumer rights are increasingly litigated. Administrative litigation sometimes succeeds against local governments, and legal aid and public-interest litigation developed since the 1990s. The space for rights-oriented legal advocacy, however, narrowed after 2015, when authorities detained or questioned hundreds of lawyers and activists in the "709" crackdown. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions is the only legal national union organization, and independent labor organizing is restricted, though strikes and wage protests occur, especially in manufacturing and construction.

Official language describes the system as "whole-process people's democracy," a slogan promoted from about 2019–2021. It emphasizes consultation, legislative outreach, and public participation in local decision-making. Proponents say that these processes give citizens influence on policy; critics say that they cannot substitute for competitive elections and independent organizations. Empirical studies of public attitudes, such as long-running surveys by academic researchers, have found relatively high reported satisfaction with the central government and lower satisfaction with local officials, though respondents may be cautious in answering sensitive questions, and results depend on survey design.

For students, the key point is the difference between participation and political competition. Citizens participate in many forms of local governance and press the state for services and fairness, and officials are responsive in some ways. The eight non-Communist parties in the CPPCC accept Party leadership, and no organized opposition party is allowed. Hierarchical supervision, consultation, and legal channels therefore coexist with the absence of independent competition for power.

Division II — Xi Jinping and Political Centralization, 2012–2026

Under Xi, the Party leadership tightened its direct supervision of the bureaucracy, ideology, the economy, security, and society. This division distinguishes three developments that are often blended: stronger personal authority for the leader, greater institutional authority for Party organs, and ideological change.

76.7 Xi Jinping's Rise to Leadership in 2012–2013

Xi Jinping was born in 1953, the son of Xi Zhongxun, a revolutionary veteran who was purged in 1962 and rehabilitated after Mao's death. Xi was sent to the countryside during the Cultural Revolution, joined the Party in 1974, and held provincial posts in Fujian, Zhejiang, and Shanghai before joining the Politburo Standing Committee in 2007. At the Eighteenth Party Congress he became General Secretary and chairman of the Party's Central Military Commission on November 15, 2012, and on March 14, 2013, state president.

The succession occurred amid the Bo Xilai scandal, in which a Politburo member and Chongqing Party secretary was removed in 2012 and later convicted. Official explanations emphasized corruption and abuse of power; scholars have debated how far the case also reflected political rivalry. Xi's early actions, including the "eight-point regulation" on official frugality in December 2012 and a high-profile discipline campaign, signaled a break with the perceived laxity of the previous decade.

Xi's biography is part of the political context but should be read carefully. His father, Xi Zhongxun, had been a vice premier before his purge in 1962; Xi spent seven years in the village of Liangjiahe, Shaanxi, joined the Party in 1974 after repeated applications, and studied chemical engineering at Tsinghua University as a "worker-peasant-soldier" student. His postings in Fujian, Zhejiang, and Shanghai, and his role as vice president from 2008, gave him experience in provincial administration, and he was seen as a candidate acceptable to different groups in the Party. These facts explain why he was selected; they do not by themselves explain later developments.

The Bo Xilai affair of 2012 exposed tensions at the top. In February, Chongqing's police chief Wang Lijun entered the US consulate in Chengdu; Bo's wife Gu Kailai was convicted of murdering a British businessman, Neil Heywood; and Bo was convicted of bribery, embezzlement, and abuse of power in 2013 and sentenced to life imprisonment. Accounts by scholars and journalists differ about how far these events were driven by personal corruption and how far by political rivalry. In the immediate aftermath, Xi in December 2012 visited Shenzhen in a trip evoking Deng Xiaoping's Southern Tour (Chapter 74), signaling continuity with reform. In 2013, an internal Party circular known as Document No. 9 warned against "Western constitutional democracy," universal values, and other trends, which reveals the ideological direction of the new leadership.

76.8 The Anti-Corruption Campaign and Party Discipline

The anti-corruption campaign, led initially by Wang Qishan at the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI), targeted both "tigers" (senior officials) and "flies" (local cadres). High-ranking cases included Zhou Yongkang, a former Standing Committee member responsible for security, and senior military leaders such as Xu Caihou and Guo Boxiong. Party announcements report that millions of officials were disciplined over the following decade. In 2018 the National Supervisory Commission broadened investigative power to cover all public employees.

Interpretations differ. Supporters inside and outside China point to wide public approval, reduced extravagance, and genuine corruption in procurement, land, and the military. Critics argue that enforcement is selective, directed by the Party and not by independent courts, and that it consolidated the leader's control by removing rivals. Many scholars hold that both elements can be true: that corruption was serious and that investigations also served political ends. Because evidence of individual cases is mostly limited to official findings, outside observers usually cannot independently verify the details.

The campaign had institutional features. The 2018 Supervision Law created a form of detention, liuzhi, under the National Supervisory Commission, which replaced the Party's earlier extralegal practice of shuanggui; critics said that it still lacked access to lawyers and judicial review, while officials said that it provided legal procedures. The "Sky Net" and "Fox Hunt" operations pursued fugitives abroad, with some governments and rights groups objecting to pressure on individuals living overseas. High-ranking cases included Zhou Yongkang, sentenced to life in 2015; Sun Zhengcai, a former Politburo member and potential successor, sentenced in 2018; and many officials in finance, energy, and the military.

Public opinion research suggests that the campaign was popular, particularly among citizens who had experienced petty corruption; it also affected behavior, including a decline in conspicuous banquets and gifts under the "eight-point regulation." Skeptics ask whether the campaign dealt with root causes, such as official discretion over land, credit, and licenses, or whether it substituted continuous enforcement for institutional reform. Scholars also argue that the campaign changed official behavior in ways that reduced initiative: some officials, fearing investigation, preferred inaction, a pattern sometimes called "lying flat" among cadres.

In 2026 the campaign remained a central feature of politics. At the Fifth Plenum (October 26–29, 2026), the Central Committee approved reports by the CCDI and the CMC on "serious violations of discipline and law" and confirmed the expulsion of 33 officials, among them former CMC vice chairman Zhang Youxia, former Politburo member Ma Xingrui, who had earlier served as Xinjiang's Party secretary, and Liu Zhenli; it also removed Li Yunze, the former head of the financial regulator, from a Party position. The communique described a strategy of ensuring that officials lack the audacity, opportunity, or desire to commit corruption. Interpretations vary: supporters see persistent enforcement against corruption; critics see a campaign that also reinforces loyalty and removes potential rivals; and many scholars argue that these purposes may coexist. Because the cases are not subject to public trial or independent verification, the details remain largely unknown.

76.9 Party-State Restructuring and the Expansion of Central Leadership

Xi expanded Party commissions and leading small groups on reform, finance and economics, cyberspace, national security, and foreign affairs, and often headed them himself. In March 2018 the Party's Plan for Deepening the Reform of Party and State Institutions merged and rearranged bodies: the Party took over functions of state media and religious and ethnic affairs through the United Front Work Department, created the National Supervisory Commission, and reorganized regulatory agencies. A further restructuring in 2023 created a Central Financial Commission and a Central Science and Technology Commission and consolidated financial regulation.

The effect has been to place more policy areas under direct Party leadership. This changes the relationship between Party supervision and ministerial expertise. Defenders argue that it improves coordination and breaks bureaucratic silos; critics and some scholars note that expertise and local initiative can be sidelined. The long-term consequences remain open to scholarly debate, since the reorganizations are recent.

The reorganizations changed institutional hierarchy in several ways. In 2013 a Central National Security Commission was created; in 2018 the Party's "leading small groups" on reform, foreign affairs, finance, and cyberspace were upgraded to commissions, with Party offices embedded in state bodies. The reforms created a Ministry of Natural Resources, a Ministry of Emergency Management, a State Administration for Market Regulation, a Ministry of Veterans Affairs, and a combined banking and insurance regulator, and placed the Coast Guard under the People's Armed Police and thus under the Central Military Commission, with consequences for maritime enforcement. A slogan associated with the period stated that in "Party, government, military, civilian, and academic" areas, "the Party leads everything."

The 2023 changes created a Central Financial Commission and a Central Financial Work Commission, a Central Science and Technology Commission, a National Financial Regulatory Administration, and a National Data Administration. In effect, areas that had been delegated to technocratic agencies were more closely supervised by Party bodies. Supporters argue that this removes overlap and aligns policy; critics and some scholars suggest that it can reduce specialized autonomy, and that officials may prioritize political signals over technical advice. Evidence is still limited, since the changes are recent and their effects overlap with economic and external shocks.

76.10 Xi Jinping Thought, Party Ideology, and National Rejuvenation

At the Nineteenth Party Congress in October 2017, the Party constitution incorporated "Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era." The ideology presents China as having entered a "new era" in which the principal social contradiction is between unbalanced and inadequate development and people's growing needs for a better life. Its signature themes include "national rejuvenation" and the "Chinese Dream," "common prosperity," comprehensive Party leadership, "security" as a pervasive concern, and "self-reliance" in technology. Party schooling, study sessions, and official media promote it. In November 2021 the Sixth Plenum adopted a third "historical resolution," following those of 1945 and 1981, which summarized Party history and placed Xi's leadership at the center of the current era.

Xi-era ideology includes new concepts introduced for particular audiences. "Chinese-style modernization," emphasized from 2022, presents a model of development that differs from Western pathways in its large population, "common prosperity," material and cultural advancement, harmony between humanity and nature, and peaceful development. "New quality productive forces," introduced in 2023, link technology to growth. Xi Jinping Thought entered school curricula in 2021.

"Common prosperity," emphasized in 2021, was accompanied by regulatory campaigns against large technology firms (including the suspension of Ant Group's public offering in November 2020 and rules on platform companies), a ban on for-profit tutoring in July 2021, and new limits on the real estate sector. Analysts have debated whether these actions reflected ideology, concern about financial risk and inequality, or political control over private capital, and whether they harmed business confidence; later policy statements stressed support for the private economy. Scholars also differ on how much officials and citizens believe the ideology; evidence suggests a range of acceptance, performance, and indifference.

76.11 The 2018 Constitutional Amendment and Leadership Tenure

In March 2018 the Thirteenth NPC approved amendments that, among other changes, removed the constitutional provision limiting the president and vice-president to two consecutive terms. The amendment did not create or abolish a comparable written term limit for the General Secretary or the CMC chairman, since the constitution had not imposed one on those offices. However, the informal norm of ten-year tenure and orderly succession in the Party had been important to the post-Deng arrangement. At the Twentieth Party Congress in October 2022, Xi was re-elected General Secretary for a third term, the Standing Committee was composed largely of his associates, and in March 2023 the NPC elected him to a third presidential term.

Historians therefore distinguish: (1) the legal change to state offices; (2) the breaking of informal norms on Party tenure and succession; and (3) political consequences, such as whether a clear successor emerged. Reactions varied: some Chinese commentators argued that unified leadership would help meet national challenges; others, inside and outside China, saw weakening of institutional restraints. Online discussion of the amendment was restricted, so public reaction is difficult to measure.

The constitutional change was accompanied by informal breaks. At the Nineteenth Congress in 2017 no obvious successor was placed in the Standing Committee, unlike the practice of 2007 and 2012 when a successor emerged five years in advance. At the Twentieth Congress, the Standing Committee consisted of Xi, Li Qiang, Zhao Leji, Wang Huning, Cai Qi, Ding Xuexiang, and Li Xi, a leadership closely associated with Xi, and the informal retirement age of sixty-eight, observed for others, was not applied to him; Premier Li Keqiang and Vice Premier Wang Yang left the leadership body. Observers differ about whether this signals the end of the post-Deng pattern of collective leadership or a variation within it.

The question of leadership after the 2027 Congress has become central. Reports on the Fifth Plenum indicated that the Twenty-First Congress would convene in the second half of 2027, where decisions about leadership are expected. The chapter does not predict the outcome. Historians should distinguish between observed facts, such as the 2018 amendment and the 2022 and 2023 appointments, and speculation about future arrangements, which depends on sources such as leaks and analysis of personnel patterns and cannot be verified until decisions are announced.

76.12 National Security, Information Governance, and Political Control

In 2014 Xi announced a "holistic" concept of national security embracing political, military, economic, cultural, technological, and other fields. New laws followed: the National Security Law (2015), the Counter-Terrorism Law and the Foreign NGO Management Law (2016), the Cybersecurity Law (2017), the Data Security Law and the Personal Information Protection Law (2021), a revised Counter-Espionage Law (2023), and a Foreign Relations Law (2023). Digital technologies enable large-scale monitoring and an extensive online censorship system, as well as services such as mobile payments and e-government. Many scholars emphasize that the same infrastructure serves convenience, commerce, and control.

The legal frameworks also respond to real concerns: data protection, platform power, cyber risk, and foreign pressure. Scholars disagree about whether the new laws primarily protect citizens' data from companies, protect the state from external threats, or expand the state's reach into private life; the laws can serve all three purposes, and enforcement differs by case. Evidence about surveillance often comes from official procurement documents, company records, leaks, and journalism, each with limits.

The system of information control includes legal regulation, platform responsibility, technical filtering (the "Great Firewall"), and the management of public opinion by propaganda authorities. The Cyberspace Administration of China regulates content, algorithms (rules adopted in 2022), and generative artificial intelligence (interim measures in 2023). Real-name registration links online identity to individuals. In Xinjiang, surveillance included a database platform, the Integrated Joint Operations Platform, documented in leaked materials and academic analysis. The widely discussed "social credit system" is not a single national score; it is a collection of blacklists, regulatory records, and local pilot programs, a point that scholars emphasize against some popular portrayals.

Technology also provides services. Mobile payments, e-commerce, and digital government platforms lowered transaction costs for millions of people; during the pandemic, color-coded health codes were used to restrict movement. In November 2022, after a fire in Ürümqi in which ten people died, protests spread in several cities and on campuses, with some participants holding blank sheets of paper, and authorities soon relaxed Covid restrictions. Researchers see this episode as evidence that citizens can express discontent and that policy may change, while also noting that participants faced detention and monitoring.

76.13 The Strengths, Limits, and Consequences of Centralized Governance

Evidence suggests that centralization strengthened some capacities. Large infrastructure programs, rapid mobilization for poverty alleviation (declared completed by the government in 2020), and coordinated industrial policy have been cited as outcomes of strong central direction. Zero-COVID policies, however, showed both mobilization capacity and costs: in late 2022, after protests in several cities, authorities abruptly relaxed controls. Local governments facing demanding targets sometimes responded with excessive enforcement or with formalism.

Information flow is a persistent issue: when higher authorities penalize bad news, subordinates may suppress it. The 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, the 2003 SARS response, and the early 2020 COVID-19 outbreak in Wuhan are cases that scholars have used to study both rapid response and reporting delays. Because contemporary evidence is incomplete, the effects of centralization must be assessed case by case.

Historical Debate

Political Centralization and Institutional Effectiveness

The question: Does greater centralization under Xi make governance more effective, or does it reduce adaptability and accountability?

Interpretation A: Centralization addresses fragmentation. Proponents argue that the earlier system allowed corruption, local protectionism, and policy drift; stronger Party coordination enables long-term strategy, discipline, and national mobilization for large objectives.
Interpretation B: Centralization narrows feedback. Critics argue that concentrating decisions, rewarding loyalty, and restricting criticism increases the risk of error, discourages local experimentation, and weakens succession certainty.

Evidence: Each side cites different cases: infrastructure delivery and poverty targets for A; the costs of rigid campaigns, property-sector problems, and the limits on public criticism for B. Comparative studies of other states and quantitative data on policy outcomes are used, but they are contested because outcomes depend on many factors besides institutions.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians interpret the Xi era: as continuity with earlier Party strength, as a decisive break, or as both.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would best test each interpretation, and what would be misleading?

Assessment requires attention to counterfactuals and different kinds of evidence. Officials announced that extreme poverty in rural areas had been eliminated by the end of 2020 using a national standard that is above the World Bank's extreme-poverty line but below its line for lower-middle-income countries; scholars debate how to compare the measures and how durable the gains are. The 2020 Wuhan lockdown, from January 23, and the early suppression of warnings by local authorities, including the reprimand of doctor Li Wenliang, illustrate both the capacity to mobilize and the danger of silencing information. Subsequent policies, including repeated lockdowns during the 2022 Omicron waves and the abrupt reversal in December 2022, show the costs of a strategy that was difficult to adjust.

Scholarly frameworks help. The "fragmented authoritarianism" model developed by Kenneth Lieberthal and Michel Oksenberg in the 1980s explained how bureaucratic bargaining limited central policy; later work on "experimentation under hierarchy" and on "consultative authoritarianism" showed adaptation. Studies of the Xi era ask whether the dynamics have shifted toward greater central control while local fragmentation persists in implementation. The evidence points to both: greater Party supervision and continuing variation in practice. Different readers, including those who see efficiency gains and those who see growing risk, can find support for their positions in particular cases, which is why the debate below is presented as a question of evidence and not of verdicts.

Division III — Nationalism, Historical Memory, and Political Identity

Historical interpretation became a visible component of political legitimacy in the PRC. This division examines how the state uses and promotes narratives about the past, how those narratives are contested, and how identities within and beyond the PRC differ.

76.14 Patriotism, National Identity, and the Education of Historical Memory

After 1989 the Party leadership launched a Patriotic Education Campaign (formalized in 1994), emphasizing national history, the humiliations of the nineteenth century, and the Party's role in national salvation. Textbooks, museums, memorials, film, and television carry these narratives. In October 2023 the NPC Standing Committee adopted a Patriotic Education Law, effective January 1, 2024, which provides legal grounding for patriotic education in schools, in public institutions, and across society.

Scholars debate how much patriotic education explains popular nationalism. Some argue that the campaign shaped attitudes systematically; others point to the independent roles of commercial media, the internet, travel, social media, and personal experience, and note that nationalist sentiment is also expressed in ways that criticize officials. The campaign has a documented institutional history. A 1994 Outline for Patriotic Education Implementation directed schools, museums, and media to emphasize national history; "patriotic education bases," numbering in the hundreds of sites, such as museums and memorials, were designated for school visits. A 2019 outline updated the program for "the new era," and from 2017 centrally compiled textbooks in history, Chinese language, and ideology and politics became standard in schools. National Security Education Day on April 15 became an annual event from 2016. In these materials, Party leadership, the achievements of socialism, and national unity are presented as lessons of history.

Students' and teachers' reception is harder to document. Surveys and ethnographic work find that many young people feel pride in national achievements, particularly in technology, infrastructure, and sports, while holding a range of views about specific policies. Commercial entertainment and online communities, including nationalist influencers, also shape attitudes, sometimes in tension with official messaging or in ways that pressure officials to appear tougher. Historians therefore distinguish among state propaganda, popular nationalism, and the interactions between them. They also note that other societies, including Japan, India, and the United States, conduct patriotic education in their own forms, a comparison that helps avoid treating the PRC as exceptional in kind while still recognizing differences in political context.

76.15 The Century of Humiliation and Narratives of National Rejuvenation

The official narrative frames the period from the Opium Wars (Chapter 52) to 1949 as a "century of humiliation," ended by the Party's founding of the PRC, and frames the present as a stage in "national rejuvenation." The narrative draws on events documented in this textbook: unequal treaties, treaty ports, the Sino-Japanese wars, foreign concessions, and the violence of the Japanese invasion (Chapters 51 to 56 and 64). These events are not inventions. What is historically debatable is the interpretive frame: how it simplifies Qing and Republican history, how much agency it attributes to Chinese actors, and how it connects the past to current policy.

Historical Evidence

Competing Uses of Historical Memory

Historians identify several uses of the same events. State narratives stress unity, victimization by imperialism, and Party leadership in recovery. Historians within and outside China have emphasized the roles of Qing and Republican reformers, regional elites, merchants, and foreign partners in modernization; some stress the diversity of experience across regions. Taiwanese, Hong Kong, and diaspora communities draw on other memories: Japanese colonial rule and democratization in Taiwan, Cold War refuge in Hong Kong. Evidence includes textbooks, museum exhibits, speeches, memoirs, oral history, and survey data. Each reveals a viewpoint, and none can serve alone as the history of an event.

The "century of humiliation" has its own history. The language of national humiliation emerged in the Republican era, when Chiang Kai-shek's government and student movements marked "National Humiliation Day" on May 9 in memory of Japan's Twenty-One Demands of 1915; the Maoist period emphasized class struggle and revolution more than humiliation. After 1989, as scholars such as William Callahan and Zheng Wang have argued, the narrative of victimization and rejuvenation was elevated in education and public discourse as the Party sought a basis for legitimacy beyond revolutionary ideology. In November 2012, soon after becoming General Secretary, Xi visited the National Museum's exhibition "The Road to Rejuvenation" and spoke of the "Chinese Dream" of national rejuvenation.

Historians question the frame without denying the events. Foreign imperialism did involve military aggression, unequal treaties, extraterritoriality, and humiliations that Chapters 52 to 57 describe. But scholars of the Qing and Republic also stress that Chinese actors were not merely victims: they shaped treaty-port societies, built industries, reformed institutions, and sometimes negotiated advantages. The Taiping and other rebellions caused more deaths than foreign wars, and the Qing was itself an expansionist empire. Interpretations of the period that foreground humiliation can also overlook Chinese diversity: for Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Tibet, the memory of the same events differs. This textbook uses the term as a historical object, to explain how the past is mobilized, and does not adopt it as a neutral description.

76.16 Public Commemoration, National Anniversaries, and Official Historical Narratives

Public ceremonies give the narrative visible form. The 2008 Beijing Olympics were presented as a national coming-out; the 2010 Shanghai Expo and later events reinforced the theme. Anniversaries include the seventieth anniversary of the PRC in 2019, the centenary of the Party in 2021, and the military parade of September 3, 2025, held to mark the eightieth anniversary of Japan's surrender; that year's commemorations of the war emphasized China's role in the anti-fascist coalition. Memorial days such as National Memorial Day for the Nanjing Massacre (December 13), established by the NPC Standing Committee in 2014, connect wartime memory to national identity.

Commemorations also expose omissions. Official narratives give limited place to the Great Leap Forward famine and the Cultural Revolution beyond brief acknowledgments, and the events of 1989 are not publicly commemorated on the mainland (Chapter 73). In 2021, the Party's resolution on history warned against "historical nihilism," which it defines as distorting Party history. Historians of memory study these silences as carefully as the ceremonies.

Commemoration involves specific legal and institutional choices. The Heroes and Martyrs Protection Law (2018) and a 2021 criminal-law amendment penalizing defamation of heroes and martyrs apply the state's view of historical figures; a few high-profile prosecutions and platform bans followed. The military parades of September 3, 2015, and September 3, 2025, commemorated the end of the war against Japan, with the latter emphasizing China's role in the anti-fascist coalition and showing new weaponry; observers noted the attendance of leaders from Russia and North Korea and interpreted the event in terms of both history and contemporary diplomacy. On July 1, 2021, Xi's speech on the Party's centenary asserted that the Party had led the nation to rejuvenation and that foreign attempts to bully China would fail.

The selective character of commemoration matters for historians. The Party's three historical resolutions, of 1945, 1981, and 2021, each revised interpretation to serve current needs: the 1981 resolution assessed Mao's errors, including the Cultural Revolution, while retaining his overall status; the 2021 resolution emphasized continuity between Mao-era and reform-era achievements and warned against "historical nihilism." The Great Famine and the Cultural Revolution are acknowledged in official history in brief terms, while discussion beyond these parameters faces restrictions, and Chapter 73's events of 1989 are not publicly commemorated on the mainland. Historians who study memory ask what is remembered, forgotten, or reframed, and for whom.

76.17 Media, Intellectual Life, Dissent, and Competing Interpretations of the Past

Chinese media are largely Party-led, with commercial outlets operating within guidelines. The internet created new spaces for discussion, followed by increasing regulation: real-name registration, content filtering, platform responsibility, and removal of accounts. Academic history in China ranges across many topics and methods, from rigorous archival work to Party-history research, with constraints on certain subjects, and archives open and close to researchers. Public intellectuals with differing views have sometimes lost platforms. Dissent takes forms including petitions, legal advocacy, online satire, and, for some, exile.

For students, this affects sources. Researchers should ask which topics are censored, which are tolerated, and how that shapes what is available. They should neither assume that the absence of public debate means unanimity nor that every critical voice represents a majority. The evidence base for public opinion is limited: surveys exist, but respondents may avoid sensitive subjects.

The environment of media and scholarship changed after 2012. Document No. 9 of 2013 warned officials against "Western" ideas including press freedom and civil society; in 2013 a New Year editorial at the Guangzhou newspaper Southern Weekly became a dispute between journalists and propaganda authorities; in 2016 Xi visited major media outlets and stated that media must serve the Party. Investigative reporting continued in some outlets, notably on corruption, pollution, and food safety, while political topics, particularly those connected to the top leadership, the military, or sensitive regions, were closely controlled. Foreign correspondents faced visa restrictions at times, and several Chinese journalists were detained.

In scholarship, Chinese historians produce a large volume of work on social, economic, and cultural history, as well as on Party history within official frameworks. Debates exist: for example, over the "New Qing History," which emphasizes the Qing's Inner Asian dimensions and Manchu identity (associated with Evelyn Rawski, Mark Elliott, and others, in debate with earlier "sinicization" arguments by Ho Ping-ti) and which some Chinese scholars criticize as undermining national unity. Access to archives has become more limited for some periods, and for foreign researchers, restrictions have varied. Scholars outside China rely on published documents, local archives, and oral history, and should be aware that gaps in the record reflect politics as well as chance. When writing about the present, these limits mean that conclusions are provisional.

76.18 Chinese Identity, Cultural Diversity, and the Global Chinese Diaspora

The PRC officially recognizes fifty-six ethnic groups, a classification built in the 1950s on earlier categories (Chapter 66). Han Chinese form the great majority, about nine-tenths of the population; the others include Zhuang, Hui, Manchu, Uyghur, Miao, Yi, Tibetan, Mongol, and many others. Language is another dimension: Mandarin (Putonghua) is promoted as the common language, while Cantonese, Wu, Min, Hakka, and other Sinitic languages are widely spoken, and Tibetan, Uyghur, Mongolian, and other languages belong to different families. Religious practice, regional identity, class, and generation further differentiate Chinese society.

Beyond the PRC, tens of millions of people of Chinese descent live overseas, with diverse histories: nineteenth-century labor migrants, Southeast Asian merchant families, postwar migrants from Hong Kong and Taiwan, and recent students and professionals. Their relationships to the PRC vary widely; many are citizens of other countries, and the Chinese state's outreach through "united front" work and diplomacy has drawn both support and suspicion. Ethnicity, civilization, and PRC citizenship are not synonymous: a Hong Kong resident, a Taiwanese voter, a Singaporean of Chinese descent, and a Tibetan in India may all relate differently to the term "Chinese."

National Identity and Political Belonging

A mainland urban graduate: May combine pride in national achievements with concern about jobs and housing; identification with the nation can coexist with criticism of specific policies.
A Hong Kong resident after 2019: May identify as Hongkonger, as Chinese, or as both, with views shaped by generation and by the events of 2014, 2019, and 2020; some welcome stability and closer integration, others fear loss of autonomy.
A Taiwanese voter: Surveys by Taiwanese research institutions have repeatedly found that many residents identify as Taiwanese, a smaller share as both Taiwanese and Chinese, and few solely as Chinese, though the categories carry different political meanings.
A Uyghur or Tibetan family: Their experience depends on region, religion, language, and local policy; official narratives emphasize unity and development, while many exiles and some scholars emphasize cultural loss and coercion.

Policy toward ethnic groups has changed in important ways. Under the 1950s Soviet-influenced classification project, "nationalities" (minzu) were identified and given rights to autonomy, and for decades official policy combined preferences with promotion of integration. Since about 2014, and particularly after 2017, the Party has emphasized "forging a strong sense of community for the Chinese nation" (zhonghua minzu gongtongti), putting greater weight on a shared national identity, Mandarin as the common language, and the "sinicization" of religion. Scholars have debated whether this represents a shift toward an assimilationist "second-generation ethnic policy," as proposed by some Chinese academics such as Hu Angang and Hu Lianhe, or a continuation of earlier patterns; critics of the shift such as Ma Rong have argued for a different approach, though the policy debate itself is not publicly open. Examples of friction include the 2020 protests in Inner Mongolia, where parents and students objected to plans to teach core subjects in Mandarin in Mongolian-language schools.

Language policy is part of identity formation. The 2000 National Common Language Law promoted Putonghua, while Cantonese, Shanghainese, Hokkien, and other topolects remain in daily use, the subject of occasional controversy over broadcasting and education. For the diaspora, the Chinese state in 2018 merged the Overseas Chinese Affairs Office into the United Front Work Department, which scholars interpret as increasing Party involvement in outreach; communities abroad have different attitudes, and many governments, including those of Southeast Asian states with large ethnic Chinese populations, insist that their citizens' loyalty is to their own states. Historical experiences also differ: Chinese Indonesians suffered violence in 1998, while Chinese Americans have faced both discrimination and suspicion.

Division IV — Hong Kong and the Transformation of One Country, Two Systems

Hong Kong's return to Chinese sovereignty and its economic significance are covered in Chapter 74. This division concentrates on governance and political status since 1997. The 2014 Umbrella Movement, the 2019 protests, the 2020 National Security Law, and the 2024 Article 23 ordinance are distinct events with distinct triggers.

76.19 One Country, Two Systems and the Post-1997 Constitutional Framework

The Sino-British Joint Declaration of 1984 and the Basic Law, adopted by the NPC in 1990, established the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) from July 1, 1997. The Basic Law promised a "high degree of autonomy," executive, legislative, and independent judicial power including final adjudication in Hong Kong, a continuing common-law system, and the retention of the capitalist system. It also provides that the NPC Standing Committee holds the power of interpretation of the Basic Law, a mechanism used several times, which many Hong Kong lawyers viewed as potentially limiting judicial autonomy.

Between 1997 and 2012 Hong Kong's economy remained a major financial center. Politically, the Chief Executive was chosen by an election committee of some hundreds, later some 1,200 members, and the Legislative Council included both directly elected and functional-constituency seats. The first attempt to enact Article 23 legislation in 2003 drew a mass protest on July 1 and was shelved.

Hong Kong's institutions under the Basic Law combine local and national elements. The Chief Executive is the head of government; the Legislative Council passes laws and approves budgets; the Court of Final Appeal, including non-permanent judges from other common-law jurisdictions, has final adjudication. Article 158 vests interpretation of the Basic Law in the NPC Standing Committee, and the court may seek interpretations on matters concerning central authority. The Standing Committee issued interpretations in 1999 (on right of abode for mainland-born children), 2004 and 2005 (on electoral reform and the term of the Chief Executive), 2016 (on oaths), and 2022 (on foreign lawyers in national security cases), each used to clarify a dispute and each contested.

Politics and identity changed over the first two decades. The 2003 SARS crisis and the Article 23 protest of July 1, 2003, were followed by a Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement with the mainland and an increase in mainland tourism and investment. Surveys by the University of Hong Kong's public opinion program showed a growing share of respondents, especially young people, identifying primarily as "Hongkongers," a trend sharpened by events such as the 2012 protests against a "national education" curriculum, led by the student group Scholarism. In June 2014 the State Council issued a white paper on one country, two systems stressing Beijing's "comprehensive jurisdiction" over Hong Kong, which many local observers read as a sign of a narrower understanding of autonomy.

76.20 Political Reform Disputes and the Umbrella Movement of 2014

In 2007 the NPC Standing Committee stated that universal suffrage for the Chief Executive could be implemented in 2017. On August 31, 2014, it issued a decision setting the framework: candidates would be nominated by a nominating committee, and two or three candidates would go to a popular vote, with each requiring majority support in the committee. Critics argued that this screening would prevent genuine choice; Beijing and its supporters argued that it was consistent with the Basic Law and the principle that the Chief Executive must "love the country and love Hong Kong."

Student and scholar organizations and the Occupy Central campaign led protests; after police used tear gas on September 28, large crowds occupied roads in Admiralty, Mong Kok, and Causeway Bay for seventy-nine days. The movement, called the Umbrella Movement after the protesters' use of umbrellas, did not change the decision. In June 2015 the Legislative Council rejected the government's reform proposal, leaving the existing system in place. Later, disqualifications of elected legislators in 2016–2017 for their oaths of office showed how the powers of interpretation could affect politics.

The movement had distinct groups. The idea of "Occupy Central with Love and Peace" was proposed by legal scholar Benny Tai in 2013 as civil disobedience if electoral reform failed; student groups including the Hong Kong Federation of Students and Scholarism, whose leaders included Alex Chow and Joshua Wong, began class boycotts in September 2014 and occupied Civic Square, leading to arrests and to the decision by the Occupy organizers to join. The police use of tear gas on September 28 and the sight of citizens sheltering behind umbrellas drew crowds into the streets. The government held televised talks with student representatives in October but made no concessions on the August 31 framework, and the occupation ended in December after court injunctions and police clearances.

The consequences were political and cultural. Surveys indicated lasting increases in support for local autonomy and, among some, more radical positions, including "localist" groups that emerged in the 2016 Mong Kok unrest and in the Legislative Council elections that year. The 2015 disappearance of booksellers associated with Causeway Bay Books, some of whom later appeared in mainland custody, was another source of concern. In November 2016 the Standing Committee's interpretation on oaths led to the disqualification of elected legislators. For supporters of Beijing's position, the movement showed risks of foreign influence and disorder; for supporters of the movement, it showed that peaceful petitions and elections could not secure change. Both perspectives shaped the later period.

76.21 The 2019 Extradition Bill Protests and the Crisis of Governance

In 2019 the government proposed amendments to allow case-by-case transfers of suspects to jurisdictions with which Hong Kong had no extradition agreement, including mainland China. The immediate stimulus was a 2018 murder case in Taiwan, but opponents feared exposure of residents to mainland courts. A march on June 9 drew very large numbers by organizers' estimates; on June 12, clashes with police occurred; on July 1 demonstrators entered the Legislative Council building. The Chief Executive announced the bill's suspension in June and formal withdrawal in September, but the movement broadened to include "five demands": withdrawal, an independent inquiry into police conduct, amnesty for arrested protesters, retraction of the label "riot," and universal suffrage.

The protests were decentralized, with a mix of peaceful marches and increasingly violent confrontations and vandalism, including a siege at Polytechnic University in November. Opinion within Hong Kong was divided about tactics and aims. In the district council elections of November 2019, pro-democracy candidates won a large majority of seats on high turnout, which many interpreted as an expression of support for the protesters' broader demands. The government and Beijing described the unrest as violence supported by external forces.

The scale of participation is itself debated. Organizers claimed that about two million people marched on June 16, 2019; police estimated roughly 338,000 at the peak of the procession, and independent counting varied. The protests were organized largely online and without central leadership, adopting the slogan "Be Water" to describe flexible tactics. Incidents included the July 21 attack on passengers at Yuen Long station by men in white shirts, which fueled distrust of the police, the August airport occupation, the use of tear gas in dense areas, and by autumn increasingly violent clashes in which protesters threw petrol bombs, damaged infrastructure, and attacked perceived opponents, while police fired live rounds in some instances. In October the government invoked the Emergency Regulations Ordinance to ban face masks, which a court later ruled unconstitutional in part.

The December 2019 outcomes mattered politically. In the district council elections on November 24, with a turnout of over seventy percent, pro-democracy candidates won control of seventeen of eighteen councils, an outcome widely read as an expression of public opinion at least on the handling of the crisis. The Independent Police Complaints Council released a report in 2020 that criticized some police practices but did not support an independent commission of inquiry, which remained a central demand. Beijing's response in 2020, through the National Security Law and the electoral changes, drew on its assessment that the unrest had threatened national security, a judgment critics reject as an overstatement that treats local opposition as foreign subversion.

76.22 The 2020 National Security Law and the Restructuring of Political Institutions

On June 30, 2020, the NPC Standing Committee enacted the Law of the PRC on Safeguarding National Security in the HKSAR and inserted it into Annex III of the Basic Law, bypassing the local legislature. The law defines four offenses: secession, subversion, terrorism, and collusion with foreign forces. It created an Office for Safeguarding National Security staffed by central authorities, a local Committee for Safeguarding National Security, and provisions for designated judges and, in some circumstances, trials without juries. The government stated that the law restored stability and would affect only a small minority of persons; critics, including many legal and rights groups and several governments, argued that its definitions are broad and that it affects free expression and association.

Consequences followed quickly. The pro-democracy primary of July 2020 led to the arrest in January 2021 of dozens of participants in a case known as the "Hong Kong 47," most of whom were later convicted or pleaded guilty. The newspaper Apple Daily closed in June 2021 after its executives were arrested. Many civil society groups dissolved. In March 2021 the NPC reformed Hong Kong's electoral system under the principle of "patriots administering Hong Kong," establishing candidate eligibility screening, reducing the share of directly elected seats in the Legislative Council, and enlarging the election committee. Later changes also reduced direct election in district councils.

The law's provisions deserve careful reading. Article 4 states that human rights, including freedoms of speech, press, and assembly, shall be respected; Article 42 sets a presumption against bail for suspects; Article 38 claims jurisdiction over offenses committed outside Hong Kong by non-residents; and the Office for Safeguarding National Security can take over certain cases, with its personnel exempt from local jurisdiction. The government says that safeguards exist, including the requirement of proof beyond reasonable doubt and the right of appeal; critics argue that the definitions of subversion and collusion are broad and that the combination of designated judges, restricted bail, and national oversight reduces judicial autonomy.

The law's early years saw a rapid change in civic life. In August 2020, twelve Hong Kong residents attempting to travel by boat to Taiwan were detained by mainland authorities and tried in Shenzhen. The 2021 arrests of forty-seven pro-democracy figures for conspiracy to commit subversion over an unofficial primary election led to a long trial; in May 2024 forty-five were convicted or had pleaded guilty and two were acquitted, and in November 2024 sentences of between roughly four and ten years were imposed. Apple Daily closed in June 2021, and the Hong Kong Alliance that organized the Tiananmen vigil and the Confederation of Trade Unions dissolved in 2021. In December 2021, the Legislative Council election after the electoral reform recorded a turnout of about thirty percent. These facts are documented in court records and public reporting; the interpretations differ over whether they show restored order or reduced pluralism.

The case of Jimmy Lai is the most prominent test. Lai, a media entrepreneur and founder of Apple Daily who holds British citizenship, was arrested in August 2020. After a trial before three judges designated under the national security law, he was convicted on December 15, 2025, of two counts of conspiracy to collude with foreign forces and one count of conspiracy to publish seditious publications; he had pleaded not guilty. On February 9, 2026, the court sentenced him to twenty years in prison, describing him as the mastermind and driving force of the collusion conspiracies, including appeals to foreign governments to impose sanctions or other hostile measures on Hong Kong and China. Six former Apple Daily executives and two activists were sentenced to terms reported as between about six and ten years. Hong Kong Chief Executive John Lee called the sentence a solemn warning to those who would collude with foreign forces, and China's foreign ministry called it legitimate and reasonable. The British and Canadian governments urged his release, and British Prime Minister Keir Starmer said he had raised the case during a visit to China.

Several kinds of evidence must be separated. The court's findings, set out in its judgments, are the authoritative record of what the court concluded and of the evidence it accepted; the government's statements express an official position that the case upholds the rule of law; Lai's own statements that he was a political prisoner are a claim made by the defendant; and the views of foreign governments, legal scholars, and human-rights groups are interpretations that range from concern about the application of "collusion" to the activities of a newspaper to support for the court's reasoning. Legal scholars have noted the case's broad construction of seditious intent and of collusion in a media context. The sentence is also the longest imposed so far under the national security law, which gives it significance beyond one person's fate.

76.23 The 2024 Article 23 Legislation and Hong Kong's Contemporary Position

The Article 23 ordinance took effect on March 23, 2024, after rapid passage. The Hong Kong government argued that it closed gaps in protection against threats, completed a constitutional duty unfulfilled since 1997, and would not affect ordinary rights. Critics, including the UN Human Rights Committee and several foreign governments, expressed concern about vague definitions, broad police powers, and limits on lawyers and on rights of expression. Both the government and critics pointed to Hong Kong's continuing role as a financial and trading center, and to the continued operation of its courts in commercial matters. The effects on press freedom, civil society, academic life, and emigration are documented in different forms; migration data show significant outflows after 2019 as well as inflows of mainland professionals.

Division V — Taiwan and Cross-Strait Relations

Taiwan is a society with its own elected government, parties, courts, and history. The questions of sovereignty and status are disputed between Beijing and Taipei and among Taiwan's political forces, and international positions differ. The chapter does not adopt either government's terminology as neutral fact.

76.24 The Historical Foundations of the Contemporary Taiwan Question

Taiwan was incorporated into the Qing empire's administration in 1683, made a province in 1885, and ceded to Japan in 1895 (Chapter 55); it was a Japanese colony until 1945 (Chapter 64). In 1945 the Republic of China took over administration on behalf of the Allies, and in 1949 the ROC government under Chiang Kai-shek relocated to Taiwan after defeat in the civil war (Chapter 65). Martial law was in force from 1949 to 1987; the February 28, 1947 incident and the "White Terror" shaped the experiences of Taiwan's residents. Democratization followed: direct presidential election in 1996, first party turnover in 2000, and an open party system.

The ROC represented China at the United Nations until October 1971, when General Assembly Resolution 2758 seated the PRC; its interpretation, particularly whether it settles Taiwan's status, is disputed. The United States recognized the PRC in 1979, and the Taiwan Relations Act, passed by Congress that year, provides for unofficial relations and arms provision. The Three Joint Communiqués between Washington and Beijing and the Six Assurances to Taipei are further components of US policy. The ROC retained diplomatic relations with a dozen or so states by the mid-2020s.

Taiwan's history before 1949 was not simply Chinese or Japanese. Austronesian-speaking indigenous peoples, who now make up about two percent of the population, inhabited the island for millennia; Dutch and Spanish colonial enclaves existed in the seventeenth century; the Zheng family regime, loyal to the Ming, held the island from 1662 to 1683; and Qing rule was partial before provincial status in 1885. Japan's colony (1895–1945) built infrastructure and an education system while imposing assimilation policies. These layers are part of why the island's population is diverse, including Hokkien and Hakka speakers whose ancestors arrived mostly before the twentieth century, indigenous peoples, and the roughly one to two million people who came with the ROC in 1949.

The postwar experience shaped political identity. The February 28 Incident of 1947, in which the ROC military suppressed an uprising and killed thousands, and the decades of martial law and the White Terror, created lasting memories; the end of martial law in 1987 and constitutional reforms in the 1990s opened the way to democratization. Within the ROC's constitutional order, the question of the island's status is argued among parties: the KMT historically sought eventual unification under the ROC; the DPP, founded in 1986, holds that Taiwan is already a sovereign state named the Republic of China (Taiwan); and public opinion polls over three decades show that most residents prefer to maintain the status quo. The UN General Assembly's Resolution 2758 (1971) transferred China's seat to the PRC; the PRC reads it as settling Taiwan's status, while Taiwan's government and some scholars argue that it does not mention Taiwan and does not address sovereignty over it.

76.25 The 1995–1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis and the Anti-Secession Law of 2005

In 1995, after Lee Teng-hui, president of the ROC, visited Cornell University, Beijing conducted missile tests and exercises near Taiwan, repeated before Taiwan's first direct presidential election in March 1996. The United States sent two carrier groups into nearby waters. The episode deepened Chinese investment in military modernization and Taiwanese concern about security. Under Chen Shui-bian, the first DPP president (2000–2008), debates over Taiwan's identity intensified. In March 2005 the NPC adopted the Anti-Secession Law.

The 1995–1996 crisis mattered for each side. After Lee Teng-hui's visit to Cornell in June 1995, the PLA fired missiles into waters north of Taiwan in July and August 1995 and held exercises before the March 1996 presidential election, when Washington sent two aircraft-carrier groups to the area. In Taiwan, the missile tests may have strengthened support for Lee, who won the election; in China, they led to programs to build anti-access capabilities. In 2002, Chen Shui-bian stated that there was "one country on each side" of the strait, and during his presidency he promoted referendums and a new constitution, which Beijing and, at times, Washington viewed as provocative.

The Anti-Secession Law should be read with its political setting. Passed in March 2005 shortly after Chen's re-election, it consolidated Beijing's approach under Hu Jintao, who combined firmness against independence with incentives for cooperation; the KMT chairman Lien Chan visited Beijing in April 2005 and met Hu. The law has ten articles; it states that the use of non-peaceful means would be a last resort, but it is deliberately open-ended on what would trigger it. In Taiwan the law was criticized across the political spectrum as unilaterally defining the status quo, though some in the KMT viewed it as less threatening than expected. Its presence has become a reference point in later debates.

76.26 Cross-Strait Economic Integration and the Sunflower Movement

From the 1990s Taiwanese firms invested heavily on the mainland, and trade expanded. Under Ma Ying-jeou (2008–2016) of the Kuomintang (KMT), direct flights and shipping began, tourism grew, and in 2010 the Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement was concluded. In November 2015 Ma and Xi Jinping met in Singapore, the first meeting of the leaders on both sides since 1949, as leaders of their respective sides. Domestic opposition to deeper integration grew. In March 2014 student protesters occupied the Legislative Yuan for about three weeks in the Sunflower Movement, opposing the handling of a services trade agreement, which was not ratified. Scholars debate whether economic ties produce political convergence or reinforce Taiwanese concern about dependence; surveys show that identification as Taiwanese increased during these years.

Economic integration moved quickly after 2008. The "three links" of direct flights, shipping, and postal service began in December 2008; mainland tourists arrived in the hundreds of thousands, and Taiwan firms expanded operations on the mainland. At its peak, mainland China and Hong Kong accounted for roughly forty percent of Taiwan's exports, a share that later declined as firms diversified and as Taiwan's government promoted a "New Southbound Policy" toward South and Southeast Asia, launched in 2016. Semiconductors, in which Taiwanese firms hold a leading global position, became the island's most strategically significant industry.

The Sunflower Movement began on the night of March 18, 2014, when students and activists entered the Legislative Yuan in protest against the way the KMT majority had moved the Cross-Strait Service Trade Agreement through committee, and remained for twenty-four days. Participants criticized both the content of the agreement and the process; they also expressed concern about dependence on China. The agreement was not ratified, and the KMT lost heavily in local elections in November 2014 and in the 2016 national elections. Researchers also examine whether economic ties create political leverage for Beijing or constraints on it; evidence includes surveys of business owners, which show diverse attitudes, and trade restrictions imposed by Beijing on specific Taiwanese products as a political signal.

76.27 Tsai Ing-wen, Beijing's Taiwan Policy, and Rising Military Pressure

Tsai Ing-wen of the DPP won the presidency in January 2016 and was re-elected in 2020. Her government did not accept the "1992 Consensus" as defined by Beijing, which suspended official communications. Beijing reduced tourist flows, intensified diplomatic pressure so that several states switched recognition to the PRC, and increased military activity near Taiwan. In January 2019 Xi spoke of a "one country, two systems" scheme for Taiwan, which Tsai rejected; the Hong Kong protests later that year strengthened Taiwanese skepticism. After US House Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei in August 2022, the PLA held large exercises including missile launches over and around the island. Domestic debates in Taiwan involved defense spending, conscription duration, and relations with the mainland, with the KMT and DPP presenting different approaches.

Beijing's approach after 2016 combined pressure and inducements. Official channels between the Taiwan Affairs Office and Taiwan's Mainland Affairs Council were suspended in 2016; mainland tourist group numbers fell; several states that had recognized the ROC switched recognition, including Panama (2017), the Dominican Republic and El Salvador (2018), the Solomon Islands and Kiribati (2019), Nicaragua (2021), Honduras (2023), and Nauru (January 2024), leaving about a dozen diplomatic partners. Beijing also offered measures to attract Taiwanese residents, including employment and study opportunities, and sanctioned individuals and organizations that it called "diehard Taiwan independence" supporters.

The Hong Kong protests in 2019 influenced Taiwan's politics: Tsai, whose approval had been low, won re-election in January 2020 with a record vote, helped by public anxiety about "one country, two systems." Defense policy shifted: conscription was extended to one year from 2024, and the government increased spending and encouraged domestic production. In August 2022, after the visit to Taipei by US House Speaker Nancy Pelosi, the PLA launched large-scale exercises including ballistic missile firings into waters around the island and thereafter made crossing of the Taiwan Strait median line, once a tacit boundary, a regular practice. Another set of exercises followed Tsai's meeting with Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California in April 2023. Observers differ over whether these exercises are intended to deter, signal, normalize pressure, or rehearse military options, and probably serve more than one purpose.

76.28 The 2024 Taiwan Election and the Cross-Strait Relationship Through 2026

In January 2024 Lai Ching-te, who had been vice president, won the presidency with a plurality of the vote in a three-party race, extending the DPP's hold on the presidency for a third consecutive term, while the KMT became the largest party in the Legislative Yuan and the DPP lost its majority. Lai was inaugurated on May 20, 2024. The PLA held exercises named Joint Sword-2024A days later and 2024B in October, and Strait Thunder-2025A in April 2025. 76.29 Taiwan, the United States, Japan, and the International Security Question

The United States' "one-China policy" acknowledges the Chinese position that Taiwan is part of China without endorsing it as American policy, opposes unilateral changes to the status quo, and relies on the Taiwan Relations Act. Beijing's "one-China principle" asserts that there is one China, that Taiwan is part of it, and that the PRC is its sole legitimate government. The two formulations differ in legal and diplomatic meaning, and discussions that conflate them cause confusion. Japan, whose southwestern islands lie close to Taiwan, has security interests there; Japanese statements in late 2025 about contingencies drew strong Chinese criticism, adding to tension. Taiwan's role in advanced semiconductor manufacturing gives its stability global economic significance.

Analysts discuss scenarios, including blockade, coercion, accommodation, and invasion, but these are scenarios; none is an established outcome. Historians should be cautious about predictions, and attentive to dating and attributing statements, because positions evolve.

The one-China principle is the PRC's position that there is one China, that Taiwan is part of it, and that the PRC government is the sole legitimate government; Beijing requires other states to accept it as a condition of diplomatic relations. The one-China policy is the American approach, set out in the 1972, 1979, and 1982 communiqués with the PRC and the Taiwan Relations Act: Washington recognizes the PRC as the government of China, "acknowledges" the Chinese position on Taiwan, maintains unofficial relations with Taiwan, and has not stated a position on Taiwan's final status, while opposing unilateral change by force or coercion. Using these terms precisely avoids mistaking one government's formula for common ground.

The United States' commitments are legally and politically layered. The Taiwan Relations Act (1979) commits the US to provide Taiwan with defensive arms and to maintain the capacity to resist force or coercion that would endanger its security; the Six Assurances of 1982 limit certain US actions regarding arms sales and sovereignty. Washington has long avoided a precise statement about whether it would defend Taiwan, a posture known as strategic ambiguity; President Biden on several occasions said that the US would, and White House officials then said that policy had not changed. The Trump administration in 2025–2026 combined arms-sales decisions with a focus on trade with Beijing; reports of the September 2026 summit said that the two sides made no breakthrough on Taiwan. Taiwan's own policy emphasizes "all-of-society resilience" and defense reforms.

Japan's position has attracted attention. Japan's southwestern islands, including Yonaguni, lie about one hundred kilometers from Taiwan's east coast, and Japanese security debates have more openly considered a Taiwan contingency; remarks by Japan's prime minister in November 2025 on circumstances in which such a contingency could be a survival-threatening situation for Japan led to sharp Chinese protests and economic and travel measures. Analysts also discuss Taiwan's semiconductor industry, whose most advanced chips are concentrated in a few firms, as a factor that raises the economic stakes of any crisis and as a "silicon shield," an idea that others dispute. Scenario studies, including tabletop exercises, examine blockade, quarantine, limited coercion, and invasion; they explore risks and capabilities and are not predictions. 76.30 Ethnic Diversity, Regional Autonomy, and the Modern Chinese State

The constitution and the 1984 Law on Regional Ethnic Autonomy provide for autonomous regions, prefectures, and counties, with five autonomous regions: Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Guangxi, Ningxia, and Tibet. The law grants the use of minority languages, preferences in education and employment, and the right of local people's congresses to adapt policies, within the principle of national unity. Party secretaries, who hold real authority, are usually Han in the autonomous regions' top posts, and critics argue that autonomy is limited in practice; officials argue that it is substantial and that minority development has been prioritized. Scholars distinguish formal autonomy from effective administrative authority, and differences among regions are considerable.

The five autonomous regions have different origins: Inner Mongolia (1947, before the PRC's founding), Xinjiang (1955), Guangxi and Ningxia (1958), and Tibet (1965). Minority policy has had a mixture of preferences and constraints. 76.31 Tibet: Historical Sovereignty, Political Change, and Contemporary Governance

Tibet's relationship with Chinese states was varied across centuries: Tibetan empire rivalry and marriage diplomacy with the Tang (Chapter 27), Mongol overlordship in the Yuan era with Tibetan Buddhist clerics at court, patron-priest relations between Tibetan lamas and Manchu emperors (Chapter 48), and Qing garrisons and imperial representatives (ambans) in Lhasa. Chinese official historiography treats Tibet as part of China since the Yuan; Tibetan exile accounts emphasize that Tibet functioned as an independent state, especially between the fall of the Qing in 1912 and 1950. Scholars debate how to apply modern concepts of sovereignty to pre-modern relationships that did not use them.

In October 1950 the PLA entered Tibet's eastern Chamdo region; the 1951 Seventeen Point Agreement recognized Chinese sovereignty and Tibetan religious and political institutions, though Tibetans disagree on the circumstances of its signing. After revolts and the flight of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama to India in March 1959, the Tibetan government was dissolved; the Tibet Autonomous Region was established in 1965. The Cultural Revolution brought the destruction of monasteries. After 1980 policy loosened, with reconstruction of religious sites and economic investment, including the railway to Lhasa opened in 2006. In March 2008 unrest broke out in Lhasa and spread across Tibetan areas, followed by security crackdowns, and since 2009 more than a hundred and fifty Tibetans have self-immolated in protest, according to Tibetan exile and other tallies.

The historical background is intricate. The Tibetan Empire (seventh to ninth centuries) was a rival and sometime partner of the Tang (Chapter 27); a treaty of 821–822, recorded on a pillar in Lhasa, set the boundary between the two states. Tibetan Buddhist institutions later had ties with the Mongol rulers of the Yuan, who gave the Sakya lama Phagpa an elevated position, and with the Qing, where the Fifth Dalai Lama visited the Shunzhi emperor in 1652. The Qing sent troops in 1720 to expel Dzungar forces, stationed imperial residents (ambans) in Lhasa, and in 1793 promulgated regulations, including the use of the Golden Urn for selecting high reincarnations, which Beijing today cites as precedent and which Tibetan sources say were applied unevenly. The relationship was personal and religious as well as political, a patron-priest tie in Tibetan terms.

After the Qing's fall, the Thirteenth Dalai Lama's government declared itself free of Chinese control in 1913 and ruled Lhasa and central Tibet with its own army, currency, and postal service until 1950, but no major power recognized Tibet as independent, and Republican governments continued to claim sovereignty. The 1914 Simla Conference produced a draft agreement and the McMahon Line that China did not sign, a matter that still affects the India–China border (Section 76.45). The PLA's advance in 1950, the 1951 agreement, the migration of Tibetan resistance in Kham and Amdo in the 1950s, and the March 1959 uprising in Lhasa, after which the Dalai Lama and tens of thousands of Tibetans fled to India, shaped the later conflict. The Cultural Revolution brought the destruction of most monasteries, and liberalization from the 1980s allowed rebuilding.

Protests in 1987–1989 led to martial law in Lhasa in March 1989, under the regional Party secretary Hu Jintao. The Qinghai–Tibet railway opened in 2006. The riots of March 14, 2008, in Lhasa, in which Chinese authorities said that eighteen civilians and one police officer died, were followed by protests across Tibetan areas and a large security response; exile groups reported more deaths in the crackdown, figures that cannot be independently verified. Since 2009, more than 150 Tibetans, many of them monks and nuns, have set themselves on fire in protest. In recent years official policy has emphasized "sinicization of Tibetan Buddhism," border development, and "ethnic unity," and the central government has expanded infrastructure such as the Lhasa–Nyingchi railway (2021) and a major hydropower project on the lower Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra), whose construction was launched in 2025 and has prompted concern in India and Bangladesh.

76.32 Tibet: Religion, Language, Education, and Cultural Change

Tibetan Buddhism remains central to Tibetan identity; monasteries operate under state supervision through Democratic Management Committees and religious regulations. Economic development, including infrastructure, poverty alleviation programs, tourism, and migration of Han and Hui workers, has changed the regional economy. Education policy has moved toward greater use of Mandarin as the language of instruction, and a large system of state boarding schools in Tibetan areas has drawn attention. In 2023, UN human rights experts expressed concern that these schools and curriculum changes risk weakening Tibetan language and culture; the Chinese government rejected the criticism, saying that the schools provide quality education, protect rights, and teach minority languages.

Historical Evidence

Tibetan Language, Religion, and Education Policies

Evidence includes official regulations and statistics, white papers, UN communications, reports by exile organizations and international NGOs, academic fieldwork, satellite imagery of building projects, and testimony of exiles and some residents. Access for independent researchers and journalists to Tibetan areas is restricted, so many claims on both sides are difficult to verify independently. Historians weigh what can be corroborated across sources: for example, laws and curriculum changes are documentable, while estimates of numbers and the motives behind policies are more contested.

Regulations on religion operate at several levels. Monasteries are managed by committees under government supervision; monks and nuns are required to participate in "patriotic education" sessions; a 2007 State Administration for Religious Affairs measure requires government approval for the recognition of high reincarnations. Authorities demolished sections of the large Buddhist academies at Larung Gar and Yachen Gar in Sichuan in 2016–2017 and reduced their resident numbers, explaining that the sites were overcrowded and unsafe; Tibetan and international groups described the actions as restrictions on religion. Tibetan Buddhism is also diverse, with Gelug, Kagyu, Sakya, Nyingma, and Bon traditions that have different institutional histories, and lay practice includes pilgrimage and domestic devotion.

Language and education are especially sensitive. Tibetan-language instruction in schools has been reduced in recent years in favor of Mandarin in many places; in 2010, students in Tongren, Qinghai, protested against a plan to shift instruction to Chinese. The Tibetan writer-activist Tashi Wangchuk, who advocated for Tibetan-language teaching in a New York Times video, was sentenced in 2018 to five years in prison for "inciting separatism." Rural children in many Tibetan areas attend state boarding schools; in 2023, UN human rights experts said that such systems risked eroding Tibetan language and culture, and the Chinese government replied that the schools provide free, high-quality education to students from scattered pastoral communities. Parents' and students' experiences differ, and independent evaluation is hampered by access limits. The economy has changed living conditions: roads, electrification, health care, and poverty-alleviation resettlement have altered rural life, and many Tibetans have benefited from them, while also facing competition from migrants and loss of control over land and herding practices in some places.

76.33 The Dalai Lama, Tibetan Communities in Exile, and the Succession Question

The Fourteenth Dalai Lama, born in 1935, heads a Tibetan exile community in Dharamsala, India, which includes an elected parliament and a Central Tibetan Administration. In the 1980s he proposed negotiations, later the "Middle Way" seeking autonomy and not independence; talks between his envoys and Beijing in 2002–2010 produced no agreement. The succession of the Dalai Lama became a major issue. In July 2025, on the eve of his ninetieth birthday, he stated that the institution would continue and that the Gaden Phodrang Trust has sole authority to recognize his reincarnation. Chinese officials responded that the reincarnation must follow historical conventions, including the Golden Urn and approval by the central government. The competing claims reflect the earlier Panchen Lama dispute: in 1995 the Dalai Lama recognized a boy, Gedhun Choekyi Nyima, who disappeared from public view, and Beijing installed another, Gyaltsen Norbu.

Succession is not only a political question for Tibetans. The reincarnation system, by which a high lama is recognized as the rebirth of a predecessor, dates to the thirteenth century and has historically involved monastic committees, divination, and, at times, Mongol and Qing patrons. The Fourteenth Dalai Lama, born Lhamo Thondup in 1935, was recognized as a child, became head of state in 1950, and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1989. In 1988 he proposed in Strasbourg an arrangement for Tibetan autonomy within China, later called the Middle Way, and in 2011 he transferred political authority to an elected leader, the Sikyong, while retaining his religious role. Nine rounds of talks between his envoys and Chinese officials between 2002 and 2010 ended without agreement.

In 2011 he said that the question of whether the institution should continue would be decided by Tibetans, and in July 2025 he stated that it would continue and that the Gaden Phodrang Trust, his office, would have sole authority to recognize his reincarnation. Chinese officials responded that the reincarnation must be conducted according to religious ritual and historical conventions, including the Golden Urn, and be approved by the central government. In 2020 the US Congress passed legislation stating that Tibetans should select their own religious leaders. These positions are mutually exclusive, and the outcome will depend on events that have not occurred. Many Tibetans inside and outside China hold devotion to the Dalai Lama that is independent of any political arrangement, which is why the question matters for religious life as much as for sovereignty.

76.34 Xinjiang: Historical Communities, Religion, and the Evolution of State Policy

The Tarim Basin and Dzungaria were home to oasis kingdoms, Buddhist centers, later Islamic states, and steppe powers (Chapters 16 and 27). Qing conquest of the Zunghars in the 1750s (Chapter 48) created the territory later named Xinjiang ("new frontier") in 1884. Uyghurs, a Turkic-speaking, predominantly Muslim people, form the largest group, with Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Hui, Mongols, Han, and others. Short-lived East Turkestan republics emerged in the 1930s and 1940s; the PLA entered in 1949, and the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region was established in 1955. Production and Construction Corps, a paramilitary settler organization, was founded in 1954. The Han share of the population rose from a small minority in 1949 to roughly 40 percent.

After the 1990s, economic development, including oil, gas, cotton, and infrastructure, was accompanied by incidents of violence and security campaigns. In July 2009 riots in Ürümqi involved violence between Uyghurs and Han and led to many deaths and a harsh official response. Attacks in 2013–2014, including at Kunming station and Ürümqi, preceded the "Strike Hard Campaign Against Violent Terrorism" launched in 2014. From about 2016, under Party Secretary Chen Quanguo, security measures grew sharply, with extensive checkpoints, data collection, and restrictions on religious practice.

The history of Xinjiang includes interactions with Chinese empires as well as with Inner and Central Asia. The Han and Tang (Chapters 16 and 27) established garrisons and protectorates in the Tarim oases, while Buddhist kingdoms such as Khotan and Kucha were centers of translation and art; Islam spread from the tenth century with the Karakhanid dynasty, and the Chagatai and Yarkand khanates later ruled. The Qing conquest in the 1750s (Chapter 48) ended the Zunghar state; the term "Uyghur" as a modern national name was adopted in the twentieth century. After a revolt led by Yaqub Beg (1865–1877) and its suppression by Zuo Zongtang, Xinjiang became a province in 1884. In the Republican era, local rulers such as Sheng Shicai (1933–1944) maintained ties with the Soviet Union, and short-lived "East Turkestan" republics were proclaimed in Kashgar (1933–1934) and in the Ili region (1944–1949). The PLA arrived in 1949.

Under the PRC, the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, established in 1954, settled demobilized soldiers and Han migrants on reclaimed land, and Han migration, together with state-planned development, changed demographics. Episodes of unrest include the 1990 Baren uprising, the 1997 Ghulja events, and the 2009 Ürümqi riots, which followed a factory fight in Guangdong in which Uyghur workers were killed and in which, according to official figures, 197 people died, mostly Han civilians. In 2013 and 2014 violent attacks, including a vehicle crash in Tiananmen Square (October 2013), the Kunming station stabbings (March 2014), and a bombing in Ürümqi (May 2014), prompted the "Strike Hard Campaign" and a counterterrorism law in 2015. In 2017, regulations on "de-extremification" prohibited a range of religious expressions, including some beards and veils. Chinese officials point to violence as justification; Uyghur and other critics argue that the policies were disproportionate and treated religious practice and cultural identity as security threats. Studies of the period differ about how far a militant threat existed and how far policy responded to broader concerns about unrest and ethnic identity.

76.35 Xinjiang: Mass Detention, Security Policy, and International Human Rights Assessments

From 2017, evidence from official documents, leaked papers, satellite imagery, and testimony indicated a network of facilities where large numbers of Uyghurs and other Muslims were held without ordinary legal process. The Chinese government initially denied the existence of such facilities, then described them in 2018–2019 as vocational education and training centers aimed at countering extremism and terrorism, and said in December 2019 that all trainees had completed their programs. Researchers' estimates of the numbers detained at peak have ranged from several hundred thousand to over a million; the government has not provided independently verified figures. Policies also included restrictions on religious expression, family separation, birth-rate decline in certain areas, and labor transfers.

In August 2022 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights published an assessment finding credible indications of arbitrary and discriminatory detention and other abuses, and that these may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity. The Chinese government rejected the assessment as based on disinformation. A number of governments characterized the policies as genocide, while others, including many in Muslim-majority countries, expressed support for China at UN forums. Scholars differ over the legal category, while many agree about the evidence of large-scale detention and coercive measures.

76.36 Labor, Education, Religion, Autonomy, and the Comparative History of Frontier Governance

Policies since the late 2010s have included labor transfers, pairing assistance, and urbanization programs in Xinjiang, which officials describe as poverty alleviation and critics as coercive; boarding and Mandarin-language schooling; the campaign against "religious extremism," including restrictions on mosques and dress; and sinicization of religion, a campaign applying to all faiths. Experiences vary: by region of Xinjiang, between Uyghurs, Kazakhs, and Hui, and between urban and rural communities.

Comparing Tibet and Xinjiang, scholars note common themes: Party-led development, security priorities, migration, language policy, and restrictions on religion. They also stress differences: Tibet's centralized Buddhist institutions and exile leader contrast with Xinjiang's plural Muslim communities and diaspora organizations; Xinjiang's links to Central Asia and the Middle East; and different forms of documentary evidence. Treating them as identical cases would hide these differences. Comparison with earlier frontier governance, such as Qing indirect rule, is instructive but should not suggest that modern policies repeat imperial ones: the modern state's technology, citizenship concepts, and goals differ.

Historical Vocabulary
Autonomy, Cultural Rights, Sovereignty, and Self-Determination

Autonomy refers to a measure of self-government within a larger state; in the PRC it is defined by law as regional ethnic autonomy and operates under Party leadership. Cultural rights include language, religion, and education, protected in the constitution and in international covenants, although their interpretation varies. Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory, a concept that arrived in China through nineteenth-century international law and does not map neatly onto earlier tributary or patron-priest relationships. Self-determination is a principle in international law whose application to peoples within existing states is debated. Using these words precisely helps historians avoid conflating legal claims, political aims, and historical descriptions.

Policies beyond detention include programs described by the government as labor transfers and poverty alleviation, in which rural residents are placed in jobs in factories in Xinjiang or other provinces. Researchers have documented cases where the participants appeared to have little choice, and the US and other governments have restricted imports on that basis; Chinese authorities reply that employment is voluntary and promotes income. Mosques have been closed or modified, according to satellite studies and journalism, and officials have promoted "sinicization" of Islam, including a five-year plan for the period 2018–2022. Many Uyghurs and Kazakhs abroad have relatives whom they cannot contact. Meanwhile, large Han populations in cities such as Ürümqi live under different conditions, which means that experiences of the same policies are unequal.

A comparison is useful provided that differences are kept in view. In Tibet, a single religious hierarchy and an exile administration provide a unified symbolic center; the principal disputes concern the historical status of Tibet, religion, language, and succession. In Xinjiang, Muslim communities are plural, with Uyghurs, Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, and Hui differing in language and local history, and the primary issues are security policy, detention, and cultural practices. Inner Mongolia, with a much larger Han majority, and Ningxia, home to many Hui, present other forms. Hui Muslims, who speak Chinese and have historically been integrated, have also seen restrictions on mosque architecture in recent years. Historians of earlier periods can add context: the Qing governed by distinct institutions (Chapter 48), and Republican and PRC governments introduced a unitary citizen state, which, whatever the earlier forms, differs in technology and ambition. Statements from governments, exile organizations, UN experts, and scholars each need to be assessed according to access, method, and interest.

Chapter 74 described China's integration into global trade. This division examines how that integration expanded into overseas investment and infrastructure finance, and how the results should be evaluated.

76.37 From Reform-Era Globalization to China's Overseas Economic Expansion

The "Going Out" policy, encouraged from about 1999, supported state enterprises and later private firms to invest abroad. By the 2000s, Chinese companies were active in mining, oil, construction, and telecommunications in Africa, Latin America, and Asia, and large foreign-exchange reserves allowed lending through policy banks, notably the China Development Bank and the Export-Import Bank of China. After the 2008 financial crisis, overcapacity in some domestic industries, including steel and cement, and the search for investment outlets added motives. These preceded the Belt and Road label and show that much overseas activity was continuous with earlier policies.

The contemporary expansion followed policy decisions. Chinese leaders adopted a "going out" strategy in the late 1990s, allowing state-owned enterprises to acquire overseas assets. The failed attempt by CNOOC to acquire the US oil company Unocal in 2005 showed the political limits of some investments, and the creation of the China Investment Corporation as a sovereign wealth fund in 2007 expressed a search for returns on reserves. Foreign-exchange reserves reached nearly four trillion dollars in 2014; in 2015, China's outbound direct investment exceeded inbound for the first time. In Africa, Chinese banks used "resource-backed" loans, such as for Angola's oil exports, and Chinese contractors built roads, dams, and railways; in Latin America, loans to Venezuela and Ecuador were tied to oil. In 2013 China became the world's largest trading nation in goods.

These activities involved varied actors. State-owned banks and enterprises were important, but provincial governments and private firms also invested, sometimes in competition with each other, and Chinese migrants and small traders built informal networks in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America. Chinese motives included securing resources, finding markets for industries with excess capacity, earning returns, and gaining diplomatic influence; host governments pursued their own aims, including financing for infrastructure that other lenders had not provided. The experience also produced friction over labor practices, environmental standards, and the terms of loans. By the early 2010s, a Chinese leadership that wanted to coordinate and rebrand this activity was ready for a larger framework.

76.38 The Launch of the Belt and Road Initiative in 2013

In September 2013, at Nazarbayev University in Kazakhstan, Xi proposed a "Silk Road Economic Belt," and in October, before Indonesia's parliament, a "Twenty-First-Century Maritime Silk Road." In 2015 the National Development and Reform Commission published an action plan, and in 2016 the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, headquartered in Beijing, began operations; a Silk Road Fund was established in 2014. Forums on the initiative convened in 2017, 2019, and 2023.

The initiative's early institutions were rapidly assembled. After the 2013 speeches, the Third Plenum endorsed opening to the west and the sea; the Silk Road Fund was created in 2014 with an initial capital of forty billion dollars; and in March 2015 the NDRC, the Foreign Ministry, and the Commerce Ministry issued the document "Vision and Actions on Jointly Building the Silk Road Economic Belt and 21st-Century Maritime Silk Road." The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, proposed by Xi in 2013 and opened in January 2016 with fifty-seven founding members including the United Kingdom, Germany, and France but not the United States or Japan, became a multilateral complement. The initiative was written into the Party constitution in 2017. Leaders' forums took place in 2017, 2019, and 2023, and the Chinese government says that well over a hundred countries and organizations have signed cooperation documents, though what such documents commit them to varies widely.

Scholars distinguish stated aims from underlying motives. Official materials emphasize "shared benefits," connectivity, and peaceful development. Analysts also cite motives such as stimulating growth in western provinces, using excess capacity in steel and construction, reducing reliance on the Strait of Malacca for energy imports (the "Malacca dilemma"), promoting internationalization of the renminbi, and building diplomatic goodwill and prestige. The invocation of the ancient Silk Road, including Zhang Qian (Chapter 16) and the voyages of Zheng He (Chapter 42), serves as a symbolic legitimacy narrative. It does not mean that modern projects follow the routes or the historical logic of earlier exchange, in which private merchants, Central Asian and Indian intermediaries, and multiple states took part. The initiative has also been loosely defined: as new projects and sectors were added, observers noted that almost any Chinese overseas activity could be described as part of it.

76.39 Overland Corridors, Railways, Ports, and Maritime Networks

The initiative is often described through economic corridors: the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor linking Xinjiang to the port of Gwadar, the China–Central Asia–West Asia corridor, the China–Indochina Peninsula corridor, the Bangladesh–China–India–Myanmar proposal, and the New Eurasian Land Bridge, along which freight trains run between Chinese and European cities, notably since about 2011. Maritime elements include ports in Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Greece, Djibouti, and elsewhere, some owned or operated by Chinese companies, and the railway linking Kunming and Vientiane, which opened in 2021, and the Jakarta–Bandung high-speed line, opened in 2023.

Selected projects illustrate the variety. The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, announced in 2015 with pledges of tens of billions of dollars for energy plants, roads, and the port of Gwadar, has had uneven results: it expanded generating capacity and transport but also created payment obligations for the Pakistani government, security concerns after attacks on Chinese workers, and local disputes in Balochistan. The Jakarta–Bandung high-speed railway, operating since October 2023, was completed after cost overruns and renegotiation of financing. In Africa, the Addis Ababa–Djibouti railway (2016) and the Mombasa–Nairobi Standard Gauge Railway (2017), built by Chinese firms with Chinese finance, improved capacity, while debate continues over their finances and ridership. In Europe, the Greek port of Piraeus, majority owned by COSCO since 2016, expanded container traffic, and a Budapest–Belgrade railway has been delayed by disputes over procurement.

Freight services linking Chinese and European cities began around 2011, with trains running between Chongqing and Duisburg; volumes grew greatly in the late 2010s and during the pandemic, with subsidies from Chinese local governments affecting the economics, and the war in Ukraine disrupted routes through Russia and Belarus. The initiative also includes military and strategic dimensions that officials do not emphasize: China opened its first overseas military support base in Djibouti in 2017, and analysts have debated whether commercial ports could be used for dual purposes. Evidence that any commercial port has been used militarily is limited and varies by case, and the historian's task is to separate possibility from documented use.

76.40 Development Finance, Sovereign Debt, and Project Governance

Lending was provided by policy banks, state commercial banks, and funds, usually on commercial or semi-concessional terms, with contracts often involving Chinese construction firms. Observers disagree sharply about consequences. Some analysts argued that lending created "debt traps"; later studies, including detailed research on Sri Lanka's Hambantota port, found that the debt crisis there owed more to the borrowing from international bond markets, while the port's 99-year lease in 2017 was a response to repayment difficulties. Other cases involved renegotiation, write-offs, and debt restructuring through the G20's Common Framework, in which China's role as a major creditor has been prominent. The World Bank's 2019 analysis of the initiative's economic corridors estimated potential gains from reduced trade costs but stressed risks in debt, governance, and environmental safeguards; these are modeled projections, not realized results.

Historical Evidence

Development Finance and Debt Sustainability

Evidence includes loan contracts (rarely public), central bank and ministry data, creditor databases compiled by research institutions, project audits, news investigations, and country studies. Because contract terms are often confidential and sources use different definitions of what counts as BRI lending, totals vary widely. Researchers compare commitments, disbursements, and repayments; they distinguish announced pledges from completed work. Claims that lending is intended as strategic leverage require evidence of intent that is generally difficult to document.

Several features of the lending model have been documented. Policy banks, led by the China Development Bank and the Export-Import Bank of China, were the main sources of long-term finance, supplemented by state commercial banks and, increasingly, by commercial and bilateral arrangements. The research institute AidData estimated that Chinese official lending to developing countries since 2000 totaled more than a trillion dollars and that the composition changed over time, with new lending falling after about 2016 and a growing share used for emergency "rescue" loans to countries in financial difficulty. A study of contracts found that many included confidentiality clauses and terms that gave Chinese lenders strong repayment priority through collateral accounts, features that differ from the norms of multilateral lending but are not unique to Chinese lenders. In the 2020s, China became one of the largest official creditors in debt negotiations, such as in Zambia, which defaulted in 2020 and reached restructuring arrangements in 2023, and in Sri Lanka's 2022 default.

The "debt-trap diplomacy" argument holds that China lends to countries knowing that they cannot repay in order to seize strategic assets. The evidence is contested. The most cited example, Sri Lanka's Hambantota port, was financed by Chinese loans, which constituted a minority of the country's overall debt, much of which was owed to international bond markets; in 2017 the government granted a ninety-nine-year lease of the port to a Chinese state-owned company in a deal to reduce debt, though researchers such as Deborah Brautigam argue that the lease was not a seizure and that Sri Lankan decisions were driven by domestic politics. Other cases show renegotiation: in 2019 Malaysia's government renegotiated a rail link and reduced its cost. Scholars also record real risks: opaque contracts, procurement favoring Chinese firms, environmental damage, and projects that were uneconomic. World Bank economists modeled in 2019 that the corridors could increase trade and reduce poverty if accompanied by reforms, and cautioned that debt vulnerabilities in some countries were serious; these were estimates and not outcomes. For students, the lesson is to ask for each project who borrowed, who lent, on what terms, and who decided.

76.41 The Digital Silk Road, Energy, Technology, and Green Development

The initiative's scope has widened to telecommunications networks, data centers, satellite navigation, e-commerce, and "green" projects. Chinese firms are major producers of solar panels, batteries, and electric vehicles, and have built renewable plants abroad, while some coal projects drew criticism; in 2021 Xi announced that China would stop building new coal-fired plants overseas. Technology standards, undersea cables, and 5G equipment became sites of competition with the United States and other governments, with security concerns about telecommunications vendors; companies and some governments dispute those concerns.

The scope of the initiative changed. The "Digital Silk Road," mentioned from about 2015 and promoted at the 2017 forum, includes telecommunications networks, undersea cables, data centers, satellite navigation (BeiDou), smart-city systems, and e-commerce platforms. Chinese firms such as Huawei and ZTE provided network equipment in many countries, and the United States and some allies restricted Huawei in 5G networks citing security risks, which Huawei and the Chinese government deny. Questions about data governance, surveillance technology, and standards have made the digital dimension one of the most contested parts of the initiative, with partner governments weighing cost and capability against concerns about dependence.

Energy and green projects were another change. At the United Nations in September 2021 Xi announced that China would stop building new coal-fired power projects abroad, and Chinese companies have since invested in solar, wind, hydropower, and battery supply chains across Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Chinese firms dominate several stages of solar-panel production, which has lowered costs for buyers, while also prompting trade measures by other producers. At the third Belt and Road Forum in October 2023, Xi announced eight actions, including support for smaller "small and beautiful" projects, signaling a shift from the large-scale infrastructure of the 2010s. This is a policy statement, and its results will be measured in later years.

76.42 Partner Countries, Regional Agency, and the International Evaluation of the BRI

Partner governments have their own aims: building ports, securing power supply, attracting investment, and balancing among external partners. Outcomes differ: some projects, such as railways in East Africa and Laos, expanded connectivity but with debt or ridership concerns; others were canceled or renegotiated after elections or disputes, as in Malaysia in 2018–2019. Italy joined the initiative in 2019 and informed China in late 2023 that it would not renew its participation; Panama announced its exit in early 2025. By the mid-2020s, observers reported a shift toward smaller, more selective projects.

Partner countries' experiences illustrate the importance of local agency. In Kazakhstan, Chinese investment in transport and energy was welcomed by the government but provoked public protests over land and migration in 2016 and 2019. In Malaysia, the 2018 government of Mahathir Mohamad suspended and renegotiated major projects it said had been negotiated on unfavorable terms by its predecessor. In Peru, the port of Chancay, built with Chinese investment and opened in November 2024, is intended to shorten shipping times to Asia. Hungary and Greece have welcomed Chinese investment while sometimes diverging from European Union positions. India declined to join, objecting in particular to the CPEC's passage through Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Competing initiatives followed: the EU's Global Gateway (2021) and the G7's Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment (2022) offer alternative frameworks, though their implementation also takes time.

Ordinary people encountered the initiative as workers, residents, and consumers. Chinese construction workers were employed abroad on many projects, while local employment varied by country and rules; farmers and residents sometimes lost land or livelihood to projects, while others gained access to roads, power, and markets. Chinese firms have been criticized for labor and environmental violations in some cases and praised for training and speed in others. At the political level, Italy, which joined in 2019, informed Beijing in late 2023 that it would not renew its participation, and Panama, which joined in 2017, announced its exit in early 2025, partly under American pressure, showing that participation can be reversed. Observers in the mid-2020s reported a shift toward smaller, more commercial projects, and changes in the composition of lending, though the data are partial. A fair assessment recognizes that outcomes are mixed and depend on the quality of project selection and governance on both sides.

76.43 China, Japan, Korea, ASEAN, and the Changing Asian Order

China is the largest trading partner of many Asian economies. Relations with Japan combine large commercial ties with disputes over history and over the Senkaku/Diaoyu islands, particularly after 2012; tensions rose again in late 2025 after remarks by Japan's prime minister about Taiwan. With South Korea, China's retaliation over the deployment of the US THAAD missile defense system in 2016–2017 showed how political disputes affect trade. With ASEAN, China concluded a free trade agreement in the early 2000s, and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, which includes China, Japan, South Korea, ASEAN members, Australia, and New Zealand, came into force in 2022. Vietnam, whose long history of both resistance and exchange with China appears throughout this textbook, combines trade ties with maritime disputes.

History weighs on regional relations. Chinese and Korean historians dispute the interpretation of early states such as Goguryeo (Chapter 24), which each side claims as part of its heritage, and public debates over the "Northeast Project" in the 2000s showed how ancient history can affect present politics. Memory of Japanese aggression (Chapter 64) and of Japan's colonial rule shapes attitudes in China and Korea, while Japanese debates about wartime responsibility and about textbooks and shrine visits recur. These are not only government issues: tourism, popular culture, and online communities carry their own emotions. At the same time, China is the largest trading partner of Japan, South Korea, and most of Southeast Asia, which gives all sides incentives for restraint.

Specific episodes show the pattern. In 2010, a collision between a Chinese fishing boat and Japanese coast guard vessels near the Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands led to a diplomatic crisis; in 2012, after Japan's government purchased three of the islands from a private owner, anti-Japanese protests occurred in many Chinese cities and Japanese companies were affected. In 2016–2017, after South Korea agreed to deploy the US THAAD missile-defense system, China restricted group tourism and targeted Korean companies such as Lotte. In Southeast Asia, the 2014 deployment of a Chinese drilling rig in waters claimed by Vietnam triggered riots against Chinese-owned factories in Vietnam. Relations with North Korea combine a 1961 treaty, a large share of the North's trade, and disagreements over its nuclear program. ASEAN, which includes states with diverse positions, tries to balance the great powers; its trade agreements with China and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership integrate production networks. Students should recall that earlier chapters showed a far more complex pattern than a simple "tributary system" of regional diplomacy (Chapters 27 and 42), and should apply the same care to the present.

76.44 The South China Sea and Maritime Sovereignty Disputes

China, Taiwan, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei claim parts of the South China Sea. China's claim is delimited on maps by a "nine-dash line" derived from a 1947 Republic of China map. Between 2013 and 2015 China built artificial islands on reefs in the Spratly Islands, adding airstrips and facilities. Fishing, energy resources, and shipping lanes carrying a substantial portion of global trade lend the area importance.

In 2013 the Philippines initiated arbitration under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. On July 12, 2016, the tribunal, constituted under Annex VII and administered by the Permanent Court of Arbitration, issued its award. China declined to participate or accept the award.

The legal background is specific. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), in force since 1994 and ratified by China in 1996, gives coastal states a territorial sea of up to twelve nautical miles and an exclusive economic zone of up to two hundred, but only "islands" that can sustain human habitation or economic life of their own generate the latter, while "rocks" generate only a territorial sea and low-tide elevations generate none. China's map of 1947, drawn by the Republic of China, depicted eleven dashes in the sea; the PRC later used nine, and in 2009 China submitted a map to the UN to protest other states' claims. Taiwan makes a similar claim and holds Itu Aba (Taiping), the largest natural feature in the Spratlys; Vietnam holds numerous features and has also built islands; the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei claim parts. Since 2013, China built artificial islands on seven reefs, adding runways and harbors, which in later years were equipped with defensive systems, although Xi said in 2015 that China did not intend to militarize them.

After a 2012 standoff at Scarborough Shoal, the Philippines initiated arbitration in January 2013. China declined to participate, citing its 2006 declaration excluding certain disputes from compulsory procedures and arguing that the case concerned sovereignty. The tribunal held that it had jurisdiction on several submissions. It found that China's claim to "historic rights" within the nine-dash line was incompatible with the Convention where it exceeded what UNCLOS allows; that features such as Scarborough Shoal and Mischief Reef did not generate an exclusive economic zone, and that Mischief Reef and Second Thomas Shoal lie within the Philippines' zone; that China had interfered with Philippine fishing and petroleum activities and caused severe harm to the marine environment through its reclamation. It also held that no feature in the Spratly Islands was a full island, including Itu Aba, a ruling that Taiwan rejected. It did not rule on sovereignty over land features. In Chinese law and diplomacy, China maintains its "territorial sovereignty and maritime rights" and says that the award is "null and void." Several states, including the United States, Australia, and Japan, urged compliance; others, including some ASEAN states, were reserved; and the dispute persisted.

Subsequent years brought incidents between Chinese coast guard and maritime militia vessels and Philippine ones, including water-cannon use and a collision at Second Thomas Shoal in June 2024, followed by a provisional arrangement in July 2024 on resupply of the Philippine outpost there; China's 2021 Coast Guard Law authorized use of force in certain circumstances. ASEAN and China have negotiated a code of conduct for years, with a target date of 2026 reported by participants, but its content and legal status remain unsettled. For local people, particularly fishing communities in the Philippines, Vietnam, and China itself, the dispute affects access to fishing grounds. The legal analysis, the diplomatic positions, and the physical activity on the water are three separate layers, and each requires its own evidence.

76.45 India, Central Asia, and China's Continental Relationships

The boundary between China and India was never fully delimited, and the countries fought a brief war in 1962 (Chapter 69). The Line of Actual Control separates their forces; disputes involve Aksai Chin in the west and Arunachal Pradesh in the east, which China calls South Tibet. Confrontations occurred at Doklam in 2017 and, on June 15, 2020, in the Galwan Valley, where a clash killed twenty Indian soldiers; China later acknowledged four PLA deaths. After further negotiations, an arrangement for patrolling at some friction points was announced in October 2024, and leaders met afterward, though troop deployments and mistrust remained. Economic ties remain large, while India restricted Chinese investment and applications after 2020. In Central Asia, China is a leading trade and investment partner and a participant in the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, founded in 2001, working alongside Russia, whose influence in the region is historically deeper.

The Sino-Indian boundary has three sectors: western (Aksai Chin, controlled by China and claimed by India), middle, and eastern (Arunachal Pradesh, controlled by India and claimed by China in part as "South Tibet," based in part on rejection of the 1914 McMahon Line). After the 1962 war, the two sides concluded agreements in 1993 and 1996 to maintain peace on the Line of Actual Control, whose precise alignment is not agreed. The 2017 confrontation at Doklam, near the tri-junction with Bhutan, lasted seventy-three days. In 2020, following Chinese deployments in Ladakh, a clash on June 15 in the Galwan Valley, fought with clubs and stones and without firearms in accordance with existing agreements, killed twenty Indian soldiers; China later acknowledged four deaths among its own personnel. Both countries massed forces, and India banned many Chinese applications and restricted some investment.

Negotiation followed. After rounds of military and diplomatic talks, disengagement agreements were reached at several friction points, and in October 2024 the two countries announced an arrangement on patrolling at Depsang and Demchok, followed by a meeting between Prime Minister Modi and Xi Jinping at Kazan. In 2025, Modi visited China for the SCO summit at Tianjin, and the two sides resumed some people-to-people links such as direct flights. These steps reduced tension without settling the boundary, and both sides retain large forces in the region; Indian concerns also include Chinese relations with Pakistan, activities in the Indian Ocean, and the Yarlung Tsangpo project on the river that becomes the Brahmaputra. Economic ties remain large, with a significant Indian trade deficit.

In Central Asia, the Shanghai Five arrangement of 1996 on border confidence-building became the SCO in 2001; Chinese border settlements with Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan in the 1990s and 2000s resolved long disputes through compromises. China has become a leading trading partner and investor, and in 2023 and 2025 summit meetings with the five Central Asian states were held in Xi'an and Astana; in December 2024 construction began on a railway through Kyrgyzstan to Uzbekistan. Russia retains security ties through the Collective Security Treaty Organization, and Central Asian governments pursue "multi-vector" policies. Local concerns about land, labor, and the treatment of ethnic Kazakhs in Xinjiang have at times affected public opinion. In Afghanistan, China has engaged with the Taliban government on security and mining.

76.46 China–United States Relations: Cooperation, Competition, and Technology

Relations between the two countries have passed through phases since the 1970s rapprochement (Chapter 70): normalization in 1979, expanded trade, China's WTO entry, and growing mutual dependence. From the 2010s, American policy increasingly emphasized strategic competition, and in 2018 the Trump administration began imposing tariffs, to which China responded; a "Phase One" agreement in January 2020 eased tensions. Export controls on advanced semiconductors and equipment were introduced in 2022 and expanded. Tariff escalation resumed in 2025; on October 30, 2025, Trump and Xi met in Busan and agreed on a one-year truce that lowered some US tariffs, delayed Chinese export restrictions on rare earths, and resumed Chinese purchases of American soybeans. Trump visited Beijing on May 14, 2026, in a summit that reaffirmed the truce without a comprehensive trade agreement, and Xi made a state visit to Washington on September 24–25, 2026, after which the two sides extended the truce, which had been due to expire in mid-November, to January 10, 2027, without resolving the central disputes over tariffs, technology, and rare earths. These reports come from recent news coverage and are subject to revision as official records become available.

Continuity and Change Over Time

China and the United States Since the 1970s

What changed? From strategic alignment against the Soviet Union (1970s) to deep economic integration (1990s–2000s) to a mixture of competition in technology, security, and diplomacy (2010s–2020s), with periodic truces.

What stayed the same? Both governments remain committed to avoiding direct war, to maintaining some economic ties, and to managing Taiwan through different formulations of policy.

Who experienced change most directly? Exporters, farmers, semiconductor firms, students and researchers, and communities dependent on trade.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that bilateral relations changed with domestic politics, relative power, and technology, and not along a single trajectory.

A longer view clarifies the changes. Diplomatic normalization in 1979 and the expansion of trade created a relationship in which American firms found low-cost production and Chinese exporters found a large market; after China's WTO entry in 2001, imports from China rose sharply, and economists such as David Autor and colleagues documented concentrated job losses in some American communities, a finding that shaped political debate. Security frictions also accumulated: the 1999 US bombing of the Chinese embassy in Belgrade, the 2001 aircraft collision near Hainan, cyber-espionage disputes, and, in the 2010s, disputes over the South China Sea and Taiwan. The Obama administration's "rebalance to Asia" and Xi's 2015 state visit were followed in 2017 by the Trump administration's national security strategy that described China as a strategic competitor, and in 2018 by tariffs under Section 301.

The Biden administration kept many tariffs and added technology restrictions: the CHIPS and Science Act (2022), and export controls on advanced semiconductors and chip-making equipment announced in October 2022 and expanded later. The shooting down of a Chinese balloon over the United States in February 2023 disrupted diplomacy, which resumed with the Biden–Xi meeting near San Francisco in November 2023. The second Trump administration imposed steep tariffs in 2025 that escalated to triple-digit rates on some goods in April, before a May truce in Geneva; China responded with tariffs and with restrictions on rare-earth exports, on which global supply chains depend, and the Busan meeting of October 30 produced the temporary truce described above. Two-way trade remained very large, and students from China continued to study in the United States, though their numbers fell from earlier peaks. The two militaries held talks and have also had risky encounters in the air and at sea. Americans' views of China became markedly more negative in the 2010s and 2020s in surveys, and Chinese views of the US also shifted, though with variation.

The pattern, as far as evidence shows, involves both sides' calculations of cost and benefit. Both governments have shown willingness to use trade and technology controls as instruments, and both have also stepped back when escalation became costly, as the truces suggest. Specific outcomes, including the terms of any lasting agreement, remain unknown. A historian should therefore ask what is known: which tariffs were imposed on which dates, what controls were adopted, and what each government says it wants.

76.47 China and Russia: Strategic Cooperation and the War in Ukraine

After the Sino-Soviet split (Chapter 69), relations were restored in the late 1980s and developed through border settlements, arms sales, energy trade, and the SCO. In February 2022, shortly before Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Xi and Putin issued a joint statement saying that their friendship has "no limits." China has declined to condemn the invasion, called for negotiations, and criticized sanctions, while supplying Russia with consumer and industrial goods and buying energy; Western governments accused Chinese firms of supplying dual-use goods, which Beijing denies being state policy. The relationship is asymmetric: China's economy is far larger, and Russia depends heavily on the Chinese market. The war complicated China's relations with Europe, which is a major trading partner. Whether the partnership is an alliance, an alignment of convenience, or something in between is debated by analysts.

The relationship has a long and uneven history. The 1950 Treaty of Friendship and Alliance gave way to the Sino-Soviet split and border clashes in 1969 (Chapter 69); normalization in 1989 was followed by a "strategic partnership" in 1996, the 2001 Treaty of Good-Neighborliness, and the 2004 border settlement. Trade expanded, particularly in energy: a 2014 agreement for natural gas through the Power of Siberia pipeline led to deliveries from 2019, and bilateral trade reached a very high level after 2022 as Russia lost European markets. Military cooperation includes joint exercises and arms trade historically dominated by Russian sales to China, a balance that has changed as Chinese industry has developed.

After the February 24, 2022, invasion of Ukraine, China's government described the war without using the term "invasion," opposed sanctions, abstained on several UN General Assembly votes, and in February 2023 published a position paper calling for dialogue; Xi visited Moscow in March 2023. Chinese firms exported machine tools, electronics, and vehicles that support Russia's economy, and Western governments sanctioned some Chinese entities, alleging dual-use transfers. Chinese banks were cautious about secondary sanctions. The relationship is asymmetric: Russia depends on China's market and technology, while China's trade with the EU and US is far larger than with Russia. The EU, which in 2019 had described China as "partner, competitor, and systemic rival," found its relationship further strained by the war, in addition to trade disputes over electric-vehicle subsidies and tariffs in 2024. In 2025, Putin attended the September 3 parade in Beijing and the SCO summit at Tianjin. Analysts disagree on the nature of the partnership, which is best examined through specific areas such as trade, diplomacy, and technology.

76.48 China and Global Institutions: Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Multilateral Diplomacy

China holds a permanent seat on the UN Security Council, has been a member of the WTO since 2001, and participates in the G20 and BRICS, which expanded in 2024. It is a major contributor to UN peacekeeping and one of the largest trading partners of African and Latin American states. Beijing has launched diplomatic frameworks, including the Global Development Initiative (2021), the Global Security Initiative (2022), and the Global Civilization Initiative (2023), and in 2023 helped broker a Saudi–Iran agreement to restore relations. The Forum on China–Africa Cooperation, founded in 2000, convenes leaders periodically.

China and the Wider World

International Institutions and the Global South

China's engagement is an active process, not only a reaction: it builds institutions, offers finance, and proposes norms. Governments of the Global South respond according to their interests, seeking trade, loans, and technology, and in some cases worrying about dependence or environmental and labor practices. Views of China differ across regions and over time, as shown in surveys and government choices. For historians, the lesson parallels earlier eras: Chinese states, from the Han to the Ming, interacted with partners whose own agency shaped the outcomes.

China has long used multilateral institutions in selective ways. As a permanent member of the UN Security Council since 1971, it has used its veto less often than some other permanent members but has done so on issues such as Syria and sanctions; it is the largest contributor of peacekeeping troops among the permanent members. In the WTO, China has been both a beneficiary and a target of complaints about subsidies and state enterprises, and has insisted on developing-country status. It helped found the BRICS group of emerging economies (2009), whose expansion in 2024 included Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and the UAE, and the New Development Bank (2015). In 2021–2023 it launched the Global Development, Security, and Civilization Initiatives, and in 2025 proposed a Global Governance Initiative, which the government presents as an alternative framework that respects sovereignty and development priorities. Critics note the vagueness of these proposals; supporters note that they reflect demand in many countries for greater voice.

In Africa, China has been the largest bilateral trading partner for over a decade, and the Forum on China–Africa Cooperation (founded 2000) has become a regular channel for investment and loans; many African states also criticize debt terms and labor practices and negotiate with other partners, including the Gulf states, India, Turkey, and the EU. In Latin America, Chinese demand for soybeans, copper, lithium, and oil transformed trade, and investments in ports and energy have expanded. In the Middle East, China imports large quantities of oil and mediated in March 2023 an agreement for Saudi Arabia and Iran to restore diplomatic relations. China's positions on conflicts in the region, including Gaza, emphasize ceasefires and criticism of US policy. These activities show that China acts as a diplomatic and economic player, while its influence in security terms is more limited than its economic reach. Each country's choices matter: the Global South is not a single bloc.

76.49 Economic Growth, Property Markets, Local Debt, and Financial Vulnerability

Growth moderated after the double-digit rates of earlier decades. In 2020 regulators introduced limits on developer borrowing (the "three red lines"); property developers including Evergrande defaulted from 2021, and a prolonged downturn in housing affected household wealth, local government land revenue, and construction employment. Local governments' financing vehicles accumulated substantial debt, and a multiyear debt-swap program was announced in late 2024. Household consumption remains a smaller share of the economy than in many comparable states, and officials have repeatedly pledged to raise it. The government set a 2026 growth target of 4.5 to 5 percent, a modest reduction from previous years. Youth unemployment statistics were suspended in 2023 and resumed with a revised method in 2024, which illustrates how data definitions matter. Economists disagree on the depth of the problems and the best remedies.

Historical Evidence

Growth, Debt, Population, and Energy Challenges

Statistics on GDP, debt, population, and energy come from the National Bureau of Statistics, central bank, ministries, international organizations such as the IMF, World Bank, and UN, and independent researchers. Official statistics are essential but may be revised; independent estimates may differ in definitions. Students should record year, source, definition, and whether a figure is measured, estimated, or projected, and should treat targets in plans as intentions.

The pressures have specific origins. For two decades, rapid urbanization and rising incomes made real estate a primary form of household saving and local government revenue: land-use rights sales supplied a large share of local budgets. The 2020 "three red lines" restricted developers' borrowing, and sales and prices fell, with Evergrande defaulting in 2021 (and ordered into liquidation by a Hong Kong court in January 2024) and Country Garden facing a similar crisis. Home buyers who had paid for unfinished apartments organized mortgage boycotts in 2022. Local governments, which depend on land sales and on borrowing through financing vehicles, faced difficult budgets; in November 2024 the NPC Standing Committee approved a package of about ten trillion yuan over several years to swap hidden local debt into formal bonds. Household consumption is roughly forty percent of GDP, lower than in most large economies, and in the mid-2020s consumer prices were close to flat, which analysts linked to weak demand and excess capacity.

The external sector has been strong: exports of vehicles, batteries, solar panels, and machinery pushed the trade surplus to near or above one trillion dollars in 2024–2025, prompting complaints about overcapacity from the EU, the US, and emerging economies, some of which imposed tariffs. Policy debates inside China involve how much to stimulate consumption, how to reduce local debt, how to manage the property sector without systemic failure, and how to support private firms. Economists differ on the severity of the risks: some foresee a prolonged slowdown similar to Japan's after 1990; others point to China's capacity for policy action and large savings. Because the data are partly official, partly estimated, and sometimes revised, students should cite the year and source each time they use a statistic.

76.50 Population Aging, Labor, Education, and Social Inequality

China's population began to decline in 2022 after peaking the year before, and births have been below ten million a year since that year, according to official statistics. The labor force is shrinking and aging, and a rising share of the population is older than sixty. The government ended its one-child policy in 2016, permitted three children in 2021, and has expanded childcare subsidies and support, while raising the statutory retirement age in phases beginning in 2025. Large cohorts of university graduates have faced a difficult job market. Regional and urban–rural inequality persists, linked to hukou residency restrictions described in Chapter 75. The implications include pressure on pension and health finance, on local government budgets, and on the defense of the idea that continued prosperity will legitimate the political order.

Demography is slow-moving and therefore more predictable than politics, though not entirely. China's population of about 1.41 billion has been falling since 2022; births fell from roughly seventeen million in 2016 to under ten million a year from 2022, and total fertility is estimated at around one birth per woman, among the lowest in the world. More than a fifth of the population is aged sixty or over. The government raised the statutory retirement age in a gradual program beginning in 2025 (from sixty to sixty-three for most men, and for women from fifty or fifty-five to fifty-five or fifty-eight), and announced childcare subsidies in 2025. Demographers disagree on whether such measures can raise fertility significantly, since evidence from other East Asian societies suggests that costs of housing, education, and work hours shape decisions.

The labor market adds pressure. Record cohorts of graduates, more than twelve million a year, have entered an economy in which firms in technology, education, and property have cut hiring; in 2023 the unemployment rate for ages sixteen to twenty-four exceeded twenty percent before the publication was suspended and resumed with a method that excludes students. Public pension and health systems are unequal: urban employees receive far higher pensions than rural residents, and hukou restrictions still limit migrants' access to services in large cities (Chapter 75). These facts connect to politics in several ways, including budget pressures on local governments, the rising role of the state in welfare, and the government's emphasis on "common prosperity."

Everyday Life and Social History

How Governance, Security, and Global Ties Shaped Daily Life

A student who studies only institutions would miss how these changes appeared in households. A graduate in Chengdu used a smartphone for payments, rent, and a health code during the pandemic, and in 2023 found that job offers in technology had fallen. A migrant worker in a Guangdong factory depended on export orders that rose and fell with tariff announcements, and on a hukou that determined whether her child could attend a public school in the city. A farmer in Henan saw land consolidation, rural pensions that remained low, and a son working away from home. In Hong Kong, a family weighed the British National (Overseas) visa route or moving to Singapore, Canada, or the mainland Greater Bay Area, while others chose to stay and adapt to new rules. In Taiwan, a young man faced a one-year military service obligation from 2024, while a technology worker in Hsinchu sold components to global customers. In Kashgar or Ürümqi, a Uyghur family dealt with checkpoints, Mandarin-language schooling, and relatives detained or abroad, while a Han trader in the same city might experience the changes quite differently. In Lhasa, a Tibetan family might send children to a state boarding school and also visit a monastery under supervision. In Laos or Kenya, a local worker on a Chinese-built railway earned wages that otherwise were not available, and a displaced villager lost land. In Iowa or Brazil, soybean farmers watched Chinese purchases change with the trade truce. Each case shows that the same national policies and international relations changed lives unequally, by place, class, gender, age, and ethnicity, and that sources for these experiences include surveys, ethnography, oral history, journalism, and testimony, each with biases and gaps.

76.51 Energy, Environmental Pressures, and National Security

China is the largest consumer of coal and a very large importer of oil and gas, so energy security is a strategic concern as well as an economic one. It also leads the world in installed solar and wind capacity and in electric vehicle production. In 2020 Xi announced that China aimed to peak carbon dioxide emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality before 2060; in September 2025 the government announced a target of reducing economy-wide net greenhouse-gas emissions by 7 to 10 percent from their peak levels by 2035, a stated target and not an achieved outcome. Environmental protection, water scarcity in the north, food security, and air quality are areas of policy. The tension between coal as a source of energy security and decarbonization goals shapes energy policy, and observers differ on how rapidly emissions will fall.

Energy policy shows how constraints interact. Coal still supplies more than half of China's primary energy and a majority of electricity, and approvals of new coal plants rose in 2022–2024 as provinces sought supply security after power shortages in 2021 and drought-related hydropower shortfalls in 2022. At the same time, China installs more solar and wind capacity each year than the rest of the world combined, electric vehicles account for a large share of new car sales (more than forty percent in 2024), and analysts reported that emissions flattened or fell in some periods of 2024–2025, though the cause and durability are debated. The 2060 carbon-neutrality goal and the 2035 target announced in 2025 provide direction; the national emissions trading market, launched in 2021 for the power sector, is being expanded.

Security considerations also shape choices. China imports most of its oil, about three-quarters by some estimates, and a large amount of natural gas; the leadership has pursued pipelines from Russia, Central Asia, and Myanmar, strategic reserves, and domestic production, alongside concern about the Strait of Malacca. Water scarcity in the north led to the South–North Water Transfer Project, whose central route opened in 2014. A Food Security Law, effective in 2024, reflects concern about grain supplies and imports of soybeans. Air quality improved substantially after the 2013 Air Pollution Action Plan, reducing PM2.5 concentrations in major cities, an achievement that illustrates the state's ability to act when it prioritizes a goal. Environmental researchers and citizens, however, point to continuing problems with soil and water contamination and to the dependence of many regions on heavy industry.

76.52 Technological Self-Reliance, Industrial Strategy, and the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan

Industrial policy since "Made in China 2025" (announced 2015) aims at higher-value manufacturing, while US export controls and restrictions on advanced chips have strengthened the emphasis on self-reliance. Chinese firms advanced in electric vehicles, batteries, telecommunications, and artificial intelligence; the release of the DeepSeek model in January 2025 drew attention to Chinese capabilities. The Fifteenth Five-Year Plan, adopted by the NPC on March 12, 2026, following recommendations by the Party's Fourth Plenum in October 2025, makes technological self-reliance a defining priority and stresses advanced industry, "AI Plus," domestic demand, social welfare, security, and green development. The accompanying work report proposed 109 major projects and set no fixed five-year growth rate, leaving annual targets to be determined according to conditions.

Industrial policy has a long history, from the Five-Year Plans of the 1950s (Chapter 67) to the reform-era strategy of targeting sectors. In October 2025 China used export controls on rare earths as leverage in the trade dispute, demonstrating its own capacity to restrict supply chains.

Previous plans offer a guide: the Fourteenth Five-Year Plan (2021–2025) met many targets, such as infrastructure and renewable capacity, while missing or revising others, such as the share of urban hukou residents or specific consumption goals. For historians, the plan is a source about intentions; assessing results will require data after 2030.

76.53 Historical Continuity, Change, and the Limits of Predicting China's Future

It is tempting to read the present through patterns from earlier chapters: a strong center and a diverse periphery, the importance of bureaucratic discipline, recurring fiscal strain, a state concerned with legitimacy and frontiers. Such comparisons can clarify questions. They can also mislead. The "dynastic cycle," as this textbook has argued, is not an explanation; the modern PRC has a party, a constitution, a national economy, nuclear weapons, a digital information environment, and a membership in international institutions that no earlier state had. Economic growth has not mechanically produced political liberalization, nor has centralization eliminated local variation.

Historical Debate

What Can Chinese History Tell Us About the Future?

The question: Does the long history of Chinese states reveal patterns that help predict contemporary developments?

Interpretation A: Historical patterns, such as the recurring tension between center and region, fiscal strain at the end of long periods of growth, and the use of ideology for legitimacy, provide useful analogies for understanding stress points.
Interpretation B: The modern state differs so fundamentally that analogies risk misleading; prediction from history ignores contingency, technology, and global conditions.

Evidence: Comparative studies of states and empires, quantitative social science, and the textbook's own cases of rise, division, reconstruction, and change.

Why it matters: Policy discussion of China often relies on historical analogy, whether to Chinese dynasties, to the Soviet Union, to Imperial Germany, or to Japan. Analogies are tools, not proofs.

Student task: Choose one analogy and list three ways it fits and three ways it fails.

Three arguments help avoid misuse of history. First, the "dynastic cycle" notion, whereby dynasties rise through virtue and fall through corruption, is a pattern in traditional historiography and not an explanation (Chapter 1); this textbook has shown how particular fiscal, military, environmental, and political conditions explained transitions such as those of the Han, Tang, Ming, and Qing. Second, analogies draw attention to some features and hide others: comparisons with the late Qing (fiscal strain, foreign pressure) overlook the very different industrial base and state capacity; comparisons with the Soviet Union, a reference used by Chinese leaders themselves in warnings about ideological collapse, overlook differences in economy and politics; comparisons with the "Thucydides trap" between rising and established powers, popularized by Graham Allison, have been criticized by historians as selective. Third, the modern PRC is a different type of state, with a mass party, a national bureaucracy, a nuclear arsenal, a digital information system, and an economy of continental scale integrated with the world.

History nevertheless suggests questions to ask. How does the center obtain reliable information from local agents? How are fiscal resources shared among levels of government? How does the state treat diverse regions and peoples? How are leaders selected and succession handled? What ideas legitimate authority and who can contest them? How do external pressures and internal reform interact? Contingency also matters: the 1911 uprising, the death of Mao in 1976, the events of 1989, and the protests of 2022 each shows that outcomes arose from decisions and circumstances that analysts at the time did not foresee. Students should therefore treat any claim about China's future as a hypothesis whose evidence should be examined, and should be wary of both triumphalist and catastrophic narratives.

76.54 Chapter Review and China in Historical Perspective

The concluding section asks students to look back across the book. The governing question is: how have geography, political institutions, cultural diversity, economic systems, international relationships, and competing understandings of legitimacy shaped Chinese history, and what can these historical patterns help us understand, without claiming to predict, about China in the twenty-first century?

Four kinds of continuity can be distinguished. First, geographic and environmental: the Yellow and Yangtze river systems, the north–south agricultural contrast, the Inner Asian frontier, and the southeast coast (Chapters 2 to 4) still structure regional economies, water policy, and migration. Second, institutional: a tradition of hierarchical bureaucracy and the problem of controlling local agents, from the Qin commanderies to the cadre system (Chapters 13, 15, 26, 41). Third, cultural: the Chinese writing system, family and educational values, and diverse religious and philosophical traditions (Chapters 11, 12, 23, 34). Fourth, the diversity of Chinese space: Tibetan, Mongol, Uyghur, Manchu, Han, and many other communities have shaped what "China" means (Chapters 35, 38, 46 to 48).

Four kinds of change are equally important. The Chinese state became a modern nation-state with citizenship, a constitution, and international legal obligations. The Party-state, a twentieth-century Leninist invention, has no imperial precedent. The economy is an industrial and digital one that is deeply connected to the global economy. And the state is accountable to, and compared against, international norms and institutions as no earlier Chinese state was.

Textbook-Wide Historical Synthesis

Institutions, Regions, Ideas, and Global Connections

Compare the Han commandery system (Chapter 13), the Tang examination system and law (Chapters 25 and 26), the Song civil state (Chapter 31), the Qing banner and frontier administration (Chapters 46 to 48), and the modern cadre system. In each case ask: how did the center select and monitor officials, how did it finance itself, how did it govern diverse regions, and what ideas justified its rule? Compare the Silk Roads (Chapters 16 and 27), the Song and Ming maritime worlds (Chapters 37 and 42), the Canton system (Chapter 51), and the Belt and Road, asking who participated, who benefited, and who set terms. Compare nineteenth-century imperialism with twenty-first-century finance and diplomacy, noting differences in power and context. These comparisons show continuities and differences, not equivalence.

The comparison across the book's parts shows several recurring tensions, each of which took a different form in each period. The first concerns environment and region. Part I's study of the Yellow and Yangtze basins and the contrast between north and south, steppe and farmland, explains persistent differences in agriculture, trade, and identity; in the twenty-first century these appear in the geography of industry, water policy, and inequality between coastal and inland provinces. The second concerns unity and division. The long periods of division, such as the Three Kingdoms, the Northern and Southern Dynasties, the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, and the multi-state world of Song, Liao, Western Xia, and Jin (Parts IV and VI), were not merely interruptions: they produced religious, economic, and institutional change and showed that political unity and cultural continuity were different things. This matters for understanding why the modern PRC's emphasis on national unity, and the competing claims over Taiwan, Hong Kong, Tibet, and Xinjiang, are arguments about the meaning of "China" itself.

The third tension concerns the state and its agents. The Qin and Han commandery system, the Tang and Song examination order, the Ming and Qing bureaucracies, and the Republican and Communist party-states all faced the problem of controlling local officials, financing the center, and absorbing diverse populations (Parts III, V to VIII, X, and XI). In the empires of the Mongols and Manchus (Parts VII and VIII), rulers combined Chinese bureaucratic methods with distinct institutions for Inner Asian peoples, a pattern that scholars cite when they discuss the legacy of the Qing frontier for the modern state; but the nation-state, citizenship, and modern technology make the present different. The fourth concerns economy and the wider world. From the Silk Roads to the Song maritime economy, the Ming silver trade, the Canton system, and the treaty ports, to the reform-era integration and the Belt and Road, China's history includes both initiative and vulnerability in global exchange, and the Opium Wars were as much about Qing and British choices as about structural forces (Parts III to IX). The fifth tension concerns ideas, legitimacy, and memory. Confucian, Daoist, Buddhist, and other traditions, imperial ritual, the Mandate of Heaven, revolutionary ideology, and patriotic education each gave answers to why rulers should rule, and each was contested.

A sixth concerns accountability and suffering. The textbook has shown that the Taiping and Mid-Century rebellions, the wars of the twentieth century, the Great Leap Forward famine, and the Cultural Revolution caused enormous loss, and that reform and growth lifted hundreds of millions from poverty. A balanced history keeps both in view. The final synthesis question therefore asks students to use these patterns with discipline: to identify genuine continuities, to name what is new, to distinguish evidence from assertion, and to say what remains unknown.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the People's Republic of China from about 2000 to November 2026. Its central argument is that the Communist Party, the formal institutions of the state, the armed forces, local governments, and diverse societies are related but not interchangeable, and that understanding contemporary China requires keeping them analytically distinct.

The Jiang and Hu eras combined rapid growth with collective leadership norms and unresolved problems of corruption, debt, and inequality. Under Xi Jinping, who became General Secretary in November 2012 and president in March 2013, authority was centralized through an anti-corruption campaign, expanded Party commissions, restructuring in 2018 and 2023, an ideological framework centered on national rejuvenation, and the removal in 2018 of the constitutional two-term limit on the presidency. That amendment applied to the state offices; it did not itself alter Party rules, but it affected the informal norm of orderly succession. Discipline campaigns reached the highest ranks of the armed forces: at the Fifth Plenum of October 26–29, 2026, the Party confirmed the expulsion of 33 senior officials, including former Central Military Commission vice chairman Zhang Youxia, and elected Zhang Shengmin to the Politburo. Historians continue to debate whether centralization improves coordination or narrows feedback.

Historical memory became a prominent element of legitimacy. The narrative of the century of humiliation and national rejuvenation draws on real events, but it simplifies the experiences of diverse people and is contested by historians and communities. Patriotic education, commemorations, and media regulation express the state's priorities while public attitudes remain varied.

Hong Kong's trajectory shows how the same constitutional framework can be interpreted differently. The Basic Law promised autonomy and an ultimate aim of universal suffrage; the 2014 Umbrella Movement, 2019 protests, 2020 National Security Law, and 2024 Article 23 ordinance were distinct stages in a changing relationship. The February 2026 sentencing of Jimmy Lai to twenty years after conviction for collusion with foreign forces and conspiracy to publish seditious material was the most prominent application of the 2020 law. The Hong Kong and Beijing governments say that the measures restored stability and uphold the rule of law; critics, including several foreign governments, say that they reduced political pluralism and autonomy.

Taiwan has been governed separately from the mainland since 1949, developed a democracy, and sustained a distinct identity. Beijing's one-China principle, the American one-China policy, and Taiwan's own positions differ. Military pressure from the PLA grew, notably in exercises after 2022 and Justice Mission 2025, while economic ties and domestic Taiwanese politics shaped the landscape. No outcome is historically determined.

Tibet and Xinjiang have distinct histories. Tibet's relationship to earlier Chinese states was varied and is interpreted differently by Beijing and Tibetan exiles; its religion, language, education, and the succession of the Dalai Lama are central contemporary issues. Xinjiang's history of oasis states, Qing conquest, and twentieth-century incorporation preceded the security policies of the 2010s, including mass detention, assessed by the UN Human Rights Office in 2022 as possibly constituting international crimes. The Chinese government disputes the characterization. Different sources have different evidentiary status, and this textbook treats them accordingly.

The Belt and Road Initiative, announced in 2013, extended earlier overseas investment into a diplomatic and financial framework. Its results vary by project and country; partner governments are active participants; debt, governance, and environmental questions are real but not uniform, and the "debt trap" thesis is contested by researchers.

Relations with Asia, the United States, Russia, and global institutions combine interdependence and competition. The US–China relationship moved from engagement to competition with periodic truces, as in October 2025 and the summits of May and September 2026; the South China Sea dispute illustrates the difference between a tribunal's findings and a party's rejection of them; the India border, Japan, and the Russia partnership each have their own dynamics.

Structural challenges include slower growth, property and local-debt problems, population decline and aging, energy and environmental pressure, and technology competition. The Fifteenth Five-Year Plan sets targets, which are plans and not outcomes.

Finally, the chapter has argued that history clarifies questions but cannot predict. The institutions and choices of earlier periods shaped constraints and opportunities, yet the future remains contingent. Students who have read this textbook should be able to say what has persisted, what is new, and what is uncertain.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter, and the textbook, ends not with a conclusion about China's destiny but with a set of questions about how political authority, territory, memory, and international relations are shaped by choices made under constraints. The institutions described here are the product of the twentieth-century revolution described in Chapters 58 to 70 and of the reform era described in Chapters 71 to 75; they also draw on deeper habits of administration and legitimation that earlier chapters documented.

The chapter's themes reflect those of the whole work: the formation and transformation of political authority; the relation of centers to diverse regions; the role of ideas and historical memory; the organization of economic and social life; and China's connections to neighboring societies and the wider world. Hong Kong, Taiwan, Tibet, and Xinjiang show that the meaning of "China" remains contested, as Chapter 1 argued.

Understanding Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, and unfinished means resisting both celebration and caricature. It means asking what evidence supports a claim, whose perspective it reflects, and what remains uncertain. Students who carry these habits forward will be equipped to read the next chapter of China's history as it is written, and to recognize that it has not been written yet.
