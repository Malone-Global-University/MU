Society, Economy, and Culture in Republican China

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Republican China was not one society. It was a cotton mill in Shanghai and a tenant's plot in Hunan, a university seminar in Beiping and a village market where nobody could read the newspaper, a cinema showing a new film and a temple fair that followed the agricultural calendar.

Imagine three people on the same day in the early 1930s. A young woman leaves a crowded lane house in Shanghai before dawn to begin a long shift in a cotton mill, where a labor contractor decides whether she keeps her place. A professor at a university in Beiping prepares a lecture drawing on scholarship from Tokyo, Berlin, and Chicago, while students debate the future of the nation. Far inland, a tenant household weighs how much of its harvest must go to a landlord, how much to a moneylender, and how much can be sold at the market town to buy salt, cloth, and kerosene. All three lived in the Republic of China. Their experiences of that Republic had little in common.

This chapter studies the economic, social, intellectual, and cultural lives of Chinese people during the Republican era, concentrating on the years from 1912 to 1937 and especially on the 1920s and 1930s. Where Chapter 62 explained the Nationalist state, its institutions, fiscal policy, and conflict with the Communists, this chapter explains society: how people worked, married, studied, migrated, fell ill, read, and spent money, and how institutions and government policies reached them, or did not.

The period mattered because several long-developing changes accelerated together. Modern factories, banks, and department stores appeared in treaty ports and other cities. Universities, professional associations, newspapers, and publishing houses created new communities of expertise and debate. Ideas about marriage, women's education, and individual choice, which grew from the New Culture and May Fourth movements (Chapter 60), entered law and everyday argument. Film, radio, and advertising created forms of popular culture that crossed regional boundaries. At the same time, an enormous rural majority continued to depend on household labor, kinship networks, and local markets, and a global depression in the 1930s exposed how fragile many livelihoods were.

The central argument is that Republican China experienced significant but uneven transformation. New opportunities were real, but they were distributed unequally by class, gender, occupation, and region. Republican modernity was not a single movement from "tradition" to "modernity" but a set of overlapping, contested, regionally varied changes that created new possibilities without eliminating older inequalities and social structures. Students should therefore resist equating Republican China with Shanghai, or modernization with Westernization.

Historians reconstruct this social history from sources very different from court chronicles: factory inspection reports, village surveys by Chinese and foreign scholars, school and university records, municipal statistics, newspapers and magazines, advertisements, films, novels, personal memoirs, oral histories collected decades later, and the writings of social investigators. Each source has limits. Surveys were often conducted in particular regions and sometimes with political purposes; urban media overrepresented the literate and affluent; and later memories were shaped by the revolutions that followed.

What continued? Household and kin-based economic organization, the importance of local markets, the authority of senior family members, regional dialects and customs, and popular religion all remained central. What changed? Wage labor, mass-circulation print, new professions, and public debates about gender and the family altered the possibilities open to many people, especially in cities. The chapter also situates China in wider currents: Japanese and Western investment and ideas, the global silver market, migration to Southeast Asia and Manchuria, and international film and consumer culture. It ends on the eve of the full-scale Japanese invasion of 1937 (Chapter 64), which would transform nearly every pattern described here.

  10. Distinguish modernization from Westernization, legal reform from social practice, and industrial growth from general prosperity.
  11. Interpret primary sources and statistical evidence critically, identifying creator, purpose, and omissions.
  12. Connect Republican social history to late Qing precedents and to the wartime changes described in Chapter 64.

Historical Context

Republican society was built on the foundations of the late Qing. By the end of the nineteenth century, the treaty-port system (Chapter 52) had opened cities such as Shanghai, Tianjin, Hankou, and Guangzhou to foreign residence and trade, creating enclaves governed under special legal arrangements. Self-Strengthening enterprises (Chapter 54), railways, telegraphs, and shipping lines had begun to link regions. The late Qing New Policies (Chapter 56) abolished the examination system in 1905, sent students abroad, and encouraged new-style schools, chambers of commerce, and professional associations. The Republic of 1912 inherited these institutions but not a strong central authority; the Beiyang governments and the warlord era (Chapters 58–59) meant that local elites, merchant groups, and regional militarists often shaped social life as much as national policy.

Economic circumstances changed in uneven stages. The First World War reduced European competition and increased demand for some Chinese goods, helping Chinese-owned textile, flour, and other light industries expand in the later 1910s and early 1920s. Nationalist boycotts of foreign goods after the May Fourth Movement and the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 encouraged "national products" campaigns. After 1927 the Nanjing government (Chapter 62) pursued tariff autonomy, banking reform, and infrastructure projects, but its direct reach into villages was limited, and in the 1930s the world depression, Japanese seizure of Manchuria in 1931, and a silver outflow brought new strains.

Geography remained decisive. Chinese society differed sharply between the densely populated, commercialized lower Yangtze delta; the wheat- and millet-growing north China plain; the rice regions of the south and the Sichuan basin; the southeast coast with its overseas networks; the semi-arid northwest; and borderlands including Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet, where administrative control, settlement, and social organization followed different patterns. "China" in this chapter means the territory claimed by the Republic, but its effective control varied considerably between regions and over time, and Taiwan was under Japanese colonial rule, a status that gave its society a distinct trajectory.

Intellectually, the New Culture and May Fourth movements had questioned inherited authority in family, language, and politics (Chapter 60), but ideas did not translate automatically into practice. Confucian family ethics, lineage organizations, Buddhist and Daoist practice, and local temple religion continued to structure daily life, while Christian missions, Western-style hospitals, and schools introduced additional institutions. Students should keep two distinctions in mind throughout: between modernization (the spread of particular institutions, technologies, and practices) and Westernization (the adoption of foreign models, which was only one of several routes), and between what laws or reformers declared and what people actually did.

Division I — Cities, Urbanization, and the Social Geography of Republican China

Section 63.1

Population, Urbanization, and Social Change

China's population in the Republican period is usually estimated at somewhere around 450 to 500 million people, though no reliable national census existed, and estimates by officials, missionaries, and scholars differed. The great majority, probably well over three-quarters, lived in rural settlements and small market towns. Even the most urbanized provinces were dominated by agricultural labor. This fact is the starting point for any account of Republican society: change in the cities was dramatic and visible, but it affected a minority.

Urban growth was nonetheless significant. Shanghai grew to several million people by the 1930s. Tianjin, Guangzhou, Beiping, Nanjing, and Wuhan each reached populations in the hundreds of thousands to over a million, depending on how boundaries and metropolitan areas were counted. Growth came largely from migration, because urban death rates were high and many migrants were men who left families in villages. Cities were also very different from one another: some were treaty ports with foreign administrations, others were provincial capitals, educational centers, or industrial towns.

Regional differences shaped demography and society. The lower Yangtze delta combined dense population, commercial agriculture, silk and cotton handicrafts, and proximity to Shanghai's markets. North China's drier plains supported wheat, millet, and sorghum farming, with higher risks of drought and famine. The southeast coast, especially Fujian and Guangdong, sent large numbers of emigrants abroad. Sichuan and the southwest, remote from the coast until later, had their own commercial networks and political fragmentation. Manchuria received millions of agricultural migrants from Shandong and Hebei, as discussed in Section 63.13.

Historical Vocabulary
Modernization

Modernization in this chapter refers to the spread of institutions and practices such as factory production, wage labor, banking, modern schools, professional associations, mass print media, and public health systems. It is not identical to Westernization, because Chinese reformers drew models from Japan, the Soviet Union, Europe, and the United States, and also adapted existing Chinese institutions such as guilds, lineages, and native banks. Historians also stress that modernization was not a single path, and that "modern" and "traditional" practices often coexisted and reshaped each other.

Section 63.2

Shanghai: The Treaty-Port Metropolis

Shanghai in the 1920s and 1930s was divided among three jurisdictions: the International Settlement, governed mainly by a municipal council with British, American, and Japanese influence; the French Concession, administered by France; and the Chinese-administered districts, including Nanshi, Zhabei, and, after 1927, the municipal government of "Greater Shanghai." Residents moved between these jurisdictions daily, but laws, policing, taxation, and political activity differed from one to another. Chinese political groups, newspapers, and publishers exploited these divisions, because authorities in one jurisdiction could not always act in another. At the same time, foreign-controlled institutions constrained Chinese sovereignty, a grievance at the center of nationalist politics.

Economically, Shanghai was the center of finance, foreign trade, light industry, and shipping. Its cotton mills, flour mills, tobacco companies, printing houses, and banks employed large numbers of workers and drew on capital from Chinese merchants, foreign firms, and overseas investors. Commercial networks, many organized through native-place associations, linked Shanghai to the delta and beyond. The city's society was strongly stratified: foreign and Chinese elites in villas and apartments; a salaried middle class of clerks, teachers, and professionals; and a large population of workers, rickshaw pullers, and servants living in crowded housing. Migrants from Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Guangdong, and elsewhere organized communal life around their regions of origin, and local dialects mattered socially.

Shanghai's culture was cosmopolitan but also fiercely contested. It was home to department stores, cafés, dance halls, cinemas, and amusement centers, as well as left-wing literary groups and trade unions. It was also a site of organized crime: the Green Gang, whose leaders such as Du Yuesheng had ties to businessmen, police, and the Nationalist government, played a role in labor control and illicit trade, including opium. Foreign visitors often described Shanghai as symbolic of modern China, but Chinese writers frequently depicted it ambivalently, as a place of opportunity and of exploitation, and as a place where national humiliation was visible every day.

Section 63.3

Beiping, Tianjin, Wuhan, Guangzhou, and Other Urban Centers

Beiping. After the Nationalist government made Nanjing its capital in 1928, Beijing was renamed Beiping ("northern peace"). It lost its political role, and many officials and businesses left, which affected employment and commerce. But it remained a leading educational and cultural center, with Peking University, Tsinghua, Yenching, and other institutions, along with libraries, museums, and a distinctive urban fabric of courtyard houses and lanes. Its economy relied on government remnants, education, handicrafts, and services, and its residents included students, scholars, shopkeepers, rickshaw pullers, and a Manchu and Mongol population with long local roots.

Tianjin. A major northern port with multiple foreign concessions, Tianjin combined trade, textiles, flour, chemical manufacture, and banking. Entrepreneurs such as those behind the Jiu Da Salt Industries and Yongli chemical works at nearby Tanggu pursued scientific industry. Its mix of foreign administrations, Chinese merchants, and militarists made it politically complex.

Wuhan. The triple city of Wuchang, Hanyang, and Hankou sat at the center of the Yangtze and railway networks. Hankou was a commercial port with foreign concessions and tea, grain, and metal trade, Hanyang had late Qing industrial roots in ironworks and arsenals, and Wuchang was an administrative and educational center. Wuhan was linked to the revolutions of 1911 and 1926–27, but in the 1930s it mainly functioned as an inland hub.

Guangzhou. The commercial center of Lingnan was closely linked to Hong Kong, overseas Chinese networks, and a long tradition of maritime trade. Under municipal reformers in the 1920s it widened roads and built new commercial districts, though its arcaded shopfronts and older neighborhoods persisted. Guangdong in the early 1930s was ruled by a semi-autonomous regional regime under Chen Jitang rather than directly from Nanjing.

Other cities. Nanjing grew rapidly as a capital, with planned avenues and the Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum. Qingdao, Harbin, and Shenyang developed under German, Russian, and Japanese influences and, in Manchuria, a strong regional administration until 1931. Chongqing, Chengdu, Changsha, Kunming, and many smaller cities were provincial or regional centers with their own economies and cultures. Together these cities show that Republican urbanism took many forms.

Section 63.4

Migration, Housing, and Urban Neighborhoods

Rural-to-urban migration was driven by both push and pull factors: land shortage, debt, floods, war, and banditry pushed people from villages, while factory recruitment, trade, and the hope of wages or education drew them to cities. Many migrants moved seasonally or kept ties to their villages, sending money home and returning for festivals or planting. Labor recruiters often came from the same native place as the workers they hired, which meant that migration usually followed existing social networks rather than occurring as isolated individual journeys.

Housing varied with class. In Shanghai, many middle-income and working households lived in lilong (alley neighborhoods) of row houses, often subdivided so that several families shared a building, while poorer migrants lived in shanties on the city's edge or on boats. Rents were high relative to wages. Courtyard houses in Beiping, riverfront shacks in Wuhan, and Guangzhou's boat communities show how housing patterns varied by city. Segregation operated along lines of wealth, region of origin, and, in treaty ports, nationality, though crowding meant different groups lived near each other.

Neighborhood institutions mattered. Native-place associations offered burial services, schooling, and help in finding work. Guilds, temples, charitable halls, and religious societies provided support, and gangs and secret societies offered protection and enforced control in some districts. Domestic servants, many of them young women from the countryside, lived in employers' homes with little independence. These networks both helped migrants adapt and sometimes limited their opportunities, for example by reinforcing regional hierarchies among workers.

A Shanghai Working-Class Household (Reconstructed Composite)

Picture a household of five in a rented room in a crowded alley district: a father who pulls a rickshaw or works as a dockhand, a mother who takes in sewing, a teenage daughter who works in a cotton mill, and two younger children. Rent claims a large share of income. Rice is the staple; meat is rare. The daughter's mill wages are crucial, and her labor contractor, who may come from the family's home county, has influence over her job. The family sends money to relatives in the village and plans to bury the father's parents there. The younger son may attend a charity school if the family can spare his labor; the girl is less likely to be spared. Illness, unemployment, or a strike can threaten the household's balance. Social historians use such reconstructions cautiously, comparing them with wage surveys, investigators' reports, and oral histories.

Division II — Industry, Commerce, Labor, and Economic Life

Section 63.5

Industrialization and Modern Enterprise

Modern industry in Republican China was concentrated in a few places, notably Shanghai, Tianjin, Wuxi, Qingdao, Guangzhou, Wuhan, and, before 1931, Manchuria, where Japanese and Chinese investments in mining, railways, and heavy industry were significant. Light industries dominated Chinese-owned manufacturing: cotton spinning and weaving, flour milling, cigarettes, matches, silk reeling, and food processing. Foreign firms and foreign-owned factories, especially British and Japanese, operated alongside Chinese enterprises, and Japanese-owned cotton mills (often called "in-China mills") became important competitors.

Chinese entrepreneurs often organized firms as family enterprises. The Rong brothers built flour and cotton enterprises centered on Wuxi and Shanghai; the Nanyang Brothers Tobacco Company, founded by overseas-connected entrepreneurs, competed with the British American Tobacco Company; Zhang Jian's Nantong enterprises combined textiles, land reclamation, and education. Technology was often imported, but firms also adapted foreign machinery to local conditions and trained Chinese engineers and managers. Heavy industry remained limited: iron and steel, machine building, and electric power were comparatively weak, which constrained industrial depth.

Modern industry did not displace handicraft production. Household weaving, rural spinning, small workshops, and traditional processing continued, often connected to factories through putting-out arrangements or market competition. In some regions, handicraft production benefited from factory yarn; in others, it was undercut by cheap imports. The share of the labor force in modern factories remained small, probably only a few percent of the total, though concentrated in important cities.

Cause and Effect

Why Chinese-Owned Light Industry Expanded, c. 1914–1930

Causes

Short-term: reduced European exports during the First World War; high demand for flour and textiles. Long-term: late Qing industrial experience, treaty-port infrastructure, growing urban markets, accumulated merchant capital, and a pool of migrant labor. Political: nationalist boycotts and campaigns favoring "national products."

Transformation

Chinese-owned cotton, flour, and other light industries expanded rapidly in Shanghai, Wuxi, Tianjin, and elsewhere, forming a new industrial and commercial class.

Effects

New factory jobs, especially for women; growth of urban working-class districts; rising business associations; renewed foreign competition after wartime conditions ended; and vulnerability to depression in the 1930s.

Historical complexity

Growth depended on specific international conditions, foreign capital and technology, and limited state support. Industrialization was not simply "blocked" by imperialism nor spontaneous: entrepreneurs, governments, foreign firms, and workers all shaped outcomes.
Section 63.6

Commerce, Banking, and Business Networks

Commercial life rested on networks of trust. Merchants relied on kinship, native-place ties, guilds, and chambers of commerce to enforce contracts and extend credit, since formal courts were limited and unevenly trusted. Ningbo, Jiangsu-Zhejiang, Guangdong, Fujian, and Shanxi merchants each had distinctive reputations and organizations. Domestic trade moved grain, cotton, silk, tea, salt, and manufactured goods along rivers, canals, railways, and roads, with wholesalers, brokers, and market-town shopkeepers forming links between producers and consumers.

Banking developed in layers. Traditional native banks (qianzhuang) provided short-term credit to merchants and remained important, while modern Chinese banks such as the Bank of China, the Bank of Communications, and private institutions like the Shanghai Commercial and Savings Bank and the Zhejiang Industrial Bank expanded in the 1910s and 1920s. Groups of banks formed regional alliances, and foreign banks handled much international exchange. Compradors, once intermediaries for foreign firms, became more varied as Chinese firms took on larger roles. After 1927 the Nanjing government increased its influence over banking, including the 1935 currency reform (see Chapter 62), which changed how the government and bankers interacted.

Business associations also participated in public life. Chambers of commerce and merchant organizations funded schools, hospitals, charities, and sometimes militia, and they negotiated with, and sometimes resisted, taxation and regulation by local authorities and the Nationalist state. Family firms managed inheritance through division of property among sons, which could limit the scale of enterprises. Commerce thus connected economic activity to social obligations in ways not captured by a simple model of impersonal markets.

Concrete institutions show how this worked. Chen Guangfu founded the Shanghai Commercial and Savings Bank in 1915 and built it on small savings deposits as well as loans to trading and industrial firms; with the Zhejiang Industrial Bank and other Jiangsu-Zhejiang banks it formed a southern banking circle, while in the north several banks, including the Salt Industrial, Kincheng, Continental, and China and South Sea banks, cooperated through joint reserve arrangements in the early 1920s. Banks earned much of their income in the 1920s from government bonds issued by cash-hungry Beiyang governments, which tied their fortunes to political instability. In 1933 the Nanjing government abolished the silver tael as a unit of account in Shanghai in favor of the silver dollar, simplifying exchange and reducing the margins on which some native bankers relied.

Everyday commerce depended on credit and on the calendar. Many shops sold on credit and settled accounts at the three annual festival settlements (the Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn, and New Year seasons), so a bad year could ripple through chains of debts from retailer to wholesaler to supplier. Nationalist legislation of 1929 required old-style guilds to reorganize as trade associations under state supervision, part of a wider effort to bring merchant organizations under party-state control. Relations between the Nanjing leadership and Shanghai businessmen combined cooperation with pressure, including demands for loans and levies, and merchants responded by lobbying, negotiating, and sometimes moving assets into the foreign settlements.

Section 63.7

Industrial Workers and Labor Relations

Factory workers in Republican China were a small but strategically important group. Textile mills employed many women and children, whereas heavy industry, railways, mines, docks, and shipping employed more men. Workers were often recruited through personal networks and labor contractors, a system sometimes called baogong (contract labor), in which the contractor hired, supervised, and sometimes paid workers, and could take a share of wages. Many workers lived in dormitories or neighborhoods near the factory, and workplace discipline included fines, searches, and long shifts, often twelve hours or more in the 1920s. Women and children were often paid less than men; apprentices worked for little or no pay.

Workers responded in different ways. Some relied on native-place ties, secret societies, or sworn brotherhoods; others joined unions organized by the Nationalists, the Communists, or independent leaders. Strikes occurred over wages, dismissals, and conditions, and the 1925 May Thirtieth Movement showed how labor protest could combine with nationalism (Chapter 61). After the 1927 split, the Nationalist government suppressed independent and Communist-led unions and sponsored official unions, which limited workers' autonomy while sometimes offering benefits. Government labor legislation addressed conditions but was enforced unevenly, especially in foreign-controlled areas and in small workshops.

Historical Vocabulary
Baogong (labor contracting)

Baogong refers to a system in which a contractor, often called a "head" (bagong tou or baogong tou in various usages), supplied and supervised workers for a factory, paying them from sums received from management. It could give workers access to jobs through trusted personal connections, but also allowed contractors to extract fees, and made workers dependent on their patronage. Historians study it to understand how social networks, not only formal rules, structured the factory.

Primary Source Window

The National Factory Law (1929) and a 1931 Enquiry into Factory Conditions

Documents: (1) Gongchang fa (Factory Law), promulgated by the Nationalist government at Nanjing on December 30, 1929; in effect August 1, 1931; amended version in effect December 30, 1932. English edition: China's National Factory Law (English and Chinese Texts), translated by N. F. Allman and Lowe Chuan-hua. (2) A summary of an enquiry into conditions in Shanghai factories begun in May 1931 under the auspices of the Chinese National Christian Council, published in the International Labour Review, vol. 25, no. 4 (1932), pp. 535–541 · Type: Statute and social-survey report

Context: The Factory Law had 77 articles in 13 sections covering child and women workers, hours and rest, wages, dismissal, welfare, safety and hygiene, compensation, factory councils, and apprenticeship. It was followed by enforcement regulations (December 1930) and a Factory Inspection Act (February 1931). The 1931 enquiry grew out of a Christian-sponsored conference on "people's livelihood" held that February, which called for a scientific study of working conditions.

Paraphrase (not an exact quotation): The law set an eight-hour normal working day, limited the employment of children, and required measures for workers' safety and welfare; restrictions on women's night work were repeatedly postponed rather than fully applied when the law took effect. The enquiry reported, among other findings, that night shifts were worked in the cotton-spinning mills it surveyed, that large numbers of women worked at night, and that night shifts commonly ran well beyond eight hours.

Analysis: Read together, the documents show the distance between legislative intention and workplace practice. The law reveals the state's stated aims and the compromises forced by employers, including foreign-owned firms in concessions that Chinese law reached unevenly. The enquiry, produced by reform-minded Christian sponsors, shows what investigators chose to measure and how they framed it. Neither records workers' own voices, and the enquiry covered particular Shanghai factories in 1931. (Editors should check the paraphrase of the enquiry against the full ILO article and the Allman–Lowe translation before final publication.)

  2. Who sponsored and conducted the 1931 enquiry, and how might their aims have shaped what it measured?

Section 63.8

Artisans, Shopkeepers, Clerks, and Informal Work

Most urban Chinese did not work in large factories. They worked in small workshops making cloth, shoes, furniture, tools, printing, and food; as street vendors, porters, rickshaw pullers, and boatmen; as domestic servants, barbers, tailors, and laundresses; or as salaried employees in shops, banks, schools, and offices. Guilds and apprenticeship systems still regulated many trades, training young boys for years in exchange for board and eventual skills, though discipline could be harsh. Small workshops often depended on subcontracting from larger firms.

Rickshaw pullers were among the most visible urban laborers. Many were rural migrants who rented rickshaws from owners, faced competition from motor vehicles and trams, and risked injury and illness from physical strain. Domestic workers, including women employed as maids and amahs, often worked without fixed hours. Women's informal work included sewing, piecework, laundry, and street trading. A growing class of clerks, bookkeepers, typists, teachers, and shop assistants made up what contemporaries described as a petty-bourgeois or salaried stratum, aspiring to respectability but vulnerable to price changes and unemployment. In Lao She's fiction of the 1930s, discussed in Section 63.21, such precarious urban lives became literary subjects.

Occupations were often tied to regional identity. Migrants from northern Jiangsu ("Subei" people) in Shanghai were disproportionately employed as rickshaw pullers, dockers, and nightsoil collectors and faced prejudice from better-established Jiangnan residents, an example of how ethnicity in the broad sense of regional origin shaped labor markets. Rickshaws, introduced to Chinese cities from Japan in the 1870s, employed tens of thousands in cities such as Shanghai and Beiping. Sidney Gamble's social surveys of Beijing in the 1920s and 1930s, which recorded household budgets and occupations, found that many pullers came from nearby rural counties and combined city work with farm ties, and that the trade was precarious, with pullers renting vehicles from owners and facing competition from trams and buses.

Women's and rural industrial work shaped the informal economy as well. In Shandong, the export hairnet industry organized rural women to knit nets from supplied hair, paid by the piece through merchants; in many towns women folded matchboxes, braided straw, or sewed for wholesalers. Apprenticeship for boys in shops and workshops typically lasted several years, with apprentices living at the shop, performing domestic chores, and receiving training and board in place of wages. Such arrangements created both a path to skill and a source of dependency, and they sat awkwardly with new labor laws written with factories in mind.

Section 63.9

Economic Crisis and Social Vulnerability

China remained on a silver standard, so when world silver prices changed, domestic prices and trade shifted. In the early 1930s, as industrial economies sank into depression, the effects on China came partly through falling prices for agricultural exports such as silk and tea, reduced remittances from overseas Chinese, and a decline in foreign demand. Then, after the United States adopted silver purchase legislation in 1934, silver prices rose and silver flowed out of China, producing deflation, credit shortages, and business failures. Government responses, including the 1935 currency reform, are discussed in Chapter 62; here the concern is the social consequences.

Households experienced the crisis differently. Silk-producing districts of the lower Yangtze and Guangdong suffered when raw silk exports collapsed and Japanese rayon competed in markets. Cotton mills cut hours or closed. Merchants faced falling sales and tight credit. Farmers who sold crops saw prices drop while debts and rents, fixed in money terms, stayed high. Some urban workers lost jobs and returned to villages; others stayed in cities in precarious employment. Natural disasters added to the strain: the 1931 Yangtze and Huai floods displaced and killed very large numbers of people, with estimates varying widely, and drought struck parts of the north and northwest. Foreign relief, Chinese charity, and government programs offered partial help.

Historical Evidence

Interpreting Republican Economic Statistics

Historians use price indexes, trade figures, wage surveys, land surveys, and municipal statistics to study the Republican economy, but each has limits. Customs statistics were relatively systematic for foreign trade but said little about domestic markets. Rural surveys, such as those led by John Lossing Buck and Chinese researchers, covered selected villages and regions, which may not represent the whole country, and methods varied. Wage and price series in Shanghai are better documented than those in the interior. Official statistics could be incomplete or shaped by administrative aims. Estimates of national income or population carry wide margins of error.

Students should therefore ask: Who collected the data? Which regions and groups were included? How were categories such as "tenant" or "worker" defined? What was left out? Statistics are most useful when compared with other evidence, such as local gazetteers, newspaper reports, and oral histories, and when presented with explicit uncertainty.

Division III — Rural Society, Agriculture, and Migration

Section 63.10

Rural Households and Agricultural Production

The household was the basic unit of rural production. Family members divided tasks by age and gender, with men typically responsible for heavy fieldwork and women often handling spinning, weaving, silkworm rearing, food preparation, and, in many regions, fieldwork as well. Farms were small, often just a few acres or less, and fragmented into scattered plots. Tools were mostly hand-powered and animal-powered, with limited use of chemical fertilizer, though commercial fertilizers and improved seeds spread in some areas.

Regional systems differed. In the Yangtze delta and the south, irrigated rice with two crops a year, mulberry and silkworms, cotton, and fish ponds supported dense populations. In the north, wheat, millet, sorghum, maize, and beans depended on rainfall and were vulnerable to drought. In Sichuan, rice, rapeseed, and sweet potatoes supported a densely populated basin. Cash crops such as cotton, tobacco, peanuts, and soybeans tied farmers to markets, and Manchuria's soybean economy connected to Japanese and global trade. Seasonal cycles organized labor, festivals, and market visits.

Households combined farming with sideline production, such as weaving, basket making, or raising pigs and chickens, to manage risk. This diversification meant that changes in markets or imports could affect household income even when farming methods barely changed. Fei Xiaotong's study of a Yangtze delta village described how silk reeling and farming complemented one another and how the collapse of silk markets threatened the household economy, prompting efforts to establish cooperative silk factories.

The agronomist John Lossing Buck's survey, published as Land Utilization in China (1937), divided the country into a wheat region in the north and a rice region in the south, with several sub-areas, and documented differences in crops, farm size, and livestock: rice-region farms tended to be smaller but more intensively worked, while the north relied more on donkeys, mules, and larger cultivated areas per household. Buck's work has been criticized for sampling and method, but it remains a major source. Technology in the delta included foot-pedal pumps and waterwheels, and farmers used night soil, compost, and, in some areas, soybean cake imported from Manchuria as fertilizer, one example of how regions were linked by trade.

Rural handicraft industries adapted to industrial inputs. In Gaoyang, Hebei, rural weavers used machine-spun yarn and improved looms from the 1910s to make cloth sold across north China, until competition and the depression of the 1930s squeezed the trade. Such cases show that household production was not simply "traditional": it could modernize technologically while remaining organized around households and merchants. Historians still debate whether growing populations and commercialization raised rural living standards or mainly intensified labor on small plots, and the answer varied by region.

Section 63.11

Landholding, Tenancy, Credit, and Rural Inequality

Rural society included landlords, owner-cultivators, part-owners who rented additional land, tenants, and landless agricultural laborers. Proportions varied greatly. Tenancy was more common in parts of the south, the lower Yangtze, and Sichuan than in much of the north, where smaller owner-cultivators predominated, and many families moved between categories over time. Rents could be paid in kind or money and were often a large fraction of the crop. Landlords were not a uniform group: some were absentee urban owners, others local gentry, lineage trusts, temples, or small proprietors with only a little land.

Credit was crucial. Families borrowed for seed, weddings, funerals, taxes, and emergencies, from relatives, landlords, merchants, pawnshops, or moneylenders, often at high interest. Rotating credit associations offered a communal alternative. Modern cooperatives and rural banks were introduced by reformers, missions, and governments, but reached only a small share of households. Taxes, surtaxes, and local levies imposed by militarists added burdens in some regions (see Chapter 59). Political movements, especially the Communists, used the language of land inequality for mobilization (Chapters 61–62), while scholars debate the severity and causes of rural poverty, including population pressure, low productivity, rent and tax burdens, and market conditions.

Buck's survey suggested that a majority of farm families owned at least some of their land, though tenancy was more common in the south, and that rent often took about half the main crop in parts of the south. Interest rates on rural loans were commonly high, often above twenty percent a year and far higher on small or short-term loans. These averages concealed wide local variation and changes over time. Tenants did resist: rent-resistance protests occurred in the lower Yangtze, and in 1927 Zhejiang's provincial authorities proclaimed a 25 percent rent reduction. Such measures were poorly enforced and politically contested, as landlords retained influence in local administration. In the 1930s, reports describe tightening rural credit and the closing of many rural pawnshops as the depression deepened, shrinking an important source of emergency loans.

Section 63.12

Market Towns, Lineages, and Village Community

Rural life was organized not only around villages but around market systems. Periodic markets, held every few days in standard market towns, allowed households to sell produce, buy goods, hear news, and meet marriage brokers and relatives. Market towns were also hubs for teahouses, small industries, and religious festivals. Anthropologists and geographers have described how a cluster of villages around a market town often formed a social world larger than the village itself.

Kinship organizations, especially in the south and southeast, were powerful. Lineages (zongzu) owned land and ancestral halls, supported schools and rituals, and mediated disputes. In north China, lineage organization was often weaker, and village associations, temple societies, and crop-watching groups were more central. Religious life, including temple festivals, ancestor worship, local deities, Buddhist and Daoist rituals, and in some regions Islamic and Christian communities, structured the calendar and community obligations. Local leadership came from gentry, lineage heads, village heads, schoolteachers, and, increasingly in some areas, militia leaders and party or government agents. Many of these elites mediated between villages and higher authorities but also pursued their own interests.

The anthropologist G. William Skinner later argued that the standard market town, serving a cluster of villages, defined the real social horizon of most peasants: people bought and sold there, found spouses within its catchment, and joined its temple festivals. His model is debated, but it shows why market systems, not villages alone, are the useful unit of analysis. Temple fairs drew crowds beyond the village: Gu Jiegang's 1925 investigation of the pilgrimage fair at Miaofengshan near Beijing documented large numbers of pilgrims, local associations, and a mixture of religious devotion, trade, and entertainment, evidence that popular religion was a vigorous social institution rather than a fading survival.

Evidence for north China villages comes substantially from Japanese South Manchurian Railway (Mantetsu) surveys conducted in 1940–44 under occupation. Historians such as Prasenjit Duara used them to analyze village leadership and state penetration, but their timing and setting require caution when applied to the 1920s and 1930s. Local leaders mediated between villagers and outside authorities, collecting taxes, managing crop-watching and temple societies, and sometimes profiting from the role; as governments demanded more revenue, the position could become more burdensome and less attractive to respected villagers.

Section 63.13

Migration, Mobility, and Overseas Chinese Communities

Migration took many forms. Seasonal labor migrants moved between farms and towns; peddlers and craftsmen traveled for work; families fled floods, famine, and warfare. One of the largest movements was the flow of farmers from Shandong and Hebei to Manchuria, sometimes called "crossing the pass" (chuang Guandong), which in some years involved more than a million people. Many returned seasonally, while others settled permanently. Migrants to Manchuria worked in agriculture, mining, railways, and cities such as Harbin and Shenyang, under changing political authorities, including the regional regime of Zhang Xueliang and, after 1931, Japanese-controlled Manchukuo (Chapter 64).

Overseas migration, mostly from Guangdong and Fujian, created communities in Southeast Asia, North America, Australia, and elsewhere. These migrants worked in mining, plantations, commerce, and services, and some built substantial businesses. Remittances sent to home villages sustained households, funded schools, temples, and building projects, and created "qiaoxiang" (hometowns of overseas Chinese), where emigrant money reshaped local economies. In Chaozhou and nearby areas, remittance letters and banking networks connected families across borders. Overseas communities also supported Chinese political movements and education, and experienced discrimination and legal restrictions in destination countries. The depression reduced remittances, causing hardship at home.

Regional and Frontier Context

Republican social change differed across borderlands. In Manchuria, rapid migration, railways, soybean exports, and Japanese and Russian influence created a distinctive economy until 1931. In Inner Mongolia and parts of the northwest, Chinese agricultural settlement and Mongol pastoral communities competed for land, producing tension and political mobilization. Xinjiang and Tibet were governed largely through regional authorities with limited Nanjing control, and their societies, including Uyghur, Kazakh, Tibetan, Hui, and other communities, followed different religious, linguistic, and economic patterns; trade connected them to Central Asia, South Asia, and the Soviet Union. In the southwest, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi included many non-Han peoples such as the Yi, Miao, Zhuang, and Bai, whose relations with provincial governments and Han settlers varied. Taiwan, under Japanese rule, experienced colonial modernization in education, infrastructure, and agriculture alongside discrimination, a different trajectory from the mainland. Hong Kong and Macao were colonies with their own economies and migration patterns. Students should avoid treating these regions as peripheral to Republican society; they illustrate its diversity and the limits of the Republic's authority.

Division IV — Education, Knowledge, Science, and Professional Life

Section 63.14

Literacy, Primary Education, and Unequal Access

The abolition of the examinations in 1905 and the creation of new school systems changed the structure of education. The 1922 school reform introduced a system with six years of primary, three of junior secondary, and three of senior secondary education, influenced by American models, and the Nationalist government expanded curricula, including political instruction in party doctrine (Chapter 62). On paper, education became broader and more standardized.

In practice, only a minority of school-age children attended school regularly, and estimated literacy rates were low, probably well under half of adults, with large differences by region, gender, and class. Cities and prosperous coastal regions had more schools; many villages relied on private tutors, lineage-supported schools, or no formal schooling at all. Girls were less likely than boys to attend. Old-style private schools (sishu) continued in many areas alongside new ones. Teachers were often poorly paid, and schools depended on local funding.

Reformers tried to broaden access. The Mass Education Movement led by Yan Yangchu organized literacy campaigns using simplified "thousand character" texts and rural experiments at Ding County in Hebei. Tao Xingzhi promoted "life education" for villagers, and Liang Shuming's rural reconstruction work in Shandong linked education to community organization. These programs reached limited areas but influenced later thinking about rural development. Missions also ran schools and promoted literacy. Historians note that education expanded opportunities for some but could also widen gaps between those with schooling and those without.

Language policy mattered. In 1920 the Ministry of Education ordered that primary-school textbooks be written in the vernacular, and campaigns for a standard "national language" (guoyu), a phonetic script (zhuyin fuhao), and romanization schemes sought to make literacy easier, though regional dialects remained the language of daily life. Yan Yangchu's Thousand Character primers distilled a basic vocabulary for adult learners. Teachers' pay was a persistent problem: in the early 1920s teachers in Beijing struck over months of unpaid wages, and in the 1930s many rural teachers depended on irregular local funds.

Missionary and state interests collided in schooling. Nationalist regulations of the mid-1920s and 1929 required mission schools to register with the Chinese government, appoint Chinese principals, and make religious instruction optional, reflecting nationalist demands to end foreign control of education. Meanwhile many old-style private schools continued, and campaigns to reform or close them in the 1930s met resistance from villagers who valued their curriculum and flexibility. The coexistence of party-doctrine classes, vernacular textbooks, mission education, and village tutors illustrates how education reform operated through competing institutions rather than a single system.

Section 63.15

Universities, Students, and Intellectual Communities

Higher education grew in the Republican period. National universities such as Peking University, Southeast University (later National Central University), and Wuhan University; Tsinghua, funded initially through the Boxer indemnity remission; private institutions such as Nankai and Fudan; and mission-founded universities such as Yenching, St. John's, Lingnan, and Ginling College created a varied landscape. Universities were centers of research, debate, and professional training, and recruited faculty with degrees from Japan, Europe, and North America. Foreign foundations, including the Rockefeller Foundation, supported science and medicine.

Students formed societies, published journals, and took part in political movements. The May Fourth Movement of 1919 (Chapter 60) established a tradition of student activism, and protests recurred in the 1920s and 1930s, including the December Ninth Movement of 1935 in Beiping, when students demonstrated against Japanese pressure and against proposals for a Japanese-backed autonomous regime in north China. Government control of campuses, party organizations, and police surveillance created tension with academic freedom, and universities were often underfunded. Only a very small share of young people attended university, and most students came from relatively well-off families, though scholarships and, for some, family sacrifice enabled others.

Section 63.16

New Professions, Expertise, and Social Mobility

As schools, hospitals, courts, newspapers, banks, and industries expanded, new professions took shape: engineers, physicians, nurses, lawyers, judges, accountants, journalists, teachers, civil servants, architects, scientists, and social workers. Professional associations such as bar associations, medical societies, and engineering societies set standards, published journals, and lobbied governments. Chinese scientific societies, such as the Science Society of China, promoted research and publication, and institutions like the Academia Sinica (founded 1928) supported scholarship.

Professional identity offered new routes to status. An educated person could seek influence through expertise rather than through the examination degree and official rank of the old order, though family connections, regional ties, and patronage still mattered. Salaried urban professionals often formed a new middle stratum, but their positions depended on employers, government budgets, and economic conditions, and the depression led to unemployment among graduates. Women entered teaching, nursing, medicine, and clerical work in increasing numbers, though they faced discrimination in pay and promotion.

Several examples illustrate the range. Zhan Tianyou (Jeme Tien Yow), the engineer of the Beijing–Zhangjiakou railway (completed 1909), helped found a Chinese engineering society in 1913. Zheng Yuxiu, often described as the first Chinese woman lawyer, practiced in Shanghai in the 1920s and later became a judge, showing both openings and the exceptional nature of such careers. In accounting, Xu Yongzuo is often described as China's first registered public accountant in 1918, and accountants' associations followed. The Science Society of China, begun by Chinese students in the United States in 1914–15, published the journal Kexue and built networks among returned students.

Scholarly institutions linked professional life to knowledge production and to other chapters of this book. The Geological Survey of China, founded in 1916, supported the fieldwork that led to the 1929 discovery of an early hominid skull at Zhoukoudian by Pei Wenzhong (Chapter 3), and the Institute of History and Philology of Academia Sinica, founded in 1928, began systematic excavations at Anyang in 1928 (Chapter 7). Many of these experts had studied abroad, often with support from Boxer indemnity scholarships, and they navigated political pressures, limited budgets, and, in the 1930s, a graduate job market in which positions did not keep pace with the number of graduates.

Section 63.17

Medicine, Public Health, and Everyday Life

Health conditions were shaped by poverty, disease, and limited infrastructure. Infectious diseases, including cholera, tuberculosis, smallpox, typhoid, and malaria, were major causes of death, and infant and maternal mortality were high. Modern medicine expanded through mission hospitals, state and municipal institutions, and the Peking Union Medical College, which trained physicians and researchers. Wu Lien-teh's work on epidemic control, beginning with the Manchurian plague of 1910–11, led to the creation of quarantine services. The Nationalist government established a Ministry of Health in 1928, later reorganized, and municipal health bureaus attempted vaccination, sanitation, and disease reporting. The Ding County health experiment sought to build a system of village health workers, medical stations, and county health centers.

Traditional Chinese medicine remained widely used. In 1929, a proposal in the Nationalist health administration to restrict or abolish traditional practice provoked protests by practitioners and supporters, who organized and defended their profession, and the government retreated from the most restrictive measures. The dispute shows that modernization of medicine was contested, and that traditional and Western-style practitioners competed, cooperated, and borrowed from each other. Midwifery reforms, school health programs, and anti-footbinding campaigns also reflected public-health aims, though they met resistance and limited resources.

Everyday Life and Social History

Medical Care in Town and Countryside

For a wealthy Shanghai or Beiping family, illness might mean a visit to a Western-trained physician, a hospital stay, or consultation with a respected traditional doctor, sometimes both. For a village household, care typically began at home with herbs and folk remedies, followed by a visit to a local herbalist, bonesetter, or midwife, and appeals to temple deities or ancestors. Foreign missionary clinics and the occasional county health station offered another option where they existed. Costs, distance, trust, and gender norms limited access, and many people experienced modern medicine only in epidemics or vaccination drives. Historians reconstruct these patterns from hospital records, health surveys, missionary reports, local gazetteers, and memoirs, all of which reflect particular vantage points.

Division V — Women, Family, and Changing Social Relationships

Section 63.18

Women's Education, Employment, and Public Participation

Women's opportunities expanded unevenly. Girls' schools, begun in the late Qing by missionaries and reformers, multiplied in the Republic, and women's colleges such as Ginling and Yenching's women's college offered higher education. Women first attended Peking University as auditors in February 1920, and formal undergraduate admission followed later that year; other universities followed. Educated women worked as teachers, nurses, doctors, writers, and activists, and some entered politics and social movements. Early suffrage campaigns, led by figures such as Tang Qunying in the 1910s, did not win national voting rights then, though women's rights became part of party programs and legal reform.

Working-class women formed a large part of the textile labor force, and women also worked in silk reeling, tobacco, matches, domestic service, and agriculture. They often earned less than men, faced harassment, and were expected to keep domestic duties. In parts of the Pearl River delta, silk-reeling women gained earnings and some independence, and a minority of "sworn spinsters" chose lifelong celibacy, an example of how economic conditions created unusual social arrangements. Rural women's work in farming, textile production, and household management was essential but less visible in public discourse. Anti-footbinding campaigns, supported by reformers and government decrees, reduced the practice, especially in cities and among younger girls, though it persisted in some rural areas for years.

Section 63.19

Marriage, Family Reform, and Household Authority

For centuries, marriage had been arranged by parents and matchmakers, in a patrilineal system where a bride joined her husband's household and her labor and children served his lineage. In the Republic, reformers and many students advocated free choice of spouse, "companionate" marriage, and the rejection of arranged betrothal and concubinage. Newspapers published advice columns, personal advertisements, and debates, and writers dramatized young people's conflicts with parents. Some urban couples held "civilized weddings," with simplified ceremonies, and divorce, while still stigmatizing, became more available, especially in cities.

Yet most marriages in rural areas and among many urban families continued to be arranged or strongly influenced by parents, with brideprice, dowry, and household needs shaping decisions. Kinship obligations, including support for elderly parents and ancestral rites, remained strong, and many young people tried to combine new ideals with family duty. Intergenerational conflict was real, but so was accommodation: some parents consulted their children, and some couples met through families but formed affection later. Concubinage and the sale of girls persisted in some places despite reformers' condemnation (the law's treatment of concubinage was ambiguous), and women with little property had limited ability to leave unhappy marriages. The Nationalist government's New Life Movement of the 1930s (Chapter 62) promoted conservative moral discipline alongside modernization, showing that state projects could support both reform and traditional norms.

Individual cases show how people negotiated ideals and obligations. The writer Xu Zhimo's 1922 divorce from his wife, Zhang Youyi, was widely cited in the press as an early modern divorce by agreement, and Zhang later built a successful career in Shanghai. The scholar Hu Shi, a prominent advocate of reform, remained in the marriage arranged by his family. Lu Xun, who had an arranged marriage he never accepted, lived separately from his first wife and later with Xu Guangping. These examples suggest that elite reformers experienced the tension between ideals and obligation in different ways and that the "new" marriage was often accommodated within existing family structures.

Practices varied across regions and classes. Child betrothal and the adoption of a future daughter-in-law as a child (tongyangxi) persisted in parts of the south and southeast, brideprice and dowry were negotiated by families, and uxorilocal marriage, in which a husband joined the wife's family, was an option for households without sons. Historians such as Kathryn Bernhardt have examined how Republican courts handled women's property and inheritance claims, showing that changes in written law and local practice interacted in complicated ways. Divorce suits and property claims in courts, where records survive, show women using the new legal framework, but these records are concentrated in cities and richer regions and capture only those with access to courts.

Section 63.20

Youth, Gender Ideals, and Changing Social Norms

Youth emerged as a distinct social identity. Students formed a recognizable culture of reading, clubs, sports, and public protest. Ideals of masculinity shifted from the scholar-official toward the patriotic student, the athlete, the engineer, and the revolutionary soldier, while debates about "weak" bodies and national strength linked physical culture to nationalism. Competing ideals also circulated for women: the "New Woman" (xin nüxing), an educated, independent, politically aware figure; the "virtuous wife and good mother" promoted by educators and the state; and the modeng nülang, or "Modern Girl," associated with fashion, cinema, dance halls, and consumer goods.

Historical Vocabulary
Modern Girl (modeng nülang)

The Modern Girl was an image in 1920s and 1930s print and advertising of a young woman with fashionable clothing, cosmetics, short or permed hair, and social freedom. Similar figures appeared in many countries. Historians treat her as both a lived social type, to the extent that real women adopted aspects of the style, and a symbol used by advertisers, writers, moralists, and nationalists to praise or criticize social change, sometimes blaming women for national weakness or consumerism.

Historical Debate

The New Woman and the Limits of Emancipation

The question: Did the new ideals, laws, and opportunities of the Republican period substantially emancipate women, or did they leave deeper patterns of inequality largely intact?

Interpretation A — Significant change: Girls' schooling, women's employment, legal reforms, anti-footbinding campaigns, new public roles, and ideals of choice in marriage represented real shifts. Women gained access to education, wages, and public debate, and these changes affected later generations.
Interpretation B — Limited and uneven change: Benefits were concentrated among urban, educated, and wealthier women; rural and working-class women faced economic hardship and persistent patriarchal norms. Laws were weakly enforced, and the state and commercial culture sometimes redefined women's roles in ways that served nationalism or consumer markets rather than women's autonomy.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite school enrollment, new professions, legal provisions, and women's writings. Supporters of B cite surveys of rural households, labor investigations, court cases, and the persistence of arranged marriage and concubinage, and note that many sources were produced by elites or by men.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians understand the relationship between legal reform, social practice, and later developments, including the Communist marriage reforms of the 1950s (Chapter 66).

Division VI — Literature, Mass Media, Popular Culture, and Uneven Modernity

Section 63.21

Literature and Republican Intellectual Culture

Building on the vernacular literary reform introduced in Chapter 60, Republican literature diversified. Lu Xun's short stories and essays offered sharp social criticism; Mao Dun's novels, including Midnight (1933), depicted Shanghai's business and industrial world; Ba Jin's Family (1931) portrayed generational conflict in a large household; Shen Congwen wrote about rural and border communities in western Hunan; Ding Ling explored women's inner lives and later turned to left-wing themes; and Lao She wrote about Beiping's urban residents and, in Rickshaw Boy, about a laborer's struggle. Poets such as Xu Zhimo and Dai Wangshu experimented with form, while "popular" fiction, including serialized romances, detective stories, and martial arts tales, found large readerships and was sometimes criticized by reformers.

Literary societies and journals, including the Literary Research Association, the Creation Society, and the Crescent Moon Society, debated the purpose of literature. In 1930 the League of Left-Wing Writers was formed in Shanghai, advocating socially engaged literature, and clashed with the Nationalist government's censorship and with other literary currents. In the mid-1930s debates over "national defense literature" and "mass literature" reflected both literary and political disagreements.

Primary Source Window

Section 63.22

Newspapers, Magazines, Publishing, and Public Debate

Commercial publishing grew rapidly. The Commercial Press, Zhonghua Book Company, World Book Company, and many smaller publishers produced textbooks, translations, dictionaries, scholarly works, and popular books. Newspapers such as Shenbao in Shanghai and Dagong bao in Tianjin reached large urban audiences, and magazines such as Dongfang zazhi (Eastern Miscellany), the pictorial Liangyou, and Zou Taofen's Shenghuo weekly combined news, commentary, photographs, and advertising. Cartoons and illustrated periodicals reached readers with limited literacy, and storytellers, public readings, and shared newspapers extended their reach.

Publishing created a public sphere in which readers could debate politics, literature, and social life, but that sphere was constrained. The Nationalist government introduced censorship and pre-publication review in the early 1930s, and publishers and editors faced harassment or violence; the assassination of Shenbao proprietor and publisher Shi Liangcai on November 13, 1934, shocked the press. Foreign settlements gave some protection but not complete immunity. Commercial pressures also shaped content, as publishers sought advertising revenue and popular audiences. Rural readership was small, though newspapers and pamphlets reached towns and teachers.

Section 63.23

Film, Radio, Theater, and Popular Entertainment

Cinema became the most visible new mass entertainment. Early Chinese filmmaking in the 1910s and 1920s was dominated by small companies and imported films, particularly from the United States. By the late 1920s and 1930s, Shanghai-based companies such as Mingxing, Lianhua, and Tianyi produced popular genres including martial arts and costume dramas, melodramas, and social-realist films. Directors such as Cheng Bugao, Cai Chusheng, and Wu Yonggang, and performers such as Ruan Lingyu and Hu Die became well known. Films like The Goddess (1934), a silent film about a mother who works as a prostitute to support her child, and Spring Silkworms (1933), adapted from a Mao Dun story about sericulture families, and New Women (1935), which dealt with the pressures on a woman writer and the press's role in them, addressed social conditions. Sound film arrived in the early 1930s, and left-wing screenwriters influenced studio output. Ruan Lingyu's death by suicide in 1935 sparked public debate over press intrusion and the treatment of women in the media.

Radio spread more slowly. Stations began in the 1920s, and the Nationalist government developed broadcasting as a tool of communication and politics. Gramophone records and popular songs, including Shanghai "modern songs" associated with composers and performers such as Li Jinhui and, later, Zhou Xuan, circulated widely. Regional opera, puppet theater, storytelling, and temple-fair performance remained vital outside the commercial centers, and Peking opera, with stars such as Mei Lanfang, drew large audiences and international attention. Amusement centers like Shanghai's Great World combined many forms in one site.

China and the Wider World

Republican culture was shaped by global exchange in both directions. Hollywood films competed with Chinese productions; Japanese and Soviet films and literature influenced intellectuals and filmmakers; and translations connected Chinese readers to European and Russian literature. Chinese entrepreneurs, performers, and artists traveled abroad, as when Mei Lanfang toured Japan and the United States and the Soviet Union. Overseas Chinese communities supported newspapers, schools, and theaters, and Shanghai culture shaped Hong Kong and Southeast Asian popular culture. At the same time, nationalism meant debates over "national" forms, and Chinese creators adapted foreign techniques rather than merely copying them.

Section 63.24

Consumer Culture, Advertising, and Uneven Modernity

Department stores such as Sincere and Wing On on Nanjing Road offered fixed prices, display windows, restaurants, and entertainment, and their advertisements promoted cosmetics, fabrics, soaps, cigarettes, patent medicines, and household goods. Calendar posters, newspaper ads, and billboards used images of fashionable women, and companies competed over foreign and "national" brands. Fashion changed, with the cheongsam (qipao) becoming a popular women's dress in the late 1920s and 1930s, and men adopting Western suits or Sun Yat-sen-style tunics. Consumption was entangled with politics: the National Products Movement urged consumers to buy Chinese goods, and boycotts targeted foreign, especially Japanese, products, though such campaigns were enforced unevenly and sometimes by pressure on merchants.

Consumer culture was socially stratified. Wealthy and middle-class urban households could buy imported goods, attend films, and travel, while workers' consumption centered on food, rent, and basic cloth. In the countryside, consumption of kerosene, cigarettes, matches, and machine-made cloth spread in market towns, showing that commercial goods reached beyond cities, but at smaller scale and in different forms. Advertising also reflected inequalities in its images of leisure and its assumptions about readers.

Historical Debate

Was Republican Modernity Mainly an Urban Phenomenon?

The question: Should Republican modernity be understood primarily through the cities, or did it also transform rural and small-town society?

Interpretation A — Mainly urban: Factories, banks, universities, mass media, and new consumer culture were concentrated in treaty ports and large cities, reaching a small share of the population. Most villagers lived in conditions that changed slowly, and urban modernity could coexist with rural stagnation or decline.
Interpretation B — Broader and more diffuse: Markets, migration, remittances, manufactured goods, rural schools, reconstruction experiments, and new state demands affected villages and towns, producing real if different forms of change. Treating modernity as urban overlooks these processes and the interactions between town and country.

Evidence: A draws on distributions of factories, schools, and media; B draws on rural surveys, remittance records, market-town studies, and local histories. Both depend on the scale and region chosen.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians explain the later appeal of rural revolutionary movements, the uneven results of reform, and the legacy of Republican institutions.

Section 63.25

Chapter Review and Republican Social History Checkpoint

The central question of this chapter is: Who experienced modernization in Republican China, how did they experience it, and why were those experiences so unequal? A Shanghai mill worker encountered modernization as wage labor, factory discipline, and new urban networks, but with little security. An urban professional encountered it as new careers, professional associations, and consumer goods, yet remained exposed to inflation and unemployment. A rural tenant household experienced it through market prices, taxes, credit, and occasionally a new school or cooperative, while kinship, rent, and harvest remained central. A university student experienced it as access to new knowledge and political activism, but belonged to a privileged minority. A woman's opportunities depended on class, region, family, and education, as well as law.

Students should distinguish modernization from Westernization, legal reform from social practice, industrial growth from general prosperity, and cultural influence from political power. Republican modernity was real, but it was partial, contested, and regionally varied. Chapter 64 examines how Japanese invasion in 1937 disrupted the cities, migration patterns, industries, schools, and families described here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Republican China underwent significant but uneven social, economic, and cultural transformation. Industrial enterprise, modern banking, universities, new professions, print culture, film, and new ideas about family and gender expanded opportunities and reshaped public life, particularly in cities. These changes were unequally distributed by class, gender, occupation, and region.

Urban China was not synonymous with Shanghai. Shanghai's treaty-port jurisdictions, industries, finance, and culture were extraordinary, but Beiping, Tianjin, Wuhan, Guangzhou, Nanjing, and other cities had different economic functions and social characters. Migrants relied on native-place and kin networks, and housing and neighborhood life reflected class and regional divisions.

Industry and commerce grew, especially in light manufacturing, helped by wartime conditions, nationalism, and merchant capital, but modern factories employed only a small share of workers. Small workshops, artisans, and informal laborers remained essential, while labor relations involved contractors, unions, and state regulation with uneven enforcement. The depression and silver crisis of the 1930s exposed the vulnerability of households across regions.

Most Chinese lived in rural communities where household production, kinship, local markets, credit, and landholding structured life. Tenancy, debt, and rent burdens were significant but varied regionally, and villages were linked to markets, remittance networks, and migration streams. Rural change occurred through prices, taxes, and selected reform projects rather than a single transition.

Education and professional life expanded, but access to schools and universities was limited and unequal. New professions created new social positions and identities, while medicine and public health combined modern institutions with persistent traditional practice, and many people encountered modern medicine only occasionally.

Women's lives were reshaped by schooling, employment, legal change, and debates over marriage and family, but patriarchal norms, economic hardship, and limited enforcement constrained these changes. The New Woman, the Modern Girl, and the virtuous wife and good mother represent competing ideals rather than a single path.

Literature, publishing, film, radio, and advertising created new publics and forms of entertainment, and they were influenced by commerce, censorship, and global exchange. These media gave voice to social criticism while also reflecting the perspectives of urban, literate, and often male creators.

Historians disagree about how far modernity extended beyond cities, how deeply laws changed family life, and how severe rural poverty was. The chapter therefore emphasized evidence: statutes, surveys, statistics, fiction, student declarations, and media, each with its own limits.

Republican society was not merely a bridge between the Qing and the People's Republic. It had its own institutions, creativity, inequalities, and possibilities. Its prewar patterns also influenced later developments, including wartime displacement (Chapter 64), the appeal of political movements, and the social policies of the early People's Republic (Chapter 66). Understanding Republican society helps avoid both a celebratory and a purely tragic account of the period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shifts attention from governments to people. Republican China cannot be understood only through warlords, parties, and armies; it was also a society of mills and markets, classrooms and clinics, households and neighborhoods, magazines and cinemas. The picture that emerges is one of creativity and constraint, opportunity and inequality, side by side.

These developments shaped later Chinese history. Urban industry and commerce, professional and educational institutions, and print culture provided resources and models for later governments. Rural hardship, debt, and tenancy affected how political movements recruited support. Debates over marriage, women's roles, and education set agendas that the People's Republic would take up in new forms (Chapter 66). At the same time, the chapter warns against teleology: the patterns of the 1930s did not inevitably lead to any single outcome, and many possibilities were closed by war.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: regional diversity, the interaction of state and society, the role of commerce and migration, the importance of evidence, and China's participation in global exchange, from silver markets and Hollywood films to overseas migration and international medical philanthropy. Chinese entrepreneurs, teachers, writers, workers, and farmers were active historical agents who adapted foreign models and older institutions to their own needs.

Understanding Republican society helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, and unfinished. It also prepares them for Chapter 64, in which the Japanese invasion of 1937 disrupted these social worlds and forced millions of Chinese to migrate, fight, resist, collaborate, and endure under new and often terrible conditions.
