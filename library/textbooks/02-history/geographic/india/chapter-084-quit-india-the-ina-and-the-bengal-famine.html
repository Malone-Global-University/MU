Quit India, the INA, and the Bengal Famine

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the evening of 8 August 1942, the All-India Congress Committee, meeting on the Gowalia Tank maidan in Bombay, approved a resolution demanding an end to British rule in India. Before dawn the next morning, police began arresting Mahatma Gandhi and the Congress leadership across the country. Within hours, the organization that had passed the resolution was declared unlawful and its leaders were in custody, most of them without any sentence or any announced date of release. What followed was neither the disciplined nonviolent campaign Gandhi had envisioned nor the swift, quiet collapse the government had expected. It was a sprawling, uneven, and in places violent upheaval that the Viceroy privately compared with the rebellion of 1857.

That upheaval is one of three connected crises of British authority that this chapter examines. The second unfolded far from the streets of Bombay and Patna, in the prisoner-of-war camps of Singapore, the cities of Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia, and the jungles on India's eastern frontier, where Indian nationalists and Japan's military built an Indian National Army (INA) and, from October 1943, a Provisional Government of Free India under Subhas Chandra Bose. The third was a humanitarian catastrophe: the Bengal Famine of 1943, in which a very large number of people, estimated by scholars at between roughly one and a half and three million, died of starvation and the epidemic diseases that accompanied it, in a province that was at the same time a base for the war against Japan and the seat of the second city of the British Empire.

These three crises occurred at the same time, in the same global war, and they overlapped geographically and politically. They were not, however, parts of one movement. Congress's leaders in Ahmednagar Fort did not direct Bose, and Bose did not command the villagers of Midnapore. The INA's soldiers were vastly outnumbered by the Indian soldiers of the British Indian Army, who made up the bulk of the Allied force that fought Japan in Assam and Burma. The famine's victims were, in large part, people with little connection to any of these political projects. Historians must therefore resist the temptation to merge simultaneous events into a single narrative of "the collapse of the Raj." The central question of this chapter is more precise: how did mass rebellion, armed anticolonial resistance, and humanitarian catastrophe each reveal a different weakness in British imperial rule during the Second World War?

The answers differ. Quit India displayed both the breadth of popular anger and the state's capacity for repression, since the government held its ground and the movement did not achieve its immediate aim. The INA demonstrated the appeal of independence even among soldiers whom the British had trained and who had taken an oath to the Crown, while also exposing the moral and strategic contradictions of seeking liberation through alliance with Imperial Japan, a power whose own record in occupied Asia was brutal. The Bengal famine exposed a different kind of failure: not a challenge to British rule from outside, but a collapse of the basic responsibilities of government, carried out under an administration that was still functioning and that was still taking decisions about food, shipping, transport, and priorities.

This chapter builds on Chapter 83, which examined India's entry into the war, the Congress ministries' resignations, the Lahore Resolution, the August Offer, the Cripps Mission, and the wartime mobilization of the economy, and it does not repeat that material. It also draws on Chapter 82 for Bose's earlier Congress career, on Chapter 79 for Gandhi's political method, on Chapter 80 for peasant, labor, and women's movements, and on Chapter 72 for the colonial history of famine and famine policy. It stops in 1945, with Britain victorious but facing a transformed political situation. The trials of INA officers, the naval uprising of 1946, the elections, the Cabinet Mission, and the negotiations for independence belong to Chapter 85, and the division of the subcontinent to Chapter 86.

The evidence for these events is large, but it is also contested and politically charged, and students should read this chapter with attention to who produced each source and why. British official records, notably the documents published in the Transfer of Power series, preserve the government's perspective, its fears, and its private disagreements. Congress and other nationalist sources preserve the movement's own accounts, along with a tradition of commemoration that sometimes hardened into legend. Japanese, German, and INA materials, survivors' memoirs, and the testimony of ordinary participants offer perspectives that the official file does not. For the famine, the 1945 Famine Inquiry Commission report, press photographs, relief society records, price series, and a large body of modern scholarship allow a reconstruction of both what happened and the reasons historians disagree about why.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to describe each of the three crises in its own terms, explain why they should be kept distinct, identify the places where they intersected, and assess how, together, they altered the conditions under which Britain would face the question of Indian independence after 1945, without treating the end of the Raj as inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Congress adopted the Quit India resolution in August 1942 and why the resolution cannot be described as support for Japanese expansion.
  2. Analyze the government's decision to arrest the Congress leadership on 9 August 1942 and the legal and administrative instruments it used.
  3. Distinguish among nonviolent civil disobedience, spontaneous crowd violence, organized sabotage, and local insurgency in the events that followed.
  4. Compare the regional geography of Quit India, explaining why Bihar, eastern United Provinces, Midnapore, Satara, and parts of Bombay Presidency saw intense resistance while other regions did not.
  5. Assess the underground movement and the parallel governments of Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara, separating documented achievements from later legend.
  6. Evaluate the immediate and longer-term political results of Quit India, including the reasons several organizations did not join it.
  7. Distinguish Subhas Chandra Bose's Indian National Army from the first INA of Mohan Singh and from the far larger British Indian Army.
  8. Assess the strategic reasoning, moral ambiguities, and political contradictions of Bose's cooperation with Germany and Japan.
  9. Trace the Burma campaigns, including the 1942 retreat, the reorganization of Allied forces, Imphal and Kohima, and the reconquest of Burma.
  10. Reconstruct the Bengal Famine of 1943 as a human catastrophe, distinguishing reported deaths from excess mortality and noting the uncertainty of the estimates.
  11. Compare supply-centered and entitlement-centered explanations of the famine and evaluate how administrative, military, and political decisions interacted with them.
  12. Synthesize how rebellion, armed resistance, and famine each placed pressure on British legitimacy without producing immediate independence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Congress choose mass confrontation in 1942, at the moment of gravest military danger, and what did its leaders disagree about?
  2. How did the government's decision to arrest the leadership shape the character of the movement that followed?
  3. Why was resistance intense in some regions and limited, divided, or absent in others?
  4. What do the parallel governments of Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara reveal about the possibilities and limits of local popular authority?
  5. Why did the Muslim League, the Communist Party of India, and the Hindu Mahasabha leadership not support Quit India, and how uniform were these positions?
  6. How did Bose's strategy differ from Gandhi's, and what were its moral and political costs?
  7. In what ways did the INA's first and second phases differ, and why must the INA be separated from the British Indian Army?
  8. How did the military war in Burma and Assam shape the conditions of civilian life in eastern India?
  9. Was the Bengal Famine primarily a failure of food supply, of access to food, of administration, or of political priorities, and how do historians weigh the evidence?
  10. How did these three crises, though separate, change the political environment that Britain would face after 1945?

Historical Context

By the spring of 1942, the strategic position of British India had altered drastically. Japan's attacks on Malaya, Hong Kong, and the Philippines in December 1941 were followed by the fall of Singapore on 15 February 1942, the loss of Rangoon in March, and the Japanese occupation of the Andaman Islands. In April Japanese naval aircraft attacked Colombo and Trincomalee in Ceylon and Allied ships in the Bay of Bengal, and the east coast of India and Calcutta began to be treated as potential targets. India, the largest source of Allied military manpower outside the great powers and a vital supply base, had become a frontline region. The administrative and political arrangements that governed it, however, had been reshaped only slightly by the crisis.

Chapter 83 traced how those arrangements had unraveled. The Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, had declared India at war in September 1939 without consulting Indian legislators, and the Congress ministries had resigned in the provinces in protest. The Muslim League had meanwhile moved toward the Lahore Resolution of 1940. The August Offer of the same year promised a postwar constituent process but no immediate change, and Congress had responded with a limited, symbolic individual satyagraha. In March and April 1942, as Japanese forces advanced, the British War Cabinet sent Sir Stafford Cripps with a draft declaration offering dominion status after the war and a right for provinces to remain outside the future union. The negotiations broke down in April. The collapse left Congress without a political route to a share in power and with growing fear that India would be defended, or lost, without Indian consent.

The wartime economy, also examined in Chapter 83, put heavy pressure on the population. The Government of India expanded military procurement, requisitioned materials, increased taxation and borrowing, and printed currency to finance purchases made on Britain's behalf, producing an inflation that fell hardest on people who bought food from the market. Bengal and the eastern provinces were especially exposed. Burma, which had exported rice to India, Ceylon, and Malaya in large quantities, was lost to Japan in the first half of 1942. Eastern India meanwhile became a staging area for air bases, roads, and troop concentrations. These conditions provide the background both for the rural unrest of 1942 and for the famine of 1943, though historians dispute how directly they caused either.

Politically, the Indian landscape was divided. Congress was the largest nationalist organization, but it did not speak for all Indians. The Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah had grown rapidly during the war and opposed any Congress-led demand for immediate transfer of power that might leave the League without safeguards. The Communist Party of India, banned at the start of the war, changed its policy after the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, describing the conflict as a "People's War." The Hindu Mahasabha's leaders encouraged members to remain in official positions. The princely states, covering roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent, remained largely under the authority of their rulers and the Paramount Power (see Chapter 71). Any assessment of 1942 must start from this plurality of positions.

A final point concerns the global setting. The United States, a new ally, and China's Chiang Kai-shek, who visited India in February 1942, both pressed Britain to reach a settlement with Indian nationalists. Winston Churchill's government resisted such pressure. At the same time, the Atlantic Charter of 1941 had declared a general principle of self-government that Indian nationalists seized on, and that Churchill insisted did not apply to India. These international debates are part of the background to Quit India, and they help explain why the Congress leaders and their British opponents each believed that world opinion was watching.

Division I · April–August 1942

Quit India: The Decision for Mass Resistance

Why Congress chose confrontation, how the government responded, and how the uprising began.

Section 84.1

From the Failure of the Cripps Mission to the Quit India Resolution

The breakdown of the Cripps negotiations in the second week of April 1942 left Congress's leaders in a difficult position. The proposals had offered dominion status after the war and had allowed provinces to remain outside a future union, an arrangement that Congress regarded as a threat to Indian unity, and they had left control of defense in British hands for the duration of the war. Congress had also been unable to obtain a meaningful Indian role in the wartime government. A remark often attributed to Gandhi, that the offer was a post-dated cheque, captured the widespread view that Britain was offering promises for the future in exchange for support in a moment of danger. The Mission's failure, however, did not simply restore the previous stalemate. It happened against the background of Japanese advance, and it prompted Gandhi to rethink his position.

In the weeks that followed, Gandhi argued in his journal Harijan and in conversations that British rule had become part of India's danger. His reasoning, which was contested within Congress, ran as follows. Japan was attacking India because India was a British possession; if Britain withdrew in an orderly way, the pretext for attack would diminish, and an independent India could organize its own defense, including nonviolent resistance to any invader. He also argued that the British government's evident inability to defend Burma, Malaya, and Singapore, and the reports of colonial officials and troops abandoning civilians during the retreat, undermined its claim to protect India. His proposals shifted over the summer. Early statements implied that British forces might simply leave; in the weeks before Wardha, after discussions with colleagues and in response to criticism, he accepted that Allied forces might remain in India under an agreement with a free Indian government so that the war against Japan could continue.

Gandhi's colleagues were not united behind him. Jawaharlal Nehru had spent the preceding years insisting that Congress's sympathies lay with the Allied cause against fascism, and with China and the Soviet Union in particular; he feared that a mass struggle might weaken the defense of India and that its chaos would benefit Japan. Maulana Abul Kalam Azad shared reservations about timing but ultimately supported the resolution. Vallabhbhai Patel and Rajendra Prasad were more willing to pursue open confrontation. C. Rajagopalachari, who had urged that Congress accept some arrangement with the Muslim League to make a national government possible, left the Working Committee and broke with the party's leadership over the issue. Others, including some socialists, were impatient and felt that Gandhi had been too slow. The decision of July 1942 therefore reflected both a real division and the weight of Gandhi's authority, which was large enough to bring reluctant colleagues behind him.

The Congress Working Committee met at Wardha and on 14 July 1942 adopted a resolution authorizing, in principle, a mass movement of nonviolent resistance if Britain did not accept the demand for withdrawal. The resolution said that India's freedom was necessary both for India and for the success of the Allied cause, argued that the Indian people would only be energized to resist Japan if they believed that they were fighting for their own country, and stated that Congress had no wish to embarrass the Allied powers or to encourage Japanese aggression. It was to be placed before the All-India Congress Committee in Bombay in August. In interviews that summer Gandhi spoke of an "open rebellion," a phrase the government noted. He also intended to seek an interview with the Viceroy before launching any campaign, an intention that events overtook.

Why did the demand for immediate British withdrawal intensify while the war against the Axis continued? Several factors converged. The military reverses in Southeast Asia seemed to demonstrate that the Raj could not defend the people it governed. Inflation, shortages, requisitions, and the dislocation of the war were generating hardship that Congress's cadres were willing to present as the consequence of a government that lacked Indian consent. Many Congress workers, after two years of frustration and the near-dormancy of the 1940–41 individual satyagraha, wanted action. And Gandhi himself believed that, if Britain would not recognize Indian independence in practice, the moral and political basis of the Raj would be exposed by a mass movement, which he envisioned as a final struggle, to be led by himself and carried out nonviolently. On the British side, Linlithgow and the India Office had already concluded that if Congress launched a campaign, the government would arrest its leaders at once rather than allow another prolonged confrontation. The government's contingency plan for this, sometimes called "Operation Zero," was prepared before the AICC met.

Section 84.2

The Bombay Congress Session, 8 August 1942: Quit India and "Do or Die"

The All-India Congress Committee assembled in Bombay on 7 August 1942 and held its principal session in the open-air Gowalia Tank maidan, soon to become one of the best-known places in the history of Indian nationalism. The resolution before the delegates was moved by Jawaharlal Nehru and seconded by Vallabhbhai Patel, and was adopted on the night of 8 August after debate. A small minority, including the Communist members of the Committee, opposed it. Gandhi addressed the meeting twice, in Hindustani and in English, and the speech that has been remembered since is the one in which he gave the delegates what he called a mantra: "Do or Die." He explained the phrase as a pledge that Indians would either free their country or die in the attempt. The expression is short and was published widely at the time; the longer text of the speech should be read in the published edition of Gandhi's collected works.

The resolution itself was longer and more qualified than later memory tends to suggest. It declared that the immediate ending of British rule in India was an urgent necessity both for India and for the success of the cause of the United Nations. It proposed that a provisional government be formed after British withdrawal, representing the main sections of the Indian people, and that this government would then work with the Allied powers in the common struggle against aggression. It said, in terms that critics often overlooked, that Congress did not wish to hamper the defense of China or the Soviet Union, or to jeopardize the Allied position in the war, and that the Allied forces might continue to operate from India if necessary. The resolution also contained the threat of action: if the appeal for withdrawal were rejected, Congress would be compelled to authorize "the starting of a mass struggle on non-violent lines under the leadership of Gandhiji."

The text therefore needs to be read as a statement about three things at once: an assertion of independence as the basis for a credible war effort; a declaration that India would not fight for an empire; and a claim that the British government, by refusing Indian participation as an equal, was weakening the Allied cause. Whether a free India's defense would have been effective is a different question, and one that the British government, the Muslim League, the Communists, and many Indians answered negatively. But historians should not describe the resolution as support for Japan. Congress's leaders were hostile to Japanese imperialism, and their language about China shows an awareness of its aggression in Asia. Indeed, Nehru's position within Congress was based on this very hostility. The accusation that Congress was aiding the enemy, which the government made loudly in August 1942, was a political charge and a legal justification, not a reading of the text.

The resolution did not provide a detailed plan of action, and Gandhi did not issue one. In the days before the session he had said that he would first approach the Viceroy, and that the struggle would not begin immediately. His speeches in Bombay, however, instructed the people to regard themselves as free men and women and to act on their own responsibility if they were unable to receive orders. Those words were later interpreted in different ways: by some as an instruction to follow the discipline of nonviolence even in the leader's absence, and by others as permission to improvise, including through sabotage. The ambiguity would prove consequential after the arrests. It is one reason why historians distinguish carefully between the text of the resolution and the events that followed it.

  4. What can historians not establish from the resolution alone about what happened after 9 August?
Section 84.3

The Arrest of Congress Leadership, 9 August 1942

In the early hours of 9 August, police in Bombay and across India carried out the government's prepared plan. Gandhi, with Mahadev Desai, Mirabehn (Madeleine Slade), and others close to him, was taken from Birla House in Bombay to Pune and held at the Aga Khan Palace, which the government had designated as a detention site. Kasturba Gandhi was arrested soon afterwards and also held there. Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Maulana Azad, and other members of the Working Committee were arrested and taken to Ahmednagar Fort, where they would remain until 1945. In Patna, Rajendra Prasad was arrested, and throughout the country thousands of Congress officials, provincial committee members, and local organizers were taken into custody on the same day or in the next few days. Many were detained without trial under the emergency regulations in force.

The legal basis was the apparatus of wartime emergency government. The Defence of India Act of 1939 and the rules made under it allowed the executive to detain individuals without trial, to censor the press, to ban organizations, and to confiscate property. The Congress Working Committee, the AICC, and provincial Congress committees were declared unlawful associations. Offices were searched, funds seized, and records taken. Newspapers that carried material critical of the arrests or reporting disturbances were subject to censorship, and Gandhi's own journals, including Harijan, ceased publication. The government issued a communiqué asserting that Congress was preparing a movement to paralyze communications and to disrupt the war effort, and that the Congress leadership had to be restrained to protect India's defense.

Government records show the reasoning behind this decision. The Viceroy and his advisers believed that Congress would need time to organize a mass campaign, and that if its leaders were arrested before the campaign had begun, they could prevent it from developing. This was a lesson drawn from earlier campaigns, in which the government had allowed Gandhi to build momentum before intervening. The correspondence among the Viceroy, the provincial governors, and the Secretary of State for India also shows that the authorities regarded the war emergency as justification for a firmer policy than in earlier decades, and that Churchill's government was unwilling to make concessions. Linlithgow and Amery shared the objective of putting down the movement; they differed more in tone than in substance, and Amery tried to explain the policy to American and Indian audiences as a necessary wartime measure.

The expectation was that the movement would be broken quickly. The assumption was reasonable in light of previous experience, in which arrests of the leadership had often demoralized followers. But the arrests of 1942 had a different effect. They came at a moment of high tension, after weeks of speeches in which Gandhi had announced an imminent struggle and after the resolution had been passed. Congress's supporters therefore learned that the leaders had been seized and that the organization had been outlawed, but had not been told what they were supposed to do. The government had removed the leadership that might have restrained them. Whether the removal of the leadership increased or reduced violence is a question that historians continue to discuss, because it is impossible to know what Gandhi's own presence would have produced. What is clear is that the government's decision altered the form of the movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Defence of India Rules

The Defence of India Rules were regulations issued under the Defence of India Act of 1939, which gave the Government of India broad emergency powers for the duration of the war. They authorized preventive detention without trial, press censorship, bans on organizations and meetings, control of movement and property, and special penalties for offenses against the war effort. They were the legal instrument by which Congress was outlawed in August 1942 and by which many of its members were detained.

The Rules matter because they illustrate how wartime emergency government could be used against domestic political opposition and not only against enemy agents. They should not be confused with ordinary criminal law, under which a person would be tried and sentenced. Detention under the Rules meant imprisonment on executive order, which is why many Quit India prisoners had no trial and no fixed term of release.

Section 84.4

The August Uprising: Strikes, Demonstrations, Sabotage, and Popular Rebellion

The first reaction came in the cities. On 9 August, crowds gathered in Bombay, where Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the national flag at Gowalia Tank after the police had dispersed the AICC meeting, and in Delhi, Pune, Ahmedabad, Calcutta, and Madras. Students walked out of colleges and schools in many towns. Textile workers in Ahmedabad stopped work and kept the mills closed for several months, one of the longest sustained strikes of the movement. Industrial workers at Jamshedpur, in the steel works of the Tata company, struck for nearly two weeks in August, partly over wartime wages and conditions. In Bombay, Delhi, and elsewhere, hartals and processions encountered police batons, tear gas, and firing. In Patna, on 11 August, police fired on a procession of students who were attempting to raise the national flag over the Secretariat, killing several of them; the incident became one of the movement's best-known martyrdoms.

Within days, the disturbances spread from cities to small towns and countryside, particularly in the Gangetic plain. Crowds attacked police stations, post offices, railway stations, and government offices, which represented the machinery of colonial authority. Telegraph and telephone wires were cut, railway lines torn up, bridges damaged, and trains stopped. The official returns recorded attacks on hundreds of railway stations, post offices, and police stations, as well as widespread damage to communications. In some districts, the civil administration effectively ceased to function for days or weeks, and in a few places, discussed in §84.7, local people attempted to establish their own governing bodies. The government responded with police firing, mass arrests, collective fines on villages, flogging, and the dispatch of army units; in some districts aircraft were used to fire on crowds or saboteurs, an escalation that was recorded in official correspondence.

It is important to separate several kinds of action that were often described together. The first was peaceful civil disobedience in the Gandhian tradition: processions, hartals, flag-raising, picketing, courting arrest, and refusal to pay taxes. The second was spontaneous crowd violence, in which large gatherings attacked police or officials, sometimes killing them, as in the case of Chimur in the Central Provinces, where a mob killed several officials in mid-August, or at several sites in Bihar and the United Provinces. The third was organized sabotage, in which small groups, often with some training or direction from underground organizers, cut wires, destroyed bridges, and attacked railways, usually at night and often with explosives or tools. The fourth was local insurgency, in which armed groups, sometimes organized around Congress, socialist, or peasant networks, attempted to control territory or attack the apparatus of the state systematically.

These categories mattered to contemporaries because Gandhi's commitment to nonviolence was central to his understanding of the movement. He had told the AICC that the struggle was to be nonviolent, and during his detention he and his associates repeatedly distanced themselves from violence, while also blaming government repression for provoking it. But it would be misleading to say that Congress's official position described what happened. Once the national leadership was in prison, the movement was directed by local organizers, by younger activists who were less committed to Gandhian discipline, and, in many places, by people who had no organizational affiliation at all. Some Congress socialists, notably Jayaprakash Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia, explicitly endorsed sabotage as part of a revolutionary strategy against what they regarded as an illegitimate government. Gandhi himself, in correspondence with the Viceroy from detention, held the government responsible for the violence. The ambiguity of the movement's relationship with nonviolence remains one of its central historical problems.

The scale of the disturbances shocked the government. Linlithgow, in his private letters to Churchill, described the situation as the most serious rebellion since 1857, and he noted that the disruption of communications in eastern India had at some points been severe. Yet by the end of September the worst of the mass disturbances had been suppressed in most areas. The suppression was not simply a matter of force; it also reflected the movement's lack of a unified command, the absence of a program beyond the immediate crisis, and the government's retention of control over the army, the police, and the railways. The next division examines how this uneven pattern developed across regions.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Regional Participant in the August Uprising (Illustrative Reconstruction)

Note: this is a composite reconstruction, not an account of any one named person. It is based on patterns recorded in official returns, trial records, and later memoirs of participants in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh.

Imagine a young man of a cultivating family in a district of western Bihar, enrolled at a small-town college. In the first week of August, news arrives through newspapers and word of mouth that Gandhi and the Congress leaders have been arrested. He has no instructions from the Congress committee, whose office in the town has been closed by the police. Students gather, some from nearby villages, and march to the local government offices carrying the flag. Over the next days the procession grows; people from the surrounding villages, some of them tenants with grievances against landlords or moneylenders, some of them reacting to rising prices and to requisitions of grain and carts for the war effort, join in. Some are armed with lathis and sickles.

What happens next depends on local circumstances: whether a police sub-inspector decides to open fire, whether the crowd decides to attack the thana, whether a small group decides, at night, to cut the railway line. A participant might be a bystander who becomes involved, an organizer who has deliberately chosen sabotage, or a villager whose village is later fined for the actions of others. The consequences are also uneven: some are arrested, flogged, or shot, others escape into the countryside and join the underground, and villagers who took no part may have their property seized in a collective punishment. The experience of this composite participant illustrates why generalization about "the movement" is hazardous: it was made up of thousands of local actions, each shaped by local conditions, and its meaning for those who took part was bound up with grievances that preceded the war.

Section 84.5

The Regional Geography of Quit India: Bihar, the United Provinces, Bengal, and Maharashtra

The movement's intensity varied sharply across regions. It was most severe in a broad belt across eastern and northern India. In Bihar, the districts of Patna, Bhagalpur, Monghyr, Saran, Champaran, and Gaya and neighboring areas experienced widespread attacks on police stations, railway lines, and government offices, and the province became the principal site of the government's military response. The eastern United Provinces, notably Ballia, Azamgarh, Ghazipur, Gorakhpur, and Jaunpur, saw similar disturbances, with Ballia at the center. In Bengal, the movement was strongest in the Midnapore district, particularly in the subdivisions of Tamluk and Contai, where a long tradition of nationalist organization combined with local grievances. In Bombay Presidency, Satara, Ahmednagar, Khandesh, and parts of Gujarat such as Surat and Kaira were active centers, while in Orissa, Balasore and Talcher were affected. Smaller but significant episodes took place in Assam, where Kanaklata Barua, a teenage activist, was killed while leading a procession to a police station at Gohpur in September 1942, and in parts of Karnataka and the Andhra coast.

Other regions were relatively quiet. Punjab, Sind, and the North-West Frontier Province, where Muslim-majority provincial politics were dominated by the Unionists, the League, or their allies, and where Congress had a limited following among the rural population, saw little participation. In much of Madras Presidency and the southern states, Congress organization was strong in some districts but the rural disturbances were weaker, and Kerala and most of Tamil Nadu avoided sustained rebellion. The princely states, with few exceptions, were largely quiet, though some saw local agitations and the arrests of Congress sympathizers. The Quit India movement was thus intense but not universal, and its geography does not match any simple division of "Hindu" and "Muslim" India or of "north" and "south."

What explains the pattern? Historians point to several overlapping factors. The strength of Congress's organization before 1942 mattered; places with a dense network of committees, volunteers, and local leaders, such as Midnapore or the Gujarat districts, were more likely to generate sustained activity. So did the presence of agrarian grievances: in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, small cultivators and tenants experienced rising prices, indebtedness, and wartime requisitions, and the Kisan Sabha tradition of the 1930s had created networks of peasant mobilization. In Satara, an older non-Brahmin movement had created a structure of rural leadership that was able to adapt to the political moment. Student networks gave the movement much of its energy in towns, and railways, as the principal means by which the government moved police and troops, were both a target and a means of control. The police and military capacity of the government also varied: where troops arrived quickly the rebellion was often contained; where communications were severed for days, the movement could consolidate.

Social composition was also uneven. The urban middle classes, students, and teachers took part in large numbers; so did sections of the peasantry, especially middle peasants and in places tenants and agricultural laborers, and workers in some mills and industrial centers. Large landlords and many officials usually supported the government. Business interests were divided, with some contributing funds to the movement while others profited from wartime contracts. Muslim participation was real but limited; Muslim leaders in Congress such as Azad were imprisoned, and Muslim individuals took part in the movement in various places, but the League's opposition and the reluctance of many Muslim voters to support Congress meant that Quit India did not draw broad Muslim support. Lower-caste and Dalit communities also had varied reactions, shaped by their local relationships to landholders and to Congress; some, in places, participated, while leaders such as B. R. Ambedkar, in the Viceroy's Executive Council at the time, were skeptical of the movement. Any claim that Quit India was "the whole Indian people rising" needs, therefore, to be qualified by this evidence.

Section 84.6

Underground Resistance: Socialists, Women, Students, and Secret Communications

After the leadership's arrest, a younger generation of organizers, many of them Congress Socialists, took over. Jayaprakash Narayan, who had been detained in the Hazaribagh jail in Bihar, escaped with several companions on the night of Diwali in November 1942, an escape that became a legend in its own right. Once at large, he worked to build a network of underground cells and of guerrilla squads, known as the Azad Dasta or "freedom squads," some of which trained in camps in the Nepal Terai. Ram Manohar Lohia and Achyut Patwardhan worked in a similar way in western and central India. Aruna Asaf Ali, who had hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank on 9 August, went underground and helped to edit the movement's clandestine journal Inquilab with Lohia. Sucheta Kripalani, who would later become a significant political figure, also operated underground.

The best-known piece of clandestine communication was the Congress Radio. It began broadcasting from Bombay on 14 August 1942 and was organized by a small group that included the young student Usha Mehta, Vithalbhai Jhaveri, Chandrakant Jhaveri, and Nanka Motwani, with technical help from some radio engineers. The station used shortwave transmissions, moved among locations to avoid detection, and broadcast news of the movement, appeals to continue the struggle, and recorded speeches. It was a modest operation, and its audience is difficult to estimate, but it had an influence far greater than its technical reach because it contradicted the government's censorship and showed that the movement could still communicate. Police traced the transmitter in November 1942, and Usha Mehta and several others were arrested; she was tried and sentenced to imprisonment. The history of the radio shows both the ingenuity of the organizers and the practical risks they faced: broadcasting in this way was illegal, and anyone caught faced imprisonment and prosecution.

Other forms of underground communication included cyclostyled pamphlets, bulletins, and handwritten notices circulated through couriers, many of them students and women, who could often move more easily than men under police surveillance. The activities of these networks included distributing information, raising funds, hiding fugitives, supplying materials for sabotage, and, in some cases, organizing attacks. The government's interest in them is documented in police reports. The underground faced serious limitations: it was always at risk of infiltration, it lacked a unified command, it depended on local sympathizers who could be intimidated, and it was divided over methods. Gandhi's criticisms from detention of acts of violence and the open disagreements between the socialists and the Gandhian wing of Congress compounded these difficulties.

Women played prominent roles. Besides the organizers named above, thousands of women took part in processions and picketing, and some were killed or injured. Matangini Hazra, an elderly Congress worker from Midnapore, was shot dead on 29 September 1942 while leading a procession toward the Tamluk police station, reportedly holding the national flag, and Kanaklata Barua was killed in Assam in the same month. Their deaths have become part of the commemorative tradition of Quit India. Historians treat the evidence about them with the care that applies to all heroic narratives. The basic facts of their deaths are well attested in contemporary and later sources; many of the details, such as their last words, come from later memory. Students should distinguish what is documented, what is reconstructed from testimony, and what is the language of commemoration. This distinction does not diminish their significance; it clarifies what kind of historical claim is being made.

Section 84.7

Parallel Governments: Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara

The most striking local experiments in 1942–1944 were the parallel governments that attempted to replace colonial authority at the district or subdivision level. They differed in leadership, duration, and character, and treating them as a single phenomenon misrepresents all of them.

Ballia, in the eastern United Provinces, was the earliest and shortest. After days of demonstrations and attacks on government offices, crowds in August 1942 forced the release of Congress prisoners, including the local leader Chittu Pandey, and on or about 19 August a popular administration took over the district headquarters. Pandey and his colleagues assumed authority, and some of the district's police and officials either cooperated or withdrew. The experiment lasted barely a week; British troops, including a force sent from outside the district, reoccupied Ballia, and its leaders were arrested or fled. The Ballia episode shows how quickly local state authority could be paralyzed by a combination of mass action and administrative weakness, and how quickly it could be restored when the army arrived. Its legacy in local memory was larger than its duration.

Tamluk, in the Midnapore district of Bengal, was a more organized and longer-lasting effort. The Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar, or Tamralipta National Government, was proclaimed on 17 December 1942, after months of local agitation, the killings of September, and the devastation of the October cyclone. Led by Satish Chandra Samanta and other Congress workers, it established an administrative structure with departments, a volunteer force called the Bidyut Vahini, informal courts, and relief arrangements, and it collected funds from local supporters. It attempted to coordinate cyclone relief and to supply rice to affected areas at a time when the official administration was disrupted and the denial policies were harming the district's livelihoods. It also used coercive methods, including the punishment of alleged informers and offenders by its own courts, and its relationship with the Congress organization, which had been banned, was necessarily indirect. The government attempted to suppress it and largely failed to eliminate it until 1944, when it was dissolved after Gandhi, who had been told of its work, advised that it should end following his release.

Satara, in the western Deccan, produced the longest-lived experiment. The Prati Sarkar, or parallel government, led by Nana Patil and associated with Y. B. Chavan and others, took shape from late 1942 and 1943 in the countryside of Satara and neighboring districts. It had its roots in the non-Brahmin movement and in peasant networks, and it relied on village-level organization, an armed volunteer force known as the Tufan Sena, people's courts that adjudicated local disputes, and campaigns against moneylenders, black marketeers, and government informers. It drew popular support in places, particularly from peasant families who saw it as an alternative to a state that taxed and requisitioned, but it also used violence, including attacks on police and on those it regarded as enemies, and at times came into conflict with local landlords and other groups. It continued into 1945 and beyond, with the exact end date varying across accounts, and its history overlaps with the later politics of the region.

Comparing the three suggests several conclusions. They were all products of local conditions, and none was created by a central Congress decision. They were organized by people with strong local standing and previous political experience, and they relied on a combination of consent and coercion. Their popular support was real but not unanimous, and they should not be presented as uniformly democratic: their courts, volunteer forces, and punitive activities exercised power in ways that were contested by some of those subject to them. Their durability depended on geography, on the strength of local networks, and on the capacity and attention of the colonial state, which was stretched by war and could not devote unlimited resources to remote districts. Finally, their relationship to Congress was complex: they acted in its name, but they were not directed by its leadership, and Gandhi, from detention, was ambivalent about methods that included force.

Section 84.8

Colonial Repression, Political Disagreement, and the Historical Results of Quit India

The government's repression was extensive. The extent of repression depends on the date and the measure used. On 11 February 1943, Secretary of State Leo Amery reported to the House of Commons that 60,229 people had been arrested by 1 December 1942 in connection with the disturbances, of whom 39,498 remained under arrest on that date; these were cumulative arrests and a point-in-time custodial figure, not two totals to be added. For the same reporting period he listed 470 occasions of police firing and 68 of military firing, but did not provide a reconciled death toll in that answer. A later official historical account cited approximately 940 deaths from firing across roughly the first five months of disturbances; nationalist accounts sometimes gave substantially larger figures. These series differ in their time spans, reporting practices, and definitions and must not be treated as interchangeable totals for the whole movement through 1943. Other measures included collective fines imposed on villages and towns for the actions of individuals, the burning of property, the use of troops and aircraft, and heavy censorship of the press. Many individuals were tried under special ordinances, and some were sentenced to death or transportation.

Why did several groups remain outside the movement? The Muslim League under Jinnah denounced Quit India as an attempt by the Hindu-majority Congress to coerce Britain into handing power to itself, and it instructed its members not to join. The League gained politically from Congress's absence and from the cooperation of its provincial governments with the wartime administration. At the end of 1943, Jinnah's followers at Karachi adopted the slogan "Divide and Quit" as a counter to Congress's "Quit India." But the Muslim response was not uniform. Individual Muslims took part, Muslim members of Congress were in prison, and some Muslim leaders outside the League disagreed with its policy. The Communist Party of India, whose policy had shifted after June 1941 to support for the war against Germany, opposed the movement as undermining the anti-fascist effort; its members were released from detention and its ban was lifted in July 1942, and it gained organizational advantages by cooperating with the government, but also lost standing among nationalists who viewed it as having abandoned the struggle. Some Communists, at local level, disregarded the party line. The leadership of the Hindu Mahasabha, including V. D. Savarkar, advised its members to remain in government posts and to support the war effort in order to strengthen Hindu interests, and Mahasabha ministers participated in the Bengal coalition; members varied in their actions. Many princely rulers cooperated with the government. These positions reflected different judgments of the war, of Congress, and of their own communities' interests, and should not be reduced to disloyalty or to simple opportunism.

Gandhi's detention became a political event in its own right. In his correspondence with the Viceroy from the Aga Khan Palace, he rejected the charge that Congress was responsible for the violence and argued that the government's provocation had led to it. Linlithgow declined to accept his explanation and demanded that he disavow the disturbances publicly. In February 1943, Gandhi began a fast of twenty-one days, announced as a fast to protest the charges against him rather than as a fast against the government, which was widely understood as a challenge to it. His condition became critical, and the government, fearing that his death in custody would provoke a major crisis, prepared plans for the aftermath; three members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned in protest against the government's handling of the fast. Gandhi survived and broke the fast on 3 March 1943. The personal costs of detention were severe. His secretary and close associate Mahadev Desai died in the Aga Khan Palace on 15 August 1942, only days after the arrests, and Kasturba Gandhi died there on 22 February 1944. Gandhi, who suffered from malaria and declining health, was released on 6 May 1944, on grounds of ill health, though the government also judged that his release would no longer pose a risk.

How should historians evaluate Quit India's results? In immediate terms, the movement failed: the British government did not agree to withdraw, Congress was outlawed and its leadership was imprisoned for nearly three years, and by the end of 1942 the worst disturbances were suppressed. The government's control of the army, police, and communications remained effective and Indian troops continued to fight for the Allies. In some respects, indeed, the movement strengthened rivals such as the Muslim League, whose wartime expansion is described in Chapter 83 and whose position in the 1945–46 elections is examined in Chapter 85. But Quit India's longer-term significance was substantial. It demonstrated that British rule depended on Indian cooperation, in police, administration, and the army, and that much of that cooperation could not be taken for granted. It restored Congress's standing as the main nationalist organization after its leaders emerged from prison in 1945. It established "Quit India" as a lasting symbol of the struggle, and it left a political memory in regions such as Midnapore, Satara, and Ballia. It cannot, however, be claimed that Quit India directly ended British rule. The transfer of power in 1947 resulted from a wider set of causes, including the war's effects on Britain, the shifts of 1945–46, and the politics of communal division, addressed in the following chapters.

Section 84.9

Subhas Chandra Bose: Escape from India and the Search for Axis Support

Subhas Chandra Bose's political career before 1941, discussed in Chapter 82, had taken him from the presidency of Congress in 1938–39 to a break with Gandhi and the formation of the Forward Bloc, and from there to repeated imprisonment by the British. In 1940 he was detained in Calcutta and, after a fast, was placed under house arrest. In mid-January 1941 he left India secretly. Disguised, he traveled by train to the northwest and crossed into Afghanistan with the help of Forward Bloc colleagues, and from Kabul, with assistance from the Italian and German legations, he made his way through the Soviet Union to Germany, arriving in Berlin in early April 1941. His escape was a sensational event and was long wrapped in secrecy, which contributed to the legend that grew around him.

His strategic calculation was straightforward and was one he had held since the 1930s: Britain's difficulty was India's opportunity, and Britain's enemies might assist Indian independence. Bose did not claim to admire all of the regimes he approached, though he was impressed by their discipline and organization and had frequently argued that India needed a strong, centralized movement. He believed that the Allied cause, with its imperial ally in Britain, was no guarantee of freedom for India, and that the Axis powers' war effort could be turned to help an Indian liberation army. He also believed that Congress's methods alone would not be enough to remove the British. He sought, accordingly, German support for an independent Indian organization, military assistance, and the right to speak to Indians by radio.

In Berlin, Bose was given a Special India Department in the Foreign Office and the Free India Centre, and he began radio broadcasts to India from early 1942 under the name Azad Hind Radio. It was in Berlin that he came to be called "Netaji," or leader. Germany also assisted in the formation of the Indian Legion, a military unit recruited from Indian prisoners of war captured by Axis forces in North Africa, which grew to a few thousand men. The Legion swore an oath of allegiance that referred to Hitler as well as to the cause of Indian freedom, a fact that has been a source of controversy; it later served in German-controlled territory in Europe, and in 1944 was attached to the Waffen-SS. Bose's own authority over the Legion was limited. His meeting with Hitler in May 1942 produced little, and Hitler showed no willingness to make a public commitment to Indian independence. The German government was cautious about promising anything that might commit it to a long-range Asian campaign, and Hitler's own writings had disparaged India's capacity for self-rule. During this period, Bose also formed a relationship with Emilie Schenkl, whom he had known since the 1930s; their daughter was born in November 1942.

Frustrated by Germany's limited interest, Bose turned to Japan, whose rapid conquests in Southeast Asia had created a very different strategic setting. In February 1943 he left Germany in a German submarine and, after a long voyage around the Cape, transferred in April to a Japanese submarine off Madagascar, an extraordinarily rare and risky transfer between two submarines in the Indian Ocean. He reached Sumatra in May, went to Tokyo in June, where he met the Prime Minister Hideki Tojo, and arrived in Singapore in early July 1943. These movements reflect the exceptional commitment of Bose and the support of the German and Japanese navies, but they also show how his project depended on belligerent powers whose interests were not identical to India's.

Historians have to examine, without either celebrating or condemning in advance, the implications of this alliance. Bose never concealed his willingness to work with the enemies of Britain, and he argued in public that his goal was independence, not the victory of any foreign power. Critics, then and later, have argued that the Axis powers were not liberators, that Nazi Germany's racial ideology and Imperial Japan's record in China and Southeast Asia were incompatible with the freedom he proclaimed, and that no Indian nationalist should have been willing to cooperate with them. Defenders respond that Bose operated in a world in which the dominant imperial power, Britain, was also an oppressor, that he sought leverage rather than ideological identification, and that his actions should be judged in the context of the political choices available to him. The question is revisited in the Historical Debate that follows §84.12.

Section 84.10

The First Indian National Army: Mohan Singh, Rash Behari Bose, and Indian Prisoners of War

The Indian National Army, as a first organization, did not begin with Subhas Chandra Bose. Its origins lay in the Japanese victories in Malaya and Singapore in early 1942. When the British surrendered Singapore on 15 February, tens of thousands of Indian soldiers of the British Indian Army, and many thousands more from other units in Malaya, became prisoners of war. A Japanese intelligence officer, Major Fujiwara Iwaichi, who headed a liaison organization and had earlier made contact with Indian officers in northern Malaya, arranged the transfer of the Indian prisoners to the authority of Captain Mohan Singh, an Indian officer of the 1st Battalion, 14th Punjab Regiment, who had been separated from his unit during the retreat. At a meeting at Farrer Park in Singapore on 17 February 1942, the Indian prisoners were handed over to Indian officers, and Mohan Singh announced the formation of an Indian army to fight for India's independence. Roughly forty thousand Indian prisoners are often described as having been handed over to Mohan Singh's authority, though the numbers of those who actually enrolled and trained are lower and uncertain.

The political dimension of the project was provided by Indian nationalists living in East and Southeast Asia. Rash Behari Bose, a veteran revolutionary who had lived in Japan since 1915 (see Chapter 77), played a central role. A conference of Indian representatives in Tokyo in March 1942 and a larger conference in Bangkok in June led to the formation of the Indian Independence League, with branches throughout Southeast Asia and a council of action, and to the establishment of the INA as its military arm. Mohan Singh formally constituted the First Division of the INA in September 1942. The League drew on a large civilian base, since Indian communities in Malaya, Burma, Thailand, and elsewhere included merchants, professionals, and, in great numbers, plantation and manual workers, many of them Tamils, and these communities provided money, volunteers, and political support.

The first INA collapsed within a year of its creation. The central problem was the relationship with the Japanese military. Mohan Singh wanted an autonomous Indian army that would fight only on terms agreed with the Japanese authorities, would not be treated as a mere auxiliary, and would not be used against its own principles. The Japanese, who regarded the INA as chiefly a means of propaganda and subversion among Indian troops, were unwilling to grant the degree of independence he demanded, and tensions increased over arms, command, and the deployment of Indian soldiers. In December 1942, after a series of disputes, Mohan Singh ordered the dissolution of the force; the Japanese military authorities arrested him on 29 December and he was held in custody. The first INA ceased to exist. It would be a mistake to credit Subhas Chandra Bose with founding it; the army he commanded later in 1943 was, to a large extent, a reconstitution of the force that Mohan Singh and Rash Behari Bose had created.

Students should also consider the circumstances in which soldiers made their choices. The men who joined the first INA were not all driven by the same motives. Some were political nationalists; some believed that Britain had abandoned them; some had resentments against the officer corps or the imperial hierarchy; some hoped to escape the conditions of captivity, which, for Indian prisoners in Japanese hands, included hunger, disease, and, for those who refused to cooperate, severe treatment. Reports and later testimonies record that soldiers who declined to join were in some cases beaten, deprived of food, or sent to forced-labor camps, and that Indian troops transferred to labor service in Southeast Asia and the Pacific died in large numbers. Others joined out of a mixture of conviction and pressure, and some joined while privately intending to return to British service when the opportunity arose. The evidence does not support a single account of the prisoners as either fully voluntary or fully coerced. The question of what loyalty meant, and what an oath to the Crown required of a soldier whose government had surrendered, became a central issue in the postwar trials of INA officers examined in Chapter 85.

Section 84.11

Azad Hind: Bose's Leadership, the Provisional Government, and the Rani of Jhansi Regiment

Bose arrived in Singapore on 2 July 1943. Two days later, at a meeting of the Indian Independence League, Rash Behari Bose, who was elderly and in poor health, handed over its leadership to him. Within weeks, Subhas Bose was addressing large crowds of Indian civilians, who responded with enthusiasm, and he called for "total mobilization" and for a march on Delhi. The slogans of his movement, "Jai Hind" and "Chalo Delhi," would become part of India's political vocabulary. He reorganized the INA, drawing in former members of Mohan Singh's force, new recruits from among prisoners of war, and a large number of civilian volunteers from the Indian communities of Southeast Asia, and he reconstituted the army's command. Some units were named after Indian leaders, including Gandhi, Azad, and Nehru, a decision that reflected his wish to present the INA as the continuation of the broader nationalist movement. Hindustani, written in Roman script, was adopted as the army's language, in part to emphasize its unity across regional and religious lines.

On 21 October 1943 in Singapore, Bose announced the establishment of the Provisional Government of Free India, or Azad Hind. He served as head of state, prime minister, and minister of war and foreign affairs, and he appointed a cabinet that included representatives of the Indian communities in the region and several military officers. The government claimed authority over all Indians and over the territory of India, and it adopted a constitution-like set of principles, a flag, and a declaration of war on Britain and the United States. Japan recognized it within days, followed by Germany, Italy, and several other Axis or Axis-aligned governments, including those of Burma under Ba Maw, Thailand, and the Philippines. Japan handed over administration of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands to the Provisional Government in a ceremonial transfer in December 1943, which Bose visited, though effective control remained with the Japanese military.

What was the Provisional Government? It was not a government in the ordinary sense. It did not control Indian territory, apart from the nominal arrangements in the Andamans, and it depended on Japan for security, arms, supplies, and much of its funding. It did, however, raise considerable money and goods from the Indian communities of Southeast Asia, through voluntary donations, a savings and fund-raising apparatus, and, in some accounts, pressure on wealthy individuals, and it maintained a civil administration for its own organizations, a propaganda machinery, and a bank. It was also a claim of sovereignty that mattered symbolically. It asserted that Indians were capable of constituting their own state, that the independence struggle could be conducted from outside the territory of British India, and that a government in arms stood against British rule. Whether it possessed political legitimacy in the eyes of Indians within India is a different question; the British authorities had tightly censored information about it, and Congress's leaders, in detention, had no opportunity to respond. When they were released, their responses reflected both admiration for Bose's courage and discomfort with the alliance.

The Rani of Jhansi Regiment, formed on 22 October 1943, the day after the proclamation, was among the most distinctive features of Bose's movement. It was a women's combat unit under the command of Lakshmi Swaminathan, a doctor who had practiced in Singapore and who would later be known as Lakshmi Sahgal, and it recruited several hundred, and eventually about a thousand, women, most of them young women from the Indian communities of Malaya and Singapore, many of them daughters of plantation workers and shopkeepers. Their training included drill, weapons use, and physical endurance, and some of the regiment's members served as nurses and in other support roles. The regiment's name recalled Lakshmibai, the Rani of Jhansi, who had been a leader in the rebellion of 1857 (see Chapter 69). Lakshmi Swaminathan was also appointed minister for women's organization in the Provisional Government. The regiment's military role was limited; it saw little combat, and some of its members were sent into Burma in 1944–45, where they suffered hardship during the retreat. Its importance was symbolic and social: it showed women taking part in a military force in a colonial society in which women's public roles were sharply restricted, and it expressed the movement's claim to represent all sections of the Indian community.

Section 84.12

The INA as an Armed Independence Movement: Organization, Military Ambitions, and Political Contradictions

The INA that Bose commanded was a force of tens of thousands of men, of whom a considerable proportion were former prisoners of war and a significant number were civilian volunteers from the Indian communities of Malaya, Burma, and Thailand, with a smaller number of women in the Rani of Jhansi Regiment and in auxiliary roles. It was organized into divisions and brigades with an officer corps drawn partly from former officers of the British Indian Army and partly from civilians who were commissioned. Training was conducted at camps in Malaya and Burma, with Japanese instructors and equipment in limited quantity. It was lightly armed, much of its equipment was captured or supplied by the Japanese, and its supply system was weak. The army's moral claims were strong: it presented itself as a national army of liberation whose soldiers were Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs sharing food and quarters, an image of unity deliberately cultivated in contrast to the colonial army's classification of soldiers by caste and "martial race" (see Chapter 73).

Bose's goal was to bring the INA into India as a fighting force that would inspire a wider rising. He hoped that, once the army set foot on Indian soil, Indian soldiers in the British forces and the civilian population would turn against the British. This expectation rested on a political reading of 1942 and on the belief that the Indian Army's loyalty was fragile. He insisted that the INA should fight as an independent national army under its own command and its own flag, rather than as a unit subordinated to the Japanese, and the agreements he negotiated with the Japanese command provided for the INA's participation in the planned offensive on the Indo-Burma frontier, discussed in §84.15. The Japanese, for their part, regarded the INA as a source of propaganda value and as a modest military auxiliary, and some Japanese officers distrusted its reliability.

The movement's contradictions were substantial. The first concerned dependence. An army that claimed to embody Indian sovereignty depended on a foreign power for arms, transport, food, and the means of reaching India. Bose repeatedly sought to preserve autonomy, and in some respects he succeeded in negotiating terms, but the structure of the relationship was unequal. The second was the character of the ally. Japan's occupation of Southeast Asia included the massacre of Chinese civilians in Singapore in 1942, forced labor on the Burma-Thailand railway that killed many Asian laborers, including large numbers of Tamil and other Indian workers, and brutal treatment of prisoners and civilians. Bose and his colleagues were aware of some of this, and the extent to which they could influence it was limited. The third was internal: Bose's rhetoric of national unity and equality coexisted with an authoritarian style of command, a cult of the leader, and disciplinary procedures that included punishment of those regarded as traitors, a subject that would become relevant at the postwar trials. Admirers argue that these features were necessary for a revolutionary army fighting under extreme conditions; critics contend that they reflected a leadership style that was not democratic.

Students should distinguish carefully between the INA's military capability and its ideological and symbolic influence. Its military role was small compared with the vast Allied and Japanese armies in the theater, and its battlefield performance was hampered by shortages, disease, and the failure of the Japanese offensive. Its symbolic effect, however, was large. News of the INA, when it reached India in 1945 through the trials and the press, would stir public emotion far beyond its military weight, as Chapter 85 describes. Reserving the trials, the defense campaigns, and the political rehabilitation of the INA for that chapter prevents an anachronistic reading of 1943–45, when most Indians knew little of the movement and when, in the Allied war effort, Indian soldiers of the British Indian Army were the larger, and more consequential, military force.

Bose's own fate belongs to the closing of this narrative. As the Japanese position in Burma collapsed in 1945, he left Rangoon in late April and traveled through Bangkok and Saigon. He was reported to have died on 18 August 1945 of burns after the plane in which he was traveling crashed at Taihoku (Taipei) in Japanese-held Taiwan. Many historians accept this account, but it was denied by some of his supporters for decades, and several official inquiries in independent India reached differing conclusions. This unresolved controversy, which belongs to the memory of Bose rather than to the history of the war, is discussed in Chapter 111.

Historical Debate

Bose's Alliance with the Axis Powers and the INA: Competing Interpretations

The question: How should historians evaluate Bose's decision to seek help from Germany and Japan, and what was the INA's significance?

Interpretation A — Anti-imperial strategy and national inspiration: Scholars sympathetic to Bose, such as Sugata Bose, emphasize his consistent anti-imperialism, his rejection of fascist ideology in his political ideas, his insistence on the INA's autonomy and its secular character, and the inspiring effect of the INA on Indian opinion and on the morale of Indian soldiers. On this view, his alliance was a calculated use of the enemy of his enemy, and the INA shifted the terms of Indian politics by showing that Indians would fight for independence.
Interpretation B — Moral and strategic failure: Critics argue that cooperation with Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan was morally indefensible and strategically naive, that the Axis powers had no intention of granting India real independence, that the INA was a Japanese instrument whose military value was slight, and that Bose's authoritarian style and admiration for the discipline of the Axis states reveal affinities that cannot be explained away. Some also argue that the movement's symbolic value has been exaggerated by later nationalist memory.

Evidence: Both sides rely on Bose's speeches and writings, German and Japanese records, INA documents, memoirs, and British intelligence reports. They disagree on how much weight to give to his stated intentions and to the practical effects of his actions, and on the comparison with other nationalists who did not take this course.

Why it matters: The question affects how the nationalist movement is remembered and how choices under colonial rule are judged. It cannot be resolved by simply praising or condemning Bose. A historian can accept that his motives were anti-imperial and that he was a skilled and courageous leader while also recognizing that the alliance involved moral compromise and a limited military outcome.

Section 84.13

The Fall of Burma and the Retreat to India, 1942

Japan began its invasion of Burma from Thailand in January 1942. The British forces, composed largely of British Indian Army divisions, a Burmese contingent, and some Chinese units, were poorly prepared for the campaign. They lost Moulmein at the end of January, suffered a disaster at the Sittang River in February when a bridge was destroyed before many of the troops had crossed, and evacuated Rangoon on 7–8 March. The campaign then moved northwards along the Irrawaddy and Sittang valleys. Mandalay fell on 1 May, and by then the British and Indian forces, commanded in the field by Lieutenant-General William Slim, who led the Burma Corps, were in retreat toward India across the Chindwin and over the mountains. The retreat ended in mid-May, as the monsoon broke, at Imphal in Manipur. The army arrived exhausted and depleted but largely intact, and it was a defeat of the first order for British prestige.

Alongside the soldiers, hundreds of thousands of civilians fled. Before the war, about a million people of Indian origin lived in Burma, many of them laborers in the rice mills, ports, and plantations, merchants and moneylenders, clerks, and professionals (see Chapter 75). When the Japanese advanced, a large number of them attempted to reach India. Some left by sea or air from Rangoon, but most traveled overland, along trails through the Hukawng valley and over the Chin and Naga hills toward Assam and Manipur. Estimates of the numbers who made the journey are several hundred thousand, and the number who died on the way, from disease, exhaustion, hunger, and exposure, is unknown but has been estimated at tens of thousands. The government's evacuation arrangements were chaotic and, according to contemporary reports, showed discrimination in favor of Europeans, whose routes and facilities were better than those provided for Indians. This discrimination was bitterly resented and reinforced the argument made in Gandhi's circle that the Raj valued its own people above its subjects. The refugees who reached Assam and Bengal brought stories of the retreat that spread through the eastern provinces, and many were housed in relief camps that strained local resources.

The loss of Burma had immediate military and economic consequences. For the army, the Chindwin and the mountains of Manipur and the Naga hills were now the frontier of India, and the Japanese were poised to exploit any weakness. Assam became a front-line province, and the road, rail, and river systems of the northeast were put under heavy military demand. For the economy, Burma had been one of the world's greatest rice exporters, and before the war it had sent roughly a million and a half tons of rice annually to India, a modest proportion of India's total consumption but significant for deficit regions and the cities, and a larger share for Ceylon and Malaya. When the supply ended, the Government of India had to adjust the distribution of grain among provinces, and prices began to rise. The loss of Burma also intensified fears of an invasion of Bengal, which led, in coastal districts, to the denial policies discussed in §84.18. In December 1942, Japanese aircraft began raiding Calcutta, causing a partial exodus from the city; the raids were limited in scale but their psychological and economic effects were significant.

The retreat from Burma also affected Indian politics. For Congress's leaders and for Gandhi, it confirmed the government's inability to defend its subjects. For Indian soldiers, it was an experience of defeat and humiliation, though not, in most cases, of disloyalty. For the Japanese and for Bose, it offered an opening. These different effects help explain why the events of 1942 cannot be understood as a single event. They were separate interpretations of the same military disaster.

Section 84.14

Reorganizing the Eastern War: Allied Forces, Supply Networks, and the Burma Campaign

In 1943, the Allied command in the east was reorganized. At the Quebec conference in August 1943, the Allies agreed to create the South East Asia Command (SEAC) and selected Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten as Supreme Allied Commander, seeking to coordinate British, American, and Chinese efforts against Japan. The command's formal assumption of operational responsibility occurred at midnight on 15–16 November 1943; Mountbatten's headquarters subsequently moved from Delhi to Kandy in Ceylon in 1944. The forces on the Indo-Burma frontier were reorganized as the Fourteenth Army, commanded by Slim from October 1943. Slim's approach emphasized rigorous training in jungle warfare, improved medical discipline, notably against malaria, which had caused more casualties than combat in the earlier campaigns, better supplies, and the use of aircraft for the movement of troops and the delivery of supplies. The army's morale improved, and its tactics, which stressed standing fast against Japanese infiltration rather than retreating, became more effective.

The Fourteenth Army was among the most diverse armies of the war. Its largest component consisted of Indian divisions of the British Indian Army, supported by British, Gurkha, and African formations, including West African and East African divisions, and, in the north, Chinese forces under American command. The Indian Army, which expanded to more than two million men by 1945, the largest volunteer army in history, supplied the majority of Slim's infantry. The experience of these soldiers, who fought in Burma for pay, for professional pride, for regimental loyalty, for family security, or from commitment to the Allied cause, cannot be reduced to a single political motive. Local communities, including Nagas, Kachins, Chins, and others, also played a major role as scouts, porters, and guerrillas, and as the people on whose territory the war was fought; their contributions, and their suffering, are often under-recorded.

The logistics of the campaign were formidable. The theater was a region of mountains, rivers, and dense forest, with few roads and a heavy monsoon, and all supplies had to be moved over long and fragile lines of communication. The route from Calcutta to Assam depended on a single-track railway of varying gauge, river steamers on the Brahmaputra, and a limited road network, and the system was strained by the demands of the army, the American airfields that supplied China over the "Hump," the construction of the Ledo road, and the requirements of the civil economy. Military construction required land, labor, and materials, which were often obtained by requisition. Thousands of laborers were recruited for airfields, roads, and other works, and local grain and transport were taken for the army. The pressures on Assam, Bengal, and Ceylon, the latter as the site of SEAC's headquarters and the base of naval operations, rose steadily, and shipping was needed to carry troops and equipment across the Indian Ocean. These strategic demands are essential to understanding the economic context of the Bengal famine, though, as §84.18 argues, they did not cause it alone.

Section 84.15

The Battles of Imphal and Kohima, 1944: The Japanese–INA Offensive and Allied Resistance

In early 1944 the Japanese Fifteenth Army, under Lieutenant-General Mutaguchi Renya, launched an offensive code-named U-Go into Manipur and Assam. Its objectives were to destroy the British forces around Imphal, to capture the supply bases at Imphal and Dimapur, and to cut off the lines of communication that supported the American air route to China and the Allied position in northeast India. Some Japanese commanders, and Bose himself, hoped that the offensive would also open a route into India for the INA and trigger political upheaval. The operation began on 8 March 1944, with three divisions advancing across the Chindwin over jungle-covered mountains, expecting to capture British stockpiles of food and ammunition as they moved, as they had in earlier campaigns.

The Allied forces, aware of the offensive, had planned to let the Japanese come on, to defeat them at the end of their extended supply lines, and to rely on air supply to sustain the defenders. Slim's IV Corps held the Imphal plain, in a basin ringed by hills, while a smaller force held Kohima, on the ridge that controlled the road from Imphal to Dimapur and the Brahmaputra valley. The Japanese attack cut the Imphal road and surrounded the town, and at Kohima, from early April, a small garrison composed of British, Indian, and local troops was besieged on a hill-top position, where fighting at close quarters, across the garrison commander's tennis court and the District Commissioner's bungalow, continued for about two weeks before relief arrived. Further relief and reinforcement came from the airlift of divisions, including the Fifth Indian Division, from the Arakan to Imphal, and from a major flow of supply aircraft. Between April and June, Imphal was supplied largely by air.

The Japanese offensive failed principally for logistical reasons. Its commanders had relied on rapid victory and on captured supplies; when the British held, the Japanese troops, who had advanced with limited rations, found themselves short of food, ammunition, and medicine, and their supply lines were strained by distance and by air attack. The monsoon broke in May, turning tracks to mud and rivers to torrents and increasing malaria, dysentery, and other disease. One Japanese divisional commander withdrew from Kohima against orders because of lack of supplies, an act of insubordination that reflected the extent of the breakdown. The road from Kohima to Imphal was reopened on 22 June, and from July the Japanese forces retreated across the Chindwin in conditions of severe hardship. Casualty estimates differ, but Japanese losses were very heavy, on the order of tens of thousands, with a large proportion dying of starvation and disease during the retreat; Allied casualties in the two battles were far smaller, though still in the thousands. The defeat was the largest Japanese land defeat to that point in the war and changed the course of the Burma campaign.

The INA took part in the offensive, though in a subordinate role. Several thousand INA troops, organized in brigades and attached to Japanese formations, moved into the Chin Hills, Manipur, and Arakan. They were used as scouts, in propaganda, and in some operations; at Moirang in Manipur, INA personnel raised the INA flag on Indian soil in April 1944, an event that Bose celebrated as a symbolic achievement. The INA's men fought in difficult conditions and suffered heavily from hunger, disease, and the lack of supplies, and the movement did not produce the large-scale defections or the popular uprising that Bose had hoped for. In July 1944, in a radio address to Gandhi from Rangoon, Bose described him as the "Father of our Nation," asked for his blessing, and explained that the INA was fighting for India's freedom. The INA's retreat was part of the Japanese retreat.

Historians should be careful not to blur the INA with the British Indian Army. Imphal and Kohima were won mainly by Indian soldiers, Gurkhas, British troops, and local Naga communities, who fought on the Allied side. Indian divisions of the British Indian Army, including the Fifth, Seventeenth, Twentieth, and Twenty-third Indian Divisions and others, were central to the defense. The INA's soldiers, who had once been in the same army, were now on the other side. A small number of Indian soldiers deserted or defected to the INA in Burma, but the great majority of Indian troops stayed in service, and the failure of the INA to induce mass defections was a key reason for the failure of Bose's political calculation. The shared origins of the two forces would later complicate the way in which Indians remembered them.

Section 84.16

The Reconquest of Burma and the Military Consequences of the Eastern War, 1944–1945

After the victory at Imphal and Kohima, the Fourteenth Army pursued the retreating Japanese across the Chindwin in the second half of 1944. Slim then planned a campaign across the central Burmese plain. In February 1945 his forces crossed the Irrawaddy at several points, including Nyaungu, in operations that included deception and the large-scale use of river craft, and in early March, Indian divisions, including the Seventeenth, led by tanks and supported by air supply, captured the communications center of Meiktila, cutting the Japanese rear. Mandalay was taken on 20 March after fighting for Fort Dufferin and Mandalay Hill, and the Allied forces drove south toward Rangoon. The Japanese position was complicated by the defection, on 27 March, of the Burma National Army, led by Aung San, to the Allied side, a sign that the Burmese nationalists who had initially cooperated with Japan had concluded that its cause was failing.

The race for Rangoon was concluded by the combined airborne and amphibious Operation Dracula: Allied parachutists landed near Elephant Point on 1 May 1945, amphibious landings followed on 2 May, and troops entered Rangoon over 2–3 May after the main Japanese withdrawal. The distinct landing and occupation dates should not be compressed into one launch date. The Fourteenth Army arrived from the north a few days later. The campaign was a major Allied victory, involving Indian, British, Gurkha, and African formations, as well as air and naval support, and Japan's remaining forces in Burma were broken up or in retreat. It also marked the beginning of a rapid political transformation in Burma, which would become independent in 1948.

For the INA, the reconquest meant disintegration. Its units were pulled back with the Japanese, suffering casualties and desertions, and its commanders struggled to maintain discipline and supplies. In late April, Bose left Rangoon with a small party, and the INA's rear elements in the city were left to surrender to the advancing Allied forces. Lakshmi Swaminathan and other members of the Rani of Jhansi Regiment were captured in Burma, and many INA soldiers became prisoners of the British. The processes by which they were interrogated, classified, and, in some cases, tried belong to Chapter 85, and the larger political effects of the INA's collapse are discussed there. This chapter stops at the military result: the defeat of the INA's military project, alongside Japan's defeat.

The eastern war had broader consequences that reached into civilian life. It required food, shipping, roads, rail capacity, labor, and finance on a large scale, and it brought heavy military demand to the region of eastern India that included Bengal. Calcutta was a major base for supply and shipbuilding. Troops, laborers, and refugees moved into and through the region, and Allied forces relied on local purchase. The army's priorities were not necessarily the same as the civilian population's. It is important to be precise about what this meant. The military demand and the strategic importance of Bengal contributed to a climate of scarcity, inflation, and administrative prioritization that formed the background to the famine; they did not by themselves cause it. How these pressures interacted with the loss of Burmese rice, the harvest, the economy, and the decisions of governments is the subject of the next division.

Section 84.17

The Bengal Famine of 1943: Starvation, Disease, Displacement, and Social Breakdown

Bengal entered the war as one of the most densely populated and economically fragile regions of the subcontinent. Rice was the staple food, and a large proportion of the rural population depended on the cultivation of small holdings, on sharecropping, or on wage labor, with very little margin against shocks. Between the 1920s and the 1940s, the proportion of landless or nearly landless households had increased, indebtedness was high, and the jute economy, a major source of cash income, had been depressed by the world depression. The Floud Commission of 1938–40 had documented the extent of subdivision of holdings and the growth of a class of agricultural laborers and sharecroppers. These were the people on whom the famine fell most heavily. The vulnerability was structural: it did not cause the famine, but it determined who would suffer most when conditions deteriorated.

The crisis emerged gradually. After the poor aman rice harvest of late 1942, harvested in December and the main crop of the year, prices began to rise in early 1943, and they continued to rise through the spring and summer. From a level in 1942 of the order of ten to thirteen rupees per maund, prices rose in 1943 to thirty rupees or more in much of the province, and to several times that in some districts at the height of the crisis. Wages did not keep pace. In the rural districts, families sold what they owned, including land, cattle, tools, and utensils, in order to buy food, and many then had nothing left to sell. By the middle of the year, large numbers of people were leaving their villages in search of food, and the roads to Calcutta, Dhaka, and other towns were crowded with destitute migrants. Calcutta, which had received little relief provision, saw its streets fill with starving people, with corpses collected daily by municipal and voluntary agencies, and in late August 1943, The Statesman published photographs of the dying and the dead that, as discussed in §84.19, changed the political climate.

The mortality peaked in the last months of 1943. Famine deaths were caused partly by starvation but largely by disease, since people weakened by hunger were extremely vulnerable to malaria, cholera, dysentery, and smallpox, and the movement of populations, the breakdown of sanitation in crowded relief centers and urban areas, and the seasonal malaria peak in the autumn of 1943 all contributed. In the later months of the year and into 1944, a large proportion of the excess deaths were from epidemic disease. Relief facilities, including gruel kitchens set up by the government and by voluntary organizations, were overcrowded, and the conditions in some of them were themselves a source of infection. Families were separated: men went to look for work while women and children remained or traveled, and abandoned children appeared in large numbers in the cities. Contemporary press accounts and relief organizations also recorded the sale of children, the destitution of women, and cases of sexual exploitation, evidence that must be treated with care but that points to the social breakdown that accompanied the crisis.

Different groups experienced the famine differently. Landless agricultural laborers and sharecroppers, who purchased their food and had no stocks, were the largest category of victims. Fishing communities, whose boats had been removed or destroyed under the denial policy and whose catch was their main source of food and income, suffered severely in the coastal districts. Weavers and other artisans, whose incomes collapsed as the price of grain rose, were hard hit, as were petty traders and lower-paid service workers. Small cultivators with some land were more likely to survive, though many lost their holdings, and larger landholders and traders in some cases profited from rising prices. Women and children, and the elderly, faced particular vulnerability: they were less likely to migrate in search of work, were less able to compete for relief, and were more exposed to disease. The famine was also regionally uneven; the districts of eastern and southern Bengal, including Dacca, Mymensingh, Bakarganj, Noakhali, Chittagong, Midnapore, and Khulna, were severely affected, while other districts experienced less severe distress.

The scale of mortality is uncertain and has been estimated in different ways. The official registration of deaths in rural Bengal relied on village watchmen and was incomplete even in normal times, and the Famine Inquiry Commission itself acknowledged serious deficiencies in contemporary death registration. the Commission judged 1943 excess mortality at approximately one million deaths, above the incomplete official registration, and added the continued excessive mortality in 1944 to reach an overall estimate of approximately 1.5 million deaths attributable to the famine and ensuing epidemics. Later scholars have produced higher figures: Amartya Sen estimated around three million deaths from the famine, Tim Dyson and Arup Maharatna, using demographic methods, arrived at an estimate of excess mortality of around two million, and Cormac Ó Gráda has summarized the range as approximately two to three million. These estimates differ in method, in the period covered, in what is counted as famine-related, and in the baseline assumptions about normal mortality. Students should distinguish reported deaths, which are far lower and reflect the weakness of the registration system, from estimates of excess mortality, which attempt to estimate the number of deaths above what would have occurred under normal conditions, and should note that deaths continued at an elevated level into 1944.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Bengal Household's Struggle for Survival (Illustrative Reconstruction)

Note: this is a composite reconstruction, not an account of any one named family. It is based on patterns recorded in the Famine Inquiry Commission's report, relief society records, press reports, and later oral histories.

Imagine a household of a landless laborer and his family in a district of southern Bengal. In the early months of 1943, the family's daily wage buys a little less rice each week. They first reduce the number of meals, then sell the few brass utensils and the goat, and then the roof's tin sheets. Some neighbors with small holdings borrow from moneylenders and traders, pledging their land; the laborer, with no land to pledge, is not offered credit. By midsummer the laborer leaves to look for work in a distant town, and the household's remaining members, the mother, the children, and an elderly relative, depend on what they can gather: wild greens, roots, and whatever grain can be had from relatives.

As the year passes, the mother and children walk toward the nearest town, where a government or voluntary gruel kitchen has been opened. They join thousands of others. In the crowded conditions they fall ill, and one of the children dies of fever. The family may be separated in the crowd, and some members may be taken into a relief home or left on the pavement. Some do not survive the winter; others return to their village after the harvest of late 1943, having lost their land and their possessions, and must begin again as indebted laborers or as dependents on relatives. The experience of this composite household illustrates the way in which famine acted through market relationships, indebtedness, gender and age, and the loss of social support, rather than only through the absence of food.

Section 84.18

Food Availability, Market Entitlements, and the Economic Causes of Famine

Historians who study the famine begin with several factors affecting the supply of rice. The first was the loss of Burma. Imports from Burma had been small relative to India's total consumption but were important for the markets of Bengal, Madras, and the port cities, and their sudden end in 1942 removed a source of supply and increased the pressure on domestic supplies. The second was the harvest. The aman rice crop of 1942, the main crop, was reduced by a cyclone and storm surge in October 1942 that devastated parts of Midnapore and the 24 Parganas, and by a fungal disease, brown spot, which affected rice in parts of Bengal and which some historians, notably Mark Tauger, argue was a more serious cause of crop loss than the official reports recognized. The Famine Inquiry Commission treated the shortfall of the 1942 crop as a significant factor.

The third factor was inflation. The war economy in India, financed by the Government of India's purchases on Britain's behalf and by monetary expansion, had produced a general rise in prices, and prices of food rose faster than wages. The fourth was the behavior of markets. As scarcity and the threat of invasion spread, traders, landholders, and households hoarded grain, speculation increased, and the trade in rice became fragmented, with provinces imposing restrictions on movement. In the spring of 1943 the Government of India attempted a policy of freeing interprovincial trade in grain in the eastern region, a measure that, according to the Famine Inquiry Commission and later historians, contributed to a spiral of prices as traders competed to buy in Bengal. The fifth factor was the military and strategic setting, discussed in earlier sections, in which the army and strategic industries had priority in the allocation of transport and supplies, and the authorities took measures aimed at preventing resources from falling into Japanese hands.

Among these measures, the denial policies were particularly significant for coastal livelihoods. In response to the threat of Japanese invasion of Bengal, the government ordered the removal or destruction of boats in the coastal districts, in order to deprive an invader of transport, and the removal or destruction of surplus rice stocks in areas near the coast. Tens of thousands of boats were seized or destroyed, and since boats were the principal means of transport, fishing, and trade in the delta, the effect on the livelihoods and food supply of the communities that depended on them was severe, and compensation, where provided, was inadequate. The effects were uneven: they were most damaging in Khulna, Bakarganj, Midnapore, and other districts with water-based economies. The denial policy illustrates how a military decision with a strategic rationale could produce serious civilian costs.

Historical Vocabulary
Denial Policies

Denial policies were measures taken by the military and civil authorities in 1942 to deprive a possible Japanese invasion force of resources that it might use. In coastal Bengal, the "boat denial" policy required the removal or destruction of boats and the "rice denial" policy the removal or destruction of surplus stocks in specified areas.

The policies mattered because they reduced access to transport and food and damaged the livelihoods of fishing and boat-dependent communities. They should be distinguished from the broader wartime requisitions and from the later procurement of grain for cities and the army.

These factors complicate the picture of the supply of food. Amartya Sen argued, in his work on famines, that the total availability of rice in Bengal in 1943 was not markedly lower than in earlier years that did not produce famine, such as 1941. He proposed instead that the famine arose from a collapse of the "entitlements" of particular groups: that is, the ability of people to command food through production, trade, labor, or transfer. In 1943, as prices rose and wages and the value of assets fell relative to the price of food, rural laborers, fishermen, artisans, and others lost their ability to purchase enough food, even though grain existed in the market. The expansion of wartime spending and employment in Calcutta and near the military establishments increased the purchasing power of some groups, and the government's policy of procuring food for priority groups in Calcutta, including industrial workers, officials, and the military, meant that some of the limited supply was directed to the city and to the army, at the expense of the rural population. On this analysis, the question is not simply whether there was enough rice but who could obtain it.

Other historians have contested or modified this account. Some stress that there was a real shortfall in supply, whether from the loss of Burmese imports, the cyclone, and crop disease, and that Sen's estimates of availability depend on crop statistics of uncertain reliability. Tauger and others argue that the crop failure was more severe than the official data show. Others, such as Paul Greenough, emphasized the cultural and political dimensions of the way in which the poor were treated, and the way in which local elites and officials responded to scarcity. Madhusree Mukerjee and others have emphasized the role of imperial policy, including the War Cabinet's decisions on shipping and imports. Many scholars now argue that the explanations are not mutually exclusive: a modest shortfall, in conditions of inflation, panic, and a rigid priority system, was transformed into catastrophe by failures of distribution, policy, and administration.

Historical Vocabulary
Entitlement

In the analysis of famines developed by Amartya Sen, a person's entitlement is the set of commodities they can obtain through the legal means available to them, such as growing food, selling their labor or goods, or receiving transfers. A famine occurs, on this view, when a group's entitlements collapse, even if the total supply of food is not sharply reduced.

The concept matters because it shifts attention from the total quantity of food to questions of income, prices, employment, and social position. It should be distinguished from a "food availability decline" explanation, which attributes famine primarily to a fall in aggregate food supply. Historians have debated how well each approach fits the Bengal case.

Historical Debate

The Causes of the Bengal Famine and the Question of Responsibility

The question: Was the famine chiefly caused by a shortage of food, by the collapse of the ability of the poor to buy food, by wartime and imperial policies, or by some combination, and how should responsibility be assigned?

Interpretation A — Supply shortfall and its effects: The Famine Inquiry Commission and some later scholars, including Tauger, emphasize the shortfall in the 1942 harvest and the loss of Burma imports, and argue that the shortage, together with panic and hoarding, created the conditions for famine. They stress the real physical limits on supply and the constraints of a wartime economy.
Interpretation B — Entitlements, policy, and political structure: Sen, Greenough, Mukerjee, and others emphasize that food existed but that wartime inflation, speculation, priority systems, denial policies, and the decisions of governments denied access to the rural poor. On this view the famine was the result of political and economic arrangements that placed the burden of the war on those least able to bear it, and responsibility lies with the decisions of governments, including the War Cabinet in London.

Evidence: Both views draw on crop and trade statistics, price series, mortality data, the Commission's report, and the record of the discussions of the Government of India and the War Cabinet. They differ in the reliability they assign to crop statistics and in the weight they give to supply, distribution, and political decisions.

Why it matters: How the famine is explained affects judgments about responsibility, about the nature of colonial governance, and about what could have been done. It also shapes memory in India and Bangladesh, and in Britain. Scholars increasingly emphasize that causal factors interacted and that no single explanation, whether a single harvest failure or the decision of a single individual, is sufficient.

Section 84.19

Colonial Governance, Relief, Responsibility, and the Political Consequences of Famine

Responsibility for food policy was divided. In Bengal, the provincial government held primary responsibility for food, and its leaders changed during the crisis: the coalition ministry of Fazlul Huq, which included Hindu Mahasabha ministers, was succeeded in April 1943 by a Muslim League ministry under Khwaja Nazimuddin, in which H. S. Suhrawardy became the minister for civil supplies. The Governor retained wide powers and tensions between governor and ministers weakened provincial decision-making. Above the province, the Government of India's Food Department and the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, were responsible for coordination among provinces, for the allocation of grain, and for the policy of requests for imports. Above them stood the Secretary of State for India, Leo Amery, and the War Cabinet under Winston Churchill, who controlled shipping and the strategic allocation of resources among theaters of war.

The authorities were slow to recognize the severity of the crisis. Through the first half of 1943, the Bengal government and the Government of India treated rising prices as a problem of hoarding and of price control, and they were reluctant to declare a famine, because such a declaration under the Famine Code would impose obligations of relief and would signal a failure of administration. Procurement for Calcutta, intended to secure supplies for priority groups, diverted grain from the rural areas. Interprovincial coordination was poor, as some provinces with surpluses restricted exports of grain, and the efforts to impose controls on prices were largely ineffective. Relief in the first half of the year was limited, and it was only after the public outcry of August and the arrival of Wavell in October 1943 that relief efforts were substantially expanded.

Public attention was drawn by journalism and art. Ian Stephens, the editor of The Statesman, decided in August 1943 to publish photographs of the starving, a defiance of the pressure of the authorities to minimize the crisis, and the images and editorials had a considerable effect in India, Britain, and the United States. Other newspapers, including Indian-owned papers, also reported on the suffering, though the government's censorship limited coverage of some aspects of the war. Artists such as Zainul Abedin and Chittaprosad recorded the famine in drawings, and Chittaprosad's published sketches of the famine in Midnapore were banned by the government. Voluntary organizations, including the Marwari Relief Society, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Ramakrishna Mission, and the Communist-led People's Relief Committee, among others, provided gruel kitchens, hospitals, and distributions of food. Many Congress leaders were in prison and could not take part, and the Muslim League's relief work was also limited. Political parties, in short, divided over the famine in the same way that they were divided over the war.

Wavell, who took office as Viceroy on 20 October 1943, visited Calcutta and immediately concluded that military assistance was necessary. The army was ordered to assist in transporting grain and in setting up relief, and military trucks and personnel were deployed to distribute supplies to the districts. Wavell also pressed the War Cabinet in London to authorize imports of wheat and other grain, arguing that the situation required large shipments. The Cabinet's response was reluctant. The shipping needed to carry grain to India was in demand for the war effort, including preparations for the invasion of Europe, and ministers, including Churchill, expressed doubts about the severity of the crisis and about the Indian authorities' management of food. Wavell recorded his frustration with the Cabinet's attitude in his journal, and Amery, who was more sympathetic to the case for imports, argued within the government. A fraction of the amount that Wavell had requested was eventually provided from Australia and elsewhere, and relief grain began to arrive in early 1944. Historians continue to debate how far the shortage of shipping was a genuine constraint and how far it reflected the political priorities and attitudes of the leadership.

The Famine Inquiry Commission was appointed in 1944 under the chairmanship of Sir John Woodhead, with Indian and British members, to examine the causes of the famine and to make recommendations. Its report on Bengal, published in 1945, described the famine as an avoidable catastrophe and attributed it to a combination of causes, including the shortfall of the 1942 crop and loss of Burmese imports, the failure of the governments to act in time, mismanagement of the food trade, inflation, and the panic that followed, and it criticized both the Bengal government and the Government of India. It also made recommendations on food administration, rationing, and relief, and later proposals for a national food policy. The report's findings are examined in the source window below.

How should responsibility be assessed? Historians distinguish between structural causes, administrative failures, and political decisions. It is not enough to name an individual: the famine involved the interaction of a vulnerable agrarian society, wartime economic strain, the denial and priority policies, the performance of a provincial government that was politically unstable, the central administration's delays, and the imperial government's decisions on shipping and priorities. Some scholars hold the War Cabinet and Churchill primarily accountable for refusing imports; others point to the earlier failures of the provincial and central governments to maintain supplies and to respond to warning signs, and to the limits imposed by the global shipping crisis. Claims about the personal attitudes of individual leaders, including remarks attributed to Churchill, are contested and depend on the reliability of the surviving evidence, and should not be treated as a complete explanation. What can be said is that many of the decisions that contributed to the catastrophe were taken by identifiable authorities, that the consequences were foreseeable by the autumn of 1943, and that the response, though eventually substantial, came too late for many people.

Cause and Effect

The Bengal Famine of 1943

Long-term causes

Dense population and structural poverty; a large class of landless laborers and sharecroppers; indebtedness; dependence on rice and on imports in deficit years; weakness of the colonial state's welfare capacity.

Short-term causes

Loss of Burmese rice; poor 1942 aman harvest, cyclone, and crop disease; wartime inflation; speculation and hoarding; denial policies; the 1943 free-trade experiment; priority procurement for Calcutta and the military; delayed recognition and relief.

Event

Rising prices and collapse of access to food in 1943, with widespread starvation and epidemic disease, peaking in the last months of the year.

Immediate effects

A very high death toll, displacement, loss of land and assets, family breakdown, and the expansion of government and voluntary relief after the public outcry of August–October 1943.

Long-term consequences

Deep damage to confidence in colonial governance; a major official inquiry; later reforms in food administration; and a lasting place in the political memory of Bengal, India, and Bangladesh.

Historical complexity

No single cause is sufficient. Supply, entitlement, administrative, military, and political factors interacted, and historians differ in how they weigh them.

Review and Historical Synthesis: Rebellion, Armed Resistance, Famine, and the Crisis of the Raj

This chapter has followed three crises through the years between 1942 and 1945. Each had its own causes, actors, and geography, and they should not be merged into a single story. Their connection lay in the setting: a global war that placed unprecedented pressure on the colonial state, drew India into a front-line role, and exposed the limits of imperial governance. They were also connected in specific ways. The fall of Burma was the context for the failed defense of the eastern frontier, for the retreat of civilians, for the denial policies in Bengal, for the loss of rice imports, and for the opening that Japan and the INA sought to use. The war strained the transport system and food supply on which both the army and the civilian population depended. And the imprisonment of Congress's leaders removed from public life the figures who might have been able to organize relief or to take part in the debate over how to respond to the famine.

Quit India challenged British authority from within colonial India. It was a mass movement, not simply an elite campaign, and it showed that large numbers of ordinary people were willing to confront the state, in ways that the leadership neither fully controlled nor fully disavowed. It also showed the limits of that challenge. The government was able to arrest the leaders, to hold its administrative and military structure together, and to suppress the movement, and it did so with substantial force. The movement's strength was concentrated in particular regions, and several important political forces stood outside it. Its achievement was to reaffirm Congress's claim to represent the national movement and to make clear that the cost of ruling India without Indian consent would be high.

The INA challenged British authority through armed struggle alongside Japan. Its significance lay in its social composition, its claim of national unity, and the symbolism of Indians in arms for independence, while its military role was limited and its alliance with a brutal imperial power was deeply problematic. It did not produce the uprising that Bose expected, and the great majority of Indian soldiers remained in British service and won the battles of Imphal and Kohima. The INA's political impact would come after the war, when its story became public, a subject for Chapter 85.

The Bengal famine exposed a different weakness. It was not a challenge to British rule from outside but a failure within it: an administration that continued to function, to collect revenue, to allocate transport, and to maintain war production, while a very large number of its subjects died of hunger and disease. The failure was not the result of any single decision. It arose from a vulnerable agrarian economy, from wartime inflation and shortages, from denial and priority policies, from the delays and mistakes of provincial and central governments, and from the strategic priorities of an imperial government at war. For many Indians, the famine became a defining experience of colonial indifference, and for many historians it is the clearest illustration of the costs of governing a population without accountability to it.

These developments did not produce immediate independence, and the participants did not share a single program. Congress's leaders disagreed with Bose about alliance with the Axis, the Muslim League and the Communist Party took different positions on the war, and those who suffered from the famine were, for the most part, not engaged in any of these political projects. Britain emerged from the war victorious. Yet the political conditions had changed. The government's moral and political authority had been reduced, its reliance on the Indian Army and civil services had been exposed, its claim to protect its subjects had been damaged by the retreat from Burma and the famine, and the postwar world would be one in which Britain was financially exhausted and under pressure from the United States and from its own electorate. By 1945, the question was no longer whether power would be transferred but how, when, and to whom.

Chapter 85 takes up that question. It examines the trials of INA officers, which turned a wartime episode into a national political cause; the unrest in the Royal Indian Navy in 1946; the elections of 1945–46; the Cabinet Mission; the violence of Direct Action Day; the Interim Government; and the decision to partition the subcontinent. Students should carry from this chapter a caution that applies to all of these developments: the end of the Raj was not inevitable, and it was the outcome of the interaction of many forces, of which the three crises examined here were important parts.

British Authority, 1939–1945

  • Imperial priorities continued to determine the allocation of resources.
  • The colonial state's capacity for coercion remained greater than its capacity for relief.

Who experienced change most directly: the imprisoned leaders, the participants in the uprising, INA personnel, and the rural poor of Bengal. Who experienced greater continuity: the colonial officials and soldiers whose institutions held. Why the pattern matters: it shows that legitimacy can erode even while institutions continue to function.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined three connected but distinct crises of British authority in India during the Second World War: the Quit India uprising of 1942 and its repression, the armed independence movement of Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army, and the Bengal Famine of 1943. It has argued that these events occurred simultaneously, in the setting of a global war and the fall of Burma, but that they were not parts of one coordinated movement, and that each exposed a different weakness of imperial rule.

Quit India grew out of the failure of the Cripps Mission and the danger created by Japan's advance. Gandhi argued that British withdrawal was necessary for India's defense and freedom, and the Congress Working Committee at Wardha on 14 July 1942 and the All-India Congress Committee at Bombay on 8 August adopted a resolution demanding immediate independence while accepting that Allied forces might remain and expressing concern for China and the Soviet Union. It was not a declaration of support for Japan. Gandhi's call to "Do or Die" gave the movement its slogan.

The government arrested Gandhi and the Congress leadership on 9 August 1942 under emergency powers, declared Congress unlawful, and censored the press. With the leadership imprisoned, the movement took a diffuse and uneven form: peaceful protest, strikes, spontaneous crowd violence, organized sabotage, and local insurgency. Resistance was most intense in Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Midnapore, Satara, and parts of Bombay Presidency, and limited in Punjab, Sind, the North-West Frontier, and much of the south.

Underground leaders, including Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali, and Usha Mehta, maintained communications through clandestine radio and publications, and local movements established parallel governments at Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara that differed in character and duration. The government's repression involved mass arrests, firing on crowds, fines, and the use of troops and aircraft, with casualty figures that differ widely across sources. The Muslim League, the Communist Party, the Hindu Mahasabha leadership, and many princely rulers did not join the movement, for different reasons. Quit India failed in its immediate aims but affirmed the strength of popular nationalism and the dependence of British rule on Indian cooperation.

Subhas Chandra Bose left India in 1941, sought support from Germany, and in 1943 reached Singapore, where he took over a movement that had begun with the first INA of Mohan Singh and Rash Behari Bose's Indian Independence League. The first INA, created from prisoners of war after the fall of Singapore, collapsed in December 1942 over its relationship with Japan. Bose reorganized the force, proclaimed the Provisional Government of Free India on 21 October 1943, and raised the Rani of Jhansi Regiment under Lakshmi Swaminathan.

The INA was a force of national appeal, communal unity, and symbolic importance, but it was dependent on Japan, militarily modest, and tied to a brutal occupying power. Its soldiers made choices under conditions that ranged from conviction to coercion. It took part in the Japanese offensive of 1944 but did not trigger defections from the British Indian Army, and it disintegrated with Japan's defeat. Historians debate Bose's alliance with the Axis, and the chapter has presented both his anti-imperial strategy and its moral costs.

The eastern war shaped both the INA's ambitions and the conditions in Bengal. Japan's conquest of Burma in 1942 produced a disastrous retreat and mass flight of civilians and cut off rice imports. The Allies reorganized under South East Asia Command and the Fourteenth Army, and at Imphal and Kohima in 1944, largely through the efforts of Indian soldiers, Gurkhas, British troops, and local communities and with decisive air supply, they defeated the Japanese offensive. The reconquest of Burma followed in 1945.

The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated one and a half to three million people, mostly from starvation and epidemic disease, and fell hardest on landless laborers, fishing and artisan households, women, and children. Its causes included the loss of Burmese imports, a poor 1942 harvest, wartime inflation, speculation, denial policies, the free-trade experiment, priority procurement, and delayed relief, interacting with the vulnerability of Bengal's agrarian economy. Historians differ on how to weigh supply and entitlement explanations and on the apportionment of responsibility among the provincial government, the Government of India, and the War Cabinet.

Together these crises did not produce immediate independence, and their participants did not share a program. They did, however, reduce the moral and political authority of the colonial state and altered the conditions under which Britain would confront the question of India's future in 1945. Chapter 85 examines the INA trials, the naval uprising, the elections, the Cabinet Mission, and the negotiations that led to the end of British rule, and Chapter 86 the Partition that accompanied it.

A local authority created by nationalists to replace the colonial administration; examples include Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara.

The parallel government of Satara, led by Nana Patil, with its volunteer force and people's courts.

A penalty imposed on a whole village or community for actions attributed to some members.

The political organization of Indians in East and Southeast Asia, formed in 1942 and led successively by Rash Behari Bose and Subhas Chandra Bose.

The Indian National Army raised in 1942 by Mohan Singh from prisoners of war in Malaya, dissolved in December 1942.

The force of Indian prisoners and civilian volunteers, reorganized under Subhas Chandra Bose in 1943 and allied with Japan.

The Allied command created in 1943 under Mountbatten to coordinate operations against Japan in the region.

Wartime measures in 1942 to remove or destroy boats and rice stocks in coastal Bengal to deny them to a Japanese invader.

The main winter rice crop of Bengal, harvested in December; its 1942 shortfall contributed to the famine.

In Amartya Sen's analysis, the goods a person can command through production, trade, labor, or transfer; famine results from a collapse of entitlements.

Deaths above the number expected under normal conditions, the standard measure of a famine's toll.

Groups, such as industrial workers, officials, and the military, given preferential access to supplies in Calcutta during the famine.

The 1944–45 commission, chaired by Sir John Woodhead, whose Report on Bengal analyzed the causes of the famine.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a single global war could produce different kinds of political and human crisis in the same territory at the same time. A student who understands Quit India, the INA, and the Bengal famine as three separate stories, each with its own causes and its own evidence, is better prepared to understand the end of British rule as the product of many pressures, not of a single decisive event.

The chapter also reveals the different ways in which colonial authority could be challenged. Quit India showed popular defiance and the limits of repression; the INA showed that independence could be pursued by arms and by alliances that were morally fraught; the famine showed that legitimacy can be damaged even where no one is in open revolt, when a government fails in its most basic duties. The effects were cumulative, and they shaped the political memory of Bengal, of the Congress, of the Indian Army, and of the postwar generation.

Several continuities extended beyond 1945. The memory of Quit India became part of the founding narrative of independent India, with regional variations, and the INA's story, as Chapter 85 shows, became a national cause. The famine left lasting marks on debates about colonial rule, food security, and the responsibility of governments, with direct influence on Bengal's postwar politics and its place in the later history of both India and Bangladesh, a subject taken up in Chapters 86 and 111. And the eastern war, by bringing Burma, Assam, Bengal, and the Bay of Bengal into one theater, connected regions that are often studied apart.

Within the larger sequence of this series, these crises mark the point at which the question of independence moved from whether to how. The chapters that follow examine how, in the years after 1945, Britain, Congress, the Muslim League, and many others negotiated, fought over, and ultimately divided the inheritance of the Raj.
