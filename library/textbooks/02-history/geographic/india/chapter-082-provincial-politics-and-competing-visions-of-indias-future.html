Provincial Politics and Competing Visions of India’s Future

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 1 April 1937 the provincial part of the Government of India Act of 1935 came into force. In eleven provinces, ministers answerable to elected legislatures replaced the system of divided responsibility that had operated since 1921. The Indian National Congress, which had denounced the Act as a constitution designed to perpetuate imperial control, had nonetheless fought the elections held that winter and had won them in a way few observers had predicted. The organization that had organized boycotts, picketing, and mass jail-going now had to decide whether to administer the very system it had rejected.

Chapter 80 examined movements of peasants, workers, non-Brahmins, Dalits, and women that grew up alongside the nationalist movement and often challenged its priorities. Chapter 81 traced the constitutional crisis of the early 1930s: the Civil Disobedience Movement, the Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award, the Poona Pact, and the long legislative process that produced the Government of India Act of 1935. This chapter turns from the making of that constitution to its working. Its subject is the brief but decisive period, centered on 1937 to 1939, when the politics of independence moved from the street and the prison to the legislature, the secretariat, and the ministerial office.

The central question can be stated simply. What happened when the organizations demanding independence had to show how they would govern, and discovered that they did not agree on what an independent India should become? Before 1937 it was possible to speak of a single nationalist cause, even though the movement had always contained divergent interests. Provincial office made the divergences concrete. A ministry had to decide whether to scale down rural debts, whether to cut the salaries of its own members, whether to release detainees whom the governor regarded as dangerous, whether to teach children in Hindi, Urdu, or a vernacular, and whether to share power with parties that did not accept its discipline.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first explains the constitutional framework, the franchise, and the elections of 1937. The second examines Congress as an administrator rather than only as a movement. The third turns to two rival political nationalisms, that of the reorganized Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah and that of the Hindu Mahasabha under V. D. Savarkar, and treats the controversies between Congress and the League over representation and the conduct of the ministries. The fourth shows that the political landscape was far wider than a Congress–League contest, by examining socialists, communists, Ambedkar’s Independent Labour Party, the crisis of Congress leadership around Subhas Chandra Bose, and the movements in the princely states. The fifth explores regional parties and coalitions in Punjab, Bengal, Sindh, the Frontier, Assam, and the South, compares the competing constitutional visions, and closes at the threshold of the Second World War.

Several cautions apply throughout. The 1937 elections were held on a restricted franchise, and neither Congress’s victory nor the League’s defeat can be read as an expression of the will of all Indians, still less of all South Asians. Provincial results varied enormously, and an all-India narrative can easily hide them. Moreover, because Partition occurred a decade later, it is tempting to read the politics of the late 1930s as a prologue to it. This chapter resists that temptation. The political actors of 1937 did not know that the subcontinent would be divided in 1947, and many contemporaries imagined quite different futures, including a loose federation, a strong centralized republic, a socialist state, a Hindu nation, and various arrangements with Muslim-majority provinces. Historians use election returns, party resolutions, legislative proceedings, governors’ reports to the Viceroy, newspapers, memoirs, and the partisan inquiry reports of the time. Each type of evidence reflects the viewpoint of those who produced it, and the chapter tries to say whose perspective each represents.

The connection to the rest of the series is direct. Chapter 83 will examine how the outbreak of war in 1939 ended the ministries and transformed the position of the League, and Chapter 84 will treat Quit India, Bose’s wartime politics, and the Bengal Famine. Chapters 85 and 86 follow the negotiations that ended British rule and the partition of the subcontinent. The late 1930s are therefore a hinge. Provincial government did not resolve the question of what independent India would be. It exposed that question, and it did so before the war removed any possibility of calm negotiation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how provincial autonomy under the Government of India Act of 1935 worked in practice, including the powers retained by governors and the Centre, and why the federal provisions were never implemented.
  2. Describe the franchise, separate electorates, and reserved constituencies of 1937 and assess why the elections were significant without being democratic in a modern universal sense.
  3. Analyze the results of the 1937 provincial elections province by province and explain the differing performance of Congress, the Muslim League, and regional parties.
  4. Trace the debate within Congress over office acceptance and the formation of ministries between February and July 1937.
  5. Evaluate the legislative and administrative record of the Congress ministries in education, agriculture, civil liberties, labor, and social policy, distinguishing promise, law, implementation, and outcome.
  6. Assess how peasants, workers, Dalits, and women experienced Congress rule and why mass-movement supporters sometimes became critics of Congress ministers.
  7. Explain why the Muslim League performed poorly in 1937 in Muslim-majority provinces and how Jinnah sought to reorganize the party.
  8. Evaluate competing claims about Congress–League relations, including contemporary allegations against the ministries, treating partisan reports as evidence requiring critical assessment.
  9. Compare the conceptions of national belonging advanced by Congress, the Muslim League, and the Hindu Mahasabha under Savarkar.
  10. Distinguish the political programs of the Congress Socialists, communists, B. R. Ambedkar’s Independent Labour Party, and Subhas Chandra Bose.
  11. Explain how politics in princely states differed from politics in British Indian provinces and why federation raised distinct questions for them.
  12. Compare the competing visions of federation, sovereignty, democracy, and nationhood in 1939 and explain why the late 1930s did not produce constitutional consensus.

Historical Context

By the mid-1930s the Indian National Congress, founded in 1885 and reshaped by Gandhi after 1919 (Chapter 79), was the largest nationalist organization in British India. It had mobilized peasants, merchants, students, and professionals across many regions, but its claim to speak for the entire population was disputed. Chapter 77 traced the formation of the Muslim League in 1906 and the extension of separate electorates in 1909, while Chapter 78 followed the Lucknow Pact of 1916, an agreement between Congress and the League that attempted, and for some years achieved, cooperation. The Round Table Conferences and the Communal Award of 1932 (Chapter 81) reopened the question of how communities should be represented, and the Poona Pact that followed adjusted the representation of the Scheduled Castes inside the general electorate.

The Government of India Act of 1935 was the product of that process. It was enacted by the British Parliament after consultation with some Indian delegates but without the agreement of Congress, which regarded it as imposed. It envisaged two stages, a grant of provincial autonomy and an all-India federation joining the provinces with those princely states willing to accede. Only the first stage was completed. The second never took effect, since the necessary number of princely states did not accede, and the Centre continued to operate under the older arrangements. The provincial stage, however, created governments with wide authority over education, agriculture, public health, local government, and law and order, subject to powers reserved to the governors.

The environment in which provincial government began was shaped by the economic depression of the early 1930s. Agricultural prices fell sharply after 1929, while rents, revenue demands, and debts did not fall to the same degree. Rural indebtedness was a pressing concern in Punjab, Bengal, the United Provinces, Madras, and Bombay. This is part of the reason why both Congress and its rivals campaigned in 1937 on promises of debt relief and tenancy reform, and why the character of rural society, including landlords, cultivating tenants, moneylenders, and agricultural laborers, matters as much to the story as the speeches of all-India leaders.

British India in 1937 consisted of eleven provinces under governors: Madras, Bombay, Bengal, the United Provinces, Punjab, Bihar, the Central Provinces and Berar, Assam, the North-West Frontier Province, Orissa, and Sindh. Orissa and Sindh had been created as separate provinces in 1936, and Burma was separated in 1937. Alongside these lay several hundred princely states of widely varying size, governed by hereditary rulers under British paramountcy (Chapter 71). They covered a very large area of the subcontinent and contained a substantial minority of its population, but their subjects voted in no provincial election. Any history of politics in the late 1930s must keep both territories in view, since the Act’s federal scheme depended on the princes, and since movements within the states increasingly sought a role in all-India politics.

Students should also note that the evidence for this period is unusually abundant but heavily partisan. Governors’ fortnightly reports to the Viceroy, the Viceroy’s correspondence with the Secretary of State, Congress and League resolutions, party newspapers, legislative debates, and the memoirs of participants all survive in quantity. Each of them reflects a position. Official reports reflect the anxieties of the colonial executive; party reports reflect a case being made. The strongest analysis comes from comparing them.

Section 82.1

Provincial Autonomy and the Government of India Act of 1935

Chapter 81 explained how the Act of 1935 was drafted. This section asks a different question: how did it operate? The Act was a very long statute, and its central provincial feature was the abolition of dyarchy, the arrangement introduced in 1919 by which some provincial subjects had been transferred to ministers answerable to legislatures while others, including finance and law and order, remained under the governor’s executive council. After 1937 all provincial subjects were in principle administered by ministers who held office so long as they commanded a majority in the legislative assembly. The governor appointed as chief minister, called Premier in the new usage, the leader best able to command that majority, and ministers were chosen on his advice.

The Act nevertheless retained significant discretionary powers in the hands of governors, who were British officials or politicians appointed by the Crown. The governor was directed to exercise “special responsibilities” for matters such as the prevention of grave menace to the peace of the province, the safeguarding of the legitimate interests of minorities, the rights of public servants, and the protection of the rights of princely states. In such matters he could act in his individual judgment, independent of ministerial advice, though in practice governors differed in how readily they invoked the power. The Act also allowed the governor, with the Governor-General’s authorization, to suspend the provincial constitution and assume direct control if a constitutional breakdown occurred; this is commonly referred to as Section 93 rule. It was invoked after the Congress ministries’ resignation in 1939, a development belonging to Chapter 83.

Provincial autonomy was also limited by structure. The Centre retained control of defense, foreign affairs, currency, railways, and many other subjects. The Governor-General remained powerful, and the Imperial Services, the Indian Civil Service and the Indian Police, continued to staff the secretariats and districts, owing their appointment and protection to the Secretary of State. A minister who wished to reorganize the police or modify the revenue system had to work through an officialdom that had been recruited under the old system and that retained significant statutory safeguards. The Act did, however, give ministers authority over departments that touched daily life: agriculture, education, local self-government, public health, cooperatives, and the great mass of provincial legislation on land and tenancy.

The proposed federation was a still more contested matter. The Act envisaged an all-India Federation in which the provinces and those princely states that signed an Instrument of Accession would send representatives to a federal legislature, with the Governor-General retaining special powers and certain central subjects reserved. The federation was to take effect only when states representing a prescribed proportion of the states’ seats and population had acceded. That proportion was never reached. Many princes hesitated over the terms and over the demands of the Viceroy’s office, and political parties in British India were unenthusiastic. Congress opposed the federal scheme because it gave the princes disproportionate weight and nominated representatives rather than elected ones; the League was cautious because it feared that a strong Centre would favor the Hindu majority; and the princes feared a loss of autonomy. The result was an unusual constitution: provincial autonomy was real and operating, but the Centre continued, until the end of British rule, under a modified version of the older arrangements. In effect, provincial government became the principal site of Indian participation in constitutional power, which made the provincial elections far more important than their authors had intended.

The statutory division between reserved and transferred authority mattered especially when a provincial policy touched more than one department. A minister responsible for schools could authorize a syllabus or a new school where funds and staff permitted; the same minister could not compel the central government to alter import policy, currency arrangements, or military expenditure. A proposal to alter agricultural rents might require legislation and the cooperation of district revenue officials, while a governor could ask whether the interests of an affected minority or public service required protection. These practical negotiations made provincial autonomy a continuing constitutional experiment rather than a single transfer of power completed in April 1937.

Primary Source Window 1

The Operative Provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935

Source type: Statute of the British Parliament · Date: Royal assent August 1935; provincial provisions in force 1 April 1937 · Status of this window: documentary paraphrase of the statute’s provisions, not a verbatim quotation

Context: The Act was drafted by the British government after the Round Table Conferences and a Joint Select Committee of Parliament. It was addressed to British and Indian political opinion at once, and its provisions reflect compromises among British parties, the Indian princes, and some Indian delegates.

Paraphrase of operative provisions: Provincial executive authority is vested in the governor and exercised through ministers responsible to the legislature, except where the Act directs the governor to act in his discretion or in his individual judgment. The governor holds special responsibilities for the peace and tranquillity of the province, the safeguarding of minorities, the rights of services, and other named matters, and may issue instructions to ministers in connection with them. If the government of a province cannot be carried on in accordance with the Act, the governor may, with the Governor-General’s concurrence, assume the powers of any provincial body. The federal provisions become operative only on a declaration by the Crown after a specified proportion of the states have acceded.

Analysis: The text shows that responsibility and control were deliberately blended. Ministers gained wide powers, but the governor’s reserve powers gave the colonial state a safety valve. The federal provisions show how the British intended to tie the princes to the constitutional structure. The statute is silent on the practical question that mattered most in 1937, namely how often governors would actually use their powers.

Historical Vocabulary
Provincial Autonomy

Provincial autonomy refers to the arrangement under the Act of 1935 by which provincial governments were responsible to elected legislatures across the whole range of provincial subjects, and in which the Centre’s ordinary supervision over provinces was reduced. It did not mean sovereignty or independence. Governors retained special responsibilities, the Governor-General retained powers over the provinces in many matters, and the Imperial Services remained in place.

The phrase matters because it explains why Congress’s decision to accept office in 1937 was not a decision to accept the Act as a final settlement. Congress ministers treated provincial autonomy as a platform from which to pursue reform and as a means of strengthening the national movement, while their opponents argued that Congress was now compromised by participation in colonial government.

Section 82.2

The Electorate, Reserved Representation, and the Rules of Provincial Elections

The 1937 elections are sometimes described as the largest exercise in representative politics India had yet seen, and by the standards of the colonial period that is true. The Act greatly extended the franchise, so that the number of eligible voters rose to somewhere in the region of thirty million in the provinces. Yet the elections cannot be equated with universal democratic participation. The qualification for the vote was based on a combination of property, taxation, educational attainment, and in some cases military service or office-holding, so that only a minority of adults could vote. A pre-enactment government estimate projected about 35 million provincial electors, or approximately 14 percent of the total population and 27 percent of adults; actual enrollments and later historical summaries differ. Percentages must therefore be read with their date and population denominator, and eligibility varied by province and by community.

The qualifications also interacted with gender. Women could vote if they met property or educational qualifications in their own right or, in some categories, as the wives or widows of men who qualified. The Act reserved a number of seats for women in the provincial assemblies, filled through special constituencies, and it permitted women to contest general seats. These provisions expanded women’s formal political presence, but the gap between male and female electors remained large, and the reserved seats were often contested by women from elite or educated families. The women’s organizations that had pressed for the vote, discussed in Chapters 74 and 80, were divided over whether reserved seats were a victory or a limitation, and many wanted a wider franchise and equal citizenship without special provisions.

The more fundamental structure was the allocation of seats by community. Under the arrangements developed from the Communal Award and modified by the Poona Pact, seats in the provincial assemblies were divided among general constituencies, Muslim constituencies, Sikh constituencies, and constituencies for Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans. Special constituencies existed for landholders, commerce and industry, labor, and universities. Muslims voted in separate electorates for reserved Muslim seats, which together numbered roughly 480 of the roughly 1,585 seats in the provincial assemblies. In Punjab and Bengal, where Muslims were a numerical majority, the Communal Award nonetheless allocated them a share of seats that was short of their share of the population, a point that provoked sharp dispute. In provinces where Muslims were a minority, such as the United Provinces, Madras, Bombay, and Bihar, Muslims received a larger share of seats than their numbers would have yielded, a device known as weightage.

The Poona Pact (Chapter 81) governed the Scheduled Castes. About 150 seats within the general constituencies were reserved for them, to be filled by a system in which Scheduled Caste voters first chose a panel of candidates in a primary election, after which the final contest took place in the general electorate. Many Dalit leaders, including B. R. Ambedkar, argued that the final election gave caste Hindus influence over who the Scheduled Caste representative would be, and that Congress candidates were therefore advantaged. The result was an arrangement in which Dalit voters were formally represented but in which the autonomy of Dalit politics was sharply contested, as Section 82.14 shows.

These rules shaped the contest in ways that students must keep in mind when reading election returns. Congress contested a large proportion of general seats, but only a small number of Muslim seats, and its claim to represent the nation therefore rested largely on the general electorate. The League contested Muslim seats and could claim at best to speak for those who voted in them, yet in the Muslim-majority provinces provincial parties with local roots often defeated it. Seats reserved for special interests, such as landholders, gave the landed elite a presence out of proportion to their numbers. A result that gave Congress a majority of seats did not therefore mean that Congress had the support of a majority of the population, nor did it mean that Congress represented every community equally.

Finally, the elections took place under the authority of the British administration, which regulated polling, ruled on qualification disputes, and intervened where it believed law and order or fairness were at risk. Candidates relied on party machines, local notables, caste and kin networks, and landholders’ influence. Contemporary observers reported cases of pressure, bribery, and rumor, and they also recorded impressive displays of organization, especially by Congress in rural constituencies where volunteers used bullock carts, loudspeakers, and symbols such as colored ballot boxes for illiterate voters. The mixture of restriction, communal allocation, and vigorous campaigning is the setting for the results explained in the next section.

Even among registered electors, voting did not translate mechanically into an all-India popular mandate. Constituencies differed in size, some members came from functional or communal electorates, and many votes were cast in contests where a locally influential candidate mattered more than the national party label. A seat total was therefore a measure of success under an unusual electoral design, not a plebiscite on independence or a census of communal opinion. In evaluating the League's poor performance in Punjab, for instance, one must ask whether Muslim electors preferred the policies and social relationships of local Unionist candidates, rather than concluding that those electors had rejected every form of Muslim political representation.

Historical Vocabulary
Separate Electorates and Reserved Seats

A separate electorate is a system in which voters of a defined community vote only for candidates standing for seats allotted to that community. A reserved seat in a general electorate, as used for the Scheduled Castes after the Poona Pact, is a seat set aside for candidates of a given group but contested within a wider electorate. The two systems differ fundamentally: the first confines the contest to members of the community, the second involves all voters in the constituency.

The distinction matters because Muslim politics, Sikh politics, and Dalit politics each operated under a different set of electoral rules, and parties adopted strategies suited to those rules. Treating separate electorates and reserved seats as equivalent would obscure the different political incentives they created.

Section 82.3

The Provincial Elections of 1937: Campaigns, Results, and Political Geography

The elections were held in stages between December 1936 and February 1937. Congress entered them after considerable internal debate. Its Lucknow session of April 1936, held under Nehru’s presidency, rejected the 1935 Act as a constitution forced on India, and the Faizpur session of December 1936 reaffirmed rejection of the federal scheme while deciding to contest the elections in order to demonstrate the nation’s rejection of the Act and to press its demand for a Constituent Assembly elected on an adult franchise. At Faizpur, the first Congress session held in a village, the party also adopted a program for the rural population, including demands for reductions in rent and revenue, relief from debt, and security for tenants. The election manifesto drew on this program and on Congress’s long-standing political demands.

Congress ran an organized campaign. Nehru toured widely and addressed large crowds, while provincial leaders, volunteers, and local committees prepared lists, secured candidates, and managed polling. The campaign benefited from the memory of Civil Disobedience, which many voters associated with the party, and from its network of workers developed over years of agitation. Congress also tried to extend its reach among Muslims through what Nehru called the mass contact campaign, a drive to recruit Muslim members and enlist Muslim voters, though its practical effect was limited.

The arithmetic becomes more revealing when its geography is examined. Congress's 159 seats in the Madras assembly yielded a decisive majority, whereas 85 of Bombay's 175 seats did not produce an outright majority on the same terms. The party's 54 in Bengal were fewer than one quarter of the available seats, although they made Congress an important single organization in a fragmented assembly. In Punjab Congress's 18 represented a minor parliamentary force. The same national party could therefore enter government as a commanding majority, a prospective coalition anchor, or an opposition minority. The constitutional consequences depended on the province rather than on a single all-India percentage.

A further distinction separates the winning of Muslim-reserved seats from subsequent claims to represent Muslim society. In the published classification used here, the League took 40 seats in Bengal, 26 in the United Provinces, 18 in Bombay, and only one in Punjab. Those numbers help explain why Jinnah later sought connections with provincial leaders who already commanded local organizations. They do not license an assumption that every non-League Muslim winner belonged to a unified anti-League bloc: several stood as independents, belonged to agrarian parties, or entered later understandings with the League. A serious election history must follow the political life of the assemblies after polling ended.

Everyday Life and Social History

Campaigning and Polling in a Restricted Electorate

For most inhabitants of British India, the 1937 elections were something that happened to others. Most villagers could not vote at all, since they did not meet the property or education qualifications. Among those who could, voting involved traveling to a polling place, often a school or government building, and showing identity. Because many voters were illiterate, candidates were assigned symbols or colored boxes, and Congress volunteers walked voters through the procedure.

Women who qualified faced a further set of constraints. Where norms of seclusion applied, separate arrangements were made, and the presence of women at the polls was a notable feature of reports, though it affected a small fraction of the population. The campaign was also an occasion for local rivalries: landlord against tenant, creditor against debtor, one caste group against another. Understanding the 1937 contest requires attention to these local struggles, which are only partly visible in all-India summaries.

Section 82.4

From Electoral Victory to Government: Coalition Negotiations and Provincial Power

Election results did not directly produce governments. Congress’s victory raised a difficult question: should it accept the offices that the Act made available? The debate was serious because the stakes included the party’s identity. Many Congress members, especially on the left, feared that taking office under governors with reserve powers would absorb the movement into the colonial system and blunt its radicalism. Others argued that office would allow Congress to protect its supporters, carry out reforms, and strengthen its organization. Nehru was skeptical of office acceptance in principle, while Gandhi, Patel, and Rajagopalachari were more inclined to accept if suitable assurances could be obtained.

In March 1937 the All India Congress Committee, meeting at Delhi, resolved that Congress would accept office only if the governors gave assurances that they would not use their special powers to interfere with the ministers’ constitutional activities. Governors, and the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, declined to give a formal pledge in those terms. The result was a stalemate of several months, during which Congress members refused to form governments in the provinces where they had majorities, and the governors installed interim ministries drawn from minority parties. In Bihar, Bombay, the United Provinces, Orissa, and other provinces, these caretaker governments depended on the governor’s support and lacked a legislative majority.

The office-acceptance dispute also put the meaning of a party mandate under scrutiny. Congress's March 1937 conditions asked that ministers be protected from governors' arbitrary interventions; Linlithgow's conciliatory declarations did not legally repeal the reserve powers. The Working Committee's decision of 7 July nevertheless authorized entry into office because leaders judged that practical interference might be more limited than feared. In his July public explanation, Nehru insisted that the strength needed for any future struggle remained outside the legislatures. Office was presented as an instrument of a continuing national campaign, not as a constitutional settlement with the Raj.

After the Viceroy’s statement and a further discussion in the Working Committee at Wardha, Congress decided in July 1937 to take office. It is important to distinguish election results from the later government formation. The sequence in each province involved negotiations among leaders, interventions by governors, and in some cases changes of government during the legislative term. In Bengal, for example, Congress was the largest party but could not command a majority, and the question of who would govern depended on coalition arithmetic and on political choices that were contested. In the Frontier, an interim ministry gave way to a Congress ministry only after parliamentary maneuvering. In Assam a ministry led by Syed Muhammad Saadulla held office with changing support until Congress took over in 1938. Students should therefore avoid describing 1937 as the moment when Congress “came to power” everywhere. The decision to accept office carried long-term consequences for relations between Congress and the Muslim League, discussed in Sections 82.9 to 82.11. It also had implications for relations between Congress’s leaders and its radical supporters, discussed in Section 82.13. At this stage the key point is that Congress, having decided to govern, committed itself to a long series of choices about how to govern, and these choices created the controversies that structured Indian politics until the war.

Section 82.5

Congress Ministries: Leadership, Administration, and the Responsibilities of Office

The Congress ministries were led by a generation of provincial politicians who had risen through the movement of the 1920s and 1930s. C. Rajagopalachari became Premier of Madras, Govind Ballabh Pant of the United Provinces, B. G. Kher of Bombay, Shri Krishna Sinha of Bihar, and Bishwanath Das of Orissa. In the Central Provinces the early leadership of N. B. Khare was replaced in 1938 by Ravishankar Shukla, and in the Frontier Dr Khan Sahib headed the cabinet. Most of these men had spent years in jail, and many had organized civil disobedience. Their transition from prisoner to minister was noted by observers on all sides, and it generated a sense both of vindication and of difficulty.

The ministries governed through the machinery of the existing administrative state. District collectors, police superintendents, and secretariat officials continued to carry out orders, and ministers had to learn to work with them. Some Congress ministers reported that officials adapted with surprising ease; others complained of reluctance or obstruction. The relationship was complicated by the fact that many officials had enforced the repression of 1930–34 against Congress workers, and some of Congress’s own supporters expected a reckoning. Ministers generally avoided wholesale dismissals, preferring to use the services while seeking to alter their orientation, and they were criticized for it from both directions: by those who wanted a sharper break, and by officials and Europeans who feared Congress dominance.

Congress also set out to regulate the conduct of its own ministers. The party’s Parliamentary Sub-Committee, composed of Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, and Maulana Azad, supervised the ministries on behalf of the Working Committee, and ministers were expected to consult the party’s leadership on major policy. The system, often called the High Command, was intended to maintain discipline and to ensure that office-holding did not distract from the larger purpose of the movement. It also drew criticism. Opponents alleged that provincial governments were being directed by a body that was not accountable to the legislature, and some Congress legislators resented the reach of the Working Committee. Congress ministers took a deliberate decision to limit their salaries to a modest monthly sum, far below the earlier ministerial scale, as a symbol of austerity and of difference from colonial officials.

The fundamental contradiction was obvious. Congress was a movement committed to ending British rule, yet its ministers swore allegiance to the Crown, operated under the governor’s authority, and administered a police force that was sometimes used against Congress’s own supporters. Gandhi regarded office as a means to educate the people in self-government and to carry out constructive work; Nehru saw it as a tactical necessity, to be used without illusions; the left saw it as a risk. The experience would test each view. At the least, it demonstrated that Congress could administer large populations with a reasonable degree of competence, a fact that colonial commentators, who had long argued that Indians were unready for self-government, were forced to acknowledge, if sometimes grudgingly.

A revealing episode occurred in the Central Provinces in 1938. Khare, the Premier, came into conflict with the party’s high command after he reshuffled his cabinet in ways that the Working Committee believed violated party discipline. The dispute became a public controversy over the relationship between a ministry responsible to a legislature and a party leadership outside it. The outcome, in which Khare was removed and replaced by Shukla, strengthened the principle of party control, but it also showed how Congress’s internal politics could produce governmental crises and how provincial and regional rivalries within the party interacted with the high command’s authority.

That dependence on the party organization created a distinctive chain of accountability. A provincial premier needed a workable majority in an assembly elected under the Act, but Congress expected him to respect the wider national leadership and its program. An assembly member could call for a ministry to address a constituency grievance, a local Congress committee might insist on another priority, and the Working Committee could object to the behavior of a provincial cabinet. When those demands conflicted, neither the provincial constitution nor the organizational rules supplied a universally accepted answer. The relationship became especially contentious when cabinet appointments, police conduct, or the handling of dissident Congress members entered the dispute.

Section 82.6

Provincial Reform: Education, Agriculture, Civil Liberties, and Social Policy

What did the Congress ministries actually accomplish? The question requires a careful distinction among four things: what the ministries promised, what they legislated, what they implemented, and what resulted. The record is mixed and varied by province, and this section organizes it by field.

Civil liberties and political prisoners. One of the first demands of the manifesto was the release of political prisoners and the lifting of bans on organizations and publications. Ministers moved quickly to release many individuals imprisoned under the repressive laws of the early 1930s, to lift restrictions on the press, and to withdraw some of the prohibitions on organizations. But the question of detainees and prisoners held for violent offenses was more difficult, since the governors’ special responsibility for the peace of the province gave them leverage. In early 1938 the ministries of the United Provinces and Bihar resigned over the governors’ refusal to approve the release of certain prisoners. After negotiations in which Gandhi played a role and after the governors’ positions were softened, the ministries returned. The episode suggested that the governors’ powers could be tested and that colonial authorities, wishing to avoid a breakdown, would give some ground.

Education. The ministries expanded the provision of primary education and sought to reshape its content. In 1937 Gandhi proposed a scheme of basic education centered on handicraft, instruction in the mother tongue, and the idea that schooling should partly pay for itself through the work of pupils. A committee under Zakir Husain developed a syllabus, and the scheme was adopted in several provinces, though its implementation was constrained by financial limits and criticized by those who feared it would lower academic standards. In some provinces new schemes, such as the Vidya Mandir scheme in the Central Provinces, became sources of controversy, as discussed in Section 82.11. The language question was also contentious. In Madras, Rajagopalachari’s ministry in 1938 made the study of Hindi compulsory in a number of secondary schools, which prompted a sustained agitation described in Section 82.18.

Prohibition. Congress had long advocated the restriction of alcohol, regarding drinking as a drain on poor families. Madras introduced prohibition in Salem district in 1937 and extended it, and Bombay and other provinces adopted restrictions. Prohibition reduced excise revenue, which was a major source of provincial income, and thus constrained spending on other programs. It also created enforcement difficulties and, according to critics, encouraged illicit distillation.

Agriculture, debt, and tenancy. Rural legislation was the area in which expectations were highest. Ministries passed laws to reduce or scale down rural debts, regulate moneylending, and extend protections for tenants. Madras passed an Agriculturists’ Relief Act in 1938 to scale down debts; Bihar amended its tenancy laws in the direction of rent reduction and greater security; the United Provinces passed a major tenancy measure that extended hereditary rights to many tenants; Bombay legislated on tenancy and on certain forms of tenure. These measures gave real relief to some categories of cultivators. But they were negotiated with landholding interests that were powerful within Congress itself, and they stopped well short of the abolition of landlordism, which would not begin until after independence (Chapter 89). Many of the poorest rural people, including agricultural laborers and sharecroppers, gained little.

Social reform. The Madras ministry passed a measure in 1939 enabling temples to be opened to members of the Scheduled Castes where the managing authorities agreed, with legal protection for those who did so. Other provinces advanced similar measures and funded schemes for Harijan welfare, using the term popularized by Gandhi, though many Dalit leaders disliked it. The measures were significant symbolically but limited in effect, and Ambedkar criticized them as reliant on the goodwill of caste Hindus.

Assessing the record, historians generally agree that Congress ministers achieved real if partial reform within tight financial and constitutional limits, and that the ministries enhanced Congress’s standing as a competent political organization. They also agree that the governments’ record on the most fundamental questions of land and caste was cautious. The difference between promise and achievement was not simply a failure of will; it reflected the structure of the coalition that Congress had assembled and the constraints of a state whose revenue relied heavily on land and excise.

To assess reform, a historian must compare the same policy at several stages. Election promises might call for abolition of landlord privileges; a bill might instead improve tenancy security or postpone debt recovery; implementation then depended on district officials and the willingness of landholders and creditors to comply. Even a measure that brought real relief to one group might leave agricultural laborers outside its protections. Land revenue reduction posed a parallel dilemma: the same government that wanted to ease the cultivator's burden needed funds for primary schooling and disease control. The distance between a public resolution, a statute in the gazette, and a changed village practice was often substantial.

Everyday Life and Social History

Debt, Tenancy, and the Rhythms of the Rural Economy

For a tenant cultivator in the United Provinces, Bihar, or Madras, the decisive questions of these years were about rent, interest, and the security of occupancy rather than about constitutional design. A fall in crop prices after 1929 meant that the same rent in money required more produce to pay. A cultivator who borrowed from a moneylender against the harvest could find that interest accumulated faster than repayments, and a family’s land could be mortgaged or lost.

Debt-relief legislation affected these relationships directly, since scaling down debt altered what could be claimed in court. Moneylenders, many of whom were also traders or landholders, objected, and some reduced lending, which created short-term credit problems for the very people the laws were meant to help. Agricultural laborers, who owned no land and often depended on landholders for wages in grain, were largely outside the scope of tenancy law. Their experience of the Congress ministries was more often one of continuity than change.

Section 82.7

Peasants, Workers, Dalits, Women, and the Limits of Congress Reform

Chapter 80 traced the growth of movements among peasants, workers, Dalits, and women before 1937. Those movements did not stop when Congress took office; they confronted a new reality in which their former ally was now the government. This section examines the interaction rather than repeating the earlier history.

Peasants. The All India Kisan Sabha, formed in 1936, and its provincial branches, notably in Bihar under Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, pressed for tenancy reform, reductions in rent, and the end of illegal exactions by landlords. In Bihar, tensions arose over the return of land that tenants had lost or that landholders had taken into their personal cultivation, known as bakasht land. Congress ministers sought a compromise that would satisfy the Kisan Sabha without alienating the zamindars who supported the party, and the compromise left both sides dissatisfied. Kisan Sabha leaders complained that ministers were too deferential to landlords, while landlords complained of the excesses of agitation. Peasant processions, demonstrations, and occasional clashes put the ministries in the position of maintaining order against people who regarded themselves as Congress’s natural supporters.

Workers. Industrial labor expected the Congress ministries to be sympathetic. Strikes in Kanpur and elsewhere in 1937 and in Bombay’s textile mills during the same period tested that expectation. The Bombay ministry introduced the Industrial Disputes Act of 1938, which provided procedures for conciliation and arbitration and restricted the right to strike in certain circumstances without prior notice. Congress leaders defended it as a means of orderly settlement, while communists, socialists, and Ambedkar’s Independent Labour Party opposed it as an infringement on workers’ rights. A strike in November 1938 protested the measure. The episode showed that a party representing both mill-owners and workers could not satisfy both, and it affected the relationship between Congress and the trade-union movement.

Dalits. Congress ministers supported measures for the Scheduled Castes, including temple entry laws, access to public wells and schools, and scholarships, and Congress workers took part in the Harijan Sevak Sangh’s activities. Ambedkar and his followers argued that these reforms were handouts from caste Hindus, that they did not address economic dependence or the underlying structure of caste, and that Congress’s dominance prevented independent Dalit politics. Many Dalit voters supported Congress candidates, in part because Congress controlled patronage and in part because of local ties; others supported the Independent Labour Party or other organizations. It would be a mistake to treat Dalits as a single political bloc.

Women. Women were active in Congress as volunteers, organizers, and candidates. Several women served in the legislatures, and in the United Provinces Vijayalakshmi Pandit held a ministerial portfolio, becoming one of the first women cabinet ministers in India. Women’s organizations, including the All India Women’s Conference, pressed for reforms in marriage, property, and education and tried to use the new legislatures. Legislative achievements were modest, as the assemblies were dominated by men and as reform of personal law remained sensitive. Yet the experience of women in provincial politics contributed to a larger political presence, which would be visible in the Constituent Assembly of the 1940s.

The overall pattern was one of tension between movement and government. Congress’s popularity depended on its ability to embody the aspirations of groups with conflicting interests, and officeholding made those conflicts visible. The experience weakened the assumption that a single national party could satisfy all demands, an assumption that Gandhi’s leadership had done much to foster and that many Congress supporters continued to hold.

The changing relationship between movement and ministry could be seen in the handling of collective action. A peasant association that had once marched beside Congress volunteers might now present an inconvenient demand to a Congress revenue minister. Industrial workers might expect an administration claiming to speak for ordinary people to side unequivocally with them, only to encounter restrictions imposed in the name of industrial peace. Dalit organizations confronted another problem: a minister from Congress might endorse abolition of untouchability while relying on party networks dominated locally by higher-status castes. These were not identical conflicts, but each tested whether nationalist unity could accommodate separate organizations that retained the right to criticize a nationalist government.

Section 82.8

The Contradictions of Congress Rule: Provincial Authority, Party Discipline, and Colonial Constraints

How effective were the Congress ministries? The answer depends on what is measured. In terms of administration, they governed large provinces for over two years with a reasonable degree of stability. In terms of reform, they enacted a series of measures that touched many lives. In terms of politics, they strengthened Congress’s organization, enlarged its membership, and demonstrated its capacity to hold office. But they also revealed deep constraints and tensions.

The first constraint was financial. Provincial revenues depended heavily on land revenue and excise, while customs, income tax, and other lucrative sources belonged to the Centre. Ministers who wanted to spend more on education and health, to reduce land revenue, or to enforce prohibition faced fiscal limits. Prohibition reduced income, and the Centre’s control of key sources of revenue restricted what the provinces could do. These limits led some Congress leaders to argue that real reform required full control of the state.

The second was the governors’ powers. Governors rarely used their formal powers to overrule ministers, but their existence shaped the relationship, and the ministers were aware that major disputes could provoke intervention. The standoff over prisoners in 1938 was the clearest example. Governors also played a role in the appointment of senior officials and in maintaining the services’ privileges. The relationship was rarely one of open confrontation, but it was one in which Congress ministers often felt that real authority lay elsewhere.

The third was party discipline. The Congress high command attempted to ensure that ministers followed party policy. This produced a degree of coherence but also tension between the ministers, who were accountable to legislatures and to governors, and the party organization, which claimed a wider mandate. The Khare affair was one illustration; another was the question of how far Congress committees could interfere in administration. Gandhi himself expressed concern in 1938 and 1939 about corruption and factionalism in the Congress organization, including the enrolment of bogus members to influence internal elections, and warned that office-seeking could corrupt the movement.

The fourth was the problem of law and order. Ministers who had led agitations now had to police them. Congress governments used police against demonstrators in some cases, such as strikes and peasant processions, and used preventive provisions against opponents. In Madras the ministry arrested participants in the anti-Hindi agitation. Critics on the left argued that Congress had adopted the methods of the Raj; Congress leaders replied that a responsible government could not tolerate disorder.

Finally, accusations of favoritism arose from various directions: that Congress governments favored their own supporters in appointments and contracts, that they privileged Hindus, upper castes, or the commercial classes, and that they neglected minorities. Some accusations were politically motivated; others had a basis in the patronage practices that any governing party tends to follow. The next division examines the most consequential of these claims, those made by the Muslim League.

Taken together, these constraints explain a paradox. The ministries increased Congress’s prestige, and Congress emerged from office with greater organizational depth and administrative experience, yet the experience also exposed internal divisions and generated new opponents. Provincial governance made Congress more powerful and also made its limits clearer.

The cabinet crisis in the Central Provinces and Berar in July 1938 made this constitutional puzzle unusually sharp. Premier N. B. Khare had been at odds with colleagues over ministerial portfolios, public accusations and the authority of the Congress Parliamentary Board. After some ministers tendered resignations, other ministers refused to follow without instructions from the Congress Working Committee. Governor Sir Francis Wylie intervened in the resulting impasse, and Khare attempted to reconstruct a cabinet. The Working Committee subsequently censured him, treating his resort to the governor and his handling of colleagues as indiscipline. The precise sequence must be reconstructed from party resolutions, contemporary defenses and later historians rather than from a single retrospective version.

The dispute was not merely a feud among politicians. It exposed a tension between cabinet government, in which a premier normally commands the confidence of colleagues and legislature, and centralized party discipline, in which Congress regarded its ministers as agents of the national movement. Ambedkar later alleged that hostility to Khare's reconstructed cabinet involved the inclusion of a Scheduled Caste minister; that was an interested contemporary political accusation, not a motive that the historian can establish simply by repeating it. Congress spokesmen justified intervention as necessary to maintain the organization's policy and cohesion. Both accounts matter as evidence of what participants considered legitimate political authority.

Section 82.9

The Muslim League after the 1937 Elections: Electoral Weakness and Regional Diversity

The Muslim League emerged from the 1937 elections as a party with a claim to speak for Muslims that its votes did not support. It won a minority of the reserved Muslim seats in the provinces as a whole and did poorly where Muslims formed a majority. Understanding why requires attention to the diversity of Muslim politics, which cannot be reduced to a single organization or to the sum of the League’s leaders’ statements.

Muslims in British India lived in very different social and economic circumstances. In Punjab, Muslim landholders, tenants, and artisans were incorporated into a political order in which the Unionist Party, founded in the 1920s by Fazl-i-Husain and Chhotu Ram, built a coalition of Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh agricultural interests around shared concerns about debt, land, and the privileges of rural classes. In Bengal, Muslim peasants in the eastern districts were largely tenants and sharecroppers, and parties such as Fazlul Huq’s Krishak Praja Party appealed to them with an agenda of debt relief and tenancy reform, in competition with elite Muslim politicians and landlords. In Sindh, landholding families, religious leaders, and local rivalries shaped politics. In the Frontier, the Khudai Khidmatgar movement under Abdul Ghaffar Khan, committed to nonviolence and allied with Congress, had a mass following among Pashtun peasants, and rivals drew on tribal and landholding networks. In the United Provinces, where Muslims were a minority, the League could appeal to a middle-class and landed elite concerned with representation and employment.

These differences meant that the League, whose organization was thin and whose leadership came substantially from minority provinces and from Bombay, had little roots in the provinces where Muslim electors might have given it a majority. Provincial politicians had their own agendas, their own constituencies, and little reason to subordinate themselves to a distant all-India body. Many were tied to cross-communal parties, such as the Unionists, or to agrarian parties, which stressed class and regional interests over religious identity. Others worked with Congress or with organizations such as the Jamiat-ul-Ulama-i-Hind, led by Husain Ahmad Madani, which supported a composite nationalism and opposed the League’s claims. Muslim political opinion was divided among nationalist, regional, class-based, and communal positions.

The League also faced criticism that it was an elite organization. Its leaders were often lawyers, landholders, and professionals, and its membership was small. Its program, centered on the protection of Muslim political rights and representation, appealed more to those who feared losing influence than to peasants whose chief concerns were debt and rent. At the same time, the League’s weakness should not be exaggerated into irrelevance. In the minority provinces it had a base, and its leaders believed that a party representing Muslim interests could become stronger if it reorganized and if circumstances changed. Students should therefore avoid two errors: treating the League of 1937 as the representative of all Muslims, and treating it as a negligible force.

The election also posed a strategic dilemma. Congress’s majorities in several provinces meant that it did not need League support to govern; thus coalition options that had seemed attractive before the election appeared less necessary to Congress afterward. The League’s leaders concluded that its position in the new system would depend on how effectively it could organize, an effort Section 82.10 describes.

A provincial party could defend Muslim political interests without accepting Jinnah's leadership. Punjab's Unionists made bargaining over canal water, agricultural credit and landholding central to their appeal and incorporated Muslim, Sikh and Hindu landed figures. Bengal's agrarian politicians often spoke for Muslim cultivators whose disputes with landlords were not reducible to a single religious issue. In Sindh, locally powerful personalities and shifting ministerial alliances dominated electoral life. These differences help explain why a separate Muslim electorate did not automatically create a uniform Muslim League vote. Constitutional categories grouped voters by religion; social and political identities within each group remained multiple.

Section 82.10

Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Reorganization of the All-India Muslim League

Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a Bombay lawyer who had been active in both Congress and the League in earlier decades, returned from a period in Britain in the mid-1930s to lead the League. He had been a proponent of Hindu–Muslim cooperation in 1916 and had later become disillusioned with Congress’s turn to mass politics under Gandhi. After 1934 he worked to rebuild the League, establishing a Central Parliamentary Board in 1936 to coordinate candidates in the elections. The poor results of 1937 forced a reassessment.

Jinnah’s response combined organization and message. At the League’s session in Lucknow in October 1937, held in the capital of the province where the dispute over coalition had been most bitter, he presented the Congress as a Hindu-dominated body bent on excluding Muslims from a share of power and called on Muslims to unite under the League. The session adopted measures to broaden the membership, including a reduction of the subscription to a very small amount so that peasants and artisans could join, and to build provincial and district branches. Over the following two years membership figures reported by the League rose substantially, though such figures are partisan and not independently verified. Jinnah’s oratory and the League’s newspapers framed the Congress ministries as evidence of what Muslims could expect under a Hindu majority.

A critical element was alliance with provincial leaders. In October 1937, at about the time of the Lucknow session, Sikandar Hayat Khan of Punjab and A. K. Fazlul Huq of Bengal announced their adhesion to the League in ways that allowed them to retain control of their provincial governments. The understanding often called the Sikandar–Jinnah Pact accepted the League’s role at the all-India level while leaving Punjab’s Unionist ministry autonomy within the province; Huq’s arrangement similarly allowed him to govern Bengal with League support. The arrangements brought the League into association with the governments of the two largest Muslim-majority provinces, which strengthened its claim to speak for Muslims without giving it control of those provinces’ politics. They were, as historians have emphasized, partnerships of convenience in which provincial leaders retained their independence and could and did diverge from Jinnah.

The October 1937 Lucknow session was therefore important as an organizational intervention. Jinnah urged Muslims to join provincial and district League associations, recruit members, and negotiate from a position of collective political strength. His address treated weakness in the assemblies as evidence that Muslim voters needed more effective organization rather than as a reason to abandon parliamentary politics. The understandings reached with Sikandar Hayat Khan of Punjab and Fazlul Huq of Bengal extended the League's claims to leadership, but they did not instantly place Unionist or Krishak Praja legislators under Jinnah's day-to-day control. The difference between symbolic all-India affiliation and effective provincial discipline is essential to understanding the subsequent conflict.

Section 82.11

Congress–League Relations and the Controversy over Muslim Political Representation

The central controversy was about representation. Congress claimed to be a national organization open to all communities and to represent the entire Indian people against imperial rule. Nehru and others held that the fundamental division in Indian politics was between imperialism and nationalism, and that communal issues were secondary and would fade with economic and social change. Jinnah and the League rejected this, arguing that Muslims were a distinct political community whose interests could not be represented by a party dominated by a Hindu majority, and that the League was their authorized organization. The disagreement was about who could speak for Muslims and about what kind of political unit India was: a single nation with minorities, or a country of several communities with competing claims.

From 1937 onward the League and its provincial supporters issued a series of complaints about the Congress ministries. They alleged that Muslims were subject to discrimination in public employment, that Urdu was being displaced by Hindi, that educational schemes such as the Vidya Mandir plan in the Central Provinces and the Wardha basic-education scheme reflected Hindu cultural values, that the singing of the song Bande Mataram in schools and legislatures offended Muslim sensibilities, that the Congress tricolor was being treated as a national flag, that cow protection was being enforced to the detriment of Muslims, and that Muslims’ religious practices, including the playing of music near mosques, were being restricted. In 1938 a committee of inquiry associated with the League and chaired by the Raja of Pirpur published a report setting out such allegations, and in 1939 a similar report was issued about Bihar, known as the Shareef Report. Fazlul Huq issued a pamphlet on the sufferings of Muslims under Congress rule.

Congress’s response was to deny that it discriminated and to argue that the allegations were exaggerated or false. Congress adopted the first two stanzas of Bande Mataram as a national song after consultation with Rabindranath Tagore, who had suggested that later verses with religious imagery should not be required, a decision intended to accommodate objections. Gandhi, Nehru, and Rajendra Prasad all rejected the claim that the ministries were hostile to Muslims. Nehru and Jinnah exchanged correspondence in 1938 in which each restated his position without resolving the dispute, and Gandhi’s talks with Jinnah in the same year made little progress. Congress also pressed on with its mass contact program, seeking Muslim recruits, though its success was limited.

Three kinds of evidence must be kept separate when considering allegations against Congress ministries. A provincial circular or school order can establish what a particular government formally authorized; a petition can establish what a complainant perceived or experienced; an inquiry prepared by a rival party can reveal how grievances were assembled for political mobilization. The Pirpur and Shareef reports collected accusations that Congress rule threatened Muslim religious, linguistic and political interests, but individual claims varied in locality and supporting documentation. Congress leaders contested both the factual basis and the representativeness of such reports. Neither blanket dismissal nor blanket acceptance allows a reader to determine where administrative discrimination, local conflict, or polemical interpretation was at issue.

A related argument concerned what ordinary public culture should look like in a government claiming national legitimacy. The use of nationalist songs, flags or textbook narratives could be experienced by supporters as evidence of liberation from imperial rule, while opponents interpreted some of the same symbols as markers of a Hindu-majority public culture. The point is not that every complaint was justified or that every symbol had only one meaning. It is that representative government demanded an answer to how a majority party could exercise authority without making minorities dependent on that majority's discretion. Political disagreement about this question helped turn localized controversies into an all-India constitutional dispute.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Assess the Pirpur and Shareef Reports?

The question: Do the reports show systematic discrimination against Muslims under the Congress ministries, or are they best understood as political documents that exaggerated or generalized from isolated incidents?

Interpretation A — Documents of genuine grievance: Some scholars emphasize that the reports captured real experiences: instances of local pressure, of the use of symbols and songs that excluded some Muslims, of unequal access to patronage, and of communal tension in districts where Congress committees were strong. They argue that even exaggerated claims expressed real anxieties about the position of a minority in a majority-dominated system.
Interpretation B — Instruments of political mobilization: Others emphasize that the reports were produced by a party seeking to mobilize support and were assembled from unverified complaints without systematic investigation. They note that governors, in their own reports, often found specific allegations to be exaggerated or unsubstantiated while acknowledging local friction, and that many of the cited incidents had local causes.

Evidence: The reports themselves, governors’ confidential reports, Congress’s replies, legislative proceedings, local newspapers, and records of specific disputes. These sources point in different directions, and the accounts of colonial officials have their own interests.

Why it matters: The question affects how the late 1930s are understood. If the allegations were largely unfounded, the League’s success rested on mobilization of fear; if they had substance, Congress’s claim to represent all communities is weakened. Many historians hold that both processes operated: real local grievances existed, and the League generalized from them in ways that served its organizational aims.

The debate over symbols is instructive. Bande Mataram had been a rallying song of the Swadeshi movement (Chapter 77), but it also contained passages that some Muslims regarded as inconsistent with monotheism. The question of what song, flag, or language could symbolize a nation shared by communities with different religious traditions was therefore at the center of the dispute, and neither side was merely inventing a grievance. What the controversy showed was that Congress’s concept of a composite nationalism, which treated Indian identity as territorial and cultural, was not accepted by all who were included in it, and that symbols that seemed neutral to one community could feel exclusive to another.

Section 82.12

The Hindu Mahasabha, V. D. Savarkar, and Hindu Political Nationalism

While Jinnah rebuilt the League, another political tradition developed an alternative to both Congress’s composite nationalism and the League’s community-based claims. The Hindu Mahasabha had been founded in the early twentieth century and gained prominence in the 1920s, when it was associated with movements for the reconversion of converts and the organization of Hindu communities, and with leaders such as Madan Mohan Malaviya and Lajpat Rai. It fared poorly in the 1937 elections, winning few seats and trailing Congress even in Hindu-majority areas. Its significance lay less in its electoral strength than in the ideas it articulated and the organizational lines it developed.

Vinayak Damodar Savarkar, a revolutionary of the earlier period who had been imprisoned in the Andaman Islands and later confined to Ratnagiri district under restrictions, was released from those restrictions in 1937 and elected president of the Mahasabha at its session in Ahmedabad in December of that year. He was re-elected for several years. His pamphlet, later titled Hindutva, first published in the 1920s, set out a definition of the Hindu not primarily as an adherent of a religion but as a person who regarded the territory of India as both fatherland and holy land and who shared a common culture and heritage. This definition, which used a language of nation, race, and civilization, placed Muslims and Christians, whose holy lands lay elsewhere, in an ambiguous position in the nation as defined. Historians distinguish Hindutva as a political ideology from Hinduism as a religious tradition of great diversity, and they note that Savarkar himself was not a conventionally pious Hindu but a rationalist critic of many religious practices.

The language of Hindutva should be read alongside, but not equated with, every other form of Hindu religious identification. Savarkar described political community through the relationship between territory, ancestry and a shared civilizational heritage, and at Ahmedabad in 1937 he rejected the assumption that India already formed a homogeneous nation. His argument challenged Congress's presentation of a single composite nation and differed from the League's organization of Muslim political representation. Yet Mahasabha influence was uneven; its political impact depended on local associations, individual leaders and alliances, not on an electoral majority comparable to Congress's. Its conceptual importance in the late 1930s exceeded its provincial legislative strength.

Comparison with Congress and the League shows that the three organizations were not mirror images. The Left, Alternative Leadership, and Princely-State Mobilization

Political alternatives extended well beyond a Congress–League divide.

Section 82.13

Congress Socialists, Communists, and the Politics of Economic Transformation

The Congress Socialist Party was formed in 1934 as a group within Congress by younger members dissatisfied with the leadership’s caution on economic and social questions. Its leaders included Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, Ram Manohar Lohia, Minoo Masani, and Ashok Mehta. They argued that independence had to be accompanied by the transformation of the economic order, including the abolition of landlordism, the nationalization of key industries, and the empowerment of peasants and workers. They remained inside Congress, believing that it was the main vehicle for anti-imperial struggle, while seeking to move it toward a socialist program.

The Communist Party of India, banned in 1934, operated underground and through other organizations. After the Seventh Congress of the Communist International in 1935 endorsed a policy of broad anti-fascist and anti-imperialist fronts, Indian communists adopted a strategy of working within the national movement, including inside the Congress Socialist Party and through trade unions and peasant organizations. They played a leading role in the All India Kisan Sabha and in labor unions, and they gained influence in some regions, notably in Kerala and parts of Andhra and Bengal. Their presence within Congress’s left wing created tensions with socialists and with Congress’s moderate leadership, who were suspicious of communist discipline and loyalties.

Nehru occupied an unusual position. As Congress president in 1936 and 1937 he spoke of socialism as the solution to India’s poverty, and he promoted an analysis of imperialism and capitalism that was close to the left’s. But he remained loyal to Gandhi and to the organizational unity of Congress, and he declined to break with the leadership. The tension between his rhetoric and the practice of the ministries, which depended on landlord and business support, was apparent to critics. The Congress Socialists criticized the ministries for not going far enough, while the right of the party regarded the socialists as a destabilizing force. The old guard on the Working Committee, notably Patel and Prasad, sought to contain the left’s influence.

Debates on economic planning also developed. In 1938, under the Congress presidency of Bose, a conference of ministers of industries from the Congress provinces led to the establishment of a National Planning Committee, chaired by Nehru, to prepare a program for the economic development of India. The committee brought together economists, scientists, businessmen, and politicians in an effort to design a plan that would guide an independent state. Its deliberations, which continued into the war years and after, show that thinking about the postcolonial economy was under way well before independence, and that Congress’s version of economic nationalism included a significant role for the state in industrialization. Business leaders, some of whom supported Congress financially, took part, and their involvement shows the combination of capital, nationalism, and state planning that would characterize the Nehruvian settlement discussed in Chapters 88 and 89.

Trade-union politics also changed. The divisions that had weakened the All India Trade Union Congress in the early 1930s began to heal, and union activity increased during the ministries. Strikes in textile centers and elsewhere tested labor’s relationship with Congress governments, as §82.7 showed. The left’s arguments about class, ownership, and the limits of nationalism therefore had a concrete reference point in the experience of Congress governments, and they contributed to disputes within the national movement that became sharper during the war.

The left was itself divided about the relationship between national freedom and social revolution. Congress Socialists hoped to reshape the Congress from within, whereas communists worked through overlapping political and trade-union networks under restrictions imposed by the colonial state. The All India Kisan Sabha offered a forum in which land rents, tenancy and the indebtedness of cultivators could be framed as larger political questions. Yet local peasant interests were not uniform: small proprietors, tenants and landless laborers could pursue different reforms. Cooperation against imperial rule did not eliminate these divisions, and neither socialist rhetoric nor a provincial minister's reform program could automatically align them.

Section 82.14

B. R. Ambedkar, the Independent Labour Party, and Social Democracy

B. R. Ambedkar, whose earlier activism and role in the Poona Pact Chapters 80 and 81 discussed, founded the Independent Labour Party in Bombay in August 1936. He intended it as a party representing workers and the poor across caste lines, though its principal base lay among Dalits, particularly in Bombay Presidency. Its program addressed labor conditions, rural indebtedness, the abolition of certain forms of hereditary landholding tenure and servitude, and the removal of caste discrimination. Ambedkar’s position was that political democracy without social democracy was hollow: independence from Britain would not by itself remove the domination of the lower castes by the upper, and a government controlled by caste Hindus might perpetuate it.

The Independent Labour Party supplied concrete evidence that political leadership did not have to be monopolized by Congress. A Lok Sabha Secretariat retrospective credits the party with winning 15 of the 17 Bombay seats it contested in 1937; the contemporary return-based classification used for the all-province table lists 13 members under the ILP label. These different counts may reflect how allied and subsequently identified members were classified. They should be attributed to their distinct sources, not presented as interchangeable exact tallies. Ambedkar's electoral success was therefore substantial under either treatment, but the existence of divergent tabulations is also a lesson in historical method.

The party's original program was published in the Times of India in August 1936 and is preserved in Ambedkar's collected writings. It addressed industrial labor, the burdens of rural tenants and the degraded status of people subjected to caste exclusion. Ambedkar insisted that an electoral organization rooted in the oppressed could still welcome members from other communities; this was a deliberate attempt to build political cooperation without surrendering independent Dalit leadership. The combination of caste and labor politics distinguished the ILP from Congress social reform, from narrowly occupational unionism, and from a party organized solely around religious representation.

In the Bombay legislature, Ambedkar challenged the khoti land-revenue system, a form of intermediary exaction affecting cultivators in the Konkan, and opposed measures he believed restricted the right to strike. The November 1938 industrial stoppage, supported by Ambedkar and labor activists, linked legislative debate to collective action outside the assembly. Here the provincial experiment brought rival definitions of freedom into collision: for Congress ministers, maintaining production and negotiating labor disputes could be part of responsible government; for Ambedkar, workers' capacity to withhold labor was itself a democratic safeguard. His opposition should not be reduced to a personal dispute with Congress leaders.

Section 82.15

Subhas Chandra Bose: Haripura, Tripuri, and the Crisis of Congress Leadership

Subhas Chandra Bose, a Bengali nationalist who had been imprisoned and exiled by the British and who had worked in Europe during the mid-1930s, was elected president of Congress for the session at Haripura in February 1938. His presidency was the first sign of a leadership dispute that would shake the party. Bose admired Nehru’s socialism but was more impatient with Gandhi’s methods and with the compromises of the ministries. He favored rapid industrialization, centralized planning, and a more confrontational stance toward the British, and he was skeptical of the federal scheme and of what he regarded as the high command’s caution.

Bose's Haripura presidential address in February 1938 placed economic reconstruction and anti-imperialist strategy in the same framework. He argued that freedom would require more than a change of flags; industrialization, scientific planning and coordination of economic resources were to form part of national reconstruction. These emphases overlapped with Nehru's ideas, but their political implications were contested: a planning committee could work within the emerging machinery of Congress-led provinces, whereas a renewed confrontation with the Raj might jeopardize that machinery. Bose's prestige as an imprisoned nationalist did not settle what timetable or institutional authority Congress should adopt.

At Tripuri in March 1939, his reelection did not translate into an operative majority among senior Working Committee figures. The resolution associated with Govind Ballabh Pant was widely read as requiring a committee acceptable to Gandhi. Bose's illness during the session complicated proceedings but did not cause the underlying disagreement. His subsequent resignation revealed the distinction between winning an internal presidential contest and commanding the organizational cooperation needed to govern the party. The Forward Bloc arose from this defeated attempt to redirect Congress's immediate strategy; its later wartime course belongs to another chapter, not to an inevitable conclusion already apparent in early 1939.

At the end of 1938 Bose decided to seek a second term. In the election of January 1939 he defeated Pattabhi Sitaramayya, who had the backing of Gandhi and the senior leaders, by a modest margin of votes. Gandhi remarked that Sitaramayya’s defeat was his own, a statement that expressed his acceptance of the result but also the seriousness of the rift.

At the Tripuri session in March 1939 the conflict came into the open. A resolution reaffirming loyalty to Gandhi’s leadership and requiring the president to form his Working Committee in accordance with Gandhi’s wishes was passed, and Bose, who was ill, found that senior leaders declined to serve on a committee he constituted. After negotiations failed, he resigned in late April 1939 and was succeeded by Rajendra Prasad. In May he founded the Forward Bloc as a group within Congress that aimed at uniting the left and pressing for more radical action. The party later disciplined him, and he was barred from holding office in the Congress for a period. Chapter 84 discusses his activities during the war and the Indian National Army; this chapter ends his story at the point at which a bitter rivalry within Congress had become public.

Historians interpret the episode in several ways. Some see it as a conflict of personalities and methods, between Bose’s impatience and Gandhi’s strategic caution. Others see it as a conflict over the relationship between party and ministries, since Bose’s call for militancy threatened an arrangement in which Congress governed under the Act. Still others emphasize regional dimensions, including the role of Bengal’s politics. It is also a reminder that Congress’s unity, while strong, depended on the willingness of leaders to defer to a collective discipline, and that this willingness had limits.

Section 82.16

The Princely States, Praja Mandals, and the Demand for Responsible Government

The princely states occupied roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent’s area and contained a substantial minority of its population, including the large states of Hyderabad, Mysore, Kashmir, and Travancore and hundreds of smaller ones. They were not part of British India. Their rulers governed under treaties and the doctrine of paramountcy (Chapter 71), subject to the supervision of a British Resident. Their subjects voted in no provincial election, and most states had no representative institutions or only very limited ones. Politics in the states therefore followed a separate course, shaped by the interplay of rulers, ministers, the paramount power, and popular movements.

The Rajkot dispute illustrates why a princely state's politics could resist solutions that seemed straightforward in a British province. Popular activists sought enforceable representative reforms from the ruler, while the durbar argued over the terms of earlier undertakings and the composition of any reform committee. Gandhi fasted in March 1939, and the intervention of the paramount power led to an arbitration by Sir Maurice Gwyer, Chief Justice of the Federal Court of India, acting as a jurist in the matter rather than as a court delivering a constitutional judgment. His interpretation initially appeared favorable to the reformers, but the formula did not settle whose interests the proposed representative body should include.

The Bhayats, Muslim interests and Scheduled Caste claimants disputed aspects of the representation arrangements. Gandhi subsequently questioned his own reliance on an award backed by external pressure and renounced treating Gwyer's decision as the instrument of settlement. This reversal meant that the end of the fast and the existence of an award cannot be equated with a stable constitutional victory. The episode is especially useful alongside the 1937 provincial elections: in one setting the rules of representation had been prescribed by the 1935 Act; in the other, the prior task was to determine how a ruler, popular organization and excluded communities could agree on rules at all.

At Ludhiana in February 1939, Nehru's address to the All India States Peoples' Conference described freedom in British India and the princely states as inseparable in principle, while recognizing the need for movements in the states to develop their own organization. This position did not eliminate disputes about the pace or form of Congress involvement. Local activists often asked for direct support when faced with arrests or bans; national leaders feared that direct intervention could overwhelm their capacity or invite repression. The movement's developing relationship with the states was thus another example of a claim to all-India nationhood colliding with distinct local institutional arrangements.

Historical Vocabulary
Praja Mandal

A Praja Mandal (literally “people’s association”) was a political organization in a princely state demanding civil liberties, representative institutions, and in many cases responsible government, meaning a ministry answerable to an elected legislature. Different states had different names for such bodies, such as State Congress or National Conference.

The term matters because it names a form of politics distinct from that of British India. Praja Mandals had to confront both the ruler and, indirectly, the paramount power, and they often had less freedom to organize than provincial parties. Their relationship to the Indian National Congress was the subject of long debate.

An All India States Peoples’ Conference had existed since 1927 to coordinate such movements. Its relationship with Congress was ambiguous. Congress had long held that it should not interfere directly in the internal affairs of the states, partly from respect for the difficulties of agitation there and partly from caution about confronting the princes. At Haripura in 1938 Congress reaffirmed its sympathy with the states’ peoples’ demand for civil liberties and responsible government and its position that the states’ movements should lead their own struggles, while indicating that Congress would give moral support and could not itself take direct part in those struggles at that stage. The position shifted over the next year as events forced more involvement. In 1939 Nehru became president of the States Peoples’ Conference.

Several movements illustrate the diversity of state politics. Section 82.17

Punjab and Bengal: Regional Parties, Agrarian Interests, and Coalition Politics

Punjab and Bengal were the two most populous provinces with Muslim majorities and the two in which provincial parties most clearly resisted the categories of all-India politics. Their experiences show that the late 1930s cannot be understood as a contest between two national parties.

Punjab. The Unionist Party, formed in the 1920s by Sir Fazl-i-Husain and Sir Chhotu Ram, and led after Fazl-i-Husain’s death in 1936 by Sir Sikandar Hayat Khan, represented a coalition of landholding and agricultural interests across religious lines. Its strength lay in the rural constituencies, where Muslim, Hindu Jat, and Sikh landholders and cultivators shared concerns about debt, taxation, and the privileges of the agricultural classes under laws such as the Punjab Land Alienation Act of 1900, which restricted the transfer of land from agriculturists to moneylenders. The Unionists won a large majority in 1937, and Sikandar Hayat Khan became Premier. His government passed legislation to relieve rural debt and to regulate moneylending, measures which favored cultivators over urban moneylending classes who were largely Hindu, and which therefore gave some communal tint to what the Unionists presented as class legislation.

The Unionist position toward the League was cautious. Sikandar Hayat Khan accepted the League’s all-India leadership at Lucknow in 1937 in exchange for the League’s acceptance that the Unionist party would govern the province in its own way, and he sat in the League’s counsels while maintaining a ministry that included Hindu and Sikh members. His interest was in preserving Punjab’s internal balance, and he and Jinnah disagreed at times over the League’s claims in Punjab. Congress, which had a modest presence in the province, and the Sikh Akali Dal, which sought to protect Sikh interests, pursued their own strategies. The result was a politics of shifting alliances rather than of fixed communal blocs, though communal tensions in Punjab were growing and would erupt later.

Bengal. Bengal’s assembly of 250 members was fragmented. Congress was the largest party, but the combined Muslim members were more numerous, and they were divided among the League, Fazlul Huq’s Krishak Praja Party, and independents. Huq, a prominent lawyer and politician who had been a Congress member earlier, led a party that appealed to Muslim peasants with promises of debt relief, tenancy reform, and abolition of the zamindari system. After the elections, discussions between Huq and Congress over a coalition failed, partly because of disagreements over the program and partly because of the difficulty of agreeing on the sharing of offices. Huq then formed a coalition with the League and other groups, and joined the League at Lucknow in October 1937. His ministry passed measures on debt settlement and tenancy, such as an amendment of the Bengal Tenancy Act in 1938, and it faced constant tension between its agrarian program and the interests of the landlords and the League’s leaders from landed and urban backgrounds, such as Khwaja Nazimuddin and H. S. Suhrawardy.

Bengal’s politics also involved the Hindu bhadralok, the educated, largely upper-caste Hindu middle class, who dominated much of the Congress organization in the province and who felt threatened by the Muslim-majority ministry. Within Congress, rivalry between factions, including those aligned with Subhas Chandra Bose and his brother Sarat Chandra Bose and those aligned with others, weakened the party’s capacity to form alliances. The combined effect was a politics of intense competition among groups that were not neatly divided by religion alone. The agrarian program of the Krishak Praja Party, for example, cut across the League’s emphasis on community, and tension between the two eventually weakened Huq’s position.

Taken together, Punjab and Bengal show how provincial interests resisted simple classification. In each, landlords, cultivators, and communities shaped coalitions around local conflicts. The League’s later success in these provinces would depend on transforming this politics of agrarian and regional interests into a politics of community, a process that occurred largely after the period covered here and under the pressure of the war.

Punjab's agrarian arrangements gave the Unionists a practical program as well as an electoral coalition. Canal-colony agriculture and the regulation of rural credit connected the interests of influential cultivators to those of landed patrons, although their relative benefits were not equal. The party's ability to command support across several religious communities rested on particular political bargains, not on the disappearance of Sikh, Hindu or Muslim communal interests. Its approximately 95-member assembly grouping must also be distinguished from the initial number returned under the Unionist ticket: independent or allied legislators could join a governing bloc after the election. Confusing those stages makes a coalition appear more electorally homogeneous than it was.

In Bengal, Fazlul Huq's appeal to cultivators exposed another kind of tension within a coalition. Measures dealing with tenancy and rural debt could benefit smallholders and tenants, but proposals affecting intermediary landlord claims encountered opponents with economic interests that did not always coincide with their religious identity. Huq's ministry had to manage these contradictions while seeking legislative support from the Muslim League and others. The failure of an initial Congress–Huq understanding should therefore be studied alongside disputes over policy, leadership and party calculations, rather than being explained as an automatic outcome of Hindu–Muslim division.

Section 82.18

Sindh, the Frontier, Assam, and Southern India: Regional Alternatives to All-India Politics

This section uses selected case studies, since the regions differ considerably and cannot be treated as identical.

Sindh. Separated from Bombay as a province in 1936, Sindh had a Muslim majority and a society dominated by large landholders, religious leaders, and rival families, with a Hindu minority concentrated in trade and professions in the towns. Politics in the assembly revolved around shifting alliances among factions, such as the Sind United Party and groups led by Sir Ghulam Hussain Hidayatullah, Allah Bakhsh Soomro, and G. M. Syed. Ministries changed several times, and Congress had a small presence. Local disputes, notably over a mosque at Sukkur in 1939, could take on communal meaning. In 1938 the Sind Provincial Muslim League conference at Karachi passed a resolution about the future constitution that envisaged separate arrangements for Muslim and non-Muslim regions, a document that is often cited as an early formulation of ideas that would later take the form of the Pakistan demand, though it was a provincial resolution not binding on the all-India League.

The North-West Frontier Province. The Frontier was a Pashtun-majority province with a small Hindu and Sikh minority and a strategic position on the border with Afghanistan. Abdul Ghaffar Khan’s Khudai Khidmatgar, a nonviolent movement of Pashtun volunteers, had allied with Congress since the early 1930s. After the 1937 elections an interim ministry led by Sir Abdul Qaiyum Khan lost its majority, and in September 1937 Dr Khan Sahib, Ghaffar Khan’s brother, formed a Congress ministry. It governed with support from Khudai Khidmatgar and some independents, and its tenure showed that Congress could govern a Muslim-majority province with Muslim leaders. Its opponents, including the League and landed Pashtun families, challenged its legitimacy, and the contrast with other provinces complicated Jinnah’s claim that Congress was a Hindu organization.

Assam. Assam’s politics were shaped by the Brahmaputra valley and the Surma valley, by migration into the province from eastern Bengal, and by tensions over land and settlement. The ministry under Syed Muhammad Saadulla, formed in 1937 with Congress’s outside support in some respects, depended on shifting coalition partners and fell in 1938. Gopinath Bardoloi then formed a Congress-led ministry. Disputes over immigration and the “line system,” which restricted the settlement of immigrants in certain areas, made Assamese concerns about demographic and cultural change a part of provincial politics, and they would remain so.

Southern India. In Madras, Congress’s majority displaced the Justice Party, which had represented non-Brahmin interests and governed in the 1920s. The non-Brahmin movement did not disappear. E. V. Ramasamy, known as Periyar, who had founded the Self-Respect Movement (Chapter 80), became prominent in opposing the Congress ministry’s decision of 1938 to introduce compulsory Hindi in schools. He regarded it as an imposition of northern, upper-caste culture on Tamil speakers. The anti-Hindi agitation involved demonstrations, arrests, and deaths in custody, and Periyar assumed leadership of the Justice Party in December 1938, later reshaping it. Compulsory Hindi was withdrawn after the Congress ministry resigned in late 1939. The episode demonstrates that linguistic and caste politics in the South posed a challenge to an all-India nationalism. In Travancore and Mysore, discussed in §82.16, regional politics worked under princely rule.

These regional cases show that all-India parties had to adapt to local circumstances and that regional movements had their own logic. They also demonstrate that provincial politics could produce alliances that ran against the supposed logic of religious or national blocs: a Congress ministry led by Muslims in the Frontier, a Muslim-led coalition in Bengal involving a Hindu-Muslim cross-section, and a cross-communal Unionist government in Punjab.

Assam shows especially clearly why the labels used in an all-India election table are not sufficient to explain provincial government. Political alignments differed between the Brahmaputra and Surma valleys, while disputes over migration and land settlement affected cultivators, immigrants and existing village communities in unequal ways. An administration's position on the line system could be defended as protecting settled rural interests or criticized as restricting migrants. These competing interpretations helped shape coalition-making under Saadulla and Bardoloi. They also remind students that 'minority' and 'majority' changed meaning with the geographic scale of the political question: a provincial minority might be numerous in a particular valley or district.

In the Frontier, Congress's alliance with the Khudai Khidmatgar movement created an organizational foundation different from its relationships in Bengal or Punjab. Pashtun nonviolent discipline and anti-colonial mobilization under Abdul Ghaffar Khan contributed to the ministry headed by his brother, but the alliance faced criticism from rivals who regarded Congress's wider political connections with suspicion. In Madras, meanwhile, the opposition to compulsory Hindi education demonstrated that cultural policy could produce resistance to a government supported by a large electoral majority. Provincial autonomy thus multiplied the sites at which people contested what being represented by a national party actually meant.

On central versus provincial power, Congress simultaneously demanded a strong, sovereign national state and, through its provincial ministries, enjoyed the advantages of provincial autonomy. On majority rule and minority protection, the dispute was fundamental. Congress held that a democratic state with equal citizenship protected minorities through rights; the League held that in a society divided along religious lines, a numerical majority was a permanent, communal majority, and minorities needed structural guarantees. Ambedkar made a parallel argument on behalf of Dalits. Each side’s argument had a logic, and each was disputed. Ideas of a separate Muslim state existed, notably in the poet Muhammad Iqbal’s 1930 address and in writings by Choudhry Rahmat Ali in the 1930s, but they were not the program of the League in 1939, and many Muslim leaders disclaimed them. In 1939 Sikandar Hayat Khan circulated an outline of a scheme for a loosely knit federation with strong provinces, an example of alternative proposals that did not envisage partition.

On social equality and citizenship, the left and Ambedkar made the strongest claims, arguing that formal political rights would not suffice. Congress accepted social reform in principle but subordinated it to national unity. The princes, for their part, saw their position as sovereign rulers protected by treaties, and they feared that a democratic federation would end their authority.

The disagreements were therefore not merely about tactics but about the basic structure of the future state. No institutional mechanism existed to reconcile them. The Constituent Assembly that Congress demanded would be a forum for doing so, but who would convene it, on what franchise, and with what powers were themselves matters of dispute. This is the unresolved problem that Chapters 83 to 88 follow.

The constitutional proposals were also answers to different perceived dangers. For Congress, excessive communal or princely vetoes could prevent an elected center from redistributing resources or exercising sovereignty after British withdrawal. For the League, electoral majorities at the center could become permanent political subordination if Muslims were required to trust a rival party's guarantees. For an agrarian regional premier, concentration of powers in New Delhi threatened provincial fiscal and patronage networks; for the socialist left, decentralization might obstruct national economic planning. These positions cannot be ranked as though each party was debating the same single institutional problem with merely different rhetoric.

Ambedkar's intervention adds another dimension. A declaration of equal citizenship might coexist with caste discrimination in schools, landholding, employment and village institutions. He accordingly sought institutional safeguards and opportunities for political organization independent of the social majority. The experience of princely states posed a further challenge, because federation could bring hereditary rulers into a national legislature without giving their subjects equivalent voting rights. The unresolved constitutional question was therefore not simply whether India should be strong or federal. It was whose consent would authorize the state, whose interests its institutions could restrain, and how those outside dominant electoral coalitions could demand accountability.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Agitation to Administration, 1930–1939

What Changed

  • Congress moved from a movement of protest to a party of government in most of the Hindu-majority provinces
  • Indian ministers acquired administrative responsibility over education, agriculture, and local government
  • The Muslim League was reorganized, and a rival Hindu nationalist organization articulated a distinct program
  • Leftist, anti-caste, and princely-state movements gained visibility and organization
  • Congress’s internal divisions became public in the Bose crisis

What Stayed the Same

  • The Centre remained under British control, with the Viceroy, the Imperial Services, and the army outside popular control
  • The franchise remained narrow and communally structured
  • Landlordism, rural debt, and caste hierarchy remained fundamental features of rural society
  • The princely states remained outside the elective system

Who experienced change most directly: Provincial politicians, officials, and organized groups saw the most change. Agricultural laborers, many tenants, and subjects of princely states experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It explains why the experience of office strengthened some actors while leaving structural problems untouched.

Section 82.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: The Unresolved Future of India, 1939

The experience of provincial autonomy between 1937 and 1939 was one of achievement and contradiction. It gave Indian politicians, for the first time on a large scale, the responsibility of government. Congress ministers governed major provinces competently, enacted a series of reforms, and gained confidence in their capacity to run a state. They also faced the constraints of finances, governors’ powers, and the colonial administrative apparatus. Provincial office strengthened Congress’s organizational depth and prestige, and it also exposed the party to criticism from the left, from peasants and workers, and from groups who felt excluded.

Neither electoral victory nor common opposition to imperial rule produced constitutional consensus. This is the central conclusion of the chapter. The nationalist movement had been held together by the shared goal of ending British rule, but governing required decisions on matters on which the participants disagreed, and the elections themselves had shown that the party claiming to speak for the nation had won a minority of Muslim seats and had little purchase in some of the largest provinces. The Muslim League responded to this by reorganizing around a claim to represent Muslims, using grievances against Congress ministries as a means of mobilization. The Hindu Mahasabha elaborated a Hindu nationalist alternative. The left, the anti-caste movement, regional parties, and princely-state movements put forward further conceptions of the future. Bose’s challenge showed that Congress itself was not united on strategy.

The experience also supplies a warning about historical hindsight. The failure of parties to reconcile their competing positions by September 1939 did not make Partition an already decided outcome. Congress had learned to administer provinces without accepting the colonial settlement as final; the League had begun a more forceful organizational campaign without yet making a formally adopted partition demand; regional coalitions and anti-caste and socialist movements continued to advocate alternatives. The coming war would alter the relative power and negotiating choices of these actors. The historian's task is to explain the options still available in 1939, not to treat the later outcome as an intention shared by everyone who entered the provincial assemblies in 1937.

Cause and Effect

Why Provincial Government Did Not Produce Constitutional Consensus

Long-term causes

Separate electorates and the arrangements of 1909, 1919, and 1932; unresolved questions about communal representation; the unequal development of regional politics; and the colonial state’s reliance on categories of community and interest.

Short-term causes

The structure of the 1935 Act; Congress’s unexpected electoral strength and League weakness; the failure of coalition talks; and the controversies over symbols, schooling, and patronage.

Immediate effects

Reorganization of the League; growing mistrust between the parties; Congress internal disputes; heightened agitation in the states.

Long-term consequences

A political field in which the war would place Congress and the League in a new relationship, setting the stage for the negotiations of 1945 to 1947 and for Partition. These outcomes were not determined in 1939.

Historical complexity: The failure of consensus cannot be attributed to any single party or event. It reflected structural features of the constitution, the choices of leaders, the interests of regional elites, and the ways in which each side interpreted the other’s actions.

Students should also guard against hindsight. In 1939 a number of futures were conceivable. Congress could have pressed for a Constituent Assembly; the League might have accepted a federation with strong guarantees; a coalition might have formed; regional parties might have remained pivotal. That the subcontinent would be partitioned was not apparent to the participants, and the shift in League policy toward a demand for separate Muslim states would occur in 1940, a development belonging to Chapter 83.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the years 1937 to 1939, when the politics of Indian independence moved into provincial legislatures and ministries. Its central question was what happened when organizations demanding independence had to demonstrate how they would govern and discovered that they disagreed about what an independent India should become. The answer is that provincial government exposed rather than resolved the disagreement.

The constitutional framework was the Government of India Act of 1935. Its provincial provisions, in force from 1 April 1937, made ministers responsible to elected legislatures but left governors with special responsibilities and reserve powers, left the Centre largely under British control, and depended for its federal scheme on the accession of princes that never occurred. Provincial autonomy was therefore real but limited, and it made provincial politics the main arena of Indian participation in government.

The elections of winter 1936–37 were held on a restricted franchise with communal and reserved seats. They cannot be equated with universal democratic participation. Congress won majorities in Madras, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces, Bihar, and Orissa, and was the largest party in Bombay, Assam, the Frontier, and Bengal. The Muslim League did relatively better in minority provinces and poorly where Muslims were a majority, and regional parties such as the Unionists and the Krishak Praja Party dominated Punjab and shaped Bengal.

Congress’s decision to take office followed a dispute over governors’ powers and was resolved in July 1937 after the Viceroy’s conciliatory statement. The ministries enacted reforms in civil liberties, education, prohibition, debt relief, tenancy, and temple entry, but their record on the fundamental questions of land and caste was cautious, constrained by finances, by landlord interests within Congress, and by the governors’ powers. They faced criticism from peasants, workers, Dalit leaders, and the left, and they became entangled in disputes over language, party discipline, and law and order.

The Muslim League, weakened at the polls, reorganized under Jinnah. It expanded its membership, allied with provincial leaders such as Sikandar Hayat Khan and Fazlul Huq on terms that preserved their autonomy, and framed the Congress ministries as a threat to Muslim interests. The failure of coalition talks in the United Provinces and controversies over symbols, schooling, and patronage became central to this campaign. The Pirpur and Shareef Reports, as partisan documents, require critical assessment: they captured real local grievances while serving as instruments of mobilization.

The Hindu Mahasabha under Savarkar articulated a Hindu nationalism built on the idea of Hindutva. Its electoral support was small, but it presented a conception of nationhood different from both Congress’s composite nationalism and the League’s community-based claims. The three were not mirror images, since they differed in their definitions of the nation, their social bases, and their strategies.

Alternatives to the Congress–League framework were numerous. The Congress Socialists and communists pressed for economic transformation, Ambedkar’s Independent Labour Party argued that political independence without social democracy would be incomplete, and Bose’s challenge to the Congress leadership ended with his resignation and the founding of the Forward Bloc. In the princely states, Praja Mandals demanded responsible government, and Congress’s uncertain role culminated in the events at Rajkot.

Regional case studies showed that provincial politics resisted classification by all-India party labels. Unionist Punjab, Huq’s Bengal, factional Sindh, Congress-led Frontier, Assam’s coalitions, and the anti-Hindi agitation in Madras each followed their own logic. Cross-communal coalitions, Muslim-led Congress governments, and agrarian parties show that religious blocs were not the only organizing principle of politics.

By 1939 several visions of India’s future competed without a mechanism to reconcile them. They differed on central and provincial power, majority rule and minority protection, social equality, citizenship, and the place of the princes. The outbreak of war on 3 September 1939 interrupted this contest and opened the period examined in Chapter 83. The chapter’s principal lesson is that the future of India was unsettled in 1939, and that later outcomes, including Partition, were not foreordained by the events of 1937.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Provincial autonomy
The system under the 1935 Act in which provincial ministers were responsible to elected legislatures across provincial subjects, while governors and the Centre retained reserve powers.
Responsible government
A system in which ministers hold office only while they command a legislative majority.
Special responsibilities
Matters, such as peace, minority interests, and rights of services, for which governors could act in their individual judgment.
Individual judgment
A governor’s authority to act independently of ministerial advice in specified matters.
Section 93
The provision allowing a governor to assume direct control of a province if constitutional government could not be carried on.
Federation
The all-India structure envisaged by the 1935 Act, joining provinces and acceding princely states; never brought into operation.
Franchise
The right to vote; in 1937 it depended on property, tax, education, or service qualifications and covered a minority of adults.
Separate electorate
A system in which members of a community vote only for candidates for seats reserved to that community.
Reserved constituency
A seat set aside for a group, such as the Scheduled Castes, but contested in a wider electorate.
Weightage
The allocation to a minority community of a share of seats larger than its share of the population.
Premier
The title used for the chief minister of a province under the 1935 Act.
Office acceptance
Congress’s 1937 decision to form ministries under the Act while continuing to oppose it.
Parliamentary Sub-Committee
A Congress body of Patel, Prasad, and Azad that supervised the ministries.
High Command
The informal name for Congress’s senior leadership and Working Committee in relation to the provincial ministries.
Mass Contact campaign
A Congress effort in 1937 to recruit Muslims and expand its reach, with limited results.
Unionist Party
A Punjab party of Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh agricultural interests, led by Sikandar Hayat Khan after 1936.
Krishak Praja Party
A Bengal party led by Fazlul Huq appealing to peasants with debt and tenancy reform.
Khudai Khidmatgar
A nonviolent Pashtun movement led by Abdul Ghaffar Khan, allied with Congress.
Hindutva
Savarkar’s term for a political definition of Hindu identity based on territory, descent, and culture.
Congress Socialist Party
A socialist group within Congress, founded in 1934.
Independent Labour Party
Ambedkar’s party of 1936 focusing on labor, tenancy, and caste.
Forward Bloc
The group founded by Bose in 1939 to unite the left and press for militancy.
Praja Mandal
A popular association in a princely state demanding civil liberties and responsible government.
Paramountcy
The British Crown’s claimed supremacy over the princely states.
Constituent Assembly
A body elected to frame a constitution; a Congress demand from the mid-1930s onward.
Basic education (Wardha scheme)
Gandhi’s proposal for mother-tongue, craft-centered primary schooling, adopted in several provinces.
Pirpur Report
A 1938 League-aligned inquiry listing alleged injustices to Muslims under Congress ministries.
Bakasht land
Land that tenants had lost or that landholders had taken into personal cultivation; a source of dispute in Bihar.
Line system
An Assam policy restricting immigrant settlement in designated areas.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the nationalist movement at the moment when it began to govern. Before 1937, disagreements about the future could be postponed in the name of the common struggle against imperial rule. After 1937 they became concrete questions of administration, language, education, land, and representation. The experience revealed that a shared opposition to a colonial power does not automatically produce agreement about what should replace it.

The chapter also reveals how many political traditions were present in the late 1930s. A history that tells only of Congress and the League omits the Unionists, the Krishak Praja Party, the Khudai Khidmatgar, the Independent Labour Party, the socialists and communists, the Hindu Mahasabha, and the movements in the princely states. Each contributed to the questions that independent South Asia would face: how to balance central and provincial power, how to protect minorities, how to address caste and class, and how to integrate territories ruled by princes.

These matters shaped what followed. The ministries gave Congress administrative experience that it would draw on after 1947, and the arguments about planning anticipated the development policies of the Nehruvian period. The reorganization of the League, the controversies over representation, and the unresolved federal question shaped the negotiations of 1945 to 1947. Yet students should hold on to the contingency of these years. The people living through them did not know how events would unfold, and several outcomes remained possible.

In the context of the six-volume series, this chapter closes the story of nationalism before the war and prepares the transition to the wartime politics of Chapters 83 and 84 and the final negotiations of Chapters 85 to 87. It also looks forward, since many of the debates examined here, over federalism, minority rights, secularism, and social equality, reappear in the constitution-making of Chapter 88 and in the politics of the later volume.
