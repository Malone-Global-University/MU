Part XVIII — Liberalization and Contemporary South Asia, 1991–2026

Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives in the Twenty-First Century

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: November 2026

Chapter Opening

On 12 March 2026, Nepal's Election Commission announced results that few observers would have predicted two years earlier. The Rastriya Swatantra Party, founded in 2022, had won a parliamentary majority larger than any in the country's democratic history, and its prime ministerial candidate, the 35-year-old former mayor of Kathmandu Balendra Shah, had defeated the four-time prime minister K. P. Sharma Oli in his own constituency. Six months earlier, young Nepalis had marched on the capital after a ban on social media platforms, the Parliament building had burned, and Oli had resigned. The older parties that had governed since the republic's creation, the Nepali Congress and the Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist–Leninist), were left with a small fraction of the seats.

That election is the latest point reached in this chapter's account of three small South Asian states that moved, over three decades, along strikingly different paths. In Nepal, a monarchy that had survived the end of Rana rule and the restoration of multiparty politics in 1990 was brought down between 1996 and 2008 by a Maoist insurgency, a palace crisis, and a mass movement, and was replaced by a federal republic whose institutions have since been tested repeatedly. In Bhutan, the monarchy itself initiated a transition to parliamentary democracy, producing a written constitution in 2008 without revolution and without a mass demand from below. In the Maldives, a presidential system dominated for thirty years by Maumoon Abdul Gayoom was opened to multiparty competition in 2005 and 2008, only to enter a period of disputed transfers of power, judicial confrontations, and recurrent struggles between presidents, parliaments, courts, and opposition movements.

These three trajectories deserve comparison because they were not simply variations on a single script. The Himalayan kingdoms and the Indian Ocean archipelago share some features, notably small populations, close dependence on India, and exposure to Chinese and other external interest. But their political societies differ profoundly. Nepal is a diverse and densely settled state of roughly thirty million people, divided by caste, ethnicity, language, and region, whose political life has been shaped by the exclusion and later mobilization of groups such as the Madhesis, Janajatis, Dalits, and Tharus. Bhutan, with a population of under a million, is built around a monarchy whose legitimacy rests on the dynasty's role in unifying and modernizing the state and on a distinctive national philosophy, Gross National Happiness. The Maldives, whose roughly half-million citizens are almost entirely Sunni Muslim and Dhivehi-speaking, live scattered across hundreds of tiny inhabited and resort islands and conduct a fiercely competitive, personality-centered politics around a tourism-dependent economy.

This chapter treats the three countries as political societies in their own right, not as buffers, pawns, or backdrops to Indian and Chinese strategy. Citizens, parties, courts, activists, monarchs, insurgents, and voters appear throughout as historical actors who made choices under constraints. Chapter 91 introduced the early postcolonial paths of these states, including the end of Rana rule in Nepal, Bhutan's managed modernization, and the Maldives' independence. Chapter 96 followed Nepal's Panchayat system and the position of Bhutan and the Maldives to 1991. The present chapter resumes the story in the mid-1990s and carries it to November 2026, the research cutoff for this textbook edition.

Historians face unusual evidentiary challenges here because the events are so recent. Official documents such as the 2006 Comprehensive Peace Agreement and the 2008 and 2015 constitutions can be read directly, as can election results published by electoral commissions. But the interpretation of recent events relies on journalism, reports by international organizations and human rights bodies, think-tank analysis, and early scholarship. The sections on 2025 and 2026 are especially provisional: they describe developments whose consequences remain unfolding, and the chapter distinguishes what is established from what is still contested or unknown.

By its close, students should understand how these three states arrived at the political arrangements they had in 2026, what distinguished a revolution from below from a reform from above and from a contested opening of electoral politics, and how populations, not only elites, shaped each outcome. Questions of regional geopolitics, economic development, and environmental change, which these states also face, belong principally to Chapters 105, 106, 109, and 110 and appear here only where they help explain domestic political history.

Historical Context

The three states entered the 1990s with very different institutions. Nepal had never been formally colonized, and its modern state was built from the conquest-based kingdom unified by the Shah dynasty in the later eighteenth century and ruled by hereditary Rana prime ministers from 1846 to 1951. After the 1950–51 revolution against the Ranas, a succession of experiments in parliamentary and monarchical rule followed. King Mahendra's 1960 coup inaugurated the partyless Panchayat system, examined in Chapter 96. The 1990 People's Movement (Jana Andolan I) forced King Birendra to accept a constitutional monarchy and multiparty democracy. The 1990 Constitution kept Nepal a Hindu kingdom, vested important powers in the palace and the Royal Nepal Army, and defined a unitary state whose official language and cultural norms favored the hill-based, Nepali-speaking elite. Many groups, including Janajatis (indigenous nationalities), Madhesis of the southern plains, Dalits, and women, found the new democracy only partly inclusive.

Bhutan's history differed sharply. Unified in the seventeenth century by the Tibetan Buddhist lama Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal, it was governed through a combination of monastic and secular authority until the Wangchuck dynasty established hereditary monarchy in 1907. Bhutan was never colonized by Britain, though British India shaped its external relations, and in 1949 an India–Bhutan treaty confirmed a close relationship. From the 1950s the monarchy pursued a cautious modernization, with planned development from 1961, a National Assembly from 1953, and tightly managed contact with the outside world. The Fourth King, Jigme Singye Wangchuck (reigned 1972–2006), gave Gross National Happiness its public prominence. Bhutan's southern population of Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa and the monarchy's 1980s and early 1990s cultural policies produced the crisis discussed in §104.12.

The Maldives, a chain of coral atolls in the Indian Ocean southwest of India and Sri Lanka, was a Muslim sultanate that became a British protectorate and then, in 1965, an independent state. A republic was declared in 1968, and from 1978 Maumoon Abdul Gayoom, confirmed through single-candidate referendums, dominated the presidency for thirty years. The archipelago's economy was transformed from fishing to tourism after the first resort opened in 1972, producing a prosperous elite in the capital, Malé, and large disparities with outer islands. Islam is the constitutional religion, and citizenship is legally limited to Muslims, a feature that shapes political debate.

Geography and regional position frame all three. Nepal and Bhutan are landlocked Himalayan states whose trade and transit routes run overwhelmingly through India, with Tibet to the north offering mountain barriers more than trade corridors. The Maldives lies astride Indian Ocean shipping lanes, with a tiny land area and extreme vulnerability to sea-level rise. All three depend on India's economic weight, and all three have received growing Chinese diplomatic and financial attention in the twenty-first century. This chapter treats such relationships as part of each country's domestic politics, where choices about foreign partners became arguments among parties and publics.

Section 104.1

The Nepalese Maoist Insurgency and Its Origins

On 13 February 1996, small armed groups attacked police posts in Rolpa and Rukum in the western hills and in Sindhuli farther east, as well as an agricultural development bank branch in Gorkha, announcing the "People's War" of the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist). The attacks followed a 40-point list of demands submitted to the government a few days earlier. The demands combined nationalist grievances, such as revision of the 1950 treaty with India, with calls for a republic, a secular state, language and ethnic equality, land reform, and an end to caste discrimination. The insurgency's leaders, Pushpa Kamal Dahal, known as Prachanda, and Baburam Bhattarai, were Nepali communists who had passed through the left's divisions after 1990 and concluded that parliamentary democracy would not deliver social transformation.

The insurgency did not arise from poverty alone. Nepal in the 1990s was among the poorest countries in Asia, but many poor regions did not produce insurgency. Historians and political scientists point to a combination of conditions. Rural livelihoods in the mid-western hills were precarious, with little irrigated land, limited roads, and heavy dependence on seasonal labor migration to India. State presence was thin and, where it appeared, often took the form of police and moneylenders. The post-1990 parties were dominated by upper-caste hill men concentrated in Kathmandu, and local elites often controlled both party and administration. The 1990 Constitution declared Nepal a Hindu kingdom with Nepali as the sole national language, an arrangement that many Janajati, Madhesi, Dalit, and women activists felt ignored their identities. Some of the earliest Maoist strongholds, such as Rolpa and Rukum, were areas with substantial Magar populations, and the movement drew on ethnic as well as class grievance.

Political failures between 1990 and 1996 deepened the break. Governments changed frequently, parties fractured, and corruption allegations accumulated. The United People's Front, the communist electoral front associated with the later Maoists, had won seats in 1991, but its attempts to press land and rights issues in parliament achieved little, and it was marginalized and then prohibited from contesting elections in the mid-1990s. Police operations such as the 1995 Operation Romeo in Rolpa, which involved arrests and abuses, are often cited by the Maoists and by scholars as pushing the movement underground. Evidence on these events comes largely from human rights reports, party documents, and later testimony, and each source has limits: Maoist documents are propaganda as well as record, and state sources minimized abuses.

In its first phase the insurgency was a modest guerrilla campaign, and the government treated it as a police matter. Maoist forces built "base areas" in the hills, established parallel village administrations, ran their own courts, and recruited many young people, including large numbers of women and members of Dalit and indigenous communities. Their ideology, drawing on Mao Zedong and on Peru's Shining Path, was dogmatic, but their practical message addressed local concerns: land seizures, abolition of debts, campaigns against alcohol and domestic violence, and promises of dignity for marginalized groups. Their methods also included extortion, forced recruitment of children, intimidation, and killings of suspected informants, as later investigations documented. Understanding the war requires holding both sides of this record: it expressed real grievances and it inflicted real violence on the villagers it claimed to represent.

Section 104.2

Civil War, Royal Crisis, and the Escalation of Conflict

For its first five years the conflict was fought chiefly by the police. This changed after a shock at the center of the state. On the night of 1 June 2001, King Birendra, Queen Aishwarya, and most members of the royal family were shot dead at Narayanhiti Palace in Kathmandu. An official inquiry concluded that Crown Prince Dipendra, who then fatally shot himself, was the perpetrator, and this remains the official account. Many Nepalis found the explanation unsatisfying, and conspiracy theories, some implicating Birendra's brother Gyanendra, who was absent, circulated widely. No evidence has established such claims, but the suspicion damaged the new king's standing. Gyanendra, a businessman and former political figure with little public affection, took the throne.

The massacre coincided with a diplomatic process. A ceasefire and peace talks began in mid-2001 under Prime Minister Sher Bahadur Deuba, but they collapsed in November. The Maoists attacked army posts, and the government declared a state of emergency and deployed the Royal Nepal Army, which had until then stayed out of the conflict. The army, nominally under the king's command, was thus drawn into a war that expanded its power and its political importance. The United States and India, concerned by Maoist advances, increased military assistance, and the Maoists were designated a terrorist organization by the United States.

Between 2001 and 2005 the war intensified. Both the army and the Maoists were accused of grave violations. Estimates of total deaths over the decade are commonly given as around 17,000, with some sources citing slightly lower figures, and more than a thousand people remained disappeared in later official counts. The Maoists carried out bombings, abductions, and killings, and imposed strikes (bandhs) and blockades on districts and on the capital. Security forces were responsible for extrajudicial killings and disappearances, including the mass detentions and enforced disappearances documented at army barracks such as Maharajgunj in Kathmandu and Bhairabnath battalion headquarters. Civilians in the hills bore the greatest burden, and tens of thousands migrated to cities and to India to escape both sides. Education, health services, and local government in many districts collapsed.

King Gyanendra used the crisis to expand royal authority. In October 2002 he dismissed Deuba's government for failing to hold elections and appointed successive prime ministers of his own choosing, claiming that the parties had failed. On 1 February 2005 he dismissed the government entirely, declared a state of emergency, imprisoned politicians, restricted the press, and assumed direct executive power, promising to defeat the Maoists within three years. The coup reversed the logic of the political situation. Before 2005 the parties and the king shared an interest in defeating the insurgents. Afterward the parties found that their principal adversary was a king who had suspended parliamentary democracy, and the Maoists saw an opening to turn the parties from allies of the palace into allies against it. The Maoists also had reason to seek political settlement: by 2005 the army had stalled their military expansion, and their leaders recognized that a decisive military victory was unlikely.

Section 104.3

The People's Movement of 2006 and the Comprehensive Peace Agreement

The political basis for the settlement was an unusual agreement. In November 2005, the Seven Party Alliance (SPA), a coalition of the main parliamentary parties led by the Nepali Congress and the CPN-UML, and the Maoists signed a 12-point understanding in Delhi. The Maoists committed to multiparty competitive politics, civil liberties, and press freedom, and the SPA accepted a Constituent Assembly election to write a new constitution. India's role in hosting the talks was significant and controversial, but the agreement's content reflected a genuine convergence of domestic interests. Its logic was that the parties and Maoists would jointly oppose the monarchy's direct rule and bring the Maoists into a negotiated transition.

In April 2006 the alliance mobilized a mass movement, sometimes called Jana Andolan II or the Loktantra Andolan. Beginning on 6 April, with a general strike called by the SPA and supported by Maoist cadres and civil society organizations, protesters filled the streets of Kathmandu and other cities despite curfews and live fire. Lawyers, teachers, doctors, journalists, trade unionists, and ordinary residents joined, and demonstrations spread to district headquarters. At least twenty people were killed and thousands injured during the nineteen days. On 21 April the king offered the parties the prime ministership, which they rejected; on 24 April he restored the dissolved House of Representatives. Girija Prasad Koirala became prime minister, and in the following weeks parliament stripped the king of command of the army and of his legal immunity, declared Nepal a secular state, and removed the king's role in the rules of succession.

Negotiations between the government and the Maoists led to a ceasefire and, on 21 November 2006, the signing of the Comprehensive Peace Agreement (CPA) by Koirala and Prachanda. The agreement ended the war, provided for the cantonment of Maoist combatants and the confinement of the army to barracks under United Nations monitoring through the UN Mission in Nepal (UNMIN), committed the parties to election of a Constituent Assembly, and promised commissions on truth and reconciliation and on the disappeared. An Interim Constitution followed in January 2007, and the Maoists entered an interim legislature and government in 2007. The agreement was, in effect, a settlement among elites, and its architects did not include other mobilized groups such as the Madhesis, who rose in the Madhes Andolan of January–February 2007 to demand federal autonomy and proportional representation, and who forced the interim government to promise federalism.

Historians discuss the CPA as both a success and a limited bargain. It ended a conflict that had killed thousands and did so with relatively little renewed violence. It integrated insurgents into parliamentary politics, an outcome that many comparable conflicts did not achieve. But its transitional justice provisions went largely unimplemented for years, and the questions of how to integrate the Maoist combatants into the army and how to address wartime atrocities became major obstacles in the following decade. Section 104.4 and the Primary Source Window accompanying this discussion examine how the agreement shaped the republic.

Historical Vocabulary
Jana Andolan (People's Movement)

Jana Andolan is the Nepali term for the mass movements of 1990 and 2006. The first forced the Panchayat monarchy to accept multiparty democracy; the second, sometimes called the Loktantra Andolan ("democracy movement"), forced King Gyanendra to restore parliament and opened the road to the republic. In both cases coalitions of parties, professionals, students, and civil society used strikes and street demonstrations as their principal methods. The term should not be confused with the Maoist "People's War" (Jana Yuddha), an armed insurgency, nor with the youth protests of 2025, which unfolded largely outside party organization.

The distinction matters because Nepal's modern politics reflect the repeated claim that legitimacy comes from mobilized publics as well as from votes.

Paraphrase of key provisions (not a quotation): The parties declared an end to armed conflict and committed themselves to multiparty democratic competition, fundamental rights, and press freedom. Maoist combatants were to be confined to designated cantonments and the army to its barracks under monitoring. The agreement provided for the election of a Constituent Assembly and promised commissions to investigate disappearances and to pursue truth and reconciliation.

Analysis: The document shows how a revolutionary movement and parliamentary parties defined a shared program: peace in return for a constitutional process that could remake the state. It reveals the priorities of its signatories, namely security arrangements and the Assembly, and it is less detailed on how transitional justice would operate, a gap visible in later years. It does not directly represent the voices of conflict victims, of Madhesi activists whose large-scale 2007 mobilization came later, or of the army, which was a central actor in practice but not an independent signatory.

Section 104.4

The Abolition of Monarchy and the Birth of the Republic

The election for the first Constituent Assembly, delayed twice, took place on 10 April 2008 under a mixed system that combined first-past-the-post constituencies with proportional representation and nominated seats, in order to guarantee representation for women and marginalized groups. The result astonished many observers, including the Maoists' own opponents. The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) won about a third of the 601 seats and finished far ahead of the Nepali Congress and the CPN-UML, while Madhesi parties performed strongly in the southern plains. Voters, it appeared, sought change and credited the Maoists with having forced the political transformation, even as many had suffered from their violence. International observers judged the election broadly credible despite intimidation in places.

At its first meeting on 28 May 2008, the Assembly voted overwhelmingly to abolish the monarchy and declare Nepal a federal democratic republic. Gyanendra left Narayanhiti Palace on 11 June, and the building was later turned into a museum. The end of a dynasty that had ruled since 1768 happened with little violence, a fact historians often highlight as a notable outcome. Ram Baran Yadav, a Madhesi from the Nepali Congress, was elected the republic's first president in July 2008, and Prachanda became prime minister in August, heading a coalition government.

The republic's first years showed how hard it was to turn a revolutionary settlement into functioning institutions. The most acute issue was the army. The Maoists wanted their roughly 19,000 verified combatants integrated into the Nepal Army, while the army and the Nepali Congress opposed mass integration. In May 2009 Prachanda attempted to dismiss the army chief, General Rookmangud Katawal; President Yadav reversed the order and Prachanda resigned. The following years saw rapid changes of government, a prolonged negotiation over the fate of the combatants, and a gradual integration of a limited number into the army and the payment of settlements to others. UNMIN's mandate ended in January 2011.

Equally difficult was writing a constitution. The Assembly's two-year mandate was extended repeatedly, but disputes over federalism, the form of government, and the electoral system prevented agreement. In May 2012 the first Constituent Assembly was dissolved without a constitution, and a second was elected in November 2013. In that election the Maoist vote collapsed, a result commonly attributed to public disappointment with the party and its factional splits, while the Nepali Congress and the CPN-UML led. The first Assembly's failure was a warning that the revolutionary promise of 2006 would not be redeemed without compromise among parties that had very different interests and constituencies. Cause and Effect

The End of the Monarchy in Nepal

Long-term causes

Exclusion of many groups under the 1990 order, rural poverty, weak state presence in the hills, and the monarchy's reliance on the army.

Short-term causes

The 2001 massacre, the failure of royal efforts to defeat the insurgency, the 2005 coup, and the 12-point understanding.

Event

The People's Movement of April 2006, the restoration of parliament, and the Constituent Assembly's abolition of monarchy in May 2008.

Immediate effects

The end of the war, the Maoists' entry into politics, and the beginning of a long constitutional process.

Long-term consequences

A federal republic, unresolved transitional justice, recurrent instability, and eventually the 2025 uprising.

Historical complexity

No single cause suffices: the insurgency, the palace's mistakes, party strategy, mass protest, and international context combined.
Historical Debate

Was Nepal's 2006–2008 Transformation a Revolution?

The question: Did the end of the monarchy and the creation of a federal republic constitute a fundamental social and political revolution, or a negotiated reorganization of elite politics?

Interpretation A — A genuine revolution: The abolition of a 240-year-old monarchy, the entry of insurgents into government, the declaration of secularism and federalism, and the mandated inclusion of marginalized groups represented profound changes in the structure and symbols of the state. Supporters point to the new prominence of Dalit, Janajati, Madhesi, and women's representation as evidence of real change.
Interpretation B — A limited transition: The same party leaders and social groups continued to dominate, land and economic structures changed little, transitional justice was neglected, and the older parties rotated in office. On this view the settlement was a pact that satisfied elites and left grievances, which resurfaced in Madhesi protests and eventually in the 2025 uprising.

Evidence: Both views use the same material: election results, the Constitution's provisions, data on representation, land distribution, the failure of transitional justice, and the pattern of prime ministers. They differ in the weight they give to symbolic and institutional change as against socioeconomic continuity.

Why it matters: Interpretation affects how historians understand the 2025 uprising, as either the delayed fulfillment of an unfinished revolution or a rejection of a failed settlement.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence for each interpretation and explain which additional evidence, such as data on income or representation over time, would help decide between them.

Nepal: Federalism, Political Instability, and Generational Change, 2008–2026

Section 104.5

Constitution-Making and the Federal Democratic Republic

On 25 April 2015, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck central Nepal, killing nearly 9,000 people and damaging hundreds of thousands of homes. The disaster created an atmosphere of urgency and nationwide solidarity, and leading parties used it to conclude a constitutional bargain that had eluded them for seven years. In June 2015 the Nepali Congress, CPN-UML, and the Maoists, together with a Madhesi party, signed a 16-point agreement that proposed a federal structure of eight provinces. A rushed process followed, with the final draft reducing the number to seven and drawing boundaries that cut across Madhesi and Tharu homelands. The Constitution was promulgated on 20 September 2015 by an Assembly in which the great majority of members voted in favor, but with Madhesi and some Janajati and Tharu representatives opposed or absent.

The Constitution defined Nepal as an independent, indivisible, sovereign, secular, inclusive, democratic, socialism-oriented, federal democratic republic. It established a bicameral federal parliament, with a House of Representatives of 275 members, of whom 165 are elected from constituencies and 110 by proportional representation, and a National Assembly representing the provinces. It established seven provinces, each with an assembly and government, and a constitutional framework for local self-government. The present 753 local units (municipalities and rural municipalities) were delineated during the 2017 implementation of federalism and gained substantial responsibilities for local services and budgets. It contained a long list of fundamental rights, including social and economic rights, and mandated inclusion of women, Dalits, indigenous nationalities, Madhesis, Tharus, Muslims, and other groups in state bodies. The mixed electoral system was intended to give small parties and minorities a voice while making single-party majorities difficult.

Several provisions were contested from the start. "Secularism" was defined by the constitution to mean protection of the religion and culture inherited from ancient times, a wording that critics argued left room for continued Hindu primacy, and campaigns for restoring the Hindu state and the monarchy gained occasional visibility. Citizenship rules, which allowed citizenship by descent through a Nepali father or mother but imposed restrictions on children of single mothers and on foreign spouses, were criticized as discriminatory by women's groups and by Madhesi activists who saw them as barriers for Madhesis with cross-border marriage ties. The distribution of seats and the boundaries of provinces, in particular the splitting of the southern plains among several provinces, were seen by many Madhesis as designed to dilute their political weight.

The promulgation was followed by an unofficial Indian blockade of border crossings from September 2015 to February 2016, which created severe fuel and medicine shortages. India denied imposing a blockade and attributed the disruption to the Madhesi protests along the border, while many Nepalis saw it as intervention. The episode, discussed in §104.18, strengthened Nepali nationalism and encouraged Nepal to diversify its transit arrangements. Domestically, the constitution became the framework around which political competition was arranged, and the first elections under it, in late 2017, brought a left alliance of the CPN-UML and Maoist Centre to power, with Oli as prime minister from February 2018.

Section 104.6

Federalism, Inclusion, and Constitutional Disputes

The 2015 Constitution made federalism and inclusion central to Nepal's politics, and the decade after its adoption was shaped by struggles over how far those principles would be realized. The Madhesi movement, which had begun in 2007 as a demand for recognition of the southern plains' identity, language, and culture and for an end to the perception of Madhesis as outsiders, remained the most prominent. Madhesis, who are a diverse group including caste Hindus, Dalits, Muslims, and indigenous communities, make up roughly a fifth to a third of the population depending on how the category is defined, a definitional uncertainty that itself shows how contested identity is. In the protests of 2015–16, around fifty people died, including police officers, in clashes in Tarai towns. An initial constitutional amendment adopted in January 2016 adjusted provisions on electoral constituencies and proportional inclusion. A proposed second amendment intended to address further Madhesi demands failed to secure the required two-thirds majority in Parliament in August 2017. Madhesi parties continued to argue that the central issues of citizenship and provincial boundaries remained unresolved.

Other groups mobilized around different issues. Tharu activists, who live in the western plains and were subject to bonded labor (the kamaiya system) until its abolition in 2000, protested the division of their homeland among provinces, and the Tikapur violence of August 2015, in which police officers and a child were killed, left lasting resentment. Janajati organizations, representing indigenous nationalities who are roughly a third of the population by official census categories, demanded ethnic-based federalism and language rights and criticized provincial borders as designed by hill high-caste elites. Dalits, who constitute about 13 percent of the population and who suffer discrimination despite a legal ban on untouchability since 1963 and a stronger caste discrimination law in 2011, pressed for implementation of reserved seats and for protection against violence. Women's groups campaigned on citizenship and on political representation.

Federalism's implementation proved halting. Provincial and local governments were created, with elections in 2017 and 2022, and local governments gained real responsibilities for schools, health posts, roads, and local planning. Many citizens experienced the new local governments as more accessible than the old centralized state. But the federal government was slow to pass the laws devolving powers, provincial governments struggled with limited revenues and unclear mandates, and the political class tended to treat provinces as an additional layer of patronage. Critics argued that federalism multiplied costs and officeholders, while supporters pointed out that the system was still young.

Constitutional politics also revolved around the 2020 and 2021 dissolutions of the House of Representatives by Prime Minister Oli, which the Supreme Court reversed in 2021, restoring the House and ordering the appointment of Sher Bahadur Deuba as prime minister. The episodes demonstrated that the judiciary could act as a check on executive overreach, while also exposing the unresolved tensions inside the Nepal Communist Party, formed by the 2018 merger of the UML and Maoist Centre and annulled by the Supreme Court in 2021 on registration grounds. Alongside these disputes, transitional justice stagnated. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission and the Commission on Enforced Disappearances, established in 2015, received tens of thousands of complaints but produced few prosecutions, and the Supreme Court and international bodies repeatedly criticized the failure to address wartime crimes. The unfinished business of the war thus remained part of the constitutional landscape.

Section 104.7

Coalition Instability, Corruption, and the Crisis of Governance

The constitutional design intended to produce inclusive and stable government instead produced remarkable turnover. Between the promulgation of the Constitution in 2015 and the end of 2024, Nepal had more than half a dozen prime ministers, with Prachanda, Oli, and Sher Bahadur Deuba trading the office among themselves. The main exception to this pattern was the 2017–2021 period, when the left alliance and then the unified Communist Party held a strong majority. Even this ended in dissolution fights and a split. After the 2022 election, in which no party won a majority, Prachanda became prime minister with an unlikely coalition, abandoned it, formed another, and was replaced in July 2024 by Oli at the head of a coalition supported by the Nepali Congress. The same three older leaders thus rotated in and out, and politics seemed to many citizens a closed circle of bargaining among men who had held power since the 1990s.

Alongside the instability, public trust fell. Corruption scandals, including a Bhutanese refugee resettlement fraud case involving senior politicians, a cooperative savings fraud, and allegations of misuse of aviation and procurement funds, fed the belief that the political class used office for private advantage. Several of these cases involved prosecutions and arrests, and others stalled. The perception was reinforced by evidence of inequality in access to state power and by social media content in 2025 that contrasted the lifestyles of politicians' children with the struggles of ordinary youth, a theme that took the name "nepo kids."

Economic conditions underlay this discontent. Nepal's economy grew moderately but created too few jobs at home, and migration became an essential livelihood. Hundreds of thousands of Nepalis, mostly young men, left each year for work in the Gulf states, Malaysia, India, South Korea, and elsewhere, while students sought education abroad. Remittances reached roughly a quarter or more of GDP in recent years, among the highest ratios in the world, sustaining households and the balance of payments but also revealing the limits of domestic opportunity. Migrant workers' conditions, including deaths in construction and recruitment fees, were recurring public concerns. In the countryside, the migration of men left villages with older residents and women, and remittance-funded consumption outpaced productive investment. The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020–2021 damaged tourism and employment and strengthened awareness of fragility.

Politics also changed in form. New parties appeared that presented themselves as alternatives to the older leadership. The Rastriya Swatantra Party, founded in 2022 by the television host Rabi Lamichhane, won 20 seats in that year's election, and independents such as Balendra Shah, the rapper and structural engineer elected mayor of Kathmandu in 2022, gained devoted followings among younger voters. Their appeal rested on anti-corruption messages, direct use of social media, and refusal of the established parties' bargaining. These developments set the stage for the events of 2025.

Section 104.8

The Youth Uprising of 2025 and Nepal's Political Realignment in 2026

On 4 September 2025, the government ordered the shutdown of 26 social media platforms, among them Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, YouTube, and X, for failing to register with the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology under newly enforced rules. The ban touched a population in which half the people are under 25 and for which social media is a central channel of news, business, family communication with migrant relatives, and political expression. In the preceding weeks young Nepalis had been circulating images of the luxury lifestyles of politicians' children, and the ban appeared to many as an effort to silence criticism. Organizations such as Hami Nepal and informal networks on platforms including Discord and Instagram called for a protest on 8 September at Maitighar Mandala in Kathmandu.

On 8 September thousands of mostly young protesters, many in school uniform, gathered and moved toward the Parliament. Police fired on them, and at least nineteen people were killed that day. Late that evening the government lifted the ban and the home minister resigned, but the killings changed the nature of the protest. On 9 September crowds set fire to Parliament, the Supreme Court, the Singha Durbar government complex, political party offices, and the homes of leading politicians, including Oli's and former prime ministers'. Oli resigned that day, and President Ramchandra Paudel also faced demands to quit. The army took control of security and imposed a curfew. Human Rights Watch reported at least 76 deaths during the violence of 8–9 September, with more than two thousand people injured in accounts published afterward. Casualty totals and responsibility for individual deaths remained subjects of official inquiry and further reporting. Organizers of the original protest stated that arson and looting were committed by infiltrators, a claim that remains disputed.

The uprising had no formal leadership. After Oli's resignation, the army invited representatives of the youth movement to talks, and an online poll among groups of activists favored the former Chief Justice Sushila Karki, known for her anti-corruption record, as interim leader. On 12 September she was sworn in as the first female prime minister of Nepal. President Paudel dissolved the House of Representatives at her request and scheduled elections for 5 March 2026. The legality of the dissolution was contested because the Constitution did not clearly provide for this route to an interim administration. Eight parties represented in the dissolved House denounced the decision as unconstitutional in September 2025; parties later made differing choices about contesting the March election and pursuing legal challenges. The transition therefore brought an elected government to office in 2026 without resolving every dispute over the interim constitutional process.

The election of 5 March 2026 was the first since the uprising. According to official results announced on 12 March, the Rastriya Swatantra Party won 182 of the 275 seats, a larger majority than any party had held, while the Nepali Congress won 38 and the CPN-UML 25. Prachanda's party held only a handful of seats, and Balendra Shah beat Oli in Jhapa-5 by a very large margin. Balendra Shah was sworn in as prime minister on 27 March. Commentators highlighted several factors: a generational shift, rejection of the older parties, the appeal of Shah and Lamichhane, and the party's strength among Madhesi voters, since Shah is widely noted to come from a family of Madhesi background. The vote was cast within the existing constitutional system and challenged its established parties, not its framework.

How far this realignment represents political renewal remains uncertain, and this textbook, written in November 2026, treats it as an unfinished story. Several limits are evident. The uprising was triggered by anger at corruption, but its demands were diffuse, and the new government faces the structural problems of a narrow economy, dependence on remittances, and an inherited state apparatus. A single-party majority under a system designed to prevent one may concentrate power in a new leadership with little experience of government and a style that critics say is personalized. Questions of accountability for the September killings, of transitional justice for the civil war, of inclusion of Madhesis, Janajatis, Dalits, and women, and of federalism's future all remain open. Historians will need to wait for documents, investigations, and the record of the government's performance before judging whether 2025–26 was a transformation or a change of cast.

Bhutan: Constitutional Monarchy, Democratic Transition, and Development

Section 104.9

Bhutan's Monarchy and the Democratic Transition of 2008

In 2001 King Jigme Singye Wangchuck appointed a drafting committee to prepare a written constitution for Bhutan. The decision was unusual: the monarchy was neither threatened by a mass movement nor pressed by an insurgency, and many Bhutanese, who respected the king, did not seek parliamentary democracy and were at first doubtful. The king traveled widely to explain the plan, and the draft was published in 2005 and discussed in meetings in every district. Officials and scholars offer several explanations of why the monarchy took this step: a conviction that institutions should outlast any individual ruler, awareness of regional democratization, concern about the quality of future kings, and a wish to secure the state's stability and independence in a changing environment. These explanations come mainly from royal statements and later interviews, and historians treat them as informative but not conclusive about motive.

In December 2006 the Fourth King abdicated in favor of his son, Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck, who became the Fifth King and was formally crowned in November 2008. The first elections preceded the Constitution's formal adoption. A nonpartisan National Council, the upper house, was elected in 2007, and the first National Assembly election followed on 24 March 2008. The Druk Phuensum Tshogpa (DPT) won 45 of the 47 seats, an overwhelming victory that reflected its association with royal-era figures and the lack of experience of the opposition People's Democratic Party. Jigme Thinley, a veteran official, became prime minister. The Constitution was signed in July 2008.

The Constitution of the Kingdom of Bhutan creates a constitutional monarchy under the Druk Gyalpo (Dragon King), who is head of state and a "symbol of unity" and who holds important powers: he is commander-in-chief, appoints key officials on recommendation of constitutional bodies, grants citizenship, land grants, and pardons, and appoints the prime minister nominated by the party that wins an Assembly majority. It establishes a parliament with two houses, the National Council with 25 members and the National Assembly with 47, an independent judiciary, and bodies such as the Election Commission and the Royal Audit Authority. Unusually, it provides for the king's removal by a two-thirds vote of Parliament followed by a national referendum, and it requires the king to retire at the age of 65. It also commits the state to maintaining at least 60 percent of the country's land under forest cover and to promoting Gross National Happiness. The extent of royal influence, the loyalty of most citizens to the dynasty, and the role of royal prerogatives mean Bhutan's democracy is a partnership between a monarch with high moral authority and elected institutions.

The chapter's Primary Source Windows and Historical Debates compare Bhutan's monarchy-led transition with Nepal's revolution from below. A central point is that the Bhutanese monarchy, unlike the Nepalese, was able to remain at the center of the new order precisely because it had initiated the change and because it was widely seen as having delivered stability and development.

Section 104.10

Political Parties, Parliamentary Government, and Democratic Consolidation

Bhutan's democracy has operated through a distinctive electoral system. For the National Assembly, a primary round among all registered parties narrows the field to the two that receive the most votes, and a general round then decides constituency seats between those two. This design aims to prevent fragmentation and to produce a stable government, but it also limits choice. The National Council is elected on a nonpartisan basis, a deliberate decision to provide a deliberative chamber insulated from party politics. Candidates for the Assembly must hold a university degree, a requirement that excludes much of the population, especially in rural areas, and has often been criticized.

The elections that followed demonstrated alternation of power. In 2013 the People's Democratic Party, led by Tshering Tobgay, a former engineer and civil servant, won 32 seats against the DPT's 15, after the DPT government's last years were marked by a currency crisis and a dispute with India over fuel subsidies. Tobgay became prime minister. In 2018 a new party, Druk Nyamrup Tshogpa (DNT), led by the surgeon Lotay Tshering, won and formed the government, with the incumbent PDP eliminated in the primary round and the DNT defeating the DPT in the general round. In the 2023–24 election, the primary round of November 2023 selected the PDP and the Bhutan Tendrel Party, and the general round of 9 January 2024 gave the PDP 30 of 47 seats; Tobgay returned as prime minister later that month. Four elections thus produced three transfers of governing authority, a record that most observers view as evidence of democratic consolidation, with the caveat that parties are small, personality-oriented, and lack deep ideological differences.

The relationship between elected government and the throne is the system's central feature. The king chairs no cabinet and does not make day-to-day policy, but his visibility is great. He travels throughout the country, grants land (kidu) to poor families and provides welfare through the royal kidu system, and exercises influence on matters such as the design of major national projects. The most conspicuous example is the Gelephu Mindfulness City, announced by the king in his National Day address of December 2023 (§104.12), which has its own governing authority and is closely associated with the throne rather than with a particular ministry. Observers differ on how to interpret this. Some argue that it shows the monarchy using elected governments to carry out a royal vision, others that it reflects a pragmatic division in which the monarch handles long-term strategy. The question of how democratic accountability applies to royal initiatives is one that Bhutan's evolving system has not fully answered.

Civic space exists but is bounded. Bhutan has a growing private press, a Parliament with an active opposition, and public debate, particularly online. Yet defamation of the monarchy is treated with great seriousness, public criticism of the throne is rare, and there is no strong tradition of organized protest. Political participation is high in elections but modest in other forms. Voter turnout has varied from one election to the next, and its fluctuations have prompted debate about disenchantment. Whether Bhutanese democracy rests on deep institutional roots or on the personal authority of the dynasty is a question that only a longer period, including royal succession in the future, can test.

Section 104.11

Gross National Happiness, Hydropower, and Bhutan's Development Model

Bhutan's development philosophy has attracted global curiosity out of proportion to the country's size. Its origins are commonly traced to remarks by the Fourth King in the 1970s that Gross National Happiness was more important than Gross National Product. Over time the idea was formalized into a framework of four pillars (sustainable and equitable socioeconomic development, environmental conservation, cultural preservation and promotion, and good governance) and nine domains, including psychological wellbeing, health, education, time use, cultural diversity, community vitality, ecological resilience, living standards, and good governance. The Gross National Happiness Commission historically coordinated national planning and assessed policy through a GNH framework, while periodic national surveys produced a GNH Index. In October 2022, Bhutan dissolved the Commission in a wider civil-service reorganization, transferring planning responsibilities to the Cabinet Secretariat and Ministry of Finance and GNH research functions to the Centre for Bhutan and GNH Studies. The institutional change did not formally abandon Gross National Happiness as a national development principle. In 2011 the United Nations General Assembly adopted a Bhutan-sponsored resolution calling for attention to happiness and wellbeing in development.

Historians should distinguish between GNH as philosophy and its practical results. In practice, the state's development has been guided by five-year plans since 1961, each assigned shares of public investment across sectors. Education and health care are provided free of charge in the public system, and the Constitution commits the state to provision of both. Life expectancy rose substantially from the 1960s, literacy and school enrollment expanded, and Bhutan graduated from the United Nations list of least developed countries in December 2023. Environmental policy includes the constitutional forest cover requirement, large protected areas, and a policy that Bhutan has emphasized as being carbon negative. Cultural policy supports Buddhist institutions, the Dzongkha language, and national dress and architectural norms, while the tourism policy of "high value, low volume," with a daily Sustainable Development Fee levied on foreign visitors, tries to limit the cultural and environmental effects of tourism.

The economic base of this model is hydropower. Bhutan's steep, fast rivers offer large potential, and with Indian financing and technical support the country built the Chukha, Kurichhu, Tala, and Mangdechhu plants, and developed further projects at Punatsangchhu. Most of the electricity is exported to India under long-term agreements, and hydropower has accounted for a major share of government revenue and export earnings. The arrangement has given Bhutan fiscal resources to finance free public services, but it also produces dependence on a single sector and a single market. Cost overruns and geological difficulties delayed the Punatsangchhu projects, and fluctuations in tariffs and in river flow affect revenue. Debt related to hydropower is a significant share of public external debt, and much of it is owed to India.

Evaluating GNH requires care. Supporters stress that the framework has influenced policy, kept environmental and cultural questions prominent, and delivered measurable improvements in health and education. Critics argue that happiness is difficult to measure, that the GNH Index has been used selectively, and that the framework has sometimes been invoked to justify cultural policies that restricted minorities, a criticism connected to the experience of the Lhotshampa. A fair historical judgment recognizes both: GNH is a real element of the state's decision-making and identity, and it is also a political language that serves the state's image. The development model's test is whether it can provide employment and opportunity to a young, educated population, an issue taken up in §104.12.

Historical Vocabulary
Gross National Happiness

Gross National Happiness (GNH) is Bhutan's official development framework. It holds that national progress should be judged by a balance of material and nonmaterial wellbeing rather than by income alone, and it is organized into four pillars and nine domains. It is not simply a measure of how cheerful citizens are, and it is not the same as the Western psychological study of happiness. Historians treat it both as a genuine instrument of policy, used in screening plans, and as a language of national identity and diplomacy. Modern equivalents such as "wellbeing economics" capture part of the idea but omit its Buddhist and cultural dimensions.

Section 104.12

Bhutan in the 2020s: Youth Migration, Citizenship, and Economic Transformation

The most pressing political issue in Bhutan in the 2020s has been outward migration. Official and press accounts report that tens of thousands of young and skilled Bhutanese, including many nurses, teachers, and civil servants, have left in recent years, especially for Australia, which has become a main destination for study and work. Estimates vary, with some reports citing figures between 60,000 and 70,000 departures over decades, a large proportion in a country of roughly 780,000, and with the Bhutanese diaspora in Australia reported to have more than doubled between 2020 and 2024. Youth unemployment has been reported at around 17 to 18 percent in the labor force surveys of 2025, and wages in the public sector, which has traditionally employed graduates, did not match overseas opportunities. Prime Minister Tobgay has called outmigration the most serious national challenge, and his government has presented it as an existential threat requiring higher growth and better pay at home.

The government's response has relied on three major strategies: the Gelephu Mindfulness City (GMC), the 13th Five-Year Plan (2024–2029), and a 21st Century Economic Roadmap. The GMC, announced by the king in December 2023 and designed with the participation of the Danish architect Bjarke Ingels, is planned as a special administrative region spanning more than 4,046 square kilometers in the southern lowlands according to its 2026 official description (earlier planning accounts referred to approximately 2,500 square kilometers), with its own legal framework, an international airport, hydropower and green-industry investment, and a goal of attracting global capital and returning Bhutanese professionals. A national youth initiative, Pelsung, was launched in October 2025 to engage young people in the project. The project is explicitly a response to migration: it seeks to create high-value jobs inside Bhutan without abandoning Bhutanese cultural and environmental commitments.

The project also presents risks, and the chapter distinguishes intended benefits from uncertainties. Financing is a major question, since a city of this scale needs large investment, and the GMC has developed instruments such as development bonds and a gold-backed digital token, innovations that reflect Bhutan's use of cryptocurrency mining but also introduce financial and regulatory risk. Analysts, including international financial institutions, have cautioned that public finances, with substantial hydropower-related debt, leave limited room for contingent liabilities. The legal separateness of the GMC raises questions of oversight, transparency, and how the region will relate to national law and to elected institutions. Cultural questions arise about how a global financial and technology hub in the south can be reconciled with the preservation of national character. As of late 2026, construction and recruitment were continuing, and the experiment's outcomes cannot be known.

The question of citizenship and belonging remains a moral and historical issue. In the 1980s the monarchy enacted a Citizenship Act in 1985, followed by a 1988 census that reclassified many southern residents as non-nationals, and promoted a "One Nation, One People" cultural policy that required national dress and etiquette, known as Driglam Namzha, and removed Nepali from schools. Resistance among the Lhotshampa, who are of Nepali origin, was met by security-force action, and from about 1990 many people, eventually around 100,000, left or were expelled and lived for years in refugee camps in eastern Nepal. From 2007–08 a resettlement program, led by the United States, moved well over 100,000 of them to third countries. Bhutan has not allowed large-scale return, and bilateral talks with Nepal produced little. Many Lhotshampa who stayed describe discrimination in security clearances and access to services, and rights organizations have criticized the state's account, while the government has defended the measures as necessary to protect Bhutan's identity and sovereignty. The refugee crisis, little visible in official happiness narratives, is a necessary counterpoint in any assessment of the kingdom's political history.

Division IV · §§104.13–104.16

Maldives: Democratic Reform, Political Competition, and Institutional Conflict

Section 104.13

From Long-Standing Presidential Rule to Multiparty Democracy

Maumoon Abdul Gayoom, an Egyptian-trained Islamic scholar, became president of the Maldives in 1978 and held office for thirty years, winning six successive terms in referendums in which the People's Majlis nominated a single candidate and voters approved or rejected him. His government delivered real development, including tourism-driven growth, schools, health services, and communications, and it promoted a state Islam that tied national identity to Sunni Islam. It also limited opposition. Political parties were not legal, critics faced arrest, exile, or imprisonment, and the press was controlled. Reform activists organized from the 1990s, many in exile, and the Maldivian Dhivehi-language press and internet, though restricted, carried criticism.

Pressure for change sharpened after September 2003, when the death in custody of a young prisoner, Evan Naseem, led to riots in Malé and to calls for accountability for abuses in the prison system. The Maldivian Democratic Party (MDP) was founded that year by exiled and domestic activists, with Mohamed Nasheed, a journalist who had been jailed many times, as a central figure. On 12 August 2004, a demonstration in Malé was suppressed by security forces in what activists call Black Friday, followed by emergency rule. The December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed around eighty people in the Maldives and caused damage amounting to a large share of GDP, intensified debate about governance and relief. In June 2005 the Majlis legalized political parties, and the MDP was registered.

Gayoom, whose government accepted a reform agenda in 2004, oversaw a constitutional process that produced the Constitution of 2008, ratified by the President on 7 August 2008. The Constitution created a presidential republic with two-term limits, a fully elected Majlis, an independent judiciary, and a bill of rights that includes freedom of expression and assembly, while keeping Islam as the state religion and limiting citizenship to Muslims. It also established independent commissions, among them the Elections Commission, the Judicial Service Commission, the Human Rights Commission, and an Anti-Corruption Commission, intended to anchor accountability.

The first multiparty presidential election, held in October 2008, was a landmark. In the first round Gayoom received the most votes, but not a majority; in the runoff on 28 October, Nasheed, supported by an alliance of opposition parties, won about 54 percent, and was sworn in on 11 November 2008. Gayoom accepted defeat, an outcome that was celebrated internationally. Nasheed's government focused on constitutional rights, decentralization, environmental diplomacy, including a widely publicized underwater cabinet meeting in 2009 to draw attention to sea-level rise, and plans for carbon neutrality. But it also inherited a judiciary and security services shaped by the previous regime, a Majlis in which his party lacked a majority, and a fiscal deficit. The institutional conflicts that followed were not simply personal quarrels. They arose from the fact that a new constitution had been written for a country whose main institutions had been staffed under the old order.

Historical Vocabulary
People's Majlis and the Presidential System

The People's Majlis is the Maldives' parliament. The Maldives uses a presidential system in which the president is both head of state and head of government, elected directly, and limited by the 2008 Constitution to two terms. Because the president can be of a different party from the Majlis majority, and because courts decide electoral and constitutional disputes, the term "presidential" does not capture the importance of parliament and judiciary in shaping outcomes. Students should compare it with Nepal's parliamentary system and Bhutan's constitutional monarchy, where the head of government is a prime minister.

Section 104.14

Democratic Instability, Political Crisis, and Opposition Movements

In January 2012, the government ordered the arrest of the chief judge of the Criminal Court, Abdulla Mohamed, who had been releasing detained opposition figures and whom Nasheed accused of corruption and bias. The detention triggered weeks of protests led by opposition parties, including Gayoom's supporters, who invoked Islam and national independence, and by sections of the police. On 7 February 2012, after police and military personnel joined protesters and with a crowd gathering outside the military headquarters, Nasheed announced his resignation, and Vice President Mohamed Waheed Hassan Manik took office. Nasheed said soon afterward that he had been forced out at gunpoint in a coup; his opponents said the transfer was voluntary and lawful. A Commonwealth-backed Commission of National Inquiry in 2012 concluded that the transfer had been constitutional, a finding that Nasheed's supporters disputed. The episode left lasting divisions over the legitimacy of the 2012 government.

The 2013 election was a second crisis. In the first round on 7 September, Nasheed won the largest share of votes, but the Supreme Court annulled the result on the basis of a claim that the voter lists were flawed, and a rerun was delayed repeatedly by the court and the police. In the runoff on 16 November, Abdulla Yameen, Gayoom's half-brother, won about 51 percent against Nasheed. International observers had judged the first round broadly credible, and many criticized the court's intervention. The episode showed the judiciary as an arbiter of electoral outcomes, a role that became central to Maldivian politics.

Under Yameen (2013–2018), the government moved toward tight control. Nasheed was convicted in 2015 under anti-terrorism law for the arrest of the judge in 2012 in a trial criticized by the United Nations and by legal experts as flawed, and sentenced to thirteen years. Several leading opposition politicians, among them former ministers and a vice president, were prosecuted and jailed or driven into exile. In September 2015 an explosion on the president's speedboat set off an investigation that ended in the conviction of Vice President Ahmed Adeeb. Legislation on defamation and assembly restricted the press and protest, and journalists were harassed and one, Ahmed Rilwan, disappeared in 2014 and remains missing. Yameen also deepened economic and diplomatic ties with China and borrowed heavily for infrastructure, such as the China-financed bridge between Malé and Hulhulé island.

The crisis of February 2018 brought the contest to a head. After the Supreme Court ordered the release of imprisoned opposition politicians and the reinstatement of dismissed members of the Majlis, Yameen declared a state of emergency, had the chief justice and another judge arrested, and the court rescinded its order. The emergency lasted about six weeks. It showed how far the institutional conflict had gone, as the executive moved against the judiciary, and it prompted a coalition of opposition parties, including factions that had previously supported Yameen, to unite behind a single candidate, Ibrahim Mohamed Solih of the MDP. The history of 2012–2018 can be read as a period of democratic backsliding, and also as evidence of a resilient opposition and civil society that kept demanding competitive elections.

Section 104.15

Electoral Change from Ibrahim Mohamed Solih to Mohamed Muizzu

On 23 September 2018, in an election that many predicted Yameen would manipulate, Solih won around 58 percent of the vote on a high turnout. Yameen conceded, a decision that observers credited to pressure from the public, the security services, and external actors. Solih took office in November 2018. In April 2019, his party won a large majority in the Majlis elections. His government released political prisoners, restored some judicial independence, and pursued an "India First" foreign policy, accepting Indian credit lines and infrastructure projects, including the Greater Malé Connectivity Project. It also faced criticism for slow prosecutions of previous corruption and for coalition frictions, notably between Solih and the Speaker, Nasheed, which eventually split the MDP.

The 2023 presidential campaign centered on questions of national sovereignty and the cost of living. Muizzu, the mayor of Malé, stood for the People's National Congress (PNC) and its allies after the Supreme Court disqualified the imprisoned Yameen. Muizzu led the first round on 9 September and won the runoff on 30 September with about 54 percent against Solih. His campaign promised an "India Out" policy, a reference to the small number of Indian military personnel stationed to operate donated helicopters and a Dornier aircraft, and charged the Solih government with undermining national independence. India's government and many Maldivian analysts disputed that characterization. Whether foreign-policy appeals were the main reason for Muizzu's win or whether economic frustration and the opposition's unity weighed more is debated, with most analysis pointing to a combination.

Once in office in November 2023, Muizzu asked India to withdraw its military personnel by a specified date in 2024, which was done, replaced by Indian civilian technicians, and sought closer relations with China and Turkey, including a first state visit to China in January 2024. He then won a decisive victory in the Majlis election of 21 April 2024, in which the PNC took about 66 of the 93 seats (the chamber had been enlarged), reducing the MDP to a small minority and giving the president a supermajority. The campaign and the early months of the new parliament included parliamentary clashes over cabinet approvals, and the new majority acted to constrain the opposition and the judiciary.

The government's first years were dominated by the economy. Foreign exchange reserves fell to very low levels in 2023–24, bond payments loomed, and the country's credit ratings were downgraded. Indian assistance, including treasury bill rollovers and a currency swap, helped avoid default in 2024, and in 2025 Prime Minister Narendra Modi visited the Maldives for the 60th anniversary of independence, a visit that signaled a repair of ties. Muizzu's balancing of India and China thus moved from campaign slogans to pragmatic diplomacy. The sections that follow show that domestic politics, not only diplomacy, determined the limits of his power.

Section 104.16

Constitutional Reform, Opposition Politics, and Maldivian Democracy through 2026

The central constitutional question in the Maldives has been the balance among the president, the Majlis, and the courts. The 2008 Constitution gave a strong presidency checked by an independent judiciary and parliamentary oversight, but practice swung with the party strength of each moment: Nasheed faced an opposition Majlis and a hostile bench, Yameen subordinated the other institutions, Solih governed with a large majority but limited resources, and Muizzu, after 2024, held a parliamentary supermajority. The rules for judicial appointments and removals, for impeachment, and for the Elections Commission were therefore not neutral technicalities but central to power. In 2025 the Majlis restructured the Supreme Court, reducing the number of justices and removing some sitting judges, a measure that the government justified as judicial reform and that opposition members and legal professional bodies criticized as a means of bringing the court under executive influence. Legislation on media regulation in 2025 likewise provoked criticism from press freedom organizations.

The government's proposal in 2026 to hold presidential and parliamentary elections on the same day illustrated both the tools and the limits of executive power. The president argued that synchronization would save money and reduce the disruption of repeated campaigns. Opponents, including all former elected presidents, argued that it would strengthen the president's coattails, weaken parliament's independence, and make it harder for voters to split their votes. A constitutional amendment passed the Majlis, but under the Constitution it required approval in a national referendum, the first held under the 2008 Constitution. The referendum was held on 4 April 2026, alongside local council and women's development committee elections. The final referendum result showed 68.74 percent voting against the amendment, and the opposition MDP won the mayoral contests in all five cities. Muizzu said he accepted the results.

The vote, in the middle of the president's term, was widely read as a verdict on the government, since the economic hardship and the perception of rising executive dominance weighed heavily on the campaign. Civil society groups and the opposition, which was divided in other respects, joined in opposing the amendment, and observers noted that the result demonstrated an independent electorate. But the observation report by Transparency Maldives, an election-monitoring organization, also repeated familiar concerns about the use of state resources, partisan broadcasting, and vote-buying, while assessing the administration of the elections as competent. The referendum thus showed both the strength of voters and the structural advantages of incumbency.

Looking across the period from 2008 to 2026, Maldivian democracy appears neither a failure nor a consolidated success. Elections have repeatedly produced changes of president and defeats for incumbents, and voters have rejected overreach, including Yameen in 2018 and the referendum in 2026. At the same time, the institutions meant to arbitrate disputes, notably the courts and the electoral authorities, have often been drawn into the contest, and the Maldivian political system revolves around a few dominant personalities, among them Gayoom, Nasheed, Yameen, Solih, and Muizzu, rather than ideologically distinct parties. For citizens, the effects are visible in public trust, in the use of social media and the press, and in the extent to which the 2008 Constitution's promise of rights is realized on the outer islands as well as in Malé.

Section 104.17

Tourism, Debt, Island Development, and the Maldivian Economy

Economic questions in the Maldives are political questions, because the structure of the economy shapes who holds power and what citizens expect of presidents. Since the opening of the first resorts in the early 1970s, tourism has become the dominant sector, accounting directly for roughly a quarter of GDP and for far more when related services, taxes, and construction are counted. Arrivals reached about two million a year by 2024. The model, in which each resort occupies its own island leased by the state to private operators, created high government revenue from lease payments, taxes, and fees, and it separated tourists from most local communities, a separation originally intended to protect Islamic social norms. Fisheries, once the economic foundation, shrank in relative terms, and the country imports most of its food and fuel.

The concentration of people and services in Malé, and in the reclaimed island of Hulhumalé nearby, is the central feature of the country's internal geography. Greater Malé contains around two-fifths of the population on a tiny area, while the other inhabited islands, scattered across the atolls, have small populations and uneven access to secondary education, hospitals, and jobs. Successive governments promised decentralization, and the 2008 Constitution provided for island and atoll councils, but financial control stayed largely with the central government. Youth from outer islands migrate to Malé for education and work, where housing is expensive and crowded. Social problems that Maldivians and international observers frequently report, including drug use, gang activity, and radicalization, are linked to this inequality of opportunity, although evidence on their scale varies by source. The 2014 water crisis in Malé, when a fire at the desalination plant left residents without water for days and India and other countries sent supplies, demonstrated the vulnerability of the urban center.

Public borrowing financed infrastructure on a large scale, especially under Yameen, who borrowed from China and other lenders for the new airport runway, the China–Maldives Friendship Bridge, housing, and other projects, and under Solih, who borrowed from India and others. A US$500 million sukuk issued in 2021 and falling due in April 2026 became a symbol of the country's financial exposure. Public debt rose to well above 100 percent of GDP, and the World Bank, the IMF, and rating agencies repeatedly assessed the Maldives as at high risk of debt distress. The dollar shortage affected ordinary life, as banks restricted foreign-currency access and importers and residents faced difficulties.

The Muizzu government sought to cope through a mixture of domestic revenue measures, spending cuts, new tourism taxes, support from India including a currency swap and treasury bill rollovers, and other bilateral assistance. It repaid the sukuk in April 2026, using the Sovereign Development Fund and official reserves, and also settled the Indian currency swap that month. Rating agencies responded with a modest upgrade, and the finance ministry presented the repayment as proof of sound management. But reserves fell sharply after repayment, to under two months of imports on some measures, public debt remained around 120 to 130 percent of GDP, and a Middle East conflict in 2026 raised fuel costs and weakened tourism, according to the Asian Development Bank. International institutions continued to describe debt distress risk as high and called for credible fiscal consolidation. The state of the economy through 2026 is therefore best described as one of averted immediate default and continuing vulnerability. Climate change, discussed in Chapter 109, adds an existential long-term pressure that bears on all economic calculations.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives in Three Political Orders

For villagers in the mid-western hills during the civil war, daily life meant navigating the demands of both Maoist cadres and security forces, losing sons and daughters to recruitment or migration, and watching schools and clinics close. After the peace agreement many returned to farming and to seasonal labor, and for a generation of young people the next route out was migration to the Gulf or Malaysia, with remittances and long separations from families shaping household life.

In Bhutan, a family in a village in the central valleys experiences free schooling and clinics, the rituals of Buddhist festivals, and national dress rules, while relatives abroad send money from Australia. For southern Bhutanese families displaced in the 1990s, the same decades meant years in refugee camps and, often, resettlement in the United States or other countries.

In the Maldives, daily life divides sharply between Malé, where crowded apartments, high rents, and traffic of small vehicles on narrow streets set the rhythm, and outer islands where families depend on fishing, small shops, and transport by boat. Resort workers, who often commute or live on staff islands, form a distinct part of the labor force alongside many foreign workers. Evidence on these lives comes from censuses, surveys, journalism, and oral testimony, each with limits.

Section 104.18

Nepal and Bhutan between India and China

For both Himalayan states, the question of how to relate to their two giant neighbors is a persistent subject of domestic debate. Nepal's modern relationship with India was framed by the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, which provides open borders, free movement and employment rights for citizens of each country in the other, and special trade arrangements. The treaty has long been criticized by many Nepalis as unequal, and Nepal's parties, including the communists, have called for its revision. Around the Kalapani–Lipulekh–Limpiyadhura area on the western border, boundary disputes flared in 2019 and 2020 when India published a new political map and opened a road, and Nepal responded with a map and a constitutional amendment incorporating the territory. These episodes, like the 2015–16 blockade, strengthened nationalism and gave politicians an incentive to appear assertive. The recruitment of Gorkha soldiers into the Indian Army, a long-standing tradition, also became contentious after India introduced a new short-term recruitment scheme in 2022.

Nepal's responses combined diversification and balancing. After the blockade, Kathmandu signed a transit agreement with China in 2016, joined the Belt and Road Initiative in 2017, and agreed to railway feasibility studies, and China financed the new Pokhara airport that opened in 2023. In December 2024 Oli, in a departure from the convention that Nepali prime ministers visit India first, traveled to China and signed a BRI framework for cooperation. At the same time, Nepal ratified a US Millennium Challenge Corporation grant compact in 2022 after fierce domestic debate in which opponents argued that it could bind Nepal to American strategic aims. These debates reveal that foreign policy was fought out among Nepal's parties and publics, with each major party accused of being too close to one neighbor or another. Nepal's geography limited its choices: India remains its principal source of imports, transit, and remittances, and Himalayan terrain makes Chinese trade routes costly. The Balendra Shah government, installed in March 2026, quickly developed a documentary diplomatic record. Foreign Minister Shishir Khanal made an official visit to India on 5–7 June 2026 and then visited China in mid-June, reaffirming Nepal's stated intention to maintain constructive relations with both neighbors. Subsequent ambassadorial meetings and discussions about connectivity and territorial sensitivities supplied further evidence of continuity alongside changes of style. As of November 2026, these contacts establish an early pattern of diplomatic engagement; they do not yet establish the long-term results of the new government's foreign policy.

Bhutan's relationship with India has been closer and more formal. The 1949 treaty, which required Bhutan to be guided by India in foreign policy, was revised in 2007 to remove that provision and to recognize Bhutan's sovereignty more explicitly, although the two countries consult closely, India funds Bhutan's five-year plans and finances hydropower, and Indian military instructors train Bhutan's army. Bhutan has no formal diplomatic relations with China or with any of the permanent members of the UN Security Council, and has negotiated its boundary with China, which is unsettled in the north and west, through a series of rounds of talks since 1984. In 2017, when Chinese road construction in the Doklam plateau, claimed by Bhutan, led to a seventy-three-day standoff between Indian and Chinese troops, Bhutan protested and left diplomacy to India, a position that illustrates the narrowness of its room for maneuver. In the following years, talks with China continued and a 2021 agreement outlined steps to speed up negotiations, but Bhutan's government has been careful in public statements. The choices are made with high awareness of national security, and the monarchy has been closely involved.

A comparison clarifies the contrast. Nepal's foreign policy is more open to public contestation and is driven by party competition, public nationalism, and a large diaspora. Bhutan's is managed by the throne and a small circle of officials, with limited public debate. Both states try to avoid dependence on one partner, but the relative size of their economies and populations, their histories, and the political systems that make decisions shape different approaches. In neither case did foreign powers determine domestic political outcomes. External actors influenced timing and options, as in India's role in the 2005 Delhi understanding and the 2015 blockade, but the principal causes of Nepal's republic, Bhutan's constitution, and the 2025 uprising were internal.

Section 104.19

Maldives, the Indian Ocean, and Small-State Diplomatic Independence

The Maldives' location in the central Indian Ocean, close to major shipping routes that carry much of the world's energy trade, makes its diplomacy attract attention disproportionate to its size. Relations with India are close and layered. India was the first country to recognize Maldivian independence in 1965, intervened in 1988 at the request of Gayoom to prevent a coup attempt (Operation Cactus), and has been a principal provider of budget support, development credit, defense cooperation, and emergency aid. China, which established relations in 1972 and expanded them considerably after 2012, has financed infrastructure, signed a free trade agreement during Muizzu's term, and become an important source of tourists. Other partners, among them Japan, Turkey, the Gulf states, and the United States, also matter.

In Maldivian domestic politics, the foreign-policy question has been fought as a contest over narratives. Yameen's government was accused by opponents of selling national assets to China and of sidelining India, and Solih's government was accused by its opponents of subordinating Maldivian sovereignty to India, the charge behind the "India Out" campaign. Both charges were exaggerated at times and served electoral purposes, though underlying issues, including the terms of defense cooperation, debt to lenders, and military installations, were real subjects of public interest and of limited public information. Chinese research vessels visiting Maldivian ports in early 2024 and agreements with India over surveillance and harbor development were examples that drew attention from regional observers, who often described the Maldives as a theater of Indian–Chinese rivalry. Maldivian officials and analysts, in contrast, frequently emphasize that the country wants good relations with all and that foreign policy should reflect Maldivian interests.

The record under Muizzu suggests how small-state diplomacy works in practice. He began with a confrontational stance toward India, then shifted as financial need and political cost accumulated. India extended a currency swap, rolled over treasury bills, and welcomed Maldivian leaders, and the Maldives repaid the swap in April 2026 as part of its debt management. Muizzu simultaneously pursued trade and investment ties with China and Turkey and with Gulf partners. Observers interpret this as the behavior of a small state that hedges among larger powers because it cannot afford to rely on one, but the experience also shows the limits: the debt owed to several creditors, the dependence on India for emergency liquidity, and the vulnerability to climate change restrict room for maneuver. The April 2026 referendum, in which domestic opposition rather than foreign pressure defeated the government's proposal, is a reminder that Maldivian citizens remain the principal check on their leaders.

Comparing the three states reveals both common and distinct constraints. All three face India as an indispensable neighbor, and all have used relations with China to widen options. Nepal, a mid-sized state with a land border to both giants, bargains with each; Bhutan, whose border with China is unsettled and whose security is bound to India, moves cautiously; the Maldives, separated by sea and dependent on external finance and tourism, shifts between partners. In each case the strategic framing by outside commentators, which treats these states as pieces in a competition, understates their own politics. Their governments responded to publics, parties, and monarchs, and the long-term question of sovereignty was always linked to economic and environmental fragility. Chapter 110 places these relationships within the wider regional and global setting.

Section 104.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The three histories examined in this chapter show that political change in small South Asian states took more than one form. In Nepal, a decade-long insurgency, a palace coup, and mass urban protest combined to abolish a monarchy that had lasted for 240 years, and the result was a federal republic written through a long and contested process. In Bhutan, the monarchy itself drafted a constitution, abdicated in favor of a successor, and supervised the first elections, retaining both a leading symbolic role and real influence. In the Maldives, a long-ruling president accepted reform under pressure and then lost an election he had allowed, after which every transfer of power was disputed in new ways. Revolution from below, reform from above, and contested electoral opening are not neat categories, and each country combined elements, but the contrasts are historically instructive.

The constitutional outcomes differ. Nepal's 2015 Constitution is the most elaborate, with federalism, secularism, proportional representation, and a broad rights chapter, and it embodies a bargain among parties and groups that did not include everyone. Bhutan's 2008 Constitution is a compact document that places a monarch with substantial prestige and limited formal powers at the head of a system of elected institutions. The Maldives' 2008 Constitution created a strong presidency within a framework of independent institutions, but those institutions were repeatedly drawn into political conflict. In all three, constitutional text has mattered, though practice has diverged from it: Nepal's inclusive guarantees are unevenly realized, Bhutan's democracy is shaped by royal moral authority, and the Maldives' balance of powers depends on shifting parliamentary majorities.

Governance challenges also have common features. Each state has a young population and limited domestic employment, and emigration is a major trend, as remittances in Nepal, student and professional departures from Bhutan, and the concentration of Maldivian youth in Malé all show. Each faces questions of inclusion, whether of Madhesis and Janajatis in Nepal, Lhotshampa in Bhutan, or outer-island residents and non-Muslims in the Maldives. Each has experienced concerns about the accountability of leaders. Each pursues a development strategy that depends on a narrow base: remittances and hydropower and tourism in Nepal, hydropower in Bhutan, tourism in the Maldives. And each has had to defend autonomy between larger neighbors while also relying on them.

The chapter's central lesson for the historian is that political change in these states was made by identifiable people and groups: Maoist cadres and villagers, party leaders and street protesters in Nepal, a monarch and officials in Bhutan, activists, voters, judges, and presidents in the Maldives. It was shaped by regional circumstances but not dictated by them. Finally, the chapter's treatment of 2025 and 2026 illustrates the need for humility. Nepal's government under Balendra Shah, Bhutan's Gelephu project, and the Maldives' post-referendum politics are unfinished, and later historians will judge them with evidence that is not yet available. The next chapter turns from these small states to the security conflicts that have shaped relations among India, Pakistan, China, and the wider region (Chapter 105).

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political histories of Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives from the mid-1990s to November 2026. It has argued that the three states followed different paths of political change: revolution from below in Nepal, reform from above in Bhutan, and contested electoral opening in the Maldives. It has also argued that each state's trajectory was shaped chiefly by domestic actors, with regional powers influencing options rather than determining outcomes.

In Nepal, a Maoist insurgency launched in 1996 drew on rural poverty, exclusion of marginalized groups, and the failures of the post-1990 parliamentary order. The war, which killed on the order of seventeen thousand people, was transformed by the royal massacre of 2001 and King Gyanendra's direct rule of 2005. An alliance between the Seven Party Alliance and the Maoists led to the People's Movement of 2006 and the Comprehensive Peace Agreement.

The Constituent Assembly of 2008 abolished the monarchy, and after a failed first assembly and the 2015 earthquake, the second produced the 2015 Constitution, establishing a federal, secular republic with seven provinces; the 753 present local government units were organized during implementation in 2017. The constitution faced immediate challenges from Madhesi, Tharu, Janajati, and other groups, and the unresolved questions of citizenship, representation, and transitional justice remained.

The republic's politics became marked by coalition instability, corruption scandals, and dependence on remittances. In September 2025, a ban on social media triggered youth-led protests and wider unrest in which at least 76 people died, government buildings were burned, and Prime Minister Oli resigned. Sushila Karki led an interim government to an election on 5 March 2026, in which the Rastriya Swatantra Party won a large majority and Balendra Shah became prime minister on 27 March. The meaning of this realignment is still unfolding.

In Bhutan, the monarchy drafted a constitution in 2001–2005, the Fourth King abdicated in 2006, and elections in 2008 inaugurated parliamentary government under a constitutional monarch. Four national elections produced transfers of governing authority, culminating in Tshering Tobgay's return as prime minister in 2024. Gross National Happiness, hydropower, free social services, and environmental conservation form the core of the development model, while the monarchy retains great moral authority and shapes major projects.

Bhutan in the 2020s faces youth outmigration, unemployment, and the legacy of the early-1990s displacement of Lhotshampa. The Gelephu Mindfulness City, announced in 2023, is the government's boldest response, and its success is uncertain. The refugee history is a necessary counterpoint to the kingdom's happiness narrative.

The Maldives moved from Gayoom's thirty-year presidency to multiparty democracy through reforms between 2003 and 2008. Nasheed's election in 2008 began a period of competitive politics, but the 2012 resignation, the annulled 2013 election, Yameen's authoritarian turn, and the 2018 emergency showed institutional fragility. Solih's 2018 victory and Muizzu's 2023 win demonstrated that voters could change governments, and the April 2026 referendum showed that they could also reject executive proposals. The economy, dependent on tourism and burdened by debt, remains vulnerable even after the April 2026 sukuk repayment.

The diplomatic choices of the three states were conditioned by their size and geography. Nepal balanced India and China within a noisy domestic debate, Bhutan relied on India while negotiating its boundary with China, and the Maldives hedged among partners as financial needs dictated. The comparison suggests that small states exercise real agency within limits.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how closely political history depends on questions of inclusion and legitimacy. In all three states the central issue was who belonged to the national community and who had a voice in its institutions: Madhesis and Janajatis in Nepal, Lhotshampa in Bhutan, outer-island residents and non-Muslims in the Maldives. Where institutions did not answer those questions persuasively, pressure built and eventually found expression in protest or electoral rejection.

The chapter also reveals how recent history differs from the more distant past studied in earlier chapters. Evidence is abundant but unsettled, scholarship is early, and the outcomes are unknown. Historians of the future will judge Nepal's 2026 government, Bhutan's Gelephu project, and the Maldives' institutions with evidence that is not available now, and the chapter's cautions about provisional interpretation are part of its argument.

These three small states also expand the meaning of South Asian history. Their experiences connect to Chapter 96, which followed them to 1991, and to Chapters 105 and 110, which place regional security and global position in wider perspective. Their histories show that South Asia's political transformations were not confined to its largest states and that small societies produced significant experiments in revolution, monarchy, and democracy.
