    Partition

    Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

    Chapter Opening

    On the night of 14 August 1947 and the morning of 15 August, two new Dominions came into legal existence in South Asia. In Karachi and in New Delhi there were ceremonies, flags, and speeches. Yet in the countryside of Punjab, in the market towns of the canal colonies, and in the villages along the rivers of Bengal, millions of people did not yet know for certain which state they lived in. The two boundary awards that divided Punjab and Bengal had been completed before independence, but they were not published until 17 August. For a few days, families, village councils, police officers, and local officials waited, guessed, and in many places began to flee, without an authoritative map to tell them where the new frontier ran.

    Chapter 85 explained why British India was divided: the failure of the Cabinet Mission plan, the electoral confrontation between Congress and the Muslim League, the violence of 1946, and the decisions of June 1947 that produced the Indian Independence Act. This chapter turns to a different set of questions. What did division actually mean for the territories, institutions, families, and individuals it affected? How were borders drawn, and with what evidence? How did the collapse of public order in Punjab and the slower, recurring insecurities of Bengal produce one of the largest forced migrations of the twentieth century? How did governments, relief workers, and ordinary neighbors respond? And how did the questions Partition raised about women's bodies, abandoned property, and citizenship shape the successor states for decades?

    The chapter's central problem can be stated briefly. The legal transfer of power was a political settlement, and it was completed within weeks. The consequences of that settlement were territorial, humanitarian, legal, and social, and they unfolded over years. Historians therefore distinguish the decision to partition from the implementation of Partition, and they distinguish the immediate violence of 1947 from the longer processes of resettlement, rehabilitation, and the redefinition of belonging that followed. This chapter follows both, concentrating on March 1947 to about 1950, and reaching forward where necessary to explain displacement, citizenship, and memory.

    Partition was not a single event with a single geography. In Punjab, a large and densely connected province whose Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs lived in interwoven districts, violence and migration were concentrated in a few months and led to something close to a population exchange. In Bengal, the experience was more protracted, with recurrent movements across a border that remained porous for years. Sindh, Delhi, Assam, Sylhet, and the Himalayan and princely frontiers each had their own patterns. A responsible account must hold these differences in view and must avoid transferring the chronology and intensity of Punjab's catastrophe automatically to every other region.

    The evidence for this history is unusually varied, and unusually uneven. It includes the Gazette of India publication of the boundary awards, the files of governors and district officials, military and police correspondence, relief and rehabilitation records, legislation, census returns, newspapers, and the papers of political leaders. It also includes a large body of oral testimony gathered decades afterward, memoirs, fiction, film, and the objects and photographs preserved in museums and family homes. Each kind of source illuminates some things and obscures others. Official records tend to count, classify, and administer; testimony remembers, and sometimes cannot speak at all. Women's experiences in particular are often recorded in files created by the states that were trying to recover or regulate them, which means that historians must read those files with a careful awareness of whose voice is present and whose is missing.

    This chapter also carries a responsibility of tone. It describes mass killing, sexual violence, and large-scale suffering. It does so without assigning collective guilt to any religious community, without treating violence as an expression of ancient hatred, and without reducing survivors to victims alone. People of every community killed, were killed, rescued, sheltered, informed, looted, and grieved. Identifying the specific actors, institutions, and circumstances behind particular events, where the evidence permits, is the most reliable way both to respect the dead and to understand what happened.

    Chapter 87 will follow the same period from the standpoint of the new states, examining independence, the accession of the princely states, and the first conflict over Kashmir. Chapter 86 occupies the ground between the political decisions of Chapter 85 and the consolidation of sovereignty in Chapter 87. Its purpose is to show how the establishment of India and Pakistan resolved the constitutional question of British withdrawal while creating profound territorial, social, humanitarian, and legal problems that independence itself could not resolve.

    Historical Context

    Chapter 85 traced the political path to the transfer of power: the Cabinet Mission of 1946, the interim government, the elections of 1945–1946, Direct Action Day, the viceroyalty of Lord Mountbatten, and the 3 June 1947 plan that provided for the division of Punjab and Bengal and the creation of two Dominions. The Indian Independence Act, which received royal assent in July 1947, set 15 August as the date for the transfer of authority. Chapter 86 does not retell those negotiations. It takes their outcome as its starting point and asks what it meant to turn a political decision into territories, administrations, and populations.

    The scale of the task was extraordinary. British India covered a vast and densely populated area. Its provinces had been bound together by railways, canals, a common civil service, a single army, a shared postal and telegraph system, and an integrated revenue and financial structure. Dividing them required the creation of two governments, the partition of the army and civil service, the allocation of assets and liabilities, and the drawing of a boundary through two provinces whose populations were intermingled. All this was to be accomplished in about ten weeks between the June plan and independence. The brevity of the timetable, whatever its justification in the political circumstances of 1947, left little room to plan for the protection of those whom the new boundary would turn into minorities.

    Punjab and Bengal were the two provinces to be divided. Punjab, in the northwest, was an agricultural heartland whose western districts had been transformed by canal colonies in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and whose population in 1941 included a slight Muslim majority, a large Hindu minority, and a politically influential Sikh community of roughly one in seven. Bengal, in the east, was a delta region of rivers, rice, and jute, whose capital Calcutta had been the center of British India's commerce and industry. Its 1941 population was also slightly more than half Muslim, with a large Hindu minority concentrated in the west and in many eastern towns. In both provinces, religion intersected with class, language, landholding, and commerce in ways that no boundary could reflect neatly.

    The year before Partition had already shown how fragile public order could become. The Calcutta killings of August 1946, the violence in Noakhali and Tippera in October 1946, and the retaliatory violence in Bihar had set precedent and fear. The Punjab crisis of March 1947, which began in Rawalpindi and neighboring districts, showed that the province was now exposed to the same dynamics. Chapter 85 introduced these events. This chapter takes them up only as the immediate background to the territorial division and to the far larger violence of the summer and autumn.

    The sources for this chapter require particular care. The two boundary awards survive in authenticated official form. The records of administrations and armed forces, collected in published documentary series and in national archives, document the policies and failures of the authorities. Relief and rehabilitation files record the efforts of new governments to count and feed displaced people. Oral testimony and memoir, much of it collected decades later, provides access to experiences that official records often omit. Estimates of deaths, displacement, and abduction are contested and rest on differing methods. Where this chapter gives figures it attributes them, specifies what they count, and notes the uncertainty.

    Drawing the Borders: The Territorial Partition of British India

    §§86.1–86.4 · June–August 1947
    Section 86.1

    From the 3 June Plan to Territorial Partition: The Creation of India and Pakistan

    The 3 June plan and the Indian Independence Act settled the principle of division, but they did not by themselves create two functioning states. The Act provided for two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, each with its own Governor-General and its own constituent assembly, to come into being on 15 August 1947. It provided that the provinces of Punjab and Bengal would be divided, and that a boundary commission would determine the line. It also lapsed British paramountcy over the princely states, leaving their rulers, in theory, to decide on accession. Chapter 87 examines the princely states. Here the focus is the territory of British India itself, divided among provinces, districts, and administrative units that had never been designed to be separated.

    Three terms help clarify what happened in August 1947. Sovereignty was transferred by law on 15 August, when authority passed to the two Dominion governments. Territorial definition was the process of determining where the boundaries ran, and it was not complete on that day, because the Punjab and Bengal awards were published only on 17 August. Administrative control was the practical capacity of a government to staff offices, command police and troops, collect revenue, and keep order, and in August 1947 this was the most uncertain of the three. Pakistan commemorates its independence on 14 August, because the transfer ceremonies in Karachi took place on that day and its Constituent Assembly met then, while the legal establishment of both Dominions is dated to 15 August. The difference is a small but telling reminder that Partition meant different things in different capitals.

    Behind the ceremonies lay an enormous administrative separation. A Partition Council, with representatives of Congress and the Muslim League under the Viceroy's chairmanship, supervised the division of the central government's assets and personnel. Committees allocated army units, civil servants, railway rolling stock, records, and treasury balances. Officers and clerks were asked to choose between the two services, and tens of thousands of government employees and their families relocated, most of them by their own choice, as the machinery of two capitals was created. Karachi, with few government buildings and little prior administrative infrastructure, had to be turned into a national capital within weeks. Delhi had to absorb arriving officials from Lahore and elsewhere while remaining the seat of the Indian government. These movements were smaller than the later refugee migrations, but they set the pattern of government-organized relocation and also showed that the machinery of state could be divided on a timetable that the protection of civilians could not match.

    Provincial governments were divided in a similar way. In Punjab and Bengal, the legislatures had met in June in separate groups of Muslim-majority and non-Muslim-majority districts, and the decisions of those groups led to the division of each province. The provinces of Punjab and Bengal were each split into two administrations, with separate governors, ministers, and secretariats. East Punjab, lacking a capital after Lahore went to Pakistan, was administered at first from Shimla and then from temporary quarters. Chandigarh would be conceived as a new capital only afterward. West Bengal retained Calcutta, while East Bengal had to build its own administrative structure around Dacca. Dacca had been a provincial town under the old province and was now the capital of Pakistan's most populous province.

    Division also meant dividing what could not be easily divided. The North Western Railway, which linked Lahore, Amritsar, Delhi, and Karachi, had to be separated into Indian and Pakistani systems. Telegraph, postal, and banking networks, the canal and irrigation systems of the Punjab, and the electricity grid of the western districts were all affected. These infrastructures had been built on the assumption of a unified province, and their separation immediately created problems that continued after independence, including disputes over canal waters in 1948. The first lesson of the territorial division is therefore that a boundary drawn quickly through an integrated landscape could not remain only a line on a map. It became an obstacle to commerce, a source of insecurity, and soon a site of conflict.

    Section 86.2

    The Radcliffe Line: Boundary Commissions, Demographic Evidence, and Contested Decisions

    Two Boundary Commissions were established, one for Punjab and one for Bengal, and Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who had never before visited India, was named chairman of both. Each commission had four additional members, two nominated by Congress and two by the Muslim League. The Punjab Commission's members were Justices Mehr Chand Mahajan and Teja Singh, for the Congress and Sikh side, and Justices Din Muhammad and Muhammad Munir, for the League. The Bengal Commission's members were Justices C. C. Biswas and B. K. Mukherjea, and Justices Abu Saleh Mohamed Akram and S. A. Rahman. Because the members were divided along the lines of the parties that had nominated them, and because they were unable to agree on most contested points, the substantive decisions were left to Radcliffe himself.

    The commissions' terms of reference were announced as part of the June plan. They were instructed to demarcate the boundaries on the basis of ascertaining the contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims, and in doing so to take into account other factors. The phrasing mattered. A principle based on contiguous majorities appeared to offer a clear rule, but the added reference to other factors left room for every party to argue that its particular concerns were relevant: irrigation, communications, land ownership, the location of shrines, the economic viability of cities, and the claims of minorities. Congress, the Muslim League, and Sikh representatives all submitted memoranda and arguments, and the commissions held public hearings in Lahore and Calcutta in July. The 1941 census was the principal demographic source, though its figures were already six years old, were disputed by some parties, and were aggregated by districts and tehsils whose populations were not segregated by religion.

    The task was difficult in principle and almost impossible in practice. In Punjab, the contiguous-majority principle left a band of central districts in which no community had a clear and compact majority. Muslims were a majority in the west and in parts of the center, Hindus in the southeast, and Sikhs, though nowhere a majority of a whole district, were concentrated in the central districts and held much of the best canal-irrigated land. Lahore and Amritsar, the two great cities of the Punjab, were mixed cities with different demographic and economic characters. The canal system, built in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, crossed what would be the international boundary at several places, and the headworks that controlled the flow of water lay in districts whose assignment depended on arguments about demography and economics. In Bengal, a similar interweaving of Muslim and non-Muslim populations, rivers, railways, and jute-growing districts made a clean division impossible.

    The commissions worked with extraordinary speed. Radcliffe arrived on 8 July, the hearings were conducted in the following weeks, and the awards were completed by about 12 and 13 August, a period of roughly five weeks for decisions that would affect tens of millions of people. He relied heavily on district-level maps and census data, was limited in his ability to visit the areas concerned, and worked under climatic and political pressure. The awards were handed to the Viceroy before independence but were not released until 17 August. Mountbatten's reasons for delay have been debated: some interpretations emphasize a desire to avoid British responsibility for the awards, or for the violence that could follow their announcement, during the independence ceremonies, while others note that publishing the awards earlier would have put pressure on the transferring administration as well. Whatever the explanation, the effect was that millions of people spent the days of independence uncertain of where the boundary lay.

    Radcliffe's awards were not simple expressions of religious majority. In Punjab, the line followed the principle of contiguous majorities in many places but diverged from it where other factors were judged to weigh more. Lahore, with a Muslim population near a majority but a very large Hindu and Sikh share of its property and commerce, went to Pakistan. Amritsar went to India. Gurdaspur, a district with a very slight Muslim majority, was divided, with three of its four tehsils going to India and Shakargarh to Pakistan. That decision was crucial because it gave India a land route toward Kashmir, a consequence debated by historians and further discussed in Chapter 87. The Ferozepur and Zira tehsils, with Muslim majorities, went to India, partly because of the location of the Ferozepur headworks. In Bengal, the line divided districts such as Nadia, Jessore, Malda, and Dinajpur, placed Khulna in East Bengal and Murshidabad in West Bengal, and assigned the Chittagong Hill Tracts, which were overwhelmingly non-Muslim, to East Pakistan. Each of these decisions was contested immediately and has continued to be debated by historians.

    The awards were deeply unpopular with all parties. Congress, the Muslim League, and Sikh leaders each believed that the awards disadvantaged their community. This did not prove that the awards were fair, but it does suggest that the exercise was impossible to satisfy everyone. For historians, the more significant point is that the boundary was drawn as an administrative and political act in conditions that offered the commissions little time, little information, and little opportunity to consider how the line would be implemented. The consequences for those who suddenly found themselves on the wrong side of the line were not part of the commissions' brief.

    Issuing authority or witness: Sir Cyril Radcliffe, as chairman of the Punjab and Bengal Boundary Commissions, transmitted to the Viceroy and published by the Government of India · Date: Awards completed about 12–13 August 1947; published 17 August 1947 · Collection or edition: Gazette of India Extraordinary, 17 August 1947; reprinted in the documentary series The Transfer of Power 1942–47 (ed. N. Mansergh, E. W. R. Lumby, and Penderel Moon) and in later compilations

    Provenance: The text of each award is an official government publication, authenticated by its publication in the Gazette. The accompanying maps were prepared by the commissions and attached to the awards. Published editions differ in format and reproduction quality, so students should compare the gazette text with the maps.

    What the record shows (editorial paraphrase, not a quotation): Each award describes the boundary by reference to districts, tehsils, thanas, rivers, and railways. In the Punjab award, the line is described district by district from north to south, naming the tehsils assigned to each side; in the Bengal award, the boundary is traced across the districts of the delta. Each award is accompanied by a statement of the principles used and, in Punjab, a note on the separate views of the commission's members, who did not agree.

    Historical limitations: The awards state decisions, not reasoning in detail, and therefore do not explain why particular districts or tehsils were assigned as they were. The commission records and Radcliffe's working papers are incomplete, and the line as described in the text and as demarcated on the ground sometimes differed. Later demarcation work and agreements with each state's survey authorities modified details of the line.

    Research route: Consult the Gazette of India Extraordinary of 17 August 1947 in the National Archives of India or the British Library, and compare the text with the maps reproduced in The Transfer of Power, Volume XII. For an accessible interpretive study, see Lucy Chester, Borders and Conflict in South Asia: The Radcliffe Boundary Commission and the Partition of Punjab (2009).

    Section 86.3

    The Partition of Punjab: Land, Religion, Cities, Canals, and Community

    Before 1947, Punjab was one of the most economically productive and militarily significant provinces of British India. Its five rivers, the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej, flowed into the Indus, and a network of canals, constructed from the 1880s onward, transformed large tracts of the western districts into irrigated agricultural land. In the canal colonies of Lyallpur, Montgomery, Sargodha, and neighboring areas, hundreds of thousands of farmers, including many Sikh and Hindu settlers from central Punjab, cultivated wheat and cotton for the market. The province also provided a large proportion of the soldiers recruited by the British Indian Army, and its rural economy was linked by railways, mandis, and moneylending networks to the ports of Karachi and Bombay.

    Punjab's society was religiously plural and socially intricate. In 1941 a slight majority of the population was Muslim. Hindus formed about three in ten, and Sikhs about one in seven, in a province where kinship, caste, landholding, and region cut across religious lines. Landlords and tribal leaders of many backgrounds dominated rural politics, and the Unionist Party, a cross-community coalition of landowning interests, had governed for most of the period before 1946. Its collapse in the elections of 1946 and the resignation of Premier Khizar Hayat Tiwana in March 1947 removed the principal political structure that had held the province together. In the central districts, Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs lived in neighboring villages and mixed urban neighborhoods, and each community had claims to the same land and cities.

    The cities illustrate the difficulty of division. Lahore, the cultural and educational capital of the province, was a Muslim-majority city whose commercial and professional elite included large numbers of Hindus and Sikhs, who owned a large share of its commercial property. Amritsar, a few dozen miles away, was the religious center of Sikhism and a major trading city with a significant Muslim population, notably of artisans and weavers. Gurdaspur, Ferozepur, and Jullundur districts, along the central belt, had complex and differently distributed populations: Gurdaspur district had a narrow Muslim majority overall, while the demographic balance in Ferozepur and Jullundur was different; each contained substantial minorities and important tehsil-level contrasts. Because the boundary was drawn partly by tehsil, a small difference in the proportions of a tehsil could determine a very large decision.

    The line cut through the Punjab's religious geography as well. A large number of Sikh holy places, including Nankana Sahib, the birthplace of Guru Nanak, Panja Sahib, and the shrine at Kartarpur where he is believed to have spent his last years, lay in districts that would belong to Pakistan. For Sikhs, whose community was concentrated in the central belt and whose political leaders had campaigned against a Muslim-majority Punjab, the division meant that their holiest sites and a large share of their best agricultural land lay on the other side of a new international frontier. Muslim shrines, Sufi dargahs, and mosques in East Punjab, notably at Sirhind and Malerkotla, lay on the other side for Muslims. Hindu temples and pilgrimage sites also lay on both sides of the boundary. Access to shrines would become a recurring issue in India–Pakistan relations, and arrangements for pilgrimage continued to be negotiated for decades.

    The economic effects were immediate. The canals of the Punjab, built as an integrated system under British administration, were now divided between two states. The headworks that regulated their flow lay in some places in India and in others in Pakistan, creating the possibility that one state could interrupt the water supply of the other. This possibility became real in April 1948, when the East Punjab authorities briefly interrupted the water supplied to canals in West Punjab, and the dispute that followed ran for years until the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960. Railways that had linked Lahore to Amritsar and Delhi were divided. Commercial networks in grain, cloth, and moneylending, which had connected Amritsar and Lahore as parts of a single regional economy, were broken.

    It is important to distinguish the territorial division of Punjab from the violence that transformed its population. The boundary awards, by themselves, did not require anyone to move. They created minorities, and in some districts large ones, who found themselves in a state dominated by another religious community. That the minorities then fled, were expelled, or were killed was a consequence of the violence that accompanied and followed the division, not a result written into the boundary. The next divisions of this chapter trace how that transformation occurred. For now, it is enough to note that the line through Punjab cut across a landscape whose density of mixed settlement made it highly vulnerable to precisely that kind of violence.

    Section 86.4

    The Partition of Bengal and Sylhet: Economic Networks and Eastern Borders

    Bengal had been divided once before, in 1905, in a partition that was reversed in 1911 after widespread protest, as discussed in Chapter 77. The partition of 1947 differed in that it was based on religion, accepted by the leadership of the main parties, and permanent. The 1941 census recorded slightly more than half of Bengal's population as Muslim, but the distribution was uneven. Muslims formed large majorities in the eastern delta districts and in the north, while Hindus formed majorities in much of the west and in some southern districts. Between these areas lay a belt of mixed districts in which neither community held an overwhelming majority.

    The economic geography of Bengal made the division especially disruptive. The port and industrial city of Calcutta, located in the west, depended on the jute grown in the eastern delta. The jute mills of the Hooghly belt processed raw jute from districts that would now lie in East Pakistan, and the great port of Calcutta exported the jute products of the whole region. East Bengal, by contrast, had few mills and no major port other than Chittagong. Partition thus placed most of the world's jute growing in Pakistan and most of the world's jute processing in India. This arrangement led to a tense interdependence that was disrupted in 1949, when India devalued the rupee and Pakistan declined to follow, and trade between the two countries was sharply reduced. In the longer run, East Pakistan's economy became increasingly oriented around Dacca, Chittagong, and new jute mills, a development explored in Chapter 90.

    The boundary commission for Bengal faced a similar set of dilemmas. Calcutta, with a Hindu majority within its municipal limits but a large Muslim minority and a hinterland that was predominantly Muslim, was assigned to India. Khulna, a district with a slight Hindu majority, went to East Bengal, while Murshidabad, a Muslim-majority district on the Bhagirathi, was assigned to West Bengal. Both decisions were publicly contested. Malda and Dinajpur, northern districts with mixed populations, were divided, with some areas going to East Bengal and others to India. The Chittagong Hill Tracts, home to a population that was almost entirely non-Muslim and mostly composed of Buddhist hill peoples, were assigned to East Pakistan, partly on the grounds of the area's economic dependence on Chittagong and its location relative to the delta. This decision, which did not follow the principle of religious majority, remained controversial and would affect the region's later political history.

    Sylhet received separate treatment. A district of the province of Assam, Sylhet had a Muslim majority of roughly sixty percent. Its inclusion in Assam was a historical arrangement of the colonial period, and it had been part of Bengal before 1874. The June plan provided for a referendum, and on 6 and 7 July 1947 the voters of Sylhet were asked whether the district should join East Bengal or remain in Assam. The total vote was 239,619 for joining East Bengal against 184,041 for remaining in Assam, a margin of 55,578; within the Karimganj voting division, the result was narrowly 41,262 to 40,536 for joining East Bengal, in a poll in which the Muslim League campaigned vigorously and Congress and Assamese political leaders campaigned in support of Assam. The referendum was followed by the division of the district: the award divided the original Karimganj subdivision rather than transferring it as a whole. Radcliffe specifically described that subdivision as having a Muslim majority, while identifying several southern thanas with non-Muslim majorities and addressing wider railway and transport considerations. India retained Karimganj town and portions including Patharkandi and Ratabari, while other areas of the old subdivision went to East Bengal. The resulting Indian Karimganj district should not be confused with the undivided subdivision as it existed before August 1947.

    The result of the Bengal division was a Pakistan composed of two wings separated by more than a thousand miles of Indian territory. East Pakistan and West Pakistan shared a religion but little else: their languages, histories, agricultural economies, and political cultures were different, and their economies were not complementary in any simple way. East Bengal was densely populated, riverine, and agrarian, with its own Bengali-language cultural tradition. West Pakistan was a mixture of Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashtun, and Baloch regions with a larger share of administrative, military, and commercial power. Chapter 90 traces the political consequences of this inequality and Chapter 92 its end in 1971. For the purposes of the present chapter, the essential point is that the territorial settlement created, in the east, a state whose internal geography and economic relations were never straightforward, and a border with India that was long, porous, and subject to repeated disturbances.

    Section 86.5

    From Communal Polarization to Mass Violence: The Escalation of 1947

    The violence of 1947 did not begin on 15 August. It had a prehistory of at least a year, and its pattern was shaped by the earlier episodes. The Calcutta killings of August 1946 killed thousands over several days and established a model of urban conflict in which neighborhood-based gangs, political mobilization, and the paralysis of the police combined. The violence in Noakhali and Tippera in October 1946, in which Hindu minorities were attacked, and the retaliatory violence in Bihar, in which large numbers of Muslims were killed, showed that attacks in one province could trigger reprisals in another. Newspapers, rumors, and the movement of survivors carried news and fear across regions, and each episode was used by political actors to justify mobilization.

    The Punjab crisis of March 1947 marked the transition to mass violence in the province that would suffer the greatest loss of life. On 2 March, Khizar Hayat Tiwana resigned as Premier in the face of League agitation, and the Muslim League's claim to form a government set off a period of intense political competition. In the following days, Hindu and Sikh communities in the Rawalpindi division, including the districts of Rawalpindi, Jhelum, and Attock, were attacked by crowds and organized groups. Villages such as Thoa Khalsa became known for the scale of the killing and for the deaths of women who, according to survivor accounts, chose death rather than capture. In Amritsar and Lahore, rioting broke out in the same weeks, and in the following months the cities of Punjab were repeatedly disturbed by arson, stabbing, and bombings.

    Historians identify several processes that drove escalation. First, fear of becoming a vulnerable minority in a state governed by another community became a powerful motive. In Punjab, Sikhs in particular feared that a Pakistan dominated by Muslims would threaten their religious and economic position, while Muslims in the east and Hindus in the west feared the opposite. Second, political leaders and organizations mobilized their communities with rhetoric that treated the other community as an adversary, and the formation of armed volunteer organizations, such as the Muslim League National Guard, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, and Akali Dal jathas, provided structures for organized violence. Third, rumors circulated rapidly: stories of atrocities, true and false, justified reprisals. Fourth, the weakness of administration, and the dilemma of police forces that were themselves divided along community lines and uncertain which government they would serve, undermined the capacity to prevent attacks.

    The term communal violence, widely used in South Asian historiography, needs careful handling. It correctly indicates that victims were targeted on the basis of religious identity. But it can also suggest that entire communities acted as undifferentiated blocs, and can thereby obscure the particular organizers, local leaders, former soldiers, police, and political groups who planned and carried out attacks. Historians distinguish between spontaneous crowd violence, which was common in cities, and organized attacks, in which armed groups, sometimes with military experience and weapons, targeted villages and convoys and often prepared their actions in advance. The latter were especially important in the rural killings and in the expulsions of minority populations, where the goal was often not only to kill but to compel flight and to seize land and property.

    The chronology of escalation is important because it prevents a retrospective reading of the summer as an uninterrupted outbreak of violence. In the months before the actual transfer, reports of attacks in the Rawalpindi region helped convince many Sikh and Hindu families in West Punjab that their future security could no longer be assured locally. Muslim residents of eastern districts, meanwhile, heard news of intimidation and began considering the opposite journey. Local political rumors could acquire the force of a deadline: an impending announcement, procession, or expected army withdrawal became a signal to move possessions or seek weapons. Such accounts must be checked against district and police records, which often reveal that conditions differed sharply between neighboring localities. Rather than explain every attack as a spontaneous response to religion, historians ask who mobilized crowds, whose property was targeted, how rumors travelled, and where officials intervened effectively. This approach recognizes both fear and intentional choices, including the choices of neighbors who shielded threatened families.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Communal Violence

    Communal violence is the term used in South Asia for violence between groups defined by religious community. In the language of the late colonial state and of later scholarship, "communalism" referred to politics organized around religious identity and "communal" to the groups themselves.

    The term matters because it frames the violence of 1946–1948 as conflict between communities. Historians caution that it can mask the roles of organizers, militias, political leaders, and state institutions, and it can imply that violence was an expression of unchanging religious hostility rather than a product of specific political circumstances. This textbook uses the term where sources and historiography use it, but tries to identify actors and conditions wherever the evidence allows.

    Section 86.6

    Punjab in Flames: Massacres, Expulsions, and the Destruction of Mixed Communities

    In the weeks after independence, Punjab became the site of the most intense and concentrated violence in the history of Partition. The first acts of violence in the countryside were reported in July and early August, but the major outbreak followed the announcement of the Radcliffe award. In the western districts, now in Pakistan, Hindu and Sikh villages and neighborhoods were attacked, their inhabitants killed, expelled, or forced to flee, and their property looted or occupied. In the eastern districts, now in India, Muslim villages and neighborhoods were attacked in similar ways. In many cases, news of an attack in one place was followed within days by reprisals elsewhere, and the stories of the survivors who arrived in refugee camps often provoked further violence.

    The violence took several forms. In rural districts, large armed groups, often organized on the basis of village or kinship connections, attacked neighboring villages, killing men, abducting women, and burning homes. In towns and cities, crowds attacked neighborhoods, set fire to shops and houses, and killed individuals found in the streets. Trains and road convoys carrying refugees were attacked, sometimes with extraordinary loss of life. In some places, the attackers were joined or supported by members of the police and armed forces, and in many places police stood by or failed to act. Princely states in the East Punjab region, including Patiala, Kapurthala, and Faridkot, have been identified by historians as places from which armed groups operated, though the extent of the rulers' own responsibility remains a matter of scholarly examination.

    The violence was neither uniform nor inevitable in every locality. In some districts, local leaders organized to protect their neighbors, and in others communities negotiated the safe passage of minorities. Records and testimony describe Muslim neighbors who sheltered Hindu and Sikh families, Sikh and Hindu neighbors who protected Muslims, and local officials who risked their lives to keep order. But these acts of protection took place against an overwhelming trend. By the end of September, many districts that had been mixed for generations had been almost entirely emptied of one or more communities. In the canal colonies, once the destination of migrants from across Punjab, the Hindu and Sikh farmers who had settled there fled to the east, while Muslims in East Punjab left for the west.

    Reconstructing these events means attending to particular places. In Lahore, which had been the scene of arson and killing since the spring, the old city's Hindu and Sikh neighborhoods were largely emptied of their inhabitants during August, and the city's Muslim population was augmented by arrivals from the east. In Amritsar, Muslims were attacked and expelled during the same weeks, and the city was transformed into a predominantly Hindu and Sikh city. In the countryside of Sheikhupura, Gujranwala, Lyallpur, Jullundur, Ludhiana, and Patiala, accounts of attacks on villages and trains were reported to the authorities and recorded in the records of relief and military organizations. Neighborhoods and villages that had existed for generations were transformed or abandoned within weeks.

    Responsibility for these events cannot be assigned to any one community. All three of the main communities of Punjab suffered grievously, and all three included people who committed violence. Historians who study the violence stress the importance of identifying specific groups, local organizers, and conditions rather than describing the events as the product of collective religious hatred. They also note that leaders on all sides issued statements calling for peace while organizations associated with their parties mobilized armed supporters, and that the relationship between political rhetoric and local violence remains an area of active research. The evidence for individual incidents varies in quality, and numbers of victims in particular incidents are often contested. What is not contested is that, in a few weeks in the late summer and autumn of 1947, many hundreds of thousands of people in Punjab were killed and millions were forced to leave their homes.

    The destruction of mixed settlements also had an economic dimension that was not incidental to the violence. In agricultural districts, expelling a household changed access to fields, livestock, standing crops, wells, and tenancy claims; in towns, it could transfer shops, workshops, rented rooms, and inventories to new occupants. These incentives do not explain every murder, but they show why attacks sometimes selected particular streets, market properties, or communities whose members were visibly prosperous. Testimonies recorded later often recall the identity of a protecting neighbor as vividly as the identity of an attacker. Those recollections cannot substitute for a district-wide count, yet they warn against a story in which every resident automatically became an aggressor. Historians can compare property records, compensation files, and survivor accounts to reconstruct how violence and material appropriation overlapped, while noting that the administrative archives commonly recorded the resulting vacancy more clearly than the coercion that had produced it.

    Section 86.7

    Beyond Punjab: Violence, Flight, and Uneven Experiences in Bengal, Delhi, and Sindh

    Partition violence varied by place, and understanding the variation is essential to avoiding the assumption that Punjab's pattern was repeated everywhere. In Bengal, violence was concentrated in 1946 and in the earlier months of 1947, and the days around independence were relatively calm in Calcutta. Mohandas Gandhi, who had been in Noakhali, came to Calcutta in August and stayed in a Muslim house in a neighborhood where violence had occurred. A joint appearance with the Muslim League leader H. S. Suhrawardy and the days of fraternization that followed were widely reported, though violence returned in Calcutta in early September. In rural Bengal, insecurity was persistent rather than catastrophic. Many Hindus left East Bengal gradually, in waves that followed incidents of violence and economic pressure, and many Muslims left West Bengal and Bihar for East Pakistan over the following years.

    Delhi, the capital of the new Indian Dominion, experienced a sudden and profound violent upheaval in September 1947. As Hindu and Sikh refugees poured into the city from Punjab bringing accounts of what they had experienced, armed groups attacked Muslim neighborhoods. Many Muslims fled to refugee camps at sites such as Purana Qila and Humayun's Tomb, or left for Pakistan. The Muslim population of the city fell dramatically over the following years, from roughly a third of the population before Partition to a small fraction of it in the 1951 census, and entire areas were resettled with refugees. The Indian government, with the strong support of Nehru, Patel, and Mountbatten, eventually restored order in Delhi, and Gandhi's fast in January 1948, which was aimed at restoring communal harmony and securing a pledge by communal organizations to protect Delhi's Muslims, was among the most significant moral interventions of the period. His assassination on 30 January by a member of a Hindu nationalist group, motivated in part by anger at his stance on Partition and Muslims, confirmed the depth of the crisis.

    Sindh offers a third pattern. Sindh was a Muslim-majority province with a large and economically influential Hindu minority, concentrated in cities such as Karachi, Hyderabad, and Sukkur, and traditionally less affected by the violence of Punjab. In the months after independence, many Hindus remained in Sindh, but the arrival of large numbers of Muslim refugees from India, the designation of Karachi as the federal capital, and incidents of violence, notably in Karachi in January 1948, led most Sindhi Hindus to leave over the following months and years. Many migrated by sea and rail to Bombay, Gujarat, and Rajasthan, where Sindhi communities took shape in places such as Ulhasnagar and Gandhidham. The Sindhi Hindu experience differed from Punjab's: it was less a massacre-driven flight than a departure shaped by political and economic changes, but it was no less irreversible.

    Despite the scale of migration, Partition did not produce complete religious separation. Substantial communities remained in place. Many Muslims stayed in India, in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Hyderabad, Gujarat, and elsewhere, and Hindus and Sikhs remained in Pakistan, particularly in Sindh and in East Bengal, where a considerable Hindu minority continued to live. Their reasons varied: attachment to land and homes, lack of resources, belief that the violence would pass, and in some cases conviction that their homeland was where they lived. Their position was often precarious, and they faced pressure, discrimination, and sometimes violence. Their survival shows that the story of Partition cannot be summarized as a total exchange of populations, and it shaped the legal and political questions of citizenship and minority rights that the successor states had to confront.

    Delhi provides a particularly revealing comparison because displacement took place both into and out of the same city. Research on its post-Partition streets and public buildings shows Muslims seeking safety in protected spaces at the same time that Hindu and Sikh refugees arrived and needed emergency shelter. Historical monuments and mosques became contested places of temporary habitation; municipal officials had to decide whether an occupation was a necessity, a trespass, or a threat to the rights of a religious community. These decisions were not uniform across the city, and the arrival of refugees did not erase the continuing claims of Muslim residents who hoped to remain. In Sindh, a different chronology emerged: many Hindu merchant and professional households initially explored whether they could stay, while mounting insecurity, disturbed commercial relations, and migration among kin networks contributed to later departure. The comparison makes clear why a single nationwide migration date cannot capture the social process of Partition.

    Section 86.8

    The Failure of Protection: Police, Military Forces, Government, and Humanitarian Intervention

    The authorities that were supposed to protect civilians during the transfer of power were weakened by the very process of Partition. British officials, who had controlled the machinery of government, were leaving, and many of those who stayed served new governments to which they owed no long loyalty. District officers and police, drawn from all communities, were divided by suspicion; many Muslim officers opted for Pakistan and many Hindu and Sikh officers for India, and in the weeks of transition, officers were often posted away from the places they knew. The armed forces were being divided, and units were being reorganized along lines of religion while still on duty.

    The principal attempt to protect Punjab was the Punjab Boundary Force, formed to maintain order in twelve districts of the province during the transition. It began operations on 1 August 1947 under the command of Major General T. W. Rees, with Indian and Pakistani officers on his staff and a force whose stated strength varies considerably across accounts. The National Army Museum gives about 55,000, but a specialist study of deployment records argues that actual personnel never exceeded roughly 25,000 even after reinforcements; these are not interchangeable measures of operational strength. It was intended to be a neutral instrument under the joint control of the two Dominions. In practice, it was overwhelmed. The area it covered was vast and the violence was widespread, with attacks on villages, trains, and roads occurring simultaneously across many districts. Its units were mixed by religion and its soldiers were exposed to accusations of partiality, and in some units the loyalty of troops to the force's neutral mandate was strained as they heard of attacks on their own communities. Command arrangements were uncertain: the force answered to a Joint Defence Council whose members represented two governments with diverging interests, and the political leaders were by early September unable to agree on how it should operate. The Force was dissolved on 1 September 1947, and the governments of India and Pakistan assumed responsibility for security in their respective territories, with military evacuation organizations created to escort refugees.

    Several factors explain the failure. Intelligence was inadequate: the authorities did not foresee the scale of what would occur, and they often learned of attacks after they happened. The number of troops was too small for the area, and the troops were exhausted by constant movement. Command was divided, and the force operated within a landscape in which the boundary had only just been announced and in which responsibility for particular districts shifted from one government to another. Politically, there was pressure from both governments to prioritize the protection of their own co-religionists, and trust between the governments eroded rapidly. These features indicate that the collapse of protection was in part structural, a product of the speed and design of the transfer.

    It would be a mistake, however, to treat administrative breakdown as a complete explanation or as absolving those who committed violence. The attacks were carried out by identifiable individuals and groups, some of whom were organized and armed, and some of whom were supported by members of the police, military, or local political networks. Officials and political leaders at the highest levels issued statements calling for peace, but there is also evidence that some local officials and politicians ignored or encouraged violence. Historians therefore emphasize both structural conditions and individual agency, and examine the specific decisions of officials, commanders, and political actors rather than attributing events to impersonal collapse.

    Alongside this failure, protection and rescue took many forms. Local officials, village headmen, and religious leaders, including Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh figures, sheltered neighbors and organized safe passage. Volunteers from voluntary organizations, social workers, and members of political groups established relief committees, and Gandhi and his associates worked for peace in Calcutta, Noakhali, and Delhi. After September, the governments of India and Pakistan cooperated, in uneven ways, in organizing military convoys and train evacuations for refugees. These efforts were not sufficient to prevent the scale of the catastrophe, but they demonstrate that coexistence and care were not entirely absent even in the worst weeks, and they form part of the history of Partition alongside the violence.

    The discrepancy between published troop-strength figures illustrates the problem of using military statistics without asking what they count. An institutional overview describes the Punjab Boundary Force as about 55,000 strong, while a specialist study of army documentation argues that the numbers actually available for deployment never exceeded roughly 25,000 after reinforcement. Scholars should therefore distinguish nominal or broadly associated strength from effective formations and personnel on the ground. Even the lower figure should not be treated as an explanation by itself. The force confronted attacks over long stretches of road and railway, often outside the districts to which it had been assigned, and its mobility and intelligence were limited. Studies of its operations show escort work and refugee-camp monitoring alongside failed attempts to suppress violence. The historical judgment must weigh these documented activities against the state’s fundamental inability to protect large populations, while recognizing that disbandment transferred responsibility before stability had been restored.

    Historical Debate

    Was the Scale of Partition Violence Preventable?

    The question: Could a different timetable, different arrangements, or more effective political and military action have prevented the scale of violence that accompanied Partition, or was mass violence an unavoidable consequence of dividing the subcontinent?

    Interpretation A — Emphasis on British timetable and administrative failure: Some historians argue that the compressed timetable for British withdrawal, the lack of planning for the protection of minorities, the withholding of the boundary awards until after independence, and the decision to rely on a force that could not command trust made large-scale violence far more likely than it need have been. On this view, a longer transition, earlier announcement of the boundary, or better preparation of security forces could have reduced the scale of the catastrophe.
    Interpretation B — Emphasis on political mobilization, organized violence, and local conditions: Other historians emphasize that the violence was driven by the mobilization of communities by political parties and armed groups, the existence of organized militias, local conflicts over land and power, and the accumulated experience of violence in 1946. On this view, even a better-prepared administration might have faced violence on a large scale, and responsibility lies substantially with those who organized attacks and with leaders who did not restrain their followers.

    Evidence: Both interpretations draw on official correspondence, military records, memoirs, and testimony. Evidence for the first includes records of the timetable and of the Boundary Force's limitations; evidence for the second includes records of organized attacks, the existence of armed volunteer groups, and patterns of expulsion that suggest planning.

    Why it matters: The question affects how responsibility is assigned and how the decision to partition is understood. It also requires distinguishing between the decision to partition, which many historians consider a political choice, and the particular scale and forms of violence that followed, which depended on additional decisions and failures.

    Refugee Movements, Displacement, and the Struggle for Survival

    §§86.9–86.12 · 1947–early 1950s
    Section 86.9

    The Great Migration: Refugee Trains, Columns, and the Partition of Punjab

    By the late summer of 1947, millions of people in Punjab were on the move. Some left because their villages had been attacked, others because of rumors that attack was imminent, and others because their neighbors, officials, or armed groups made it clear that they were no longer welcome. Some left reluctantly after waiting for weeks, taking what they could carry, and others fled with nothing. The migration was largely a response to violence and fear rather than a planned exchange, though by September the two governments had begun to organize it, and by the autumn the movement of populations had acquired a logic of its own: as one community left, the remaining members of the other community in the neighboring area felt compelled to leave as well.

    The migration took place by several means. Railways carried the largest numbers, and trains crowded with refugees, including people riding on roofs and clinging to the sides of carriages, ran between Lahore and Amritsar and on other routes. Some trains were attacked, and some arrived at their destinations with most of their passengers killed; accounts of such trains were reported widely, and they became a symbol of the horror of the period. Road convoys and long columns of people on foot, called kafilas, formed another major mode of movement. In September and October, columns of many thousands walked along the Grand Trunk Road and the country roads of Punjab, carrying belongings on carts and animals, accompanied by military escorts where they could be provided. One of the largest, which moved from the Lyallpur region to the east, was reported to contain several hundred thousand people stretched over many miles. Many people died on these journeys from violence, disease, exhaustion, and lack of water and food. Families were separated, children were lost, and the elderly and the sick often could not keep pace.

    Historians distinguish among kinds of movement. Voluntary relocation, in which families chose to move in order to be with their community or to take up employment, occurred alongside flight from threatened violence and outright forced expulsion. The line between them was not sharp: a family that decided to leave after neighbors were attacked had made a choice under duress. The distinctions matter because they bear on questions of responsibility, on the legal status of the migrants and their property, and on how the experience has been remembered. For many migrants, the experience was one of dispossession and of being made refugees by forces they could not control.

    Estimates of the scale of displacement vary. A figure of between ten and fifteen million displaced people is widely cited in scholarship, though the total depends on which regions are included and which years are counted. India’s 1951 census counted approximately 7.2 million people classified as Partition-displaced from Pakistan, while a Pakistani 1951 census bulletin reported about 7.15 million displaced from India. These are census categories at specified dates, not a complete count of every journey or a substitute for estimates of all those uprooted across the wider period. Within Punjab, a very large share of the movement took place within a few months, between August and the end of the year, producing an almost complete exchange of Muslim and Hindu-Sikh populations across the new boundary. Mortality estimates are even less certain and are discussed in §86.19. In all cases, figures should be understood as informed approximations with margins of error that historians continue to debate.

    By the end of 1947, the demographic map of Punjab had been transformed. The western districts that were now part of Pakistan had very few Hindus and Sikhs, and the eastern districts that were part of India had very few Muslims, with exceptions such as the princely state of Malerkotla, where the Muslim population was largely protected. Cities and villages that had been characterized by mixed populations were homogeneous, with new populations of refugees occupying the houses and lands of those who had left. The speed and completeness of this change distinguishes Punjab from other regions of Partition and explains the central place of Punjab in Partition memory.

    The statistical evidence also captures only an imperfect still photograph of events that unfolded in stages. India’s 1951 census classified about 7.2 million people as displaced from Pakistan; a Pakistani bulletin counted about 7.15 million displaced from India. Those figures reflect census definitions, a particular recording date, and survival and residence at enumeration. They should not simply be added to another estimate based on border-crossing movements, especially where people moved repeatedly, returned, or were missed by census enumeration. In Punjab, official evacuation routes and military assistance organized journeys that had already begun through private networks. A family might first seek shelter with relatives in a nearby village, proceed to a camp, and only subsequently cross the border. For students, separating the initial flight, the formal crossing, and eventual settlement is a necessary first step toward understanding why different historians produce different counts.

    Everyday Life and Social History

    A Punjab Refugee Family on a 1947 Journey

    This is a composite reconstruction. It combines patterns documented in relief records, memoirs, and oral testimony and does not describe a single named family. It contains no direct quotations.

    A farming family in the canal colonies of West Punjab, such as one that had settled in the Lyallpur region a generation earlier, would typically have learned of attacks on neighboring villages before deciding to leave. The decision was seldom made at once. Elders weighed the land, the harvest, and the animals against rumors of attack, while younger men might join a village defense group. When the family left, it left in a hurry, carrying what could be loaded on a cart or on the backs of people and animals: grain, bedding, cooking vessels, jewelry sewn into clothing, and documents if they had any.

    The journey might take days or weeks. The family would join a column on the road, where food and water were hard to find and where disease spread easily. Children could be lost in the crowd or separated when a convoy was attacked. Older people could not keep pace and might be left with relatives or die along the way. Women feared for their safety, and families sometimes made painful decisions about how to protect them. Arrival at a camp at the end of the journey brought safety but also loss of home, land, and community, and the start of a long process of seeking registration, shelter, and resettlement.

    Section 86.10

    The Eastern Migration: Bengal, Assam, Sindh, and Unequal Patterns of Displacement

    In the east, displacement was slower, more recurrent, and less concentrated in time. There was no sudden exchange comparable to Punjab's. Many Hindus in East Bengal stayed in 1947, and many Muslims in West Bengal stayed as well. But over the following years, repeated waves of migration took place, often following incidents of violence, rumors, economic pressure, or political developments. By the early 1950s, millions of Hindus had moved from East Bengal to West Bengal, Assam, and Tripura, and several hundred thousand Muslims moved in the opposite direction, though the exact numbers depend on definitions and on whether later years are counted.

    The decision to move or to remain depended on many factors. Landholding was a major one: families who owned land or businesses were reluctant to abandon them, while landless laborers and the poor might have had less to lose but also fewer resources to travel. Family connections across the border could provide a destination, and the existence of relatives or caste associations in Calcutta or elsewhere in West Bengal made migration easier. Language and culture mattered; for many Bengali Hindus and Muslims, the shared Bengali language and culture made the idea of leaving difficult. Uncertainty about whether the minority would be safe in the new state weighed heavily, and the news of violence in other places, including Punjab, increased fear. Among Hindus in East Bengal, middle-class professionals and upper-caste families were often the first to leave, while many Scheduled Caste and lower-caste Hindus, who depended on local agricultural and fishing economies, stayed longer. Political leaders from this community, such as Jogendra Nath Mandal, who had joined Pakistan's first cabinet, hoped to protect their interests in Pakistan; Mandal himself resigned and left for India in 1950, declaring that the position of Hindus was untenable.

    The eastern migration took several directions. West Bengal absorbed the largest number, and Calcutta and its suburbs grew rapidly as refugees settled in camps and then in squatter colonies on vacant land. Assam and Tripura also received large numbers; in Tripura, a princely state that acceded to India in 1949, the refugee population came to rival and eventually exceed the local one. Muslim migration to East Pakistan came from West Bengal, Bihar, and Assam, and the Urdu-speaking Biharis who settled in East Pakistan would later face difficult circumstances. In Sindh, as noted in §86.7, Hindu departure continued for years, and the scale of the Sindhi exodus was in some ways comparable to the eastern flow in its duration.

    Migration did not end in August or December 1947. Riots in East Bengal in February 1950, notably in the Barisal region and in Dacca, led to a new exodus of Hindus, and reprisals in Calcutta and parts of West Bengal produced a countermovement of Muslims. These disturbances led to a crisis between the two governments, with two ministers resigning from the Indian cabinet in protest at what they considered inadequate firmness. The Nehru–Liaquat Agreement of 8 April 1950 committed both governments to protect minorities, to establish minority commissions, and to allow migrants to return for a period to dispose of their property. The agreement reduced tensions for a time, but it did not end migration, and refugees continued to cross the border throughout the 1950s and later. The 1950 events are treated here as consequences of the unfinished Partition rather than as a new subject.

    Everyday Life and Social History

    A Bengal Household Confronting Migration or Remaining

    This is a composite reconstruction drawn from patterns in refugee-registration files, memoirs, and oral histories. It describes no single historical household and contains no quotations.

    A Hindu household in a market town of East Bengal in 1947 might include a schoolteacher or lawyer, a small landholding worked by sharecroppers, and relatives in Calcutta. The decision about whether to leave was not made on one day. Elders might say that the family had lived in this place for generations and would remain, while the younger members, hearing of incidents in other districts and of colleagues who had already left, might urge departure. Often one member of the family, a young man or a daughter's husband, left first to find work and housing in Calcutta, and the rest followed later, or did not.

Some families moved to East Bengal with relatives or on the strength of employment offers, while others stayed on lands and in neighborhoods where their families had lived for generations. Both households had to weigh the possibility of a return, the difficulty of disposing of property, and the knowledge that those who left might not be allowed to come back. The pattern that emerged in Bengal, of people moving in stages and sometimes returning, was a product of these calculations.

    Section 86.11

    Refugee Camps, Relief Operations, and the Humanitarian Emergency

    The arrival of millions of destitute people created a humanitarian emergency that neither government was prepared to meet. In Punjab and Delhi, tens of thousands of refugees gathered at railway stations and in open spaces, then in organized camps. The largest in India, at Kurukshetra, held several hundred thousand people at its peak, and in Delhi camps were set up at historic sites such as Purana Qila and Humayun's Tomb. In Pakistan, camps and reception centers were established in Lahore, Karachi, and other towns, and large numbers of refugees were housed in public buildings, abandoned houses, and open spaces. Conditions were harsh. Shelter was often inadequate and food was scarce. Sanitation could not cope with the numbers, and cholera, dysentery, and other infectious diseases spread. Medical staff were few, supplies were limited, and winter brought additional hardship.

    Relief operations involved governments, the military, voluntary bodies, and local communities. India created a Ministry of Relief and Rehabilitation in 1947, and Pakistan established its own ministry responsible for refugees and rehabilitation. The armies of both countries, and the military evacuation organizations created after the Boundary Force's dissolution, provided transport, escorts, and logistical capability. Voluntary associations, including religious organizations such as Gurdwara committees, the Red Cross societies, political parties, trade unions, and women's groups, ran food kitchens and medical posts and tried to help families find lost relatives. Local residents in many places fed and housed refugees, and some of the most effective relief was done by people with no official position. International humanitarian observers were present but limited in number, and the main burden fell on the new governments and on communities within South Asia.

    Certain groups were especially vulnerable. Children who had been separated from their families were taken into camps and, in many cases, into the care of voluntary organizations. Elderly people, pregnant women, the disabled, and widows without family support faced particular hardship, and mortality among infants and the elderly in the camps was high. Women who had experienced sexual violence needed medical and psychological care that the camps could not provide. The efforts to reunite separated families, through lists, notices, and the work of volunteers, achieved some successes, but many families were never reunited.

    The camps also reveal how the new states began to classify and manage populations. Refugees were counted, registered, and given identification, and their claims to assistance depended on the documents they could produce. Registration created records, and these records, today held in archives, are valuable for historians, but they also reflect administrative priorities. Categories such as "displaced person" and "evacuee" determined who would receive help and who would not, and they sometimes excluded those who did not fit, such as people who had crossed the border more than once or who could not prove their identity.

    Emergency shelter turned public architecture into a record of social disruption. In Delhi, scholarship documents the temporary occupation of forts, monuments, and religious structures by people who were displaced within the city or newly arrived from across the border. Purana Qila and the area around Humayun’s Tomb illustrate how a site could function first as protection for one population and subsequently as shelter for another, with the change exposing disputes over custody and rights. Relief agencies had to provide water, sanitation, cooking facilities, and medical attention in spaces designed for none of those purposes. Camp registers might count a family as housed while revealing little about the fear, illness, cramped conditions, or uncertainty about work that continued after admission. The endurance of some camps well beyond 1947 also challenges the division between immediate humanitarian response and long-term rehabilitation: shelter could become a prolonged and politically contested form of residence.

    Section 86.12

    Resettlement and Rehabilitation: Rebuilding Households, Livelihoods, and Communities

    As the emergency passed, the governments faced the larger problem of resettlement. Millions of people needed housing, land, work, and income, and the states had to decide how to allocate the property left by those who had gone in the other direction. In both countries, the abandoned property of departed minorities, including houses, shops, farms, and factories, formed the principal resource for rehabilitation, an arrangement discussed further in §86.15. Refugees were allotted houses and land in accordance with claims they registered, and temporary shelters gradually gave way to more permanent settlements.

    In India, the rehabilitation of refugees from West Pakistan was relatively rapid, in part because Punjabi refugees were able to occupy the land left by Muslims in East Punjab and could be moved within a defined area. Land was allocated to farmers on the basis of the land they had left, a complicated process that required verification of claims. Urban refugees were housed in new colonies, many of them built on the edges of Delhi, such as Lajpat Nagar, Rajendra Nagar, and Tilak Nagar, and in new townships such as Nilokheri and Faridabad, created as cooperative ventures. East Punjab began building a new capital at Chandigarh. Refugees became shopkeepers, traders, transporters, and small manufacturers, and many Punjabi refugees established businesses that transformed the economies of Delhi and other cities. The rehabilitation of eastern refugees was slower and less successful: the arrival of people in West Bengal was continuous, there was no equivalent exchange of land, and many ended up in camps and squatter colonies for years.

    In Pakistan, the arrival of refugees from India, the muhajirs, transformed the cities of Karachi, Lahore, and Hyderabad in Sindh. Karachi grew from a mid-sized port into a national capital and the largest city, with a large population of Urdu-speaking refugees. In Punjab, refugees from East Punjab settled in the lands and houses left by Hindus and Sikhs, and the province was relatively homogeneous by 1948. Pakistan's rehabilitation effort was strained by a smaller administrative base and fewer resources, and relations between refugees and the established population, particularly in Sindh, produced lasting tensions that shaped politics in the following decades.

    The economic outcomes were unequal. Some refugees, particularly those with skills, education, or capital, and those who were able to obtain land or premises, rebuilt successful livelihoods and in some cases became prominent in business, the professions, and politics. Others, including landless laborers, widows, and people without documents, experienced prolonged poverty, insecurity, and administrative exclusion. Claims procedures favored those who could prove ownership and navigate bureaucracy. Conflicts arose between newcomers and established residents over housing, jobs, and land, and these conflicts affected the politics of both countries. Rehabilitation thus changed the social structure of cities and regions and set patterns of advantage and disadvantage that persisted.

    Division 4

    Gender, Property, and the Reconstruction of Citizenship

    §§86.13–86.16 · 1947–1950s
    Section 86.13

    Gendered Violence: Abduction, Sexual Assault, Forced Marriage, and Family Separation

    Partition inflicted particular forms of violence on women. Women were raped, abducted, forced into marriage and conversion, and in many cases killed. These acts were committed by men of all communities, and women of all communities were among the victims. They were sometimes organized and systematic, forming part of attacks on villages and convoys, and sometimes opportunistic. Contemporary officials and later scholars have recognized that the violence had a symbolic dimension: for those who perpetrated it, the bodies of women were treated as markers of community honor, and an attack on women was an attack on the community. The same symbolism shaped the responses of families and communities, as discussed below.

    The scholarship on this subject was transformed by the work of Urvashi Butalia, Ritu Menon, and Kamla Bhasin, whose research in the 1980s and 1990s, based on interviews with survivors and on archival records, showed that the history of Partition could not be understood without the experiences of women. Their work, together with that of later historians, demonstrated that women's experiences had been largely absent from political and diplomatic accounts, and that the silence had many sources: stigma, trauma, family pressure, and the absence of institutions through which women could speak. Later research has extended the study to include women in different regions and communities, and to examine women's agency and the complexity of their experiences.

    The forms of violence and family response were various. In addition to sexual assault and abduction, there are documented cases of violence within families and communities, in which women were killed by relatives in order, as the perpetrators or the survivors later described it, to prevent them from being captured or dishonored. Accounts exist of women who died by suicide, including at Thoa Khalsa in March 1947 and in other places, and these accounts have been interpreted and commemorated in different ways, with some presenting them as acts of sacrifice and others emphasizing the coercion and pressure under which they occurred. Historians treat such accounts carefully, recognizing that they reach us through memory, family tradition, and community commemoration, and that the line between choice and coercion is difficult to draw. Abducted women were often taken to the other side of the border, married or held in households, and in some cases forcibly converted.

    The reconstruction of these experiences raises methodological problems. The records are incomplete, and many incidents were never reported. Survivors were often unwilling or unable to speak, and the testimony that exists was usually given many years later and reflects the accumulated influence of family expectations, public discourse, and the passage of time. Some records, such as those of recovery operations, were created by states that had their own purposes. Historians therefore need to read these sources with care for what they omit, to avoid sensationalizing testimony, and to avoid reducing women solely to victims. Women also acted as agents: they resisted, negotiated, escaped, sheltered others, and, in many cases, rebuilt their lives under extraordinarily difficult circumstances.

    Source criticism is especially necessary when a woman's experience survives only through a relative's recollection or through a recovery official's form. The official may record the woman's presumed religious community and where she was found while omitting how she understood the events, whether she had been threatened by relatives, and what future she wanted. A family's account can contain silences shaped by shame, grief, or fear of public judgment; a later interview may reveal them only partially. Historians of gender therefore read administrative categories alongside testimony and ask whether apparently protective practices also constrained women's autonomy. They also examine labor and care: women who survived migration frequently organized food, protected children, found paid work, or negotiated access to relief, activities often invisible in narratives centered on political leaders. This approach neither assumes complete agency amid violence nor reduces women to passive symbols of communities.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Abducted Person and Recovery

    In the official usage of the two Dominions, an abducted person was a woman or child who had been taken from one community and was living with a member of another community after the outbreak of violence. Recovery was the policy of locating such persons and returning them to their families or to the territory of their original community, if necessary by compulsion.

    The terms are historically significant because they were defined by the state. They classified women by their religion and by the circumstances of their separation, and they did not always match the wishes or self-understanding of the women concerned. Many scholars therefore place the words in quotation marks or analyze them as terms of governance rather than neutral description.

    Section 86.14

    Recovery and Repatriation: Women's Agency and the Policies of India and Pakistan

    The governments of India and Pakistan recognized the problem of abducted women early, and cooperation began in 1947. An inter-Dominion conference in December 1947 produced an agreement that each government would work to locate and return abducted women and children to their home communities, and social workers, police, and military personnel were assigned to carry out recovery. Prominent figures in the effort included Mridula Sarabhai, Rameshwari Nehru, and Kamlaben Patel in India, and women's organizations and officials in Pakistan. The work was carried on by joint teams, which traveled to villages and towns, searched for women, and arranged for their transport across the border.

    The Indian Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act of 1949 provided legal authority for recovery in India. It defined abducted persons in terms of women and children of one community who had been separated since early 1947 and were living in another community, and it created a system of officers and tribunals empowered to determine the status of women and to order their recovery. Pakistan adopted comparable arrangements. Operations continued into the 1950s, and official claims indicate that tens of thousands of women were recovered. Figures vary: governments at the time estimated that many tens of thousands of women had been abducted, and scholars have noted that a published estimate of around thirty thousand persons recovered across both countries over roughly four years depends on official counts, varying reporting periods, and the state’s definition of an abducted person. Recovery was neither identical to family reunification nor proof of the recovered person’s consent.

    The central question for historians is whether state-defined recovery corresponded to the wishes of the women concerned. Evidence suggests that it often did not. Many women had been separated from their families for months, and some had established new households, borne children, or married. For some, return to their former communities was frightening because of the stigma attached to women who had been raped or lived with men of another religion, and because families might refuse to take them back. Some women refused to be recovered, resisted officials, or fled. The policy was based on the assumption that a woman belonged with her original religious community and nation, and officials did not always accept a woman's statement that she wished to remain. In some cases women were returned to families that did not want them, and children born of unions across communities became subjects of dispute.

    The records of the recovery operations present an unusual source problem. They were produced by states and social workers who were engaged in the recovery effort and who believed in its necessity. They record the number of recoveries, the places and procedures, and, in some cases, the views of officials about the women's state of mind. The women's own voices are rarely preserved directly, and when they appear, they are often mediated by officials. Later interviews with women, family members, and social workers add further perspectives, though they are also shaped by memory and the conditions under which they were recorded. Historians have therefore treated recovery as a case study of how the state could act on women's bodies in the name of protection.

    The debates surrounding recovery legislation make the conflict between humanitarian purpose and coercive procedure visible in the historical record. Officials argued that families separated by violence deserved help in finding missing relatives; critics questioned powers that could remove a woman from the household in which she was then living without treating her expressed preference as decisive. The difficulty was heightened by disputes over marriage, conversion, and the status of children born after abduction. A recent scholarly examination of recovery records reports about 30,000 recoveries across the two countries over roughly four years, but this measure aggregates varied individual outcomes and cannot demonstrate that each return was welcomed. Some people wanted rescue; others feared return, separation from children, or rejection by kin. A serious assessment of policy must recognize both realities and the uneven evidence for them, rather than interpreting a recovered-person total as a simple measure of justice achieved.

    Historical Debate

    Did Recovery and Rehabilitation Restore Justice to Survivors?

    The question: Did the policies of recovery, rehabilitation, and property restitution adopted by India and Pakistan restore justice to those who had suffered, or did they create new inequalities?

    Interpretation A — Policies as necessary and partially successful responses: Some historians and contemporary officials emphasize that the new states faced an overwhelming emergency, with limited resources, and that recovery operations rescued tens of thousands of women and children from dangerous situations, while rehabilitation settled millions of refugees. On this view, the policies were imperfect but represented serious attempts to protect and support survivors and to stabilize new states.
    Interpretation B — Policies as reproducing hierarchies and overriding individual choice: Other scholars emphasize that recovery overrode the wishes of many women and treated them as objects of community and national honor, that rehabilitation favored men, landholders, and those with documents, and that evacuee-property rules legitimized dispossession. On this view, the policies reinforced patriarchal and communal assumptions and created inequalities that persisted.

    Evidence: Evidence includes the text of legislation and policy, recovery and rehabilitation files, statistical reports, and testimony from women, families, and officials. The first view relies more on official aims and outcomes; the second relies on testimony and on analysis of how procedures operated in practice.

    Why it matters: The question shapes how the achievements and limits of the early states are assessed, and it raises the broader question of how states should respond to mass violence: the competing claims of security, restitution, individual autonomy, and the protection of minorities.

    Section 86.15

    Abandoned Homes and Evacuee Property: Ownership, Compensation, and Dispossession

    The movement of millions of people left behind an immense quantity of property: houses, farms, shops, factories, religious buildings, livestock, and movable goods. Both states faced the questions of who owned this property, who should manage it, and how it should be used. In the immediate aftermath, abandoned property was often seized or occupied informally by neighbors, refugees, and others, and looting was widespread. Governments responded by creating legal and administrative machinery. Provincial authorities in East and West Punjab issued ordinances in autumn 1947 to take control of property left by evacuees, and central measures followed in both countries. The term evacuee was applied in India to persons who had left for Pakistan, and in Pakistan comparable terms were used for those who had gone to India.

    India's central law on the subject was the Administration of Evacuee Property Act of 1950, which built on earlier ordinances. It established Custodians of Evacuee Property, empowered to take possession of property declared to be evacuee property, to manage it, and to dispose of it, and it created procedures for claims and appeals. Pakistan created parallel institutions. The property under their control was used to rehabilitate refugees, by allotment, sale, or lease, and the revenue was used to compensate those who had lost property on the other side. Later legislation, including India's Displaced Persons (Compensation and Rehabilitation) Act of 1954, created a system for pooling evacuee property and distributing compensation. The arrangements that began as emergency measures thus became permanent legal structures.

    The system raised difficult problems of ownership. In many cases, families did not have documents proving title: land in rural Punjab was held in complicated ways, much of it by customary rights and tenancy, and records were often kept in district offices on the other side of the border. Possession was often confused with legal title. Claims depended on the testimony of witnesses and on documents that many refugees had left behind or lost. The system also had to deal with the fact that the property left in each country was not equal in value to the property left in the other, so that full compensation was impossible. Disputes arose among claimants, between claimants and the state, and between refugees and the minority residents who had remained in place and whose property might be declared evacuee property if they were found to have left temporarily.

    The effects were unequal. Landowners and urban merchants with substantial claims and access to officials fared better than tenants and agricultural laborers, who often had no formal claims to land. Women's property rights depended on family arrangements, and widows and unmarried women were often excluded or received less. Muslims who remained in India and Hindus who remained in Pakistan, as well as those who traveled temporarily, sometimes found that their property had been classified as evacuee property and taken over. For many, the system brought dispossession, and for others, a new start; in either case, it reshaped patterns of landholding and urban property ownership in both countries and bound the history of Partition to the law of property.

    The statutory definition of an evacuee also reveals how an emergency could become a durable administrative identity. India's Administration of Evacuee Property Act of 1950 empowered custodians to take charge of property connected with specified movements and circumstances; it provided mechanisms for management and claims, but its scope and exclusions must be read in the text rather than assumed to have applied uniformly to every region. The act's original territorial coverage expressly excluded several eastern and northeastern areas, underscoring how western and eastern displacement produced different legal regimes. In practice, ownership could be complicated by a family whose members had moved in different directions, a tenant whose landlord had departed, or an owner who intended to return but could no longer enter the property. Such cases demonstrate why property administration was also a politics of belonging: official classification affected whether a person's absence appeared temporary, legitimate, or evidence that a home could be reassigned.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Evacuee and Evacuee Property

    In the laws of India and Pakistan, an evacuee was a person who had left, or was treated as having left, one territory for the other because of the disturbances of 1947 and afterward, and evacuee property was the property such a person had left behind. A custodian was the official empowered to take control of it.

    The terms matter because they turned a lived event, flight, into a legal status that determined whether a person could keep property. They should not be confused with refugee, which describes a person who has sought shelter and assistance. The same household could be both an evacuee in one state's law and a refugee in the other's.

    Section 86.16

    Citizenship after Partition: Nationality, Residence, Minority Rights, and Belonging

    The creation of India and Pakistan did not automatically settle every person's nationality. The Independence Act made no comprehensive provision for citizenship, and in 1947 people who lived in one Dominion but had family and property in the other, or who moved between them, had no clear legal status. Citizenship rules developed over several years, and they developed in response to migration, to the need to manage property and security, and to the aim of distinguishing permanent settlers from temporary travelers.

    The first controls were introduced in 1948. On 19 July 1948 India introduced permit controls specifically for entry from West Pakistan, not a uniform permit regime covering the eastern border. Article 6 of the Constitution, effective from 26 January 1950, used 19 July 1948 to distinguish routes to citizenship for people who had migrated from Pakistan: qualifying earlier migrants faced different conditions from people arriving subsequently, for whom registration and residence requirements applied. The constitutional provisions and later implementing arrangements must not be collapsed into a single rule applicable identically in both east and west. The provisions on citizenship in the Constitution, discussed further in Chapter 88, therefore treated early migrants more favorably than later ones, and they also created special rules for persons who had migrated to Pakistan and then returned. Pakistan's system developed along similar lines, with citizenship defined first by orders and then by the Pakistan Citizenship Act of 1951. In 1952, India and Pakistan introduced a passport and visa system for travel between them, marking the transformation of what had been an open and porous boundary into a controlled international frontier.

    These rules affected people differently. Refugees who had moved permanently were generally recognized as citizens of their destination state, though proving their status could be difficult. People who crossed the border more than once, as many did in Bengal, or who moved back and forth to manage property, faced uncertainty about whether they were evacuees or citizens. Muslims who remained in India and Hindus and Sikhs who remained in Pakistan were citizens in law but often faced suspicion and pressure, particularly when their relatives had gone to the other country. Dalit communities, such as the Scheduled Caste Hindus of East Bengal, found themselves in a particularly difficult position: B. R. Ambedkar and Jogendra Nath Mandal had each hoped that Dalits could find a place in the new states, and many later left Pakistan, though their reception in India was uneven.

    The developing citizenship regimes thus carried an implicit distinction between permanent migration and temporary travel, and between the majority community and minorities. The Nehru–Liaquat Agreement of 1950 attempted to reconcile these questions by committing both governments to equal citizenship rights for minorities, while also providing for a limited right of return. The practical reality was that the experience of belonging varied widely, and for many the meaning of citizenship was shaped as much by documents, permits, and property status as by legal declaration. Citizenship after Partition was therefore a process rather than a moment, and it was one in which the rules of the new states were still being made years after August 1947.

    The western permit system helps illustrate the unequal legal clocks of Partition. India's Influx from West Pakistan (Control) Ordinance of July 1948 regulated entry specifically from West Pakistan; it did not initially operate as a single rule for the much longer and more porous eastern border. When citizenship provisions of the Indian Constitution later used 19 July 1948 as a dividing date for migration from Pakistan, formal legal categories intersected with these existing practices. A person could be related to the same village and family network across the border, yet encounter different documentation requirements depending on the direction and timing of travel. The difference between a migration permit, a travel document, and proof of residence became consequential in disputes about property and re-entry. Explaining this uneven legal development is more accurate than portraying citizenship as instantly and uniformly assigned on Independence Day.

    Section 86.17

    Survivors, Minorities, and the Remaking of South Asian Communities

    Partition transformed the identities and institutions of the communities it touched. Those who migrated had to build new lives in unfamiliar places, often with a strong sense of loss, and many came to define themselves by the place they had left: Punjabi refugees in Delhi, Sindhis in Bombay, Bengali refugees in Calcutta, and muhajirs in Karachi each developed distinct communal identities, with their own associations, schools, newspapers, and religious institutions. Language and cuisine, festivals and family networks were carried across the border and reconstituted, and neighborhoods took names and characters from the places left behind. At the same time, refugee communities often experienced tensions with the established population, who feared competition for jobs and housing and sometimes resented newcomers.

    Those who stayed behind also had to adjust. Muslims in India, who after Partition formed a minority in a state whose leaders had opposed the Muslim League's demand, faced suspicion and had to assert their loyalty; many of their political and cultural leaders had gone to Pakistan, leaving communities with fewer institutions and resources. Hindus and Sikhs who remained in Pakistan, and Hindus in East Bengal, similarly faced a position of vulnerability, in a state defined in part by Islam. Experiences varied by class, region, and caste. Dalits and landless households, who often lacked the resources to move, bore particular burdens, while elite families with capital and connections were better able to relocate. Women who had been abducted or recovered returned to communities in which their status was uncertain. Others, including people of mixed background, converts, and those who did not fit the dominant national narratives, found it difficult to be recognized.

    The disappearance of mixed communities from particular regions, notably Punjab, did not eliminate pluralism within either state. India remained a country with a very large Muslim minority, with Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, and many other communities, and a 1950 Constitution that protected equality and freedom of religion. Its original Preamble described India as a sovereign democratic republic; the word “secular” was added to the Preamble by the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, although secular principles were already present in the constitutional settlement. Pakistan retained Hindu, Christian, Sikh, and other minorities, and a population divided by language and region, including Punjabi, Sindhi, Bengali, Pashtun, and Baloch communities. Plurality survived, even as particular forms of coexistence were lost, and the legal and political status of minorities became a question of central importance in both states.

    The rebuilding of social and cultural institutions was a major task. Refugees reestablished schools, colleges, gurdwaras, temples, mosques, trade associations, and neighborhood groups, often with considerable energy. Business communities such as Punjabi traders and Sindhi merchants rebuilt commercial networks. Cultural life was reshaped as writers, artists, and filmmakers who had come from Lahore, Calcutta, and other cities moved, and as new centers emerged. These institutions created continuity amid rupture, and they were among the means by which communities preserved memory and adjusted to new circumstances.

    Local minority experience offers a check on explanations that describe 1947 as a complete and clean exchange. Communities that remained in Delhi, parts of Sindh, East Bengal, or other areas continued to negotiate schooling, worship, access to work, and protection of property. Their situation could depend less on formal national declarations than on the behavior of a police station, municipal office, land registry, or neighboring association. Even when leaders proclaimed equal citizenship, families still had to decide whether children could safely attend a particular school or whether an absent relative's property could be defended. Religious minorities were not socially uniform: ownership, caste, gender, and language could determine whose voice local officials heard. Attention to these differences prevents both states from appearing as homogeneous national communities created automatically by a boundary award.

    Section 86.18

    Remembering Partition: Oral Histories, Literature, Film, Memorials, and Family Testimony

    For decades after 1947, Partition was remembered in private far more than in public. Official histories in both countries emphasized independence and nation-building, and the violence and displacement were treated as regrettable but secondary. Survivors often did not speak of their experiences, out of pain, shame, or the sense that no one would listen. From the 1980s onward, a growing body of scholarship, oral history, literature, and public commemoration began to change this. Historians recognized that the experiences of ordinary people, women, and those who had not been part of political elites, needed to be recorded, and projects to collect testimony from survivors, now elderly, were established in India, Pakistan, and the diaspora. The 1947 Partition Archive, housed with Stanford Libraries, and the Partition Museum in Amritsar, which opened in 2017, hold extensive collections of testimony, objects, and documents.

    Literature has played a central role in the memory of Partition. Saadat Hasan Manto, writing in Urdu, produced short stories such as "Toba Tek Singh," which use irony and compression to convey the absurdity and horror of the division. Khushwant Singh's novel Train to Pakistan, published in 1956, portrays a village in Punjab confronted with violence, and Bhisham Sahni's Tamas, in Hindi, set in Rawalpindi, examines the beginnings of communal violence. Amrita Pritam, writing in Punjabi, mourned the destruction of Punjab and the fate of women, and Qurratulain Hyder, Intizar Husain, Ismat Chughtai, and others wrote of the experiences of their communities in Urdu. In Bengal, writers and filmmakers, including Ritwik Ghatak, addressed the experience of refugees. These works represent different linguistic and regional experiences and show that Partition was remembered differently in each.

    Historians use these varied sources in different ways, and the differences matter. Contemporary records, such as official reports and letters, were created at the time and reflect the perspectives and purposes of their authors. Retrospective interviews, collected decades later, record what people remember and how they have shaped memories, and are influenced by subsequent events, family stories, and the circumstances of the interview. Fiction is not documentary evidence of particular events, but it can capture the experience, moral atmosphere, and public memory of a period. Each type of source can inform the others, but they should not be confused: a short story is not an eyewitness account, and an interview is not an archival record.

    Memory is also shaped by politics. Museums, textbooks, and anniversaries emphasize some aspects of Partition and omit others, and national narratives often frame the events in terms of heroism, loss, or blame. In recent years, there has been increased public attention in India and Pakistan to Partition memory, including commemorations and debates over how to mark the anniversary. Historians distinguish between the work of recording the experiences of survivors, which has been valuable, and the political use of those experiences in contemporary conflicts.

    An oral-history interview is evidence of how a survivor remembers an experience, not merely a delayed substitute for a missing police report. The interviewer’s questions, language, recording date, relationship to the witness, and expectations about which events matter can shape what is said. A family story may preserve the remembered route through several towns and the name of a rescuer but misremember the precise calendar date; such variation need not make the whole testimony useless. Conversely, a vivid account should not be treated as proof that the same experience was universal. Stanford Libraries' research guide brings together different oral-history projects, while the Partition Museum preserves objects donated by survivors and their families. An ordinary item carried across the border can establish something about attachment and loss that a diplomatic file cannot. Used together with dated administrative records and regional comparison, these materials broaden the archive without confusing memory, fiction, and documentary testimony.

    Section 86.19

    Interpreting Partition: Historical Responsibility, Mortality Estimates, and Competing National Memories

    Historians have debated Partition from several directions. One set of questions concerns responsibility: how much weight to give the policies of the British, who set the timetable and organized the transfer of power; to the Congress and Muslim League leadership, whose negotiations and mobilization shaped the outcome; to local and regional political organizations and armed groups; and to the successor governments that were responsible for protection and relief once the new states were established. Some historians emphasize the structural conditions of late colonial rule, including the policies of communal representation and the militarization of the 1940s. Others emphasize contingency, the decisions of particular leaders at particular moments, and the role of local actors. Most recent scholarship avoids single-cause explanations and tries to hold several levels of explanation together, from imperial policy to village-level conflict.

    A second set of questions concerns numbers. Contemporary British and Indian officials gave early estimates of deaths that were much lower than later ones, some in the range of a few hundred thousand, and later scholarship has given estimates ranging from the low hundreds of thousands to two million, with a figure around one million frequently cited. These estimates depend on different methods: some are based on official reports at the time, which were incomplete; others on demographic reconstructions that compare census populations before and after, and attempt to estimate the number of missing persons, which requires assumptions about natural mortality and migration. None is definitive, and a single precise figure should not be presented as established. The same caution applies to estimates of displacement, of abducted and recovered women, and of property lost.

    A third set of questions concerns national memory. In India, Partition is often remembered as a tragedy that divided a country and caused enormous suffering, and public memory has sometimes emphasized the violence done to Hindus and Sikhs, though scholarship has examined the experiences of Muslims as well. In Pakistan, Partition is associated with the founding of the state and with the sacrifices made to achieve it, and the violence has been remembered as part of the cost, with attention to the suffering of Muslims. In Bangladesh, the history of 1947 is intertwined with the later history of the language movement and the liberation war of 1971, and the experience of East Bengal in Partition is viewed through the subsequent separation from Pakistan. Within each country, historians differ, and regional differences, between Punjab and Bengal, or between Sindh and Delhi, are significant. These competing memories mean that Partition remains a political as well as a historical subject.

    The question for students is how to evaluate these interpretations. The most reliable approach is to ask what evidence each relies on, whose experiences it includes and excludes, and what purposes it serves. Good history does not require choosing a single narrative, but it does require attention to evidence, precision about figures and actors, and willingness to hold together the political decisions that led to Partition and the human consequences that followed.

    Mortality estimates also expose different choices about historical method. A researcher may count documented deaths in official returns, infer missing people from two censuses, reconstruct deaths in particular districts, or combine testimony and contemporaneous reports. Each method captures different populations and periods; administrative totals can miss unregistered killings, while demographic differences can also reflect migration, underenumeration, and changes in territorial units. The aim of teaching competing estimates is therefore not to imply that every number is equally defensible, nor to select the most dramatic one. Students should ask what denominator and geographical area were used, which deaths were included, and whether uncertainty is expressed openly. A similar discipline applies to responsibility: identifying decisions made by British, Congress, League, and local authorities is compatible with recognizing that actual attacks required participants who chose to commit them.

    Section 86.20

    Review and Historical Synthesis: Borders, Violence, Displacement, and the Legacy of Partition

    This chapter has argued that Partition was simultaneously a political settlement, a territorial reorganization, a humanitarian catastrophe, and a long-lasting social transformation. The legal creation of India and Pakistan on 15 August 1947 resolved the constitutional question of British withdrawal. It did not resolve the questions that the division of two provinces raised for millions of people. The Radcliffe awards, drawn in a few weeks under a principle of contiguous majorities that left many other factors to discretion, created a boundary through landscapes whose populations, canals, railways, cities, and markets were deeply interwoven. The line was published only after independence, and it assigned some districts in ways that were immediately contested.

    The territorial decisions disrupted established relationships among communities, land, commerce, and institutions, and they created minorities on both sides of the border. Violence, which had been building since 1946 and erupted in Punjab in March 1947, escalated massively in the summer and autumn of 1947, driven by fear, organized armed groups, rumor, retaliation, and the weakness of administrative and military protection. The Punjab Boundary Force could not preserve order, and structural failure did not remove the responsibility of those who planned and committed attacks. In Punjab the result was a near-complete exchange of populations in a few months. In Bengal, Delhi, Sindh, and Assam, the pattern was different, and displacement was slower, more recurrent, or driven by different circumstances.

    The consequences extended well beyond 1947. Governments built relief systems and rehabilitation policies that settled millions, but the results were unequal. The gendered violence of Partition and the state recovery of women revealed how bodies were treated as markers of community and national honor, and how policy could override the wishes of survivors. Evacuee-property administration turned emergency measures into permanent law, and citizenship rules, developed over several years, transformed an open boundary into a controlled frontier. Survivors rebuilt communities, and Partition has been remembered through testimony, literature, and commemoration, while historians continue to debate responsibility, mortality, and the meaning of the event.

    Experiencing Partition in Punjab, 1947

    The perspectives below are analytical illustrations based on documented patterns. They are not quotations from named individuals and do not imply that members of a group shared a single view.

    A Sikh landowning family in the canal colonies Faced the loss of land that had been developed over generations and the destruction of community in West Punjab. Many fled eastward, hoping to be allotted land in East Punjab, and encountered the difficulties of claims procedures and the loss of Sikh shrines in Pakistan.
    A Muslim artisan household in Amritsar Lived in a city where Muslims were a significant minority of craftspeople and traders. Faced attack and expulsion as the city became predominantly Hindu and Sikh, and left for Lahore or elsewhere in Pakistan, losing workshops and homes.
    A Hindu shopkeeping family in Lahore Owned shops and houses in the city's commercial center. Left in fear as violence spread, sometimes after the property had already been looted, and arrived in Delhi or Amritsar as refugees with claims for abandoned property.
    A district official, Muslim or Hindu Was responsible for protecting populations in an area where forces were divided and unreliable. Some officials protected neighbors at risk to themselves; others failed to act or were complicit, and many served new governments with uncertain loyalties.
    A woman abducted and later recovered Experienced violence and separation, and then a state-directed recovery that did not always take her wishes into account. Her experience shows how policy could extend as well as end the loss of control over her life.
    Continuity and Change Over Time

    Punjab and Bengal before and after Partition

    What Changed

    • Punjab's population became almost entirely separated by religion across the boundary
    • The Punjab canal and railway systems were divided between two states
    • Cities such as Lahore, Amritsar, Delhi, Karachi, and Calcutta changed in character
    • An open boundary became a controlled international frontier
    • Property and citizenship were redefined by law

    What Remained Continuous

    • Substantial minority communities remained in both countries
    • Languages, cuisine, music, and family networks crossed the border
    • Agricultural and commercial life continued, rebuilt by new populations
    • Shrines and pilgrimage ties persisted despite the boundary
    • Bengal's linguistic and cultural unity remained a bond across the divide

    Who experienced change most directly? Punjabi families, refugees in Delhi and Karachi, women who were abducted or recovered, and Sindhi Hindus experienced the most direct and abrupt disruption.

    Who experienced greater continuity? Many Bengali families and many communities that remained in place experienced a slower, more uncertain process in which both continuity and change shaped their lives.

    Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that Partition was both a rupture and a process, and that its effects varied with region, class, gender, and community.

    Cause and Effect

    The Humanitarian Catastrophe of 1947

    Long-term causes

    A colonial political order that organized representation and politics around religious communities; the militarization of society during the Second World War; accumulating mistrust after the elections of 1945–1946.

    Short-term causes

    The violence of 1946 and the Punjab crisis of March 1947; the June plan; the speed of the transfer of power; the publication of the awards after independence; weak or divided security forces.

    Event or transformation

    The division of Punjab and Bengal and the mass violence and migration of August–December 1947.

    Immediate effects

    Mass killings and expulsions; the movement of millions; abductions and sexual violence; camps and relief; the seizure of abandoned property.

    Long-term consequences

    Legal frameworks for evacuee property and citizenship; transformed cities and communities; competing memories; continuing minority insecurity and border tensions.

    Historical complexity: The catastrophe cannot be attributed to one decision or one group. It arose from the interaction of imperial policy, party politics, local conflicts, organized violence, and administrative collapse, and different regions experienced it differently.

    Looking Ahead to Chapter 87

    Chapter 87 turns from the experience of Partition to the consolidation of the new states. It examines the independence ceremonies of August 1947, the legal succession of India and Pakistan, and the integration of the princely states, including the cases of Junagadh and Hyderabad. It also takes up Jammu and Kashmir, the first India–Pakistan war, and the United Nations intervention. Students should carry forward from this chapter the understanding that these questions of sovereignty and territory were shaped by the humanitarian crisis described here, and that the new states were attempting to build institutions while managing the consequences of the division that had created them.

    The strongest historical conclusion is not that a border mechanically caused violence, or that violence arose inevitably from religious difference. The political settlement created new minority positions and a compressed transfer timetable; organized groups, local antagonisms, institutional weakness, and decisions made on the ground shaped how particular communities experienced the crisis. The outcome then altered the new states themselves. Relief offices became instruments of settlement policy, property categories became questions of citizenship, and stories of departure were carried into schools, neighborhoods, and national memory. The differential chronology of Punjab and Bengal also warns against describing Partition as finished on the day the boundary awards were announced. Treating its consequences as historical processes makes a more meaningful transition to Chapter 87, where the successor governments sought to establish sovereignty amid continuing uncertainty over territory and political authority.

    Chapter Summary

    Chapter 86 has examined Partition as a political decision, territorial division, humanitarian catastrophe, and enduring social transformation. Its central argument is that the legal creation of India and Pakistan on 15 August 1947 resolved the constitutional question of British withdrawal while creating territorial, social, and legal problems that independence could not resolve.

    The chapter first examined the territorial division. The Independence Act created two Dominions on 15 August, but the boundary awards of the Punjab and Bengal Boundary Commissions, chaired by Cyril Radcliffe, were published only on 17 August. The commissions worked quickly, with a mandate to ascertain contiguous majority areas and to take into account other factors, and with members divided along party lines. The result was a line that did not follow religious majorities everywhere and that cut through canals, railways, cities, and markets.

    Punjab, with its canal colonies, mixed cities, and Sikh shrines, was divided in ways that disrupted agricultural, commercial, and religious life. Bengal's division separated the jute-growing east from the mills and port of Calcutta, assigned the Chittagong Hill Tracts to East Pakistan, and, after the Sylhet referendum, divided Sylhet. The creation of a Pakistan in two separated wings shaped its later history.

    The second division traced the escalation of violence from 1946 through the Punjab crisis of March 1947 to the mass violence of August and September. It stressed that violence was driven by fear, organized armed groups, rumor, retaliation, and the weakness of protection, and it cautioned that the term communal violence can obscure the actors involved. The Punjab Boundary Force failed for structural reasons, but structural failure did not remove the responsibility of perpetrators.

    Violence and its aftermath varied: Punjab experienced concentrated massacres and expulsions, Delhi sudden upheaval, Sindh gradual departure and a spike in 1948, and Bengal slower and recurrent insecurity. Substantial communities remained in place in both countries, so that Partition did not produce complete separation.

    The third division examined the displacement of an estimated ten to fifteen million people, the migration by trains, columns, and convoys, the camps, relief operations, and the vulnerability of children, the elderly, and women. In the east, migration was slower and recurrent, continuing through the 1950 crisis and the Nehru–Liaquat Agreement. Rehabilitation in both countries used abandoned property as a principal resource, and outcomes were unequal.

    The fourth division examined gendered violence, the recovery policies of the two states, evacuee-property administration, and citizenship. Recovery often overrode the wishes of the women concerned, evacuee-property rules turned emergency measures into permanent law, and citizenship rules developed over several years, transforming an open boundary into a controlled frontier.

    The fifth division examined how Partition transformed communities and how it has been remembered through testimony, literature, film, and museums, and it examined historians' disagreements about responsibility, mortality, and competing national memories. It stressed that mortality figures, with one million often cited, are estimates that depend on method.

    Throughout, the chapter distinguished the decision to partition from the implementation of Partition, Punjab from Bengal, and contemporary records from retrospective testimony. It treated people of every community as historical actors with varied experiences and avoided assigning collective guilt.

    Chapter 87 will turn to independence, the princely states, and Kashmir, the next stage in the consolidation of the new states.

    Key Terms

    Dominion
    A self-governing state within the British Empire; India and Pakistan were established as Dominions on 15 August 1947.
    Partition Council
    The body with Congress and Muslim League representatives that supervised the division of central assets, personnel, and records in 1947.
    Boundary Commission
    One of two commissions, for Punjab and Bengal, established under the June plan to determine the boundary line.
    Radcliffe Award
    The boundary decisions made by Cyril Radcliffe in August 1947, published on 17 August.
    Contiguous majority
    The principle of dividing territory by adjoining areas in which one religious community held a majority, applied together with other factors.
    Gazette of India
    The official government publication in which the boundary awards were printed.
    Canal colony
    An area in western Punjab irrigated by British-built canals and settled by farmers from other districts.
    Headworks
    The structures at which river water is diverted into canals, whose location affected the division of irrigation.
    Sylhet referendum
    The vote of 6–7 July 1947 on whether Sylhet should join East Bengal or remain in Assam.
    Communal violence
    Violence between groups defined by religious community; a term that can obscure particular organizers and conditions.
    Jatha
    An armed or organized band, especially in Sikh usage.
    Kafila
    A column of travelers, applied to the refugee columns of 1947.
    Punjab Boundary Force
    The joint military force of contested reported strength that operated from 1 August to 1 September 1947 in twelve Punjab districts.
    Muhajir
    A Muslim refugee from India who settled in Pakistan, especially in Sindh.
    Refugee
    A person who has fled violence and sought shelter, whether or not legally an evacuee.
    Displaced person
    The term used in Indian policy for refugees entitled to relief and rehabilitation.
    Transit camp
    A temporary camp where refugees were registered and housed before resettlement.
    Rehabilitation
    The process of providing housing, land, work, and income to displaced people.
    Evacuee
    A person who had left one territory for the other and whose property could be administered by the state.
    Evacuee property
    Property left by evacuees, administered by custodians and used for rehabilitation.
    Custodian
    An official empowered to take control of and manage evacuee property.
    Abducted person
    In official usage, a woman or child taken from one community and living in another after the violence.
    Recovery
    The state policy of locating and returning abducted persons.
    Honor
    A concept of family and community reputation, often attached to women's bodies, that shaped violence and responses to it.
    Domicile
    Legal residence, used in determining citizenship and entitlement.
    Citizenship
    Legal membership in a state, defined in the successor states over several years.
    Permit system
    The 1948 system controlling movement from Pakistan to India, a step toward border controls.
    Minority protection
    Commitments to protect minority communities, as in the Nehru–Liaquat Agreement of 1950.
    Oral history
    Historical evidence from interviews with participants or witnesses, usually collected long after events.

    Chapter Reflection

    Chapter 86 matters because it shows what a political settlement means when it is carried out on real land and among real people. The decision to partition British India was made in negotiations that Chapter 85 examined. Its consequences were borne by families who had to leave homes, by women whose bodies became sites of violence and policy, and by communities whose neighbors became strangers or enemies. Understanding the history requires holding together the political decision and its human consequences, without allowing either to obscure the other.

    The chapter also reveals how new states are made. India and Pakistan established their institutions while managing a humanitarian emergency, and the measures they adopted for property, recovery, and citizenship created legal and social structures that endured. The border that was drawn through Punjab and Bengal became a boundary of identity, law, and strategy, and it shaped the relations between the successor states and the position of minorities within them. Later chapters on Kashmir, the Indian and Pakistani constitutions, the Bangladesh war, and contemporary regional security show how deeply these beginnings mattered.

    Within the six-volume series, Partition concludes the story of colonial rule and begins the story of postcolonial states. It connects Volume V's history of nationalism with Volume VI's history of independent South Asia. Students should also carry forward its methodological lessons: that official records and testimony illuminate different things, that estimates require attributing methods, and that memory is shaped by politics. Chapter 87 now turns to the consolidation of sovereignty, to the princely states, and to the first conflict over Kashmir, within the territory and the circumstances that Partition had created.
