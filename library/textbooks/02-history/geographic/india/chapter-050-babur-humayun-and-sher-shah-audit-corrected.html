Part X — The Mughal Empire

Babur, Humayun, and Sher Shah

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the morning of 21 April 1526, on a flat plain about ninety kilometers north of Delhi, a ruler from Kabul drew up a small army behind a line of wheeled carts and opened fire on the army of the Sultan of Delhi. Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur, then forty-three years old, had spent nearly all of his adult life winning and losing small kingdoms in Central Asia. Ibrahim Lodi, the Afghan Sultan facing him, ruled one of the largest states in South Asia. By the afternoon Ibrahim lay dead on the field of Panipat. Yet what Babur won that day was an opportunity rather than an empire, and the thirty years that followed show how fragile that opportunity was.

This chapter studies the unstable first phase of Mughal expansion, from Babur’s accession as a boy-ruler in Ferghana in 1494 to the death of his son Humayun in Delhi in January 1556. It also studies the political innovations that preceded Akbar’s consolidation of the empire. Three overlapping political projects structure the narrative. The first is Babur’s conquest of northern India between 1526 and 1530. The second is Humayun’s loss of that inheritance in 1540, his long exile, and his restoration in 1555. The third is the competing Afghan empire of Sher Shah Suri, which for a decade and a half governed much of the same territory more effectively than the early Mughals had managed to do.

The chapter’s central argument is that winning northern India in 1526 did not establish a stable imperial system. Babur had to secure his conquest against Afghan chiefs, Rajput rulers, and the restlessness of his own soldiers. Humayun inherited a realm whose institutions were thin, whose revenues were uncertain, and whose ruling family regarded territory as a shared dynastic property. Sher Shah built a powerful alternative regime out of the Afghan political world of Bihar. Humayun returned only after a long exile and in a changed political landscape, and he died within a year of his return, leaving his thirteen-year-old son another contested imperial inheritance.

The narrative is arranged in seven divisions. The first places Babur in his Timurid and Central Asian world and in the political crisis of the Lodi Sultanate. The second and third follow his campaigns from the Punjab to Panipat, Khanwa, Chanderi, and Ghaghra, and ask what he actually governed at his death in 1530. The fourth examines Humayun’s first reign and the defeats at Chausa and Kannauj. The fifth turns to Sher Shah and the construction of the Sur state, which receives the chapter’s most detailed institutional analysis. The sixth follows Humayun’s exile and restoration. The seventh compares the three regimes and asks what the first thirty years reveal about the challenges Akbar inherited.

The kinds of evidence available shape what can be known. Babur’s own memoir, the Baburnama, written in Chagatai Turkic, is a remarkable first-person source, but it is selective, has gaps, and presents its author’s own interpretation of events. Gulbadan Begum’s Humayun-nama, Jauhar’s memoir, and the great chronicles of Akbar’s reign were composed decades after the events they describe, under a dynasty that had its own reasons for shaping the story of its early years. Abbas Sarwani’s account of Sher Shah was likewise written at Akbar’s command, long after the Sur regime had fallen. Coins, inscriptions, and buildings provide a necessary check on all of these narratives. Cultivators, servants, ordinary soldiers, and most women outside the courts rarely speak in any of them.

Historians also face the problem of retrospective labels. The word "Mughal" is the Persian form of "Mongol" and was applied to Babur’s dynasty largely by outsiders, while the dynasty emphasized its Timurid descent. It is tempting to read the early period as a prelude to Akbar’s greatness, or to treat Sher Shah either as a mere interruption or as a flawless proto-Akbar. This chapter avoids both approaches. It also avoids reading conflicts of the period as simple confrontations between Hindus and Muslims: Babur fought Muslim Afghans and Hindu Rajputs, Rajput and Afghan leaders sometimes fought together, and Mughal princes fought one another.

The chapter connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 43 examined the Lodi Sultanate, Chapter 47 the regional sultanates of Gujarat, Bengal, and Malwa, and Chapter 49 the Portuguese entry into Indian Ocean commerce. Chapter 51 begins with Akbar’s accession and the Second Battle of Panipat, and Chapter 52 treats his mature government and religious policy. Chapters 55 and 56 examine Mughal society and culture comprehensively. The present chapter therefore supplies only the context from those chapters that it needs, and it reserves for them what belongs to them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Babur’s Timurid and Chagatai background, and his experience in Ferghana, Samarkand, and Kabul, prepared him for the invasion of northern India.
  2. Analyze the political crisis of the Lodi Sultanate and explain why Babur’s invasion succeeded when earlier incursions had not.
  3. Evaluate the military explanations for Babur’s victories at Panipat and Khanwa, including artillery, field fortification, mobility, and command.
  4. Distinguish between battlefield conquest and effective government in the years 1526–1530, and describe what Babur actually controlled at his death.
  5. Interpret the Baburnama as a historical source, identifying its strengths, silences, and perspective.
  6. Assess the causes of Humayun’s defeats at Chausa and Kannauj by weighing logistics, finance, dynastic rivalry, and Afghan resurgence against personal explanations.
  7. Trace Sher Shah’s rise from a Bihar jagirdar’s son to ruler of northern India and explain the political foundations of his regime.
  8. Analyze Sur administration, land revenue, coinage, roads, and communications, distinguishing demonstrated achievements from retrospective praise and from earlier precedents.
  9. Describe Humayun’s exile, his relationship with the Safavid court, and the reconstruction of his power in Kabul.
  10. Explain how Afghan fragmentation after 1545 enabled the Mughal restoration of 1555.
  11. Compare Lodi, early Mughal, and Sur institutions of nobility, military organization, and revenue.
  12. Connect the instability of 1526–1556 to the challenges Akbar inherited, without treating his later success as inevitable.

Historical Context

By the early sixteenth century the political map of northern South Asia was fragmented. The Delhi Sultanate, once the dominant power from the Punjab to Bengal, had been shaken by Timur’s invasion of 1398–99 and had never recovered its former reach (Chapters 42 and 43). In its place a number of regional states had taken form. Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal, Jaunpur before its absorption, Kashmir, and the Deccan sultanates each had its own court, army, and commercial base (Chapters 46 and 47). The Rajput kingdoms of Mewar, Marwar, and others occupied the arid and hilly country of Rajasthan and the borderlands of Malwa. Delhi and Agra, under the Afghan Lodi dynasty, formed the largest single political unit in the north, but even the Lodi state depended on the loyalty of Afghan chiefs who regarded the Sultan as first among equals.

Beyond the northwestern passes lay a different political world. The Timurid principalities of Central Asia and Khurasan had been the home of one of the most refined courts in the Persianate world, but in the decades around 1500 they were overrun by the Uzbek confederacy under Muhammad Shaybani Khan and squeezed by the rising Safavid state in Iran. Kabul and Kandahar sat between these worlds and the plains of Hindustan. Their importance lay less in their own revenues, which were modest, than in their position on the routes that carried horses, soldiers, and merchants between Central Asia, Iran, and the Indus plain. Horses from Central Asia were essential for northern Indian armies, and the route through Kabul was the principal channel by which they arrived.

Military technology was also changing. Firearms, in the form of both guns and handheld matchlocks, had been used in South Asia before 1526, in the Deccan, Gujarat, Malwa, and elsewhere, and the Portuguese were introducing new artillery on the western coast (Chapter 49). The Ottomans and Safavids were developing integrated systems of artillery, infantry firearms, and cavalry. Babur, who had observed Uzbek mounted tactics and recruited Ottoman-trained specialists, brought a combination of these methods to the plains of northern India. Historians debate how much weight to give technology in explaining the outcome, and this chapter takes up that debate directly.

The three regimes examined here relied on different sources of authority. The Lodis drew on Afghan tribal solidarity, the inheritance of Sultanate institutions, and revenue assignments. The Timurids invoked descent from Timur and from Chinggis Khan, an ideology that legitimated rulers but also encouraged every prince of the blood to claim a share. The Surs under Sher Shah combined Afghan military networks with administrative practices that drew on earlier Sultanate models, notably those of Ala al-Din Khalji and Sikandar Lodi. The decades between 1526 and 1556 were therefore a contest among competing ways of organizing military power, revenue, and kingship, and not simply a contest between a Central Asian dynasty and an Afghan one.

Section 50.1

Babur’s Timurid and Central Asian Background

Zahir al-Din Muhammad, known as Babur, was born on 14 February 1483 in the Ferghana valley, in what is now Uzbekistan. His birthplace is commonly identified as Andijan, although the historian Stephen F. Dale places it at Akhsi, another important Ferghana settlement. His father, Umar Shaikh Mirza, ruled Ferghana as a descendant of Timur (d. 1405) through Timur’s son Miran Shah. His mother, Qutluq Nigar Khanum, was the daughter of Yunus Khan, a ruler of the eastern Chagatai Mongol line, and thus descended from Chinggis Khan through his son Chagatai. Babur therefore combined the two most prestigious genealogies in Inner Asian politics. Timur himself had not been a Chinggisid and had governed through nominal khans of Chinggis’s line, so the Timurids valued marriage connections to that lineage. Babur’s own language was Chagatai Turkic, but he was also fluent in Persian, the language of administration, poetry, and high culture across the Timurid world.

The political geography in which he grew up was a patchwork of principalities. Ferghana lay along the upper Syr Darya. To its southwest were the rich cities of Transoxiana, Samarkand and Bukhara, which carried the memory of Timur’s capital. Farther south and west lay Khurasan with its cultural center at Herat, where the Timurid sultan Husayn Bayqara (r. 1469–1506) presided over a court that sheltered poets such as Jami and Navai and the painter Bihzad. Kabul and Badakhshan formed a mountain frontier to the southeast. To the north and northeast, nomadic and semi-nomadic Uzbek groups under the Shaybanid line were gathering strength.

The Timurid system of succession shaped Babur’s career. Authority was understood as the collective property of the ruling house, and any prince with an army and a following could claim a throne. This produced constant competition among cousins, uncles, and brothers, a pattern that would later reappear in Hindustan among Babur’s own sons. It also made Babur’s early life one of recurrent flight and recovery, since a prince’s territory depended on the loyalty of begs and soldiers who could shift allegiance when circumstances changed.

Historical Vocabulary
Timurid, Chinggisid, Chagatai, and "Mughal"

Timurid refers to descendants of Timur (Tamerlane) and to the courts and culture they patronized in Central Asia and Iran. Chinggisid refers to descent from Chinggis Khan, which conferred a distinct form of legitimacy in Inner Asia. Chagatai names Chinggis’s second son, his appanage, and the Turkic literary language that developed there, in which Babur wrote his memoir. The word Mughal derives from the Persian and Arabic form of "Mongol" and was widely used by outsiders for Babur’s dynasty. The dynasty itself usually stressed its Timurid identity and sometimes took offense at the Mongol label. This textbook uses "Mughal" for the dynasty because it is the standard historical term, while recognizing that it was not the family’s preferred self-description and that Babur himself distinguished sharply between Turks and Mongols.

Section 50.2

Ferghana, Samarkand, and the Struggle against the Uzbeks

Umar Shaikh Mirza died in 1494 in an accident at Akhsi, and Babur, aged eleven, succeeded him in Ferghana. His territory was immediately threatened by relatives who wanted a share of it. Over the following years Babur made a series of attempts on Samarkand, the prize of Timurid Central Asia. He captured it in 1497 but held it for only about one hundred days, during which he lost Ferghana to rivals. He took Samarkand again in 1500 and lost it again within a short time. In 1501 he was defeated by Muhammad Shaybani Khan, who proceeded to conquer Samarkand, Bukhara, and eventually Herat (1507), establishing the Uzbek Shaybanid power across Transoxiana and Khurasan.

Shaybani’s rise ended Timurid rule in Central Asia and left Babur a dispossessed prince with a small band of followers. Babur’s sister Khanzada Begum was taken into Shaybani’s household in the aftermath of the defeat, an episode the Baburnama records with evident pain. In 1510 Shaybani was defeated and killed by the Safavid ruler Shah Ismail I of Iran, and Babur seized the opening. With Safavid support he retook Samarkand in 1511 and Bukhara briefly after, but he lost the region again in 1512 when the Uzbek commander Ubaydallah Khan defeated him at Ghujduwan. Babur’s dependence on the Shi’i Safavids may also have damaged his standing among the largely Sunni populations of Transoxiana. By about 1514 his Central Asian ambitions were effectively over.

The years of failure were not wasted. Babur learned how to hold together a mobile court, how to negotiate with kinsmen and rivals, and how to read the Uzbek system of mounted warfare, including the tulughma, a flanking and encircling maneuver. His memoir records the stubbornness and the miscalculations that accompanied these years. The experience also instilled in him a conviction that a ruler needed resources, not only descent, and that a base in the mountains could be a platform for conquest in the plains.

Section 50.3

Kabul and the Construction of a New Power Base, 1504–1525

In 1504 Babur crossed the Hindu Kush with a small following and took Kabul, which had come under the control of Muqim Arghun after the death of Babur’s uncle Ulugh Beg Mirza. The Kabul region was not wealthy. Its revenues were modest, and its population included Turkic and Persian-speaking communities, Afghan tribes in the surrounding hills, and Hazara groups of Mongol descent. But it controlled important routes, and its markets served as a clearing point for caravans moving between Central Asia, Iran, and the Indus plain. Babur governed Kabul for over twenty years, and he came to regard it with great affection, as his descriptions of its gardens and fruits show.

From Kabul he built a following of soldiers drawn from Central Asian exiles, local Turkic and Afghan groups, and other recruits. In the decade after 1512 he sought to expand by raiding and by diplomacy. He contested Kandahar with the Arghun rulers, who eventually moved to Sindh, and by about 1522 Kandahar was in his hands, soon to be entrusted to his son Kamran. Dealing with the Afghan hill tribes, Babur combined raiding with marriage ties and subsidies. As early as 1505 he had crossed the Khyber and conducted a raid into the Indus frontier, and he later spoke of the territories once ruled by Timur in the Punjab as belonging by right to his house, citing Timur’s conquests of 1398–99 as the basis of that claim.

The base at Kabul did not by itself provide the resources for conquest. It supplied a core of soldiers and officers and a secure route to the Punjab, but Babur understood that a decisive campaign required the revenues and manpower of the plains. His interest in India was thus a strategic judgment as well as an ambition: the Punjab and Gangetic plains offered what the mountains could not, and the political fragmentation of the Lodi realm made the opportunity real.

Section 50.4

Timurid Kingship, Political Culture, and the Baburnama

Babur’s court was a military household organized around a ruler, his family, and a body of begs, who held command over troops and were rewarded with revenue assignments and gifts. Decisions were taken in councils at which senior officers gave advice, and Babur routinely consulted them, though he did not always follow their counsel. Hospitality, feasts, poetry, and hunting were means of binding followers to the ruler. The ideal ruler was a skilled warrior, a patron of learning, and a person of cultivated taste. Babur wrote poetry in Turkic and Persian, composed a treatise on prosody, and prepared a verse treatise on Islamic law and practice.

His memoir, usually called the Baburnama (Babur’s book, called in Turkic the Vaqa’i), is the most extraordinary source for this period and one of the most remarkable autobiographies of the premodern world. It covers events from 1494 to 1529 with notable gaps, including a long interval between the early 1500s and 1519 and further breaks in the 1520s. Babur wrote in Chagatai Turkic, in a candid and observational style, describing battles, landscapes, plants, animals, and people, including judgments about their characters. His grandson Akbar had it translated into Persian in 1589 by Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, and illustrated manuscripts were produced for the court. Modern English translations include that of Annette Beveridge (1922) and that of Wheeler Thackston (published in the 1990s and 2000s).

The memoir must be read with care. It is a first-person account, but the text as transmitted was revised, and Babur wrote with an audience in mind, including his sons and the Timurid tradition of royal memoirs. He presents himself as a figure of resolution and cultural refinement. He interprets opponents through his own categories. He says little about the thousands of ordinary soldiers and cultivators whose labor sustained his campaigns. Where the Baburnama can be checked against other evidence, including Afghan chronicles, Rajput traditions, coins, and inscriptions, it often proves reliable on events but contestable on motives and numbers.

Section 50.5

The Lodi Sultanate and the Political Crisis of Northern India

The Lodi dynasty had come to power in Delhi under Bahlul Lodi in 1451 and continued under his son Sikandar (r. 1489–1517), who extended Delhi’s authority east toward Jaunpur and Bihar and founded the city of Agra in 1504 as a military center. These Afghan rulers governed through chiefs who commanded their own followings and expected to share in decisions. The relationship between sultan and nobles was the central political issue of the dynasty, as Chapter 43 explained. Sikandar had maintained reasonable control through personal prestige. His son Ibrahim, who succeeded in 1517, tried to assert greater royal authority by humbling powerful nobles and, according to hostile accounts, by arresting or eliminating some of them.

These efforts alienated a number of Afghan leaders. Ibrahim’s brother Jalal Khan had been given a separate territorial base in Jaunpur, and the effort to remove him produced a rebellion. Dilawar Khan, a son of Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor of the Punjab, was reportedly mistreated at court, and Daulat Khan came to fear for his own position. Alam Khan Lodi, an uncle of Ibrahim and a rival claimant, also looked beyond the frontier for support. The Punjab, which lay on the main invasion route, was thus governed by a man who had reason to distrust the Sultan.

Other powers added to the pressure. Rana Sanga of Mewar had expanded across eastern Rajasthan and into Malwa, and in clashes with Lodi forces he had gained territory and confidence. Bengal, under the Husain Shahi dynasty, controlled the lower Ganges, while Gujarat and Malwa pursued their own ambitions. In this setting, Babur’s decision to invade did not rest simply on opportunism. It reflected a calculation that the Lodi state was weaker than its size suggested, that its leaders could be divided, and that the Punjab, already under raid from Kabul, could be taken with local assistance. The conditions that made invasion possible were created by Afghan, Rajput, and regional politics as much as by Babur’s own initiative.

Division II

The Conquest of Northern India, 1519–1527

Conquest was a combination of military organization, competing political ambitions, and mobilization of resources; neither Panipat nor Khanwa was inevitable.

Section 50.6

The Punjab Campaigns and the Road to Delhi

Babur’s incursions into the Punjab began in earnest in 1519 with an attack on Bajaur, where he used matchlock firearms against a fortified position. The ruthlessness of the assault was meant to intimidate other frontier communities. He then moved into Bhera, receiving submissions and extracting supplies, but turned back when events in Kabul required his presence. A second expedition in 1520 reached Sialkot and the region of Saidpur. Saidpur (later Eminabad) is also associated with the Sikh tradition, since verses attributed to Guru Nanak are commonly read as a response to the violence that accompanied Babur’s capture of the town (see Chapter 48 for the early Sikh Panth).

The turning point came in 1524, when Daulat Khan Lodi and Alam Khan sent messages inviting Babur to intervene against Ibrahim. Babur advanced to Lahore and seized it, but the Lodi nobles soon discovered that he intended to rule the Punjab, not to serve as their instrument. Daulat Khan turned against him, and Alam Khan’s own attempt to take Delhi with Babur’s support failed. These setbacks showed that the Lodi opposition was divided and that the invasion of Hindustan could not rely on easy alliances.

In November 1525 Babur set out from Kabul on his fifth expedition, with Humayun joining him from Badakhshan. Daulat Khan Lodi submitted, and the Mughal army advanced down the Punjab toward Delhi. The invasion proceeded in the cooler season, and Babur’s sources provided information on Ibrahim’s preparations and numbers. In April 1526 the two armies converged near the old town of Panipat, a site that has repeatedly decided the political fate of northern India.

Section 50.7

The First Battle of Panipat, 1526

Babur reports that Ibrahim Lodi commanded about 100,000 men and a thousand elephants, while his own force numbered perhaps 12,000. Modern historians doubt the larger figures, since medieval authors regularly inflated enemy forces to magnify victories, but there is little doubt that the Lodi army was substantially larger. Ibrahim’s force included Afghan cavalry, infantry, camp followers, and war elephants, and its coherence was weakened by uncertain loyalties among his nobles.

Babur chose his ground with care. He anchored his right flank on the buildings of Panipat itself and covered his left with a ditch and branches of felled trees. In the center he placed hundreds of wheeled carts (araba) linked by rawhide ropes and spaced by breastworks that sheltered matchlockmen. Gaps allowed his cavalry to charge and withdraw. Ustad Ali Quli and Mustafa, firearms specialists with Ottoman-related experience, directed the artillery. Ibrahim delayed for about a week, and when he finally advanced on 21 April, the compact formation of his army was disordered by the narrowness of the front and the sight of the carts.

The battle was brief. Babur’s flanking detachments, using the tulughma, swung around the Lodi wings and attacked from the rear, while firearms disrupted the advance and frightened the elephants, which trampled their own troops. Humayun commanded the Mughal right in his first major battle. By midday the Lodi army was encircled and broken. Ibrahim Lodi was killed in the fighting, as was Raja Vikramajit of Gwalior, who had fought on the Lodi side. Babur’s claim that many thousands of Afghans died is plausible in general terms but not verifiable in numbers.

Babur sent Humayun to Agra and another detachment to Delhi to secure the capitals and their treasuries, and the Friday prayer sermon (khutba) was read in his name. This asserted sovereignty but did not end the war. Many Afghan chiefs remained at large with their followings, and those who had stood aside from Ibrahim’s cause were not necessarily Babur’s allies. Panipat removed a Sultan; it did not create a state.

Section 50.8

Artillery, Mobility, and the Military Explanation of Babur’s Victory

Babur’s victory at Panipat is often attributed to gunpowder. The firearms were indeed important. The matchlock, a handheld gun fired by a slow-burning match, gave infantry a weapon that could disrupt cavalry and intimidate elephants, and field artillery supplied noise and shock. But the battle was won by the coordination of weapons with other elements. The carts and breastworks protected the gunners and provided a fixed center. The tulughma gave the Mughals a mounted force capable of enveloping the enemy. Discipline and unified command enabled Babur’s officers to execute a plan on the battlefield, while the Lodi army suffered from divided command and from a crowding of its own men.

Firearms were not new to India. Gujarat, Malwa, the Deccan sultanates, and Vijayanagara had used cannon in sieges and field battles, and chronicles record the use of guns in several earlier campaigns. What was distinctive about Babur’s army was not simply possession of firearms but a trained team of specialists, field tactics integrating guns with cavalry, and a tradition of mobile mounted archery that Afghan and Indian armies often lacked. His own cavalry, mostly horse archers, remained central; the guns made it possible to hold a position until the flanks closed.

Historical Debate

Did Gunpowder Technology Explain Babur’s Victory?

The question: Was Babur’s conquest of northern India primarily the result of firearms technology, or of tactics, organization, and the political weakness of his opponents?

Interpretation A: Technological explanation: Some historians, drawing on the "gunpowder empires" thesis developed by scholars such as Marshall Hodgson and William McNeill, argued that the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals owed their rise to the effective use of gunpowder weapons, which allowed centralized states to overcome older military elites. On this view Babur’s artillery and matchlocks gave him decisive superiority over Afghan cavalry armies.
Interpretation B: Organizational and political explanation: Other historians, including Iqtidar Alam Khan, have emphasized that firearms were already in use in India and that Babur’s success depended on combined-arms tactics, effective leadership, and the disunity of his enemies. Gunpowder was a contributing factor, but not an automatic guarantee of victory, as shown by the very different outcomes of later campaigns in which Mughal guns did not prevent defeat.

Evidence:

The Baburnama describes the use of guns and carts at Panipat and Khanwa. Indian and Persian chronicles document earlier firearms in Gujarat, Malwa, and the Deccan. The numbers, deployment, and effect of the weapons at specific battles are only partly recoverable from sources that were written by the winning side.

Why it matters:

If technology was decisive, the early Mughal state looks like a product of a military revolution. If tactics and politics mattered more, the rise of the dynasty appears contingent on specific circumstances and on the quality of leadership, and the failures of Humayun appear less surprising.

Section 50.9

Delhi, Agra, and the Limits of the Initial Conquest

The capture of Delhi and Agra gave Babur their treasuries and the symbolic authority of the Lodi capital. He distributed substantial gifts to his begs, to the members of his family, and to people in Kabul, where he reportedly sent presents to every inhabitant of the region. The treasure helped him reward his followers, but the money was quickly spent. The revenue system of the conquered territories had to be reconstructed, since local officials, including Afghan chiefs and zamindars (local landholders), had not necessarily accepted his rule and often retained control of collection.

His soldiers presented a further problem. Many of them had come from the cool uplands of Central Asia and Kabul, and they found the heat of the northern plains oppressive. They also regarded the campaign as a successful raid whose profits should be taken home. Babur gathered his officers and told them that the conquest could not be abandoned after so much effort and that a large and wealthy country was theirs for the taking. Some chose to leave, and he let them go. The decision to remain in Hindustan, rather than withdrawing to Kabul with the spoils, was a choice with major consequences.

Babur’s attitudes toward the land were mixed. He described its climate, gardens, and public buildings with some disappointment and found little to admire in local social customs, but he appreciated its wealth and the variety of its craftspeople. The distinction between a battlefield victory and effective government was central. Local communities, Afghan chiefs, and Rajput rulers had not been conquered simply because the Sultan was dead. Babur needed to control the Doab, the roads, the forts, and the revenue-collecting intermediaries, and he lacked an administrative apparatus of his own to do so.

Section 50.10

Rana Sanga, Mewar, and the Rajput Confederacy

Rana Sanga (Sangram Singh) had ruled Mewar since about 1508 and had expanded its power across eastern Rajasthan and into the Malwa borderlands. In clashes with Lodi forces he had gained a reputation for martial skill and political acumen, and by the 1520s he was arguably the most powerful ruler in the region west and south of Agra. Rajput chiefs from many parts of Rajasthan acknowledged his leadership, though such alliances were often personal and conditional. His ambitions included a role in the politics of Delhi, which the collapse of Lodi authority made newly plausible.

The Baburnama claims that Sanga had sent an envoy to Kabul offering to attack Agra while Babur moved against Delhi. Whether this offer was made, and what Sanga meant by it, is debated. Sanga may have believed that Babur would behave like Timur, who had raided and withdrawn, rather than remain as a ruler of Hindustan. Once it became clear that Babur intended to stay, Sanga’s calculation changed, and the two became rivals for the inheritance of the Lodi position.

The coalition that formed against Babur was not limited to Rajputs. Hasan Khan Mewati, the Muslim ruler of Mewat, joined Sanga, as did Mahmud Lodi, a brother of Ibrahim whom the Afghan chiefs recognized as Sultan. Afghan leaders in the Doab and the east, with their own scores to settle, also considered joining. The result was a broad opposition that included Hindu and Muslim leaders united by a shared interest in preventing the consolidation of a new Central Asian regime. The conflict that followed is therefore better understood as a contest for political predominance in northern India than as a religious war.

Section 50.11

The Battle of Khanwa, 1527

Early in 1527 Babur’s position was precarious. Mughal outposts around Bayana fell to Sanga’s advance, and a vanguard was defeated. The troops, already unsettled by the heat and by stories of Rajput strength, were demoralized, and reports of unfavorable omens circulated in camp. Babur responded with a series of striking moves. He made a public vow to abstain from wine, had his gold and silver drinking vessels broken and distributed to the poor, and canceled the tamgha, a commercial tax on Muslims. He then addressed his officers, reminding them that death was certain and that a fight to the finish was preferable to disgrace, and they swore on the Qur’an to stand and fight.

The battle took place on 16 March 1527 near Khanwa, about sixty kilometers west of Agra. Babur once more arranged his army behind carts, chains, and trenches, with guns and matchlocks in the center and cavalry on the flanks. Sanga’s charge, delivered by heavy cavalry with great courage, was initially hard to contain, and the fighting continued for several hours. As at Panipat, flanking units encircled the Rajput army, and its coordination broke down. Sanga was wounded and carried from the field. Hasan Khan Mewati was killed. Babur took the title of ghazi, a warrior for the faith, and ordered a tower of skulls to commemorate the victory, a Central Asian practice meant to terrify opponents.

The political significance was substantial. Sanga’s defeat ended the prospect of a Rajput-led successor to the Lodis, and Sanga himself died in 1528 while reportedly preparing a new campaign. The battle did not destroy Rajput power, but it forced the Rajput states to reckon with a new northern power and weakened Mewar’s claim to wider leadership. For Babur it removed the most serious obstacle to establishing his control over the Doab and Agra region.

Historical Debate

Was Khanwa a Religious War, and Did Sanga Invite Babur?

The question: How should historians interpret the religious language of Khanwa, and was Rana Sanga’s alleged invitation to Babur a real diplomatic overture?

Interpretation A: A contest for political predominance: The coalition at Khanwa included Hasan Khan Mewati and Mahmud Lodi, both Muslims. Babur’s call for jihad was a tool of mobilization for an army wavering in morale, and it reflected a conventional political language used in frontier warfare. The underlying dispute was over who would inherit the Lodi position.
Interpretation B: Religion as a mobilizing and legitimating frame: Others argue that religious language was more than rhetoric, because Babur adopted the title ghazi and invoked the language of holy war in a way that gave the conflict a religious coloration for his supporters and shaped how later writers understood it. Rajput chroniclers and later nationalist writers have also used the battle to frame broader communal narratives.

Evidence:

The Baburnama is the main source for the invitation and for the speech; Rajput traditions recorded later and Persian chronicles give additional, though later, perspectives. Neither the invitation nor Sanga’s intentions can be verified independently.

Why it matters:

How the battle is read affects whether early Mughal rule is seen primarily as a political and military enterprise or as part of a larger religious confrontation, and shapes later popular memory.

Babur’s Indian Empire and Its Fragile Foundations

Section 50.12

Chanderi, 1528, and the Consolidation of Rajput Frontiers

After Khanwa, Babur turned to the Rajput and Afghan holdouts of the central and southern frontier. In January 1528 he besieged Chanderi, a fortress in the Malwa borderlands held by Medini Rai, a Rajput chief who had risen to prominence as a minister in the Malwa sultanate and had joined Rana Sanga’s coalition. Babur offered him another territory in exchange for the fort, an offer Medini Rai refused. The assault, supported by artillery and by an escalade of the walls, took the fort in a single day.

According to the Baburnama, the defenders did not surrender. The Rajput men performed jauhar, the ritual in which, when defeat appeared certain, women and children were killed or immolated to prevent capture, and the men then rode out to die in the fight. The practice reflected a martial code of honor among Rajput warrior groups and its memory endured in later traditions. Chanderi’s fall demonstrated the effect of artillery against even strongly held positions, and it frightened other garrisons. It did not, however, bring Malwa under stable Mughal control.

Chanderi also illustrates a persistent pattern. Babur could take fortresses but had few means of administering the territories behind them. He assigned Chanderi to a local figure, as a kind of subordinate ally, rather than building a provincial government. Over the following years the Rajput states of Rajasthan, though weakened, were neither integrated nor eliminated. They would be dealt with on very different terms by Akbar, whose Rajput policy is examined in Chapter 51.

Section 50.13

Ghaghra, 1529, and the Continuing Afghan Challenge

The eastern frontier proved harder to resolve. After Panipat and Khanwa, Afghan leaders in the Doab and Bihar had submitted conditionally, and others never submitted at all. Mahmud Lodi, the brother of Ibrahim, was proclaimed Sultan by Afghan chiefs in Bihar, where regional magnates retained substantial forces. Babur led a campaign east in 1528–1529, moving through Awadh and the Gangetic plain, relying on river routes and heavy artillery.

The decisive encounter took place on 6 May 1529 near the confluence of the Ghaghra and the Ganges. Babur’s artillery covered the crossing of the river against an Afghan force that had positioned itself to oppose him. The battle was a mixed land and river engagement in which Mughal firepower and flanking moves again prevailed. The Sultan of Bengal, Nusrat Shah, who had initially stood on the opposite bank, avoided a further confrontation and made terms with Babur. Mahmud Lodi fled and later died in 1532.

The victory broke the cohesion of the eastern Afghan opposition but did not eliminate it. Powerful Afghan leaders remained in Bihar, and some, such as Sher Khan, a rising figure whose career is traced in Division V, studied Mughal methods while serving or observing them. Later Afghan tradition states that Sher Khan spent time in Babur’s camp and concluded that Mughal power was less formidable than it looked. This anecdote comes from a retrospective source and cannot be taken as established, but it points to the dangerous afterlife of an incomplete conquest.

Section 50.14

Governing Babur’s Conquests: Nobles, Military Service, and Provincial Authority

Babur’s government in Hindustan was an extension of his military household. He rewarded followers with grants of revenue, in the form of districts, parganas, and local rights, rather than salaries, and expected his commanders to maintain troops from the assigned income. His principal officers included the minister Nizam al-Din Ali Khalifa (Mir Khalifa), the Timurid cousin Mirza Muhammad Zaman, and other begs of Central Asian origin, together with Afghan and Indian officers who had joined the Mughal side. The family was distributed across the new territories and across Kabul, Kandahar, and Badakhshan, which Babur kept as the home base.

Administration relied on existing local structures. Revenue collection continued through hereditary and local intermediaries, such as qanungos (record-keeping officials of a pargana) and village headmen, who were not easily replaced. Some districts were farmed out to officers who collected what they could. The new rulers' limited knowledge of local conditions made them dependent on these intermediaries, and the regime lacked the surveys and fiscal records that would later characterize Akbar’s government.

Financial strain was constant. Armies had to be paid and rewarded, the treasuries captured in 1526 were soon depleted, and new revenues were difficult to extract from areas not yet pacified. Babur’s own letters and the Baburnama refer to the problem of keeping his soldiers in the field. His nobles were loyal in the military sense, but their loyalty was tied to opportunities for rewards, and the system gave them considerable autonomy. The limits of centralized governance were thus built into the structure of the conquest.

Section 50.15

Babur’s Court, Gardens, Observations, and Cultural Legacy

Babur brought with him a taste for formal gardens, which he considered an essential feature of civilized life. In Agra he laid out a garden on the bank of the Yamuna, known as the Ram Bagh or Aram Bagh, with watercourses, pavilions, and planted beds on the charbagh (four-part garden) model that would become a signature of later Mughal landscapes. He also ordered wells, stepwells, and baths, and he commissioned buildings from stonecutters and masons he recruited from Hindustan. Mosques are associated with his reign at Panipat, Sambhal, and Ayodhya, the last of which later became the subject of intense controversy and is treated in Chapter 99.

His memoir reveals an acute observer of nature. He describes Indian animals, plants, and seasons, comparing them to those of Kabul and Central Asia. He recorded the rhinoceros, the monsoon rains, and the mango, which he praised, and he measured distances and listed districts with their reported revenues. These descriptions are of great historical value for environmental and economic history, though they reflect the interests of a ruler who was recording useful information as well as satisfying curiosity.

The distinction between Babur’s literary reputation and his institutional achievement is important. His memoir secured his place among the great writers of the Turkic world, and his gardens set a pattern for Mughal elite taste. But the empire he left behind lacked the administrative structure to keep it together. Culture could survive where institutions failed: the Timurid-Persianate tradition that Babur carried, and that Humayun and Akbar developed, shaped the court long after the political survival of the dynasty seemed in doubt. Chapter 56 examines Mughal culture comprehensively; here it is enough to note its formative beginnings.

Section 50.16

Babur’s Death in 1530 and the Problem of Dynastic Succession

Babur died at Agra on 26 December 1530, at about forty-seven. Later tradition tells that when Humayun fell gravely ill, Babur walked around his bedside offering his own life in exchange, after which the son recovered and the father sickened. This story, preserved in later sources, cannot be verified, but it conveys the emotional framing of the succession. Humayun was named heir and was enthroned on 30 December.

The succession was less simple than the enthronement implied. Timurid custom did not give a single heir sole right to the whole inheritance, and Babur had already given Kamran control of Kabul and Kandahar. Humayun received the Indian conquests, which were richer but much less secure. One tradition relates that the minister Mir Khalifa considered supporting another candidate, Mahdi Khwaja, though the evidence is limited and the story is told in later narratives. Whatever the truth, the succession went to Humayun without a civil war, a sign of Babur’s personal authority.

Babur was first buried in Agra and later, according to his wishes, reburied in Kabul in a garden that bears his name, Bagh-i Babur. The geography of his tomb is telling: Kabul, not Agra, remained in some sense his home. At his death he ruled a territory stretching from Badakhshan and Kabul across the Punjab to the Doab and eastern Hindustan, held together by his personal command of followers, by a handful of fortified positions, and by the destruction of his chief rivals. He had not created an administration that could outlive him, and he had not resolved the question of how to share authority within his family.

Division IV

Humayun’s First Reign and the Collapse of Mughal Rule, 1530–1540

Humayun’s failure must be explained through strategy, revenue, logistics, dynastic competition, Afghan resurgence, and political geography, not simply personal incompetence.

Section 50.17

Humayun’s Accession and the Inheritance of Empire

Humayun, born in Kabul in 1508, was twenty-two at his accession. He had commanded troops in his father’s campaigns, served as governor of Badakhshan, and played a visible part at Panipat. Contemporary descriptions emphasize his learning, his interest in astronomy and astrology, his courtesy, and his generosity. The Persian historian Khwandamir, who joined his court, composed for him the Qanun-i Humayuni, which describes the ordering of ranks and ceremonies at the new court, and records Humayun’s fondness for classifying his officials and his schedule by planetary and elemental symbolism.

The inheritance was less valuable than it appeared. Babur’s conquests in Hindustan had not been consolidated, and much of Humayun’s early reign was occupied by campaigns to subdue rebellious Afghan chiefs in the Doab and east. Revenue was insufficient to maintain the army at the level of Babur’s mobilizations, and the treasury, built on the capture of 1526, was by then depleted. Humayun’s legitimacy rested on his father’s name, his Timurid descent, and his ability to reward followers, but each of these was fragile. Challenges came from kinsmen with their own claims, from Afghan magnates in Bihar and Jaunpur, and from regional rulers such as Bahadur Shah of Gujarat.

In 1533 Humayun founded a new capital called Din-panah ("refuge of the faith") on the Yamuna near Delhi, on or near the site later developed by Sher Shah as Purana Qila. The foundation conveyed ambitions of dynastic permanence and cosmic order. In practice the king spent most of his time in the field, and the settled administrative apparatus that a capital normally implies never fully developed.

Section 50.18

The Timurid Brothers: Kamran, Askari, Hindal, and Divided Authority

Humayun had three half-brothers who played central roles: Kamran, who held Kabul and Kandahar; Askari; and Hindal, the youngest. Following the Timurid custom, each expected a territorial command and an independent role. Early in the reign Kamran marched from Kabul to Lahore and seized the Punjab, and Humayun, wishing to avoid conflict with his brother, ratified the arrangement, adding territories in the northwest. This concession gave Kamran control of the principal route to Kabul and the Punjab’s revenues, and it deprived Humayun of the recruiting grounds and communications that had sustained Babur.

Askari and Hindal received commands in the Indian territories, and from time to time the brothers took independent political positions. Hindal at one point declared himself king in Agra during Humayun’s eastern campaign, and Kamran moved to take advantage of his brother’s difficulties. Cousins such as Muhammad Zaman Mirza and Muhammad Sultan Mirza also plotted and rebelled, and Humayun’s usual response was forgiveness, in line with Timurid norms of family honor. This leniency has been criticized as weakness, but it also followed conventions that treated kinship as a bond to be preserved.

The consequences were serious. Divided command meant that Humayun could not concentrate his full resources on any single threat. Nobles and soldiers calculated their loyalties by watching which brother seemed most likely to win. The Mughal ruling family functioned as a collective enterprise whose members could undermine one another, and it was an easy target for a determined adversary.

Section 50.19

The Gujarat Campaign and Bahadur Shah, 1535–1536

Bahadur Shah, who became Sultan of Gujarat in 1526, was an energetic ruler who annexed Malwa in 1531 and in 1535 attacked Chittor, the capital of Mewar. Gujarat controlled some of the richest ports on the western coast and drew revenue from the Indian Ocean trade (Chapters 47 and 49). It was also a refuge for Afghan and Mughal dissidents, and Bahadur’s power and ambition made him a natural rival. A legend, often retold, says that the queen mother of Mewar appealed to Humayun for protection against Bahadur, but historians doubt this tale, and it is sometimes treated as later embellishment.

In 1535 Humayun marched against Gujarat. Bahadur Shah, who had just taken Chittor, was defeated and his army scattered, and Humayun captured Mandu in Malwa and then the fortress of Champaner, which his men took by night with iron spikes driven into the rock. Bahadur fled to the coast and then to Diu, where he negotiated with the Portuguese, who had already obtained concessions at Bassein in 1534 (Chapter 49). Humayun’s occupation of Gujarat appeared complete.

It did not last. Humayun’s army, after the quick victories, was unable to administer the large and wealthy province, and he left its government to Askari and other commanders. Revolts broke out, Gujarat slipped from Mughal control, and Humayun’s attention was drawn eastward by Sher Khan’s growing power. Bahadur Shah was killed in 1537 in a scuffle with the Portuguese near Diu. The episode demonstrates a recurrent pattern of Humayun’s reign: brilliant campaigns, followed by an inability to consolidate. It also shows the extent to which the Portuguese presence had begun to complicate the politics of the western coast.

Section 50.20

Bihar, Bengal, and the Rise of Sher Khan

In the east, Afghan political recovery proceeded under Sher Khan, who had risen from local jagirdar to the dominant figure in Bihar by about 1534. After defeating the Sultan of Bengal’s forces at Surajgarh in 1534, he attacked Gaur, the Bengal capital, in 1537 and forced a large payment. Humayun’s campaign to the east began in 1537 and opened with a long siege of the fortress of Chunar, which lasted several months and diverted resources from the wider contest. Sher Khan meanwhile took the fortress of Rohtas, an important stronghold, by stratagem, and extended his influence in Bengal.

Humayun reached Gaur in 1538 and took possession of the Bengal capital. He remained there for several months, a delay that hostile accounts attribute to indolence and pleasure but that was partly a consequence of the monsoon season and the need to reorganize the territory. In the meantime, Sher Khan cut his lines of communication and consolidated his hold on Bihar, while Hindal’s revolt in Agra and Kamran’s maneuvers in the Punjab compelled Humayun to hasten west.

By the time Humayun left Bengal in 1539, he was weakened by lost supplies, by disease and desertion among his soldiers, and by the loss of the initiative. Sher Khan had pursued a patient strategy of avoiding open battle until Humayun’s army was exhausted, and the Mughal advance back along the Ganges carried it into a prepared adversary’s territory. The sieges of Chunar and the long stay in Gaur reveal how a campaign could be won in the field and lost through lines of supply and time.

Section 50.21

The Battle of Chausa, 1539

Chausa, near the confluence of the Karmanasa and the Ganges, was the setting for the first of the two decisive defeats of Humayun’s reign. In the early summer of 1539 the two armies faced one another across fortified positions. Sher Khan opened negotiations, which Humayun, short of supplies and eager to return west, took seriously. Reports suggest a truce was nearly agreed. On the night of 25–26 June, as the rains began, Sher Khan attacked Humayun’s camp before dawn, catching the Mughals unprepared.

The Mughal army broke. Many soldiers were killed or driven into the Ganges, and Humayun himself, fleeing, reached the river and nearly drowned. Tradition says that a water carrier ferried him across on an inflated skin, and that Humayun later rewarded him, in some versions by granting him a short reign on the throne. Women of the royal household were captured, among them Humayun’s wife Bega Begum, whom Sher Shah, according to Mughal sources, treated honorably and later returned.

The consequences were political as well as military. Sher Khan took the royal title Sher Shah, had the khutba read and coins struck in his name, and presented himself as an independent Afghan sovereign. For Humayun the defeat meant the loss of much of his army and treasury and a serious blow to his legitimacy as a ruler capable of protecting his followers. It also tested the loyalty of his brothers, who were now able to bargain from a position of strength.

Section 50.22

The Battle of Kannauj, 1540, and the Loss of Northern India

After Chausa, Humayun returned to Agra and tried to rebuild his strength. His brothers, who had been invited to join him, produced contingents of uneven quality, and Kamran, whose troops were important to the effort, was reluctant to risk them. Hindal and Askari were present but not united in purpose. By spring 1540 the Mughal army had assembled on the Ganges near Kannauj. Sher Shah’s forces, experienced and unified, approached from the east.

The battle was fought on 17 May 1540 and ended quickly. Much of the Mughal army dissolved after a short encounter, and the Afghans pursued the fleeing troops. Humayun escaped across the Ganges, hurried through Agra and Delhi, and made for Lahore. Sher Shah occupied the Mughal heartland and began to establish his own government. By the end of 1540, the Mughals had lost Hindustan.

Kannauj ended the first phase of Mughal rule in India. It also settled the question of whether Humayun could unite the family: Kamran withdrew to Lahore with his forces, and the brothers' disagreements worsened as Sher Shah pressed northwest. Within a few years Humayun would be a fugitive without a base. The next division examines what Sher Shah did with his victory.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of Mughal Rule, 1540

Long-term causes

An incompletely consolidated conquest, thin administrative structures, and a dynastic culture that treated territory as shared property. Afghan resentment and the survival of Afghan military networks in the east and Bihar.

Short-term causes

Humayun’s overextension in Gujarat and Bengal, depleted resources, lost supplies and communication lines, the rebellion of Hindal, the ambivalence of Kamran, and Sher Khan’s strategy of patience and surprise.

Event

Defeats at Chausa (26 June 1539) and Kannauj (17 May 1540), followed by the retreat of Humayun and the occupation of Agra and Delhi by Sher Shah.

Immediate effects

Loss of the Mughal heartland, dispersal of the court, and the beginning of Humayun’s exile. Sher Shah proclaimed himself ruler and began to organize an administration.

Long-term consequences

Fifteen years of Sur rule in northern India, an exile that exposed Humayun to the Safavid court, and the shaping of the political world that Akbar would later inherit.

Historical complexity

The defeat cannot be reduced to a single cause. Personal failings, structural weakness, the skill of an opponent, and chance each played a part.
Historical Debate

Was Humayun’s Failure Personal or Structural?

The question: How much of Humayun’s defeat should be attributed to his character and decisions, and how much to the political and military circumstances he inherited?

Interpretation A: Personal and strategic failings: Many Mughal-era writers, followed by some modern historians, emphasized Humayun’s indecision, his lengthy halts at Gaur and elsewhere, his leniency toward brothers, and his lapses in vigilance. On this view a more determined ruler would have prevented the collapse.
Interpretation B: Structural conditions: Other historians stress that Humayun faced a conquest only partly consolidated, a family that treated territory as shared, revenue shortfalls, and the skill of Sher Khan. They argue that Humayun’s decisions were not unusual for a Timurid ruler and that Babur himself would have struggled under the same conditions.

Evidence:

The Mughal sources that emphasize Humayun’s flaws were composed under Akbar, who had reason to portray his father sympathetically, and they also had reason to explain defeat. Afghan sources emphasize Sher Shah’s talents. Modern interpretations weigh these accounts alongside evidence of revenue, logistics, and the geography of the campaigns.

Why it matters:

Whether the early Mughal state collapsed because of a man or because of its structure affects how the achievements of Akbar are understood: as a restoration of an interrupted success or as the first real solution to problems that earlier rulers could not solve.

Section 50.23

Farid Khan, Sher Khan, and the Afghan Political World of Bihar

Sher Shah was born Farid, probably around 1486, into a family of the Sur clan of Afghan military immigrants. His father, Hasan Khan, held a jagir (revenue assignment) in the Sasaram region of Bihar and in the neighboring pargana of Khawaspur Tanda under a Lodi-era noble. According to Sarwani, Farid quarreled with his father over his treatment in the household, left for Jaunpur to pursue an education, and returned to manage the family estates, where he is said to have introduced fair practices toward cultivators and firmness toward headmen. The accuracy of such details is uncertain, because they are told from the vantage of later admirers, but they show how his reputation as an administrator was constructed.

He entered the service of Bahar Khan Lohani, who ruled Bihar as Sultan Muhammad, and received the title Sher Khan after allegedly killing a tiger. After his patron’s death he became guardian of the child ruler Jalal Khan and effectively controlled Bihar. His power increased through the capture of the fortress of Chunar, which came to him through marriage to the widow of its holder, and through victory at Surajgarh in 1534 over the Bengal forces. Chunar and Rohtas gave him strongholds, treasure, and access to the Ganges.

His career belongs to the Afghan political world of eastern India, in which chiefs commanded personal followings, formed alliances through marriage, and sought the patronage of sultans. Sher Khan combined tribal connections with an ability to manage larger resources. He watched Mughal methods, adopted elements of them, and avoided direct confrontations until he was ready. The eastern Afghan elites who followed him did so because he offered leadership, security, and a plausible claim to continue the Afghan sultanate that Babur had overthrown.

Section 50.24

The Sur Empire, 1540–1545: Conquest and Territorial Ambition

Having defeated Humayun, Sher Shah moved to consolidate. He occupied Agra and Delhi, pursued Humayun’s remnants through the Punjab, and fortified the northwestern marches. To contain the Gakhars, a hill people who had assisted the Mughals and controlled the road to Kashmir and the Indus, he began construction of the great fortress of Rohtas in the Punjab, begun in the early 1540s and today recognized by UNESCO. It was intended to protect his northwestern frontier and hem in the Gakhars.

His later campaigns extended the realm in other directions. In 1542 he moved into Malwa, and in 1543 he besieged Raisen, where a Rajput chief, Puranmal, surrendered on terms that were later broken, an incident described differently in Afghan and Rajput traditions and remembered as a breach of faith. Multan and Sindh were brought under Sur authority. In 1544 he marched against Maldeo Rathore of Marwar, a Rajput ruler who had built a powerful state, and defeated him at Giri-Sumel after a hard-fought battle in which Rajput commanders such as Jaita and Kumpa fought with distinction. A later tradition has Sher Shah saying that he had nearly lost Hindustan for a handful of millet. The remark is probably apocryphal, but it expresses the severity of the contest.

The empire he created stretched from Bengal to the Indus and from the Himalayan foothills to Malwa, but it did not include Gujarat, Kashmir, or the Deccan. Its limits show the practical constraints on expansion in a sixteenth-century state: large armies, difficult terrain, and the need to garrison numerous strongholds. He died during the siege of Kalinjar in 1545, leaving the work of consolidation unfinished.

Section 50.25

Afghan Kingship, Nobility, and the Organization of Sur Rule

Sher Shah faced the problem that had troubled the Lodis: how to build a strong monarchy among Afghan chiefs who valued independence and an egalitarian ideal of leadership. The Afghan political tradition made the ruler something of a first among equals, who ruled by consultation and by managing the tribal and clan loyalties of his followers. Sher Shah’s response was to assert royal authority and to reduce the dependence of the army on tribal chiefs, while avoiding the confrontation that had destroyed Ibrahim Lodi.

According to Afghan chroniclers, he maintained a standing force paid from the treasury and supervised through descriptive rolls of soldiers and the branding of horses, practices known from the time of Ala al-Din Khalji (Chapter 41). He also distributed commands among nobles of different tribes, transferred officers between districts, and mixed contingents so that no single chief controlled a large block of troops. He used non-Afghans as well, including Hindu officers such as Brahmajit Gaur, a Brahman commander. The mix of tribal recruitment, centralized payment, and political management gave him greater control than the Lodis had enjoyed.

Legitimacy rested on military success, patronage of religious scholars, and the claim to justice. Sher Shah is remembered for personal attention to government, including an itinerary of inspection and quick decisions. He built a mausoleum for his father and eventually for himself at Sasaram and constructed the Qila-i Kuhna mosque in Delhi, whose architecture blended Lodi and later forms. The monarchy he fashioned still depended on the loyalty of Afghan nobles, and his successors would find that his personal authority could not be easily passed on.

Section 50.26

Provincial and District Administration under Sher Shah

Later chroniclers, especially Abbas Sarwani, describe an administrative structure in which the empire was divided into units called sarkars, each subdivided into parganas. A sarkar was supervised by two principal officers, one responsible for military and police matters and one for civil and judicial affairs. At the pargana level, a small team of officials included a revenue supervisor, a treasurer, and two clerks, one writing in Persian and one in a local script. Qanungos, who kept records of local revenue and custom, remained essential intermediaries. Sher Shah is said to have moved frequently among the provinces, inspecting officers and punishing offenders.

Policing relied on collective responsibility. Village headmen (muqaddams) were held accountable for crimes committed in their localities, and they were obliged to find the culprits or compensate the victim, a measure intended to make rural communities guardians of the roads. Justice was administered by qazis and by officials who heard petitions, with Sher Shah himself acting as a final court of appeal. The aim was to make the countryside safe for travelers and traders and to ensure that revenue reached the treasury.

It is important to distinguish what is documented from what is retrospectively attributed. Sarwani wrote at Akbar’s command, decades after Sher Shah’s death, and presented an idealized picture that suited a Mughal court interested in showing continuity of good government. Many features of the structure had earlier precedents under the Delhi Sultans, and the sarkar and pargana were in use before him. His distinctive contribution was a degree of supervision and mobility that made existing structures work more effectively, and a program that consciously sought to make officials answerable to the center. Local intermediaries continued to hold considerable power, as in every premodern South Asian regime.

Historical Vocabulary

Sarkar names a district-level administrative unit in the Sultanate and Sur systems; a pargana was a smaller territorial and revenue unit comprising villages. A qanungo maintained local revenue records and knowledge of customary assessments. These terms predated Sher Shah and varied in institutional detail by time and place. Section 50.27

Land Revenue, Measurement, and the Agrarian Foundations of Power

Land revenue was the principal source of state income, and Sher Shah’s government sought to make its assessment more systematic. According to later accounts, officials surveyed cultivated land using a standard measuring unit derived from the Sikandari gaz, the yard introduced in Sikandar Lodi’s reign, and recorded the area under each crop. A schedule of rates (sometimes called the rai) assigned a standard yield for each crop, and the state’s claim was set as a share of this estimated produce, commonly reported as one third. Cultivators could pay in cash or grain, and the officials were to record the agreement in a written undertaking (kabuliyat), with the state issuing a document of title or terms (patta).

Additional provisions are reported. The state set aside reserves for famine relief, offered remissions in cases of crop failure, and sometimes advanced loans to peasants. Officials were to be paid reasonable salaries and punished for extortion. Surveyors and tax collectors received small fees. These practices, if implemented as described, could reduce arbitrary exactions and increase predictability.

How fully these measures were carried out is uncertain. Surviving documentation from the Sur period is limited, and historians have debated how much of the system described by later writers existed in practice. K. R. Qanungo, in his biography of the ruler, presented a favorable and detailed picture, while later scholars, including Irfan Habib and Shireen Moosvi, have stressed the limited reach of systematic measurement, the continuing role of local intermediaries, and the likelihood that the state’s actual practice combined measurement in some areas with traditional sharing arrangements in others. Tradition also states that Todar Mal, later Akbar’s finance minister, began his career under the Surs, but the evidence is thin. The best-supported conclusion is that Sher Shah’s government developed an ambitious program of revenue assessment that provided a model, and that Akbar’s later reforms drew on both Sultanate and Sur precedents. Chapter 52 examines the mature Mughal system.

Section 50.28

Currency, Coinage, Markets, and Commercial Integration

Sher Shah’s monetary policy is among the best-documented aspects of his reign, since the coins survive in large numbers. His mints issued a silver coin of about 178 grains, around 11.5 grams, with high silver content, together with copper coins (dams) and gold issues in smaller quantities. The silver coin is often called the rupiya, and the name became the foundation of the rupee, though the word and coins of similar weight had earlier antecedents in the Sultanate period. Sher Shah did not invent the silver coin of this weight, but he standardized it, enforced a consistent quality, and issued it from mints across the realm.

Surviving specimens carry Arabic legends giving the ruler’s name and titles, the confession of faith, the names of the four early caliphs, and the mint and date. Some issues include legends in the Nagari script, reflecting the multilingual character of the realm and the practical need for coins to be recognized by a diverse population. The consistency of weight and metal made payment easier, tax collection more predictable, and market exchange more reliable. The system survived his regime, since the Mughals adopted the rupee standard.

Commercial integration depended on more than coins. The security of roads, the removal of internal tolls, and the encouragement of trade, all of which Sarwani emphasizes, aimed at making goods flow more freely. The Surs' policies were consistent with a long Sultanate effort to build a stable currency, but the measure of Sher Shah’s contribution was in extending the standard over a vast territory in a few years.

Section 50.29

Roads, Sarais, Postal Communications, and Internal Security

Sher Shah is widely credited with building or improving a network of roads. The most famous ran from Sonargaon in eastern Bengal through Bihar and the Doab to Delhi and on to the Indus, along a corridor that had long been used for travel and trade and that later came to be known to Europeans as the Grand Trunk Road. Other routes linked Agra with Burhanpur and with Marwar and Chittor, and Lahore with Multan. Many of these routes followed older paths, and some had been maintained or improved by earlier states. The Surs' achievement was in repairing, protecting, and equipping them on a larger scale and over a short time.

According to Sarwani, rest houses (sarais) were built at intervals of about two kos (a kos is roughly three kilometers), with wells, shade trees, and mosques, and separate quarters and provisions for Hindu and Muslim travelers. Officials supervised each sarai, and travelers received food and fodder. The post system, called the dak, relied on relays of couriers and horses stationed at the sarais to carry official messages, and it also supported intelligence-gathering, since news-writers and spies reported on local conditions. The roads were policed through the same principle of collective local responsibility.

The result was greater speed of communication and reduced risk for merchants, officials, and ordinary travelers. These measures also served the state’s interest by enabling troop movements and the swift transmission of orders. Their effects can still be seen in the surviving sarais, wells, and mile-markers (kos minars), though many of the latter were built under later Mughal rulers, and they are not all attributable to the Surs.

Everyday Life and Social History

Travel, Safety, and Daily Life on the Roads

Travel in this period meant long journeys on foot, with pack animals or carts, exposed to weather, bandits, and uncertain food supplies. Merchants moved grain, cloth, salt, and horses, while pilgrims, soldiers, couriers, and migrating laborers shared the same routes. The sarai and its well offered shelter and water, and the provision of cooked food and fodder, as reported by Sarwani, mattered most to poorer travelers. Villages along the road bore the burden of providing guides, supplies, and security, and their headmen risked punishment if travelers were robbed. For the villagers, then, the benefits of safe roads had a cost, and the experience of the roads differed between a merchant with an escort and a peasant answerable for a crime committed near his fields.

Section 50.30

Sher Shah’s Death at Kalinjar and the Sur Succession, 1545–1554

Sher Shah was besieging Kalinjar, a fortress held by the Chandela ruler Kirat Singh, in May 1545 when a shell or gunpowder charge exploded near him. He died of his injuries on 22 May, reportedly after learning that the fort had fallen. The Afghan nobles chose his younger son Jalal Khan, who took the title Islam Shah, over the elder son Adil Khan, reflecting the importance of noble consent in Afghan succession.

Islam Shah (r. 1545–1553) maintained and in some ways extended his father’s centralizing policies. He issued detailed regulations (zawabit) governing the army and administration and moved against powerful nobles who threatened royal authority. Several Afghan leaders rebelled, including factions of the Niyazi clan, and he suppressed them by force. His treatment of the Mahdawi movement, a religious community led by Shaikh Alai, was severe, and Alai died after being tried at court in 1551. These tensions reveal the limits of the political settlement that Sher Shah had built.

After Islam Shah’s death in 1553, the dynasty fragmented. His young son was killed within days by Muhammad Mubariz Khan, who took the title Muhammad Adil Shah, and he in turn relied on his minister Hemu (Hemchandra), an able administrator of mercantile background. Rival Sur leaders, including Ibrahim Sur in Delhi and Agra and Sikandar Sur in the Punjab, claimed the throne. A struggle between nobles, kinsmen, and regional commanders replaced the unified state, and the empire’s military potential dissipated. These divisions opened the opportunity for the Mughal recovery.

Section 50.31

Humayun’s Exile in Sindh and Rajasthan, 1540–1544

Humayun fled from Kannauj to Lahore, where his brothers and officers debated what to do. Unable to establish a base in the Punjab against Sher Shah’s advance, he moved to Sindh, where he hoped to find support and a route to Kandahar or Gujarat. Sindh was controlled by Shah Husain Arghun, who proved unwilling to accept him as an overlord and who provisioned him inadequately. Hindal and others began to leave him.

During this period, in 1541, Humayun married Hamida Banu Begum, a young woman of a Persian family who had been in Hindal’s household, at Patri in Sindh. Seeking help from Maldeo Rathore of Marwar, Humayun moved into Rajasthan, but he learned that Maldeo had changed his mind about welcoming him and turned back across the Thar desert, suffering heavy losses from thirst and exhaustion. At Umarkot, he found shelter with the Hindu Rajput ruler Rana Prasad.

Akbar was born at Umarkot on 15 October 1542. According to a well-known tradition, Humayun, with little to give his followers on the occasion, distributed pods of musk and expressed the hope that the child’s fame would spread like its scent. The story comes from later narratives and may be embroidered, but it conveys the emotional setting of an exiled ruler whose fortunes had reached their lowest point. In 1543 Humayun left for Kandahar and then Iran, leaving the infant and his wife in the care of others.

Section 50.32

The Safavid Refuge and Shah Tahmasp

In 1543–1544 Humayun crossed into Safavid territory in Sistan, was received with ceremony at Herat, and made his way through Mashhad to the court of Shah Tahmasp I at Qazvin. His situation was delicate. He was a refugee king seeking military help from a ruler who regarded himself as the leader of Twelver Shi’ism and whose court expected deference. The negotiations continued over many months and involved ceremonial occasions, hunting parties, and the question of religious allegiance.

The Persian sources of the period report that Tahmasp pressed Humayun to adopt the Shi’i confession, and the Mughal narratives, which were written in Akbar’s reign, treat the episode with delicacy. It appears that Humayun accepted some outward signs of association with the Safavid cause, such as wearing the red Qizilbash headgear, but the extent to which he concealed his own Sunni affiliations remains debated. Tahmasp eventually agreed to supply a contingent of several thousand troops under his son Murad Mirza and the commander Budaq Khan, with an understanding that Kandahar would be given to Iran if the campaign succeeded.

The arrangement was therefore not an equal alliance. It involved dependence, bargaining, and mutual suspicion, and Safavid and Mughal accounts do not give identical pictures. Yet the stay at Tahmasp’s court also exposed Humayun to Safavid administration, military practice, and artistic traditions, and he later recruited Iranian officers and painters. The cultural effects of this contact, including the arrival of artists such as Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, are examined in Chapter 56.

Section 50.33

Kandahar, Kabul, and the Recovery of Timurid Power, 1545–1553

With Safavid troops, Humayun besieged Kandahar, which was held by his brother Askari, and took it in 1545. When Prince Murad died soon afterward, Humayun kept the city instead of handing it to the Safavids, an act that required diplomacy with Tahmasp. In November 1545 he entered Kabul, where he was reunited with his son Akbar. The recovery of Kabul and Kandahar restored a base in the region that had sustained Babur.

Securing it proved difficult. Kamran, who had held Kabul under his own authority, drove Humayun out again in 1546, and Humayun recovered the city the next year. For years the brothers struggled for control of Kabul and the surrounding regions, with shifting allegiances among nobles and local tribes. Hindal was killed in 1551 while serving Humayun. In 1553 Kamran, who had been captured by the Gakhar ruler Sultan Adam and delivered to Humayun, was blinded, an act that his brother reluctantly sanctioned under pressure from his officers. He was then permitted to go on pilgrimage to Mecca, where he died in 1557. Askari also traveled on pilgrimage, dying in 1558.

These years restored Humayun’s authority within the dynasty and rebuilt an army and court. They also re-established the cultural character of his household, with Persianate scholars, poets, and artists joining it in Kabul. Whatever the personal cost, the elimination of the brothers as rivals removed the most persistent source of Mughal instability. When the opportunity to return to Hindustan came, Humayun was far better placed to take advantage of it.

Section 50.34

Afghan Fragmentation and the Reconquest of Northern India, 1554–1555

In 1554 Humayun, informed of the confusion in the Sur state, crossed the Indus with a force of Central Asian, Iranian, and Afghan soldiers and began his advance into the Punjab. His commanders included Bairam Khan, a noble of Turkmen origin from a family long in Mughal service, who directed much of the campaign. Lahore was occupied in February 1555. Afghan commanders, divided among competing claimants, were unable to mount a unified defense.

An earlier engagement near Machhiwara had defeated an Afghan force sent by Sikandar Sur under Tatar Khan and other officers. The decisive engagement then took place at Sirhind on 22 June 1555, where the Mughal forces defeated the army led by Sikandar Sur himself. Akbar, aged twelve, was given nominal command, with Bairam Khan as his guardian and effective leader. The victory opened the road to Delhi. In July 1555, Humayun entered the capital, which he had left fifteen years earlier, and was proclaimed ruler again.

The reconquest depended less on overwhelming force than on exploiting disunity. The Sur regime, built around Sher Shah’s personal energy and the cooperation of Afghan chiefs, had come apart in the struggle for succession. Humayun’s army was well led, had more cohesion than the divided Afghan forces, and benefited from the experience of a long period of campaigning in Kabul and Afghanistan. The speed of the recovery stands in contrast to the lengthy process of Mughal conquest under Babur, and it shows the importance of the political context.

Section 50.35

Humayun’s Restoration in 1555 and Death in 1556

After the recovery of Delhi and Agra, Humayun faced the same problems that had undermined him in 1540. Afghan leaders had not been eliminated, Hemu and others remained active in the east, and revenue, supplies, and loyalties had to be reorganized. He appointed governors, including Akbar in the Punjab under Bairam Khan’s guidance, and began to plan campaigns against the remaining Afghan forces. Many of the officers who had followed him from Kabul expected rewards, and the redistribution of territories proceeded under pressure.

On 24 January 1556, while descending the stairs of the Sher Mandal, an octagonal pavilion in the Purana Qila complex whose original patronage remains disputed between Sher Shah and Humayun, and which Humayun used as a library, he heard the call to prayer, hurried to kneel, and slipped. He died a few days later. His mausoleum, developed in the 1560s and traditionally associated with the patronage of his senior widow Bega Begum (Haji Begum), with Akbar also playing a role in the dynastic project, is treated in Chapter 56.

The succession was uncertain. Akbar, thirteen years old, was in the Punjab with Bairam Khan, and he was enthroned at Kalanaur on 14 February 1556. His accession, the immediate challenge from Hemu, and the Second Battle of Panipat belong to Chapter 51. What needs emphasis here is that the restoration of 1555 was brief and incomplete. Humayun had reestablished Mughal claims but had little time to build institutions, and the empire passed to a minor under a regent, with Afghans still powerful and the dynasty’s survival in doubt.

Section 50.36

Comparing Lodi, Early Mughal, and Sur Institutions

The three regimes that ruled northern India between 1500 and 1556 shared more than is sometimes recognized. All relied on revenue assignments to maintain military followings, all used Persian as the language of administration, all depended on local intermediaries such as qanungos and village headmen, and all legitimized themselves through Islamic kingship adapted to the Indian setting. Their differences lay in the degree to which they centralized authority, standardized procedures, and integrated their nobility.

Section 50.37

The Unstable Foundations of the Mughal Empire, 1526–1556

Between 1526 and 1556 political control in northern India changed hands repeatedly. A Central Asian invader overthrew the Lodi Sultan, an Afghan leader expelled the Mughal heir, a Sur dynasty collapsed in civil war, and the exiled Mughal returned, only to die within a year. These shifts show that the institutions of the Delhi region did not provide automatic continuity. Revenue systems, intermediaries, and commercial networks persisted beneath the political changes, but the sources of military power and the principles of dynastic authority were unstable.

Several features explain the instability. First, rulers depended on military followings whose loyalty was conditional on rewards, and each regime had to find revenue to sustain them. Second, succession rules were either contested, as in the Timurid and Afghan practices, or poorly regulated, which meant that each ruler’s death threatened a crisis. Third, the fusion of territory and kinship made family members potential rivals. Fourth, the large and varied geography of northern India, with its rivers, forests, hills, and deserts, gave local powers refuge and resources. Finally, no regime had yet built an administrative apparatus that could extend royal control into the villages and bind elites to the center in ways that outlasted the ruler.

Institutions did survive. Persian as an administrative language, the system of revenue assignments, the use of local intermediaries, standard silver coinage, and the main roads outlasted individual rulers. The next generation inherited these continuities, together with the memory of how fragile political success could be. Akbar’s reign would depend on both. Chapter 51 shows how he used the lessons of the preceding thirty years.

Continuity and Change in Northern Indian Government, 1500–1556

What changed?

  • The ruling dynasty and its sources of legitimacy: Afghan Lodi, Timurid Mughal, Afghan Sur, and Mughal again.
  • Military technology and tactics: artillery, matchlocks, and field fortifications became more important.
  • The standardization of coinage and the development of road and postal networks under the Surs.
  • Attempts by successive rulers to subordinate noble chiefs to royal authority.

What remained continuous?

  • Reliance on local intermediaries to collect revenue and maintain order.
  • The use of Persian as an administrative language and the use of revenue assignments to maintain armies.
  • The agrarian economy of the Gangetic plain and the revenue it supplied.
  • The importance of Delhi, Agra, and the Doab as the heart of political competition.

Who experienced change most directly?

Military elites, whose fortunes rose and fell with each regime, and the communities of towns and fortresses that bore the direct brunt of sieges. Rulers and nobles experienced the political changes most acutely.

Who experienced greater continuity?

Many cultivators, artisans, and village communities, for whom the main continuity lay in the obligation to pay revenue to whichever authority controlled their area, though they bore the costs of marching armies and shifting demands.

Why the pattern matters:

Recognizing both elements prevents the period from being read as simple chaos or as a prelude to Akbar. It was a time in which an unsettled political order rested on durable social and economic foundations.

Section 50.38

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has followed three overlapping political projects across the years 1494 to 1556. Babur’s conquest drew on his Timurid inheritance, his long experience of loss and recovery in Central Asia, and the weakness of the Lodi state. It was achieved by combining firearms, field fortifications, mounted maneuver, and political opportunism, and it was extended through the victories at Khanwa, Chanderi, and Ghaghra. By his death in 1530, however, Babur had conquered more than he had governed. Humayun inherited a thin administrative structure, a family accustomed to shared authority, and persistent Afghan resistance. His campaigns in Gujarat and Bengal achieved rapid victories that he could not consolidate, and he lost his kingdom at Chausa and Kannauj to Sher Khan, whose political skill and patient strategy made the most of Mughal weaknesses.

Sher Shah’s regime was the period’s most developed state, and the most analytically significant. It combined Afghan military networks with the more systematic exercise of royal authority, a standardized silver currency, an ambitious program of land revenue assessment, and a network of roads, sarais, and communications. Its achievements must be distinguished from earlier Sultanate precedents, from retrospective praise in Sarwani’s chronicle, and from the later work of Akbar’s government. The regime’s fragility, evident after Sher Shah’s death, shows that sound institutions could not substitute for a settled order of succession. Humayun’s exile and return illustrate the opposite lesson: that a dynasty could recover its position by exploiting a rival’s divisions, by drawing on the resources of Kabul and Safavid Iran, and by eliminating the family rivals who had weakened it.

Source limitations shape every part of this account. Babur’s memoir is a precious first-person record, but it presents his own perspective. Gulbadan, Jauhar, and the Akbarnama tradition were written under Akbar, and Sarwani wrote at his command. Rajput, cultivator, and ordinary soldier perspectives are known mostly through the filter of elite or later texts. Coins, inscriptions, forts, mosques, and sarais are indispensable checks, but they provide limited answers to questions about daily life. The political geography of the chapter, running from the Hindu Kush and Kabul through the Punjab, the Doab, Rajasthan, Malwa, and Gujarat to Bihar and Bengal, also reminds students that the modern borders of South Asia did not exist, and that the region was a field of shifting alliances.

The chapter prepares for what follows. Akbar inherited a realm in which Mughal rule had been twice established and once lost, in which Afghan power remained formidable, and in which institutions existed only in fragmentary and competing forms. His reign, examined in Chapters 51 and 52, would draw on Mughal, Afghan, and Sultanate precedents to build a more durable order. Questions for further study include: How might the period look if told from the perspective of Afghan chiefs or Rajput rulers? How much of Sher Shah’s administration can be confirmed independently? What did the experience of exile contribute to the later cultural outlook of the Mughal court? And how should historians weigh contingency and structure in explaining why the Mughals returned?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the unstable first phase of Mughal expansion, from Babur’s accession in Ferghana in 1494 to Humayun’s death in 1556, and has argued that the conquest of northern India in 1526 did not by itself create a stable imperial system. Three overlapping political projects shaped the period: Babur’s conquest, Humayun’s loss and recovery of his inheritance, and Sher Shah’s Afghan empire.

The chapter began with Babur’s Timurid and Chagatai background, his repeated failures to hold Samarkand against the Uzbeks, and his construction of a new power base at Kabul. These experiences gave him military and political skills and a pragmatic understanding of the value of resources. The Baburnama, his Turkic memoir, is a vivid first-person source but a selective one. The Lodi Sultanate he confronted was weakened by the tension between Ibrahim Lodi’s efforts to assert royal authority and the traditions of Afghan chiefs, and the Punjab’s governor and Lodi relatives looked for outside support.

Babur’s invasion proceeded through a series of Punjab campaigns and the First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526, where field fortification, matchlocks, artillery, and flanking maneuver defeated a larger Lodi army. Historians debate how much weight to give to gunpowder weapons, and the best-supported view treats them as one element in a combination of tactics, leadership, and enemy disunity. The capture of Delhi and Agra gave Babur treasuries and legitimacy, but not an administration, and the choice to stay in Hindustan was a major decision for an army reluctant to remain.

The battle of Khanwa on 16 March 1527 defeated a coalition led by Rana Sanga of Mewar and including Muslim Afghan and Mewati leaders. The religious language Babur used before the battle was a tool of mobilization within a contest for political predominance, and the episode illustrates the danger of reading the period through simple Hindu-Muslim confrontations. The later capture of Chanderi and the victory at Ghaghra extended Mughal reach but did not eliminate Rajput or Afghan power. At his death in 1530 Babur ruled a vast area held together by personal authority and a few fortified positions, lacking a stable administrative system or clear rules for sharing power within his family.

Humayun’s reign showed the cost of this inheritance. His half-brothers held independent commands, Afghan chiefs in Bihar and Jaunpur remained powerful, and revenue was insufficient to maintain a large army. His conquest of Gujarat in 1535–1536 revealed a pattern of quick military success without consolidation, and his eastern campaign allowed Sher Khan to cut his communications and defeat him at Chausa in 1539 and Kannauj in 1540. Historians debate whether personal failings or structural conditions were more important, and the strongest explanations combine the two.

Sher Shah’s rise from a Bihar jagirdar to ruler of northern India rested on Afghan political networks, the capture of strongholds, and patient strategy. His regime, from 1540 to 1545, built the period’s most developed state: a standing army paid and supervised from the center, a sarkar and pargana administration, a reported program of land measurement and revenue assessment, a standardized silver rupiya, and a network of roads, sarais, and communications. Many of these measures had precedents in earlier Sultanate government, and the detailed picture of the regime comes largely from Sarwani’s retrospective chronicle, so historians distinguish what is well documented, such as the coinage and certain fortifications, from what is idealized or uncertain.

After Sher Shah’s death at Kalinjar in 1545, Islam Shah maintained and centralized the regime, but the succession dispute that followed his death in 1553 fragmented the Sur state. Meanwhile Humayun had survived exile in Sindh and Rajasthan, the birth of Akbar at Umarkot in 1542, and a difficult association with Shah Tahmasp of Iran. With Safavid help he recovered Kandahar and Kabul, eliminated his brothers as rivals, and rebuilt an army and court. He reconquered northern India in 1555 by exploiting Afghan disunity, defeating Sikandar Sur at Sirhind and entering Delhi.

The restoration was brief. Humayun died in January 1556 after a fall, and his thirteen-year-old son Akbar inherited an empire that had been twice established and once lost, with Afghan power still formidable. A comparison of Lodi, early Mughal, and Sur institutions shows significant continuities, including reliance on revenue assignments, local intermediaries, and Persian administration, along with real differences in the degree of centralization, supervision, and standardization. Sher Shah’s practices influenced Akbar’s government, but the Mughals were also heirs to Sultanate and Timurid traditions.

The chapter has emphasized that this period was not simply a prelude to Akbar. It was an era in which several ways of organizing military power, revenue, and kingship competed, and in which the outcome was uncertain. Understanding that uncertainty is essential to understanding why Akbar’s later consolidation was a significant achievement, which Chapters 51 and 52 examine.

MGU Library Research Project

Investigating a Fortress, Road, or Source of the Early Mughal and Sur Period

Choose a topic. Select one fortress (such as Rohtas, Chanderi, Chunar, or Kalinjar), one road or sarai, or one primary source (the Baburnama, the Humayun-nama, or the Tarikh-i Sher Shahi). Research its history. Using library resources and reliable academic sources, investigate when and why it was built or written, who controlled it or authored it, and what role it played in the events of 1526–1556. Identify the evidence. Distinguish between contemporary evidence, later chronicles, archaeological or architectural remains, and modern scholarship. Examine a debate. Identify at least one scholarly disagreement about your topic, such as the extent of Sur administrative reform or the reliability of a memoir. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, a map with commentary, or an equivalent presentation, present your findings, use careful language, and explain what remains uncertain.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to see the beginning of Mughal history as a period of risk rather than as the opening of a golden age. The dynasty that would later rule much of South Asia for centuries entered the region as one contender among several, lost its position within a decade of winning it, and returned to find the empire it had hoped to restore transformed by the work of a rival. That uncertainty is essential to understanding what followed.

The period also reveals processes that extend beyond its three rulers. The use of firearms and field fortification altered warfare, though it did not decide it. The standardization of coinage, the development of roads, and the elaboration of revenue procedures prefigured the institutional work of later regimes. The tensions between central authority and the claims of nobles, kinsmen, and local intermediaries recurred in different forms through the subcontinent’s history, and the response to them shaped state formation.

The chapter also connects regions that later chapters examine separately. Kabul, Iran, and Central Asia remained vital to the Mughals, and Gujarat, Bengal, Rajasthan, and Malwa shaped the course of events as much as Delhi did. Chapter 51 turns to Akbar, who would inherit this unsettled legacy and, by combining and extending its institutional lessons, create a more durable imperial system. Chapter 52 will show how that system defined government, religion, and kingship, while Chapters 55 and 56 will examine the society and culture of an empire whose first thirty years were far less secure than its later reputation suggests.

Students who understand the instability of 1526–1556 are better prepared to appreciate the later empire as a historical achievement rather than as a natural outcome, and to ask, of every polity in this textbook, what held it together, what threatened it, and who bore the costs.
