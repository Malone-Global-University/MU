Economy, Cities, Technology, and the New South Asia

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

South Asia’s new economy was built not by technology alone, but by households, workers, firms, states and migrants navigating unequal opportunities across a rapidly changing region.

A garment worker leaving a rented room in the Dhaka metropolitan region, a software engineer working for clients abroad in Bengaluru, a small merchant accepting a QR-code transfer, and a Nepali family supported by earnings from the Gulf might appear to belong to separate stories. Yet their experiences form part of a single regional transformation. Between 1991 and 2026, the organization of production, movement of people, and technologies used for work and trade changed profoundly across South Asia. The question for historians is how these changes were created, who participated, and why economic opportunity remained so uneven.

The preceding chapters followed the region's changing political orders. Chapter 98 examined India's reforms in the early 1990s, Chapters 101–104 studied different national political trajectories, and Chapter 105 addressed contemporary security. This chapter takes a different approach: it investigates structures that operated across those political events. Its subjects include demographic transition, cities, industrial exports, professional services, the smartphone, financial systems, migration and the persistent difficulty of finding productive work. India receives extensive attention because of its size, but the experiences of Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan and Maldives are indispensable to the comparative argument.

South Asian development was not a single move from villages to factories or from agriculture to software. In Bangladesh, garment employment and international buyers transformed parts of the economy; in India, technology services expanded alongside a vast informal workforce; in Pakistan, textiles and urban markets coexisted with recurring financing constraints. Sri Lanka combined comparatively strong health and education achievements with exposure to severe debt and currency crises. Nepal's remittances, Bhutan's hydropower and the Maldives' tourism supplied different economic opportunities and vulnerabilities. These are not simply exceptions to an Indian pattern: they demonstrate multiple paths of structural change.

The chronology of the chapter begins with reforms that accelerated in the late twentieth century but draws on earlier histories of state-led development, trade, public health and agricultural change. Its endpoint is November 20, 2026. It distinguishes observed historical events from contemporary estimates and forecasts; in particular, the World Bank's projection of 6.9 percent regional growth for calendar 2026, published in October, must not be mistaken for a final statistical outcome. Similar care is necessary with poverty lines, population estimates and comparisons between countries that measure urbanization or employment differently.

Historical evidence for the new economy comes from policy statements, censuses, household and labor-force surveys, trade and financial statistics, company records, factory investigations, documentary reports and oral histories. Each has limits. A ministry's reform statement reveals its intentions but does not prove the reform's results; an international agency's employment ratio describes a defined population but not every worker's experience; a company's account of digital adoption is not independent evidence of improved welfare. Students should ask who produced each source, what it measures, and whose experiences remain absent.

The central argument is that economic transformation changed the scale and speed of regional connections, but its benefits depended on institutions and social conditions. Better transport could expand markets without guaranteeing fair wages. Digital transfers could simplify commerce without ensuring financial security. More education could raise capabilities without creating sufficient jobs. The distinction among economic growth, productive employment and broad-based development will recur throughout this chapter. The subsequent chapters examine social institutions, cultural life, environmental change and South Asia's wider international position in greater depth.

Historical Context

At independence, South Asian states inherited economies shaped by colonial trade routes, agrarian inequalities and uneven access to infrastructure. Governments built public-sector enterprises, universities, irrigation schemes, dams and transport networks, often seeking industrial autonomy through import substitution and planning. These achievements created skills and productive capacity, but economic performance varied, and fiscal or external-financing constraints periodically limited state ambitions. The history of postcolonial development examined earlier in this series provides the necessary background to the turn toward liberalization and global integration.

The 1990s brought changes in international production and finance alongside domestic reforms. Container shipping, improved telecommunications and the growing organization of world production across multiple countries altered the opportunities available to exporters. South Asian firms were not simply passive recipients of these developments: merchants, manufacturers and professionals negotiated contracts, invested in new capabilities and created networks across borders. Yet bargaining power varied between international buyers and local producers, and between employers and employees.

Long-running demographic transitions changed the supply of labor and the demand for education, housing and health services. Urban growth and agricultural commercialization connected rural households to cities, while migration to the Gulf and other destinations linked family budgets to economies abroad. The expansion of mobile networks in the 1990s and smartphones in the 2010s added a new layer of communication, but not all households could participate on equal terms.

Writing this history requires using estimates with care. National censuses and survey methodologies differ. Calendar-year forecasts must be distinguished from fiscal-year observations, and data issued in November 2026 may not describe an outcome from November 2026. The chapter cites official and intergovernmental sources where precise figures are needed, while using historical comparison and everyday social experience to explain the processes behind those figures.

Section 106.1

South Asia's Demographic Transformation, 1991–2026

The economic transformation of South Asia began with a demographic transformation already under way before 1991. Population continued to rise, but falling death rates, expanding immunization, improvements in drinking-water access and changing family choices progressively altered the balance between generations. The effects were not synchronized. Sri Lanka entered the period with comparatively low fertility and long life expectancy; Bangladesh achieved a remarkable reduction in fertility despite lower average income; India experienced substantial variation among states; Nepal's fertility declined rapidly; and Pakistan's transition was slower. In every case, national averages concealed differences in place, schooling, household wealth and the availability of reproductive health services. The history of economic change must begin with these uneven transformations rather than with a single regional population total.

A demographic transition ordinarily describes a movement from high birth and death rates toward lower ones, but it is not a law imposing an identical timetable. At first, mortality may fall while births remain numerous, producing unusually rapid population growth. Later, smaller families gradually slow that growth. Better child survival can alter parents' expectations; girls' education, access to health services, urban residence and later marriage may affect fertility; and migration can reshape household structures without changing a country's total fertility rate directly. These relationships are historically conditioned rather than mechanically determined. National programs, the work of community health workers, and the social choices of millions of families helped give the region its new demographic profile.

India's enormous population made its trajectory central to regional statistics, yet the contrasts within India were as important as comparisons between states. Kerala and Tamil Nadu had moved further through fertility decline than parts of northern India, while differences in health services and female literacy affected mortality and family size. Bangladesh's achievements in family planning, immunization and girls' schooling complicated the expectation that demographic transitions required high industrial income first. Pakistan's larger proportion of children and young people produced different pressures on schools and employment, while Sri Lanka's aging created concerns about elder care and financing public services. Nepal and Bhutan, though smaller, confronted their own distinctive combination of outmigration and shrinking family size.

Population change reshaped the countryside. Where landholdings were divided among heirs, additional household members could no longer expect a viable farm from family land alone. Intensification, tenancy, wage labor, commercial crops and migration became important responses. But rural population pressure did not simply push people toward cities: irrigation, transport, local manufacturing, public employment and remittances could sustain households with both agricultural and nonagricultural incomes. Some villages grew into market towns; others sent workers abroad while retaining land as a source of security. The resulting pattern was one of interdependence among rural production, domestic cities and international labor markets.

The size of a population is not synonymous with the size of its employed labor force. A national population includes children, students, retired people and adults unable or not permitted by circumstances to undertake paid work. Moreover, the standard employment statistics used by international organizations depend on survey definitions, age thresholds and whether irregular or unpaid family labor is captured. Historians must distinguish a rising working-age population from actual employment, and formal wage labor from the large volume of informal economic activity. Without such distinctions, the idea of a youthful region can become misleading shorthand for prosperity.

The evidence requires its own chronology. Censuses give detailed snapshots but may be delayed, politically contested or incomplete; intercensal estimates and United Nations population projections fill gaps through modeling. The United Nations World Population Prospects revisions can change historical estimates as well as future projections, and fertility measures from household surveys need not agree precisely with administrative records. A responsible comparison specifies the date, population definition and statistical vintage. The large regional figures used in this chapter are therefore guides to historical scale, not counts exact to the individual.

By 2026 the demographic story had acquired a second dimension. The population of working age remained vast, but the share of older adults was rising, and the window during which an unusually favorable age structure might raise output was narrowing in some countries. The World Bank's November 2026 demographic study drew attention to this tension. For historians, its projections are contemporary expectations, not established descriptions of 2050. What can be established is that more people survived into adulthood, families generally became smaller, and political economies designed for a young and predominantly rural population increasingly had to confront both employment pressures and old-age security.

Section 106.2

The Youth Population, Demographic Dividend, and Emerging Aging Societies

In development debates, the phrase demographic dividend refers to a potential economic advantage when the proportion of people in working ages grows relative to children and older dependents. Such a shift may permit more saving, higher production and increased investment per child. It does not create factories, teaching positions or skilled jobs by itself. A young adult who cannot find productive work does not generate the same economic outcome as one with secure employment, and a woman who cannot enter paid work because of safety risks, unpaid care obligations or discriminatory recruitment may be excluded from the gains that age structure appears to promise. The concept is best treated as a historical opportunity shaped by policy and social institutions.

South Asia entered the twenty-first century with unusually large cohorts approaching working age, and the scale was immense. India's expanding labor force offered the potential to support manufacturing and services; Bangladesh's working-age population contributed to the growth of export garments and overseas labor; Pakistan faced persistent demands for schools, housing and employment; and Nepal's young workers often looked abroad when domestic salaried opportunities were scarce. These countries did not lack labor in the abstract. Their challenge was matching people with productive capital, reliable infrastructure, useful skills and institutions that permitted businesses to grow. The distinction helps explain why large numbers of graduates could coexist with businesses reporting difficulty recruiting workers with particular competencies.

Education was an indispensable but insufficient part of the transition. Enrollment in primary schooling expanded widely, and tertiary education grew with new state colleges, private universities and technical institutes. Yet attendance is not a direct measure of learning; basic literacy, numeracy, vocational practice and language proficiency could vary sharply. Families might invest heavily in tutoring or examination preparation while finding few positions that rewarded qualifications. Students in rural districts had different opportunities from those able to attend urban institutes. Young women's education often rose faster than their measured participation in paid employment, revealing that labor demand, transport, norms and care infrastructure also mattered.

The demographic dividend also intersects with the politics of aspiration. As young people became more educated and digitally connected, government positions, professional work and stable wages became widely sought markers of social mobility. Where such jobs remained scarce, competition produced long examination queues, prolonged dependence on parents, precarious self-employment and pressure to migrate. The scale and emotional importance of these processes varied among societies, but they formed part of the political background to debates about unemployment and governance. A demographic explanation must not replace analysis of employers, labor law, industrial demand and the distribution of opportunity.

At the other end of the age structure, Sri Lanka experienced the pressures of an older society sooner than most of the region. Some Indian states also approached these conditions, even as others retained youthful populations. Fewer working-age relatives per older person could challenge household systems that had historically provided much elder support. Health expenditures, chronic disease, disability, pensions and the financing of long-term care became economic questions. Most workers throughout the region had careers outside formal contributory pension systems; consequently, a population could grow older before formal old-age protection had reached most people. This differed from the retirement histories of wealthier industrial economies.

Migration complicated the arithmetic. When a Nepali or Bhutanese graduate settled abroad, a family might gain remittances while the domestic economy lost a potential worker or professional. Return migration sometimes restored skills and savings, but it could also be interrupted by recession, political changes or recruitment restrictions abroad. Similarly, internal movement toward large cities might raise productivity while increasing housing costs and weakening rural care networks. Governments could not treat population dynamics as purely domestic; labor markets extended beyond borders, and household responsibilities often remained divided across them.

The World Bank's November 2026 assessment estimated a regional working-age population of roughly 1.17 billion and emphasized a weak connection between demographic potential and actual employment. These figures support concern about job creation, but their regional average obscures country variation and their projections are conditional. The historical lesson is not that aging will inevitably cause decline or that youth guarantees growth. Rather, changes in population age structure interact with investment, institutions and social expectations. States confronting a youthful labor force and those anticipating rising old-age dependency must make different choices, sometimes at the same time within one national economy.

Section 106.3

Megacities, Urbanization, and the Transformation of Settlement

Urbanization was among the most visible changes in South Asia after 1991, but it took more than one form. Delhi and its surrounding National Capital Region spread across administrative boundaries; Mumbai's built-up area extended into suburban and satellite districts; Dhaka drew migrants and factories into its periphery; and Karachi and Lahore became ever larger concentrations of work, housing and public services. Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune and Gurugram also expanded quickly, driven in part by technology and professional services. These were not interchangeable cities. Their industrial histories, land markets, municipal institutions and social composition differed, as did the availability of transport and basic infrastructure.

People arrived in cities for several reasons: paid work, secondary education, medical treatment, marriage, refuge from disaster, or access to relatives already established there. Factories drew rural labor into garment districts around Dhaka; construction attracted seasonal migrants to India's metropolitan edges; trade and transport generated opportunities in Pakistan's urban corridors. Yet urban growth also came from births among existing residents and the administrative reclassification of settlements. A town could become statistically urban because its population and occupational profile changed, without experiencing a sudden migration wave. Comparing urbanization rates requires attention to how each country defines an urban settlement.

The most consequential transformation often occurred beyond older city centers. Villages along highways acquired workshops, warehouses, apartment buildings and commuter housing. Agricultural land was converted to industrial estates or speculative real estate, while remaining farmers sold, leased or resisted the sale of land. Such peri-urban regions blurred the boundary between urban and rural. Residents might earn wages in the city while retaining land or relatives in a village, and municipal services frequently lagged behind the actual built-up area. Land conversion created wealth for some owners and displacement for tenants, agricultural workers and those without formal claims.

Industrial geography helped determine which cities grew. Mumbai's ports, financial institutions and older textile districts formed a different economic base from Bengaluru's technology clusters. Dhaka's garment production linked it to export-processing networks and global retail orders. Chattogram's port connected Bangladeshi factories to sea routes, while Karachi combined commerce, industry and port functions. Chennai's automobile and engineering activities linked manufacturing to skilled labor and supplier systems. Urban economies thus accumulated advantages across decades; firms benefited from nearby workers and suppliers, and those concentrations attracted further investment. Congestion, high rents and pollution eventually created costs that could undermine the same advantages.

South Asian cities were also sites of unequal citizenship. Wealthier neighborhoods obtained stable electricity, private water storage, formal property rights and easier access to schools and hospitals, while informal settlements faced unreliable services and risks of eviction. City planners often treated settlement informality as a physical defect instead of recognizing the affordability constraints that pushed workers toward insecure housing. Migrants might contribute essential labor yet be excluded from some welfare schemes because documentation or residence rules were tied to a home district. Women's safety and long travel times influenced which jobs could be accepted; for care workers, factory workers and vendors, transport was an economic institution rather than merely an amenity.

Urban infrastructure changed substantially. Metro rail, bus systems, highway interchanges, bridges and airports altered commuting and investment possibilities in major centers. The Dhaka Metro Rail, India's expanding rapid transit networks and new expressways illustrated the ambition to manage mobility, while persistent traffic congestion showed the limits of infrastructure built after settlement patterns had already formed. Ports, dry ports and freight corridors mattered as much for employment as for trade: a delayed container could affect hundreds of workers along a garment supply chain. Large capital projects also raised concerns about debt, procurement, displacement and the difference between visible construction and effective public service.

Cities remained dependent on rural and overseas connections. Rural relatives supplied food and seasonal labor; migrants sent money back to villages; and urban household budgets were influenced by weather, harvests and energy prices beyond municipal borders. Urbanization therefore cannot be read as a simple departure from agriculture. The chapter's geography study should ask students to map economic corridors and the routes connecting ports, inland cities and migrant-sending districts. The broader historical interpretation is that urban growth intensified exchanges across locations, while concentrating investment, political attention and economic risk in particular metropolitan regions.

Section 106.4

Housing, Infrastructure, and Everyday Urban Life

The city encountered by a low-paid construction worker could differ radically from the city experienced by a software executive, even when both lived within the same metropolitan region. Housing markets were central to this division. Formal apartments near jobs often required deposits, documentation and rents beyond the reach of informal workers. Many households therefore rented single rooms, shared premises with other migrants, lived in unregistered settlements or commuted from distant suburbs. In Bangladesh and Pakistan as in India, the housing shortage was not simply an absence of buildings; it reflected unequal incomes, insecure land tenure, weak planning coordination and a shortage of affordable serviced land.

Tenure affected political and economic security. Families without legal title could be denied credit against property, face sudden removal or hesitate to improve buildings they might lose. Regularization policies offered security in some districts but also carried risks of speculation and rising rents. Evictions around transport projects or urban renewal schemes could move workers farther from employment, reducing earnings and increasing travel costs. Owners, renters, street vendors, domestic workers and developers had different interests in land-use decisions. Municipal policy therefore redistributed economic opportunities, even when presented as a neutral technical matter of widening roads or clearing encroachments.

Water and sanitation demonstrate how infrastructure becomes social history. Where piped water flowed only intermittently, households stored it, purchased tanker deliveries or relied on private wells. Those solutions imposed different costs on rich and poor residents. Women and children often bore the labor of securing water or managing sanitation where services were inadequate. Sewage systems and drainage failed especially under rapid construction or monsoon rainfall, with health consequences for crowded neighborhoods. Improvements in piped supply, toilets, waste collection and treatment were substantial in some locations, but reliable service and equality of access cannot be inferred from installation counts alone.

Electricity illustrates a similar distinction. Extending the grid into a neighborhood was different from providing uninterrupted, affordable power. Small enterprises needed stable electricity for machines, refrigeration and digital payments; households needed it for lighting, cooling and study. Load-shedding and high tariffs could shift costs onto generators, batteries and lost business. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, energy shortages repeatedly affected manufacturing and daily life; in Indian cities, service quality differed among states and utilities. The electrification of transport and buildings introduced new distribution demands, while expensive infrastructure sometimes increased municipal or national borrowing obligations.

Transport reshaped time as well as space. A daily commute of several hours could reduce the practical wage earned by a worker, constrain childcare arrangements and limit women's access to employment. Bus and informal transport systems carried large numbers of passengers long before many cities invested in metro lines. Rail expansion improved journeys along selected corridors, but last-mile connections and affordability remained decisive for ordinary riders. New highways could accelerate freight movement while cutting pedestrian routes or displacing nearby settlements. Urban improvement was therefore not a single measurable achievement; it depended on whose travel time, health and livelihood were included in the assessment.

An urban household could also experience opportunity and insecurity simultaneously. A garment wage or platform job might be higher than earnings available in the home village, yet rent, food, transport, medical costs and school fees consumed much of it. Migrant households often used rotating savings groups, informal lending, family transfers or employer advances to manage emergencies. Neighborhood associations and trade unions sometimes provided political voice; informal providers supplied services where government was absent. Such arrangements demonstrated adaptability but could reproduce unequal power, especially where residents lacked formal protections or reliable legal recourse.

The everyday-life perspective prevents a triumphalist account of skyline growth. Urbanization increased access to education, specialized health care, employment, information and social networks for millions. It also produced new forms of crowding, exposure to pollution and dependence on volatile cash wages. Comparisons among Delhi, Dhaka, Karachi, Colombo and smaller towns show how governing capacity, income, infrastructure and land tenure shaped the results. The history of housing and services belongs in this economic chapter because a city's productivity depended not only on factories and offices but on whether the people who staffed them could live safely and travel affordably.

Section 106.5

Economic Liberalization, Manufacturing, and Industrial Policy

The liberalizing turn of the late twentieth century did not mean that South Asian governments simply withdrew from the economy. India's balance-of-payments crisis in 1991 accelerated changes already contemplated by officials and business groups. The government reduced industrial licensing, revised restrictions on large firms, lowered trade barriers over time and altered the terms on which foreign investors could enter selected sectors. Fiscal and exchange-rate measures accompanied these changes. They transformed the institutional environment, but the historical record must distinguish a policy announcement from its effects: the 1991 statement described the government's intended direction, whereas investment, productivity and employment responded unevenly over the following decades.

The earlier system of industrial protection had emerged from postcolonial priorities, including the desire for economic autonomy, import substitution and domestic heavy industry. It helped establish engineering, pharmaceuticals and public-sector enterprises, but it also created licensing delays, insufficient competitive pressure in some industries and vulnerability to chronic foreign-exchange shortages. Liberalization changed incentives rather than erasing the older industrial base. State governments retained important powers over land, electricity, labor administration and infrastructure, and India's industrial performance consequently varied considerably among regions. National reforms interacted with long-standing local capacities in engineering, business networks, technical training and public administration.

The regional comparison is essential. Sri Lanka had begun significant trade and investment liberalization in 1977, well before India's 1991 reforms, yet civil war and later macroeconomic instability constrained the resulting opportunities. Bangladesh's export-oriented garment industry expanded with incentives, private entrepreneurship, international trade arrangements and an abundant workforce. Pakistan periodically liberalized and privatized enterprises while repeatedly facing external financing problems and shifts in political stability. Nepal's industrial potential was shaped by landlocked transport costs and access to the Indian market; Bhutan's development remained closely linked to planned investment and hydropower exports; the Maldives developed tourism rather than a large manufacturing base. There was no single regional reform package or common sequence of success.

Public policy continued to influence industrial location. Export-processing zones offered infrastructure and regulatory arrangements intended to attract investors and generate foreign exchange. India's later special economic zones, production-linked incentives and industrial corridors reflected renewed efforts to promote particular activities. Bangladesh and Sri Lanka also used zones to coordinate factories, services and customs administration. Such programs had mixed results. A zone that supplied reliable power and transport could reduce production costs; a zone that depended on costly public subsidies without developing local suppliers might create fewer lasting benefits. Economists and historians therefore examine actual employment, linkages and revenue, not merely announced investment commitments.

Manufacturing also required more than inexpensive labor. Firms needed skilled supervisors, machinery, working capital, quality control, reliable inputs and access to buyers. In sectors producing standardized goods for global markets, narrow delivery windows made electricity and transport reliability decisive. Small enterprises faced especially difficult credit conditions and might remain outside formal tax or regulatory systems. Meanwhile, automation and productivity improvements could raise output without producing employment on the same scale. The contrast between output growth and job creation became central to debates about India's relatively service-led development and the wider region's persistent informality.

Liberalization redistributed risks. Consumers often gained access to cheaper or more varied products, while some producers faced stronger international competition. New exporters prospered, but protected industries that could not adjust might close or reduce employment. Workers whose livelihoods depended on public enterprises, small manufacturing or older trade practices faced uncertain transitions. Governments had to balance consumer welfare, industrial upgrading, regional development and labor protections. Those goals were sometimes complementary and sometimes in conflict. A historical account should neither portray liberalization as a universal cure nor attribute every subsequent economic difficulty to reforms alone.

By 2026 the relationship between states and markets had become more complex, not less. Governments promoted electronics assembly, renewable-energy equipment, pharmaceuticals, logistics and digital infrastructure while continuing to manage trade and macroeconomic pressures. International disruptions and supply-chain diversification encouraged new industrial ambitions, but their realized results remained uncertain. The evidence of the preceding thirty-five years suggests that market access mattered, yet the decisive differences often lay in education, infrastructure, financing, public capacity and the ability of firms to learn. Industrial development was an institutional and social process, not simply a switch between state control and free markets.

Section 106.6

Textiles, Garments, Manufacturing Workers, and Export Growth

The rise of Bangladesh's ready-made garment industry is among the defining economic histories of post-1991 South Asia. Garment exports grew from early experiments in the late 1970s and 1980s into the country's largest merchandise export sector. Entrepreneurs developed factories and subcontracting networks connected to foreign buyers, while government incentives, trade preferences and commercial relationships allowed the industry to expand. Its growth drew young workers from villages toward Dhaka, Gazipur, Narayanganj and Chattogram. For many families, factory wages were an important source of regular cash income even when the work remained insecure. The transformation changed settlement patterns, household bargaining, consumption and expectations of women's employment.

Bangladesh was not the only garment exporter. Pakistan's textile industry drew on domestic cotton production, spinning, weaving and apparel manufacturing, and cities such as Faisalabad and Karachi built substantial industrial networks. Sri Lanka's apparel firms developed export relationships and skills in specialized production, notwithstanding the long civil war. India's textiles combined large modern factories, export-oriented garment workshops, small producers and extensive informal employment. Different forms of organization created different labor outcomes: vertically integrated firms could coordinate production and quality, while subcontracting systems shifted costs and deadlines onto smaller workshops where oversight was often weaker.

A garment's price concealed a long chain of relations. Retail brands placed orders with suppliers subject to seasonal demand, changes in fashion, tight delivery schedules and intense price competition. Factories purchased fabric and accessories, recruited and supervised workers, and organized machinery and finishing. Some work moved to subcontractors whose conditions were less visible to international buyers. Governments valued exports and employment, while workers sought higher pay and safer conditions. Global value chains thus connected consumers in wealthy markets to labor and regulatory systems far away. A failure at any point could result from local managerial decisions and wider competitive pressures operating together.

On April 24, 2013 the Rana Plaza building near Dhaka collapsed, killing more than 1,100 people and injuring many more. Its significance extended beyond the death toll. The disaster exposed the consequences of weak building oversight, production in unsafe premises, deficient worker protection and the fragmented accountability of brands and suppliers. The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, signed in May 2013 by brands and unions, established inspection and remediation commitments, while other initiatives followed different models. Later assessments found important safety improvements in covered factories, but concerns over wages, organizing rights and the conditions of workshops outside effective oversight persisted. The event belongs in economic history because the labor conditions were part of the production system itself.

The industry also transformed women's choices without automatically securing equality. Paid factory work could give women bargaining power over marriage, household purchases and children's schooling. But long shifts, restricted mobility, harassment, inadequate childcare and unsafe transport could limit those gains. Many workers contributed to households that still depended on agriculture or migrant income. Migrants might live in crowded rented accommodation and send money to parents or children in villages. The social meaning of a wage therefore depended on living costs, household obligations and actual control over earnings, not merely the number of women reported as employed.

Exports faced changing external conditions. Competition among low-cost producers, shifts in buyer sourcing, rising energy costs, transport disruptions, exchange-rate changes and the prospect of losing trade preferences all influenced investment decisions. The COVID-19 pandemic caused order cancellations and interruptions, illustrating the vulnerability of workers to decisions by distant customers. Firms sought productivity gains, product diversification and higher-value manufacturing; some succeeded, while smaller suppliers struggled with finance and compliance costs. Export success required constant adaptation, and the economic benefits did not flow automatically to workers in proportion to foreign-exchange earnings.

The comparative lesson is that garments connected South Asia to global markets through very different national systems. Bangladesh developed a concentrated export industry with transformative social reach; Pakistan retained a broad cotton-textile chain; Sri Lanka combined export garments with relatively developed human capital; India sustained diversified textile and clothing activities on multiple scales. Their experiences demonstrate that manufacturing can generate extensive employment, but labor standards, industrial productivity and the distribution of profits depend on institutions and bargaining power. The historian should measure export earnings alongside wages, factory safety, migration and the durability of employment.

Section 106.7

Global Supply Chains, Logistics, and Industrial Competition

Participation in international production did not require a country to manufacture an entire product. In a global value chain, different firms and locations supply components, services, assembly, distribution or design. South Asian businesses joined such chains through garments, automobiles, pharmaceuticals, electronics, engineering goods and increasingly specialized services. The potential gain was access to large markets and new knowledge; the risk was dependence on external orders, imported inputs and market conditions beyond local control. Not every economy integrated in the same way, and a rising export value did not necessarily indicate that most of a product's value was created domestically.

Logistics made these differences concrete. A clothing order needed imported machinery or fabric, factory production, quality inspection, road transport, customs clearance and a shipping connection. A delayed container could trigger penalties or lost orders. Chattogram, Mumbai, Jawaharlal Nehru Port, Chennai, Karachi, Colombo and other ports had distinct roles in serving exporters and transshipment routes. Inland freight corridors, rail connections, border facilities and warehousing shaped which cities could attract factories. Landlocked Nepal and Bhutan confronted particular transit and border costs. Geography was a constraint, but effective agreements and investment could modify its consequences.

Energy infrastructure was equally important. Textile production, cold storage, pharmaceutical manufacturing, computing facilities and modern assembly all required stable electricity. Where firms relied on generators, the cost of backup power raised prices and reduced competitiveness. Pakistan and Bangladesh encountered recurrent energy constraints; Indian states differed in distribution quality, industrial tariffs and the provision of reliable power to clustered enterprises. Electricity policy also involved political trade-offs among subsidies, financial viability, access and industrial competitiveness. A factory in a low-wage district might be less attractive than one with more expensive labor and dependable power.

The industrial cluster offered another mechanism for growth. Firms located near suppliers, skilled workers, transport and specialized repair businesses could benefit from shared knowledge and reduced transaction costs. India's automobile industry developed networks of component producers around major centers, while pharmaceutical clusters combined chemical inputs, manufacturing expertise and regulatory experience. Electronics assembly expanded in selected locations with policy support and multinational investment. But assembly employment was not the same as control over design, intellectual property or high-value components. Assessments of industrial upgrading must consider local supplier development, technological learning, wages and the resilience of firms when incentives end.

South Asian governments promoted special economic zones, industrial parks, highways and trade agreements as tools to reduce barriers. Outcomes varied because infrastructure projects needed competent administration, financing and connections to existing markets. A newly built zone without enough skilled workers, reliable utilities or credible investors might remain underused. Moreover, exemptions granted to large firms could create unequal competition with domestic smaller enterprises. Infrastructure was a public investment choice with opportunity costs: funds committed to an expressway or port were funds not available for schools, health services or other priorities unless growth subsequently expanded public revenue.

Changes in the international economy complicated industrial planning. The pandemic exposed shortages in transport capacity and imported components. Tensions among major trading powers led some firms to consider additional suppliers outside existing production centers, creating opportunities for South Asian economies. Yet announcements about a new supply-chain location should not be confused with completed investment or a durable relocation of production. Skilled labor, contract enforcement, trade policy and access to finance mattered to whether prospective opportunities materialized. Import dependence in energy and intermediate goods could also create severe pressure when export earnings or foreign investment weakened.

The region's uneven industrial geography therefore reflected cumulative history. Port towns, manufacturing districts and technology corridors grew where state capacity, infrastructure, private networks and access to customers reinforced one another. Other areas faced high transport costs and weak services despite abundant labor. The contrast challenges simple claims that wage differences alone explain export success. It also connects to the employment question: higher productivity and diversified exports could generate revenue, but the number and quality of jobs depended on which activities expanded and how much labor they required.

Definition: A global value chain divides design, inputs, production, logistics and sales among firms and territories. An export-processing zone offers designated infrastructure or regulatory incentives to firms producing for export.

Context: Garment factories, component manufacturers and service providers joined different segments of international production networks.

Misunderstanding to avoid: A factory situated in a global chain does not necessarily own the brand or design of what it produces.

Section 106.8

Information Technology, Outsourcing, and the Professional Service Economy

The rise of India's technology-services industry followed a distinctive path that began before the internet became a mass household technology. Computer engineers, software exporters and public-sector technical institutions developed capacities during the 1970s and 1980s, while policy adjustments gradually enabled private firms to serve overseas customers. In the 1990s telecommunications improvements, foreign demand, English-language skills among parts of the graduate population and the global expansion of computing accelerated growth. Work related to the year-2000 software transition helped establish relationships with international clients, but the industry's development was broader than any single programming problem.

Bengaluru became internationally associated with software services, yet it was part of a larger geography. Hyderabad, Chennai, Pune, Gurugram and other centers combined engineering institutes, business parks, telecommunications and expanding professional labor markets. Firms offered programming, system maintenance, customer services, accounting, data processing and business-process support. Later, multinational companies established engineering and research operations and increasingly complex global capability centers. These activities created professional careers and associated demand for transport, housing, hospitality and education. Their growth also produced urban congestion and sharp property-price increases in some metropolitan districts.

Outsourcing was not simply a transfer of identical jobs from one country to another. International firms reorganized work across time zones, contract relationships and specialized skills. A customer-support center required language training and standardized procedures; sophisticated software services demanded engineering knowledge and project management; data-center operations relied on reliable electricity and network connections. These differences affected wages, entry requirements and the possibility of advancement. Firms serving overseas clients were exposed to recessions, contract cancellations, currency movements and technological change, even while earning foreign exchange that mattered to national accounts.

India's experience influenced neighboring countries without being reproduced exactly. Pakistan developed software houses, export-service businesses and freelance digital work, particularly around Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad. Bangladesh's expanding technology sector included software services, technology-enabled administration and a growing freelance workforce. Sri Lanka cultivated skilled information-technology and business-service professionals while facing the challenges of a smaller domestic labor pool and later economic instability. Nepal's technology workers combined domestic firms, remote work and migration, while Bhutan explored digital public services alongside its more established hydropower economy. These sectors differed greatly in scale; calling them a single regional technology boom would obscure real inequalities.

The most important limitation was employment reach. Information technology could generate high value per worker and expand demand for university education, but direct employment remained small relative to the enormous South Asian labor force. The prerequisites for entry—school quality, language, computers, connectivity and access to urban professional networks—were distributed unequally. Many households financed private tuition in the hope of obtaining these jobs; some graduates instead encountered competition and underemployment. A sector may transform a country's exports or international image without providing employment to most citizens. That distinction is indispensable when weighing the development effects of service-led growth.

Technology-sector growth also changed social expectations and local economies. New professional households purchased apartments, financed higher education, supported restaurants and travel, and sometimes returned to establish businesses. International migration and temporary assignments circulated skills and capital across borders. At the same time, night shifts and long hours changed family routines, and the concentration of skilled opportunities in major centers increased regional disparities. Automation, artificial intelligence and remote contracting offered possibilities for both new service activities and disruption of established ones. At the research cutoff of November 2026, their eventual net employment effects were uncertain.

The historical debate about liberalization consequently cannot be settled by citing software-export growth alone. Supporters emphasize productivity, new investment, public revenues and the expansion of an internationally competitive professional class. Critics point to narrow employment opportunities, uneven educational access and the continued dominance of informal work. Both may describe genuine facets of the same transformation. The task is to connect the sectors' documented achievements to the larger labor market and to ask which institutions would permit their benefits to spread. The service economy was a remarkable part of the new South Asia, but it was not a substitute for the history of manufacturing, farming or ordinary wage labor.

Interpretation A — Opportunity and productivity: Reform removed barriers to enterprise, improved access to technologies and markets, and helped establish competitive export and service industries. Its effects supported higher incomes and new choices for consumers and workers.
Interpretation B — Unequal access and insecurity: Benefits accrued disproportionately to places with capital, skills and reliable infrastructure; formal employment did not expand enough to absorb the labor force, while informal workers remained exposed to shocks.

Section 106.9

Mobile Telecommunications, Smartphones, and Mass Connectivity

In the early 1990s a fixed telephone line remained a scarce asset in many South Asian communities. Public telephone offices, postal services and journeys to administrative centers were important ways of contacting relatives, employers or customers. Mobile networks began to change that landscape in the middle of the decade, initially serving wealthier urban customers. Competition, prepaid connections, changes in telecommunications regulation and falling handset prices subsequently expanded access. For millions, a mobile phone arrived before a private landline, and its economic importance came from being portable, relatively inexpensive and usable without the older infrastructure of fixed telephony.

The diffusion of mobile service was neither spontaneous nor inevitable. Governments licensed operators, allocated spectrum, regulated competition and sometimes imposed taxes or identification requirements that affected affordability. Coverage expanded where companies expected demand or where public policy encouraged extension, while mountainous districts, remote islands and sparsely settled areas remained more costly to serve. India experienced rapid growth in competition and scale; Bangladesh and Pakistan also developed mass mobile markets; Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and Maldives faced different combinations of geography, competition and regulation. The mobile revolution was therefore built by firms and public institutions, not only by consumers choosing new devices.

Cheap voice and text communications transformed market coordination. Farmers could compare prices or contact traders before traveling to town; transport operators could manage pickups and deliveries; small shopkeepers could call suppliers; migrants could speak with families without costly intermediary journeys. These benefits were uneven, and knowing a price did not automatically grant a farmer bargaining power when transport or credit options remained limited. Still, communications lowered some search costs and gave households new ways to respond to emergencies. The economic significance was often found in ordinary adjustments to familiar activities rather than in the creation of entirely new occupations.

Smartphones and mobile data added another layer during the 2010s. Devices became platforms for messaging, video learning, maps, advertising, banking, retail ordering and public-service information. Lower data prices and growing coverage allowed small businesses to reach customers beyond their immediate neighborhood. Online classes, agricultural advice and telemedicine widened possibilities, particularly where professionals or institutions were scarce. Yet effective use required literacy, reliable electricity, local-language interfaces and trust in services. A connected household was not necessarily a digitally capable household, and an advertised broadband speed was not a reliable measure of service quality.

Social hierarchies shaped access. Men often had greater control over household devices, spending and movement, leaving women less likely to own or independently use mobile internet in many settings. Rural residents might share a handset among family members, while urban professionals owned several devices. Inexpensive smartphones could be purchased through installments that placed pressure on budgets. Language, disability, educational attainment and the cost of replacement or repairs also affected use. Treating the number of SIM subscriptions as identical to the number of distinct people connected would exaggerate inclusion, since individuals could hold several subscriptions or maintain inactive connections.

Public institutions increasingly used mobile channels to distribute notices, register beneficiaries, authenticate users and communicate with citizens. During the COVID-19 pandemic such systems could support remote work or essential information, but they also exposed those without connectivity to exclusion. Internet shutdowns or restrictions, used in some political and security contexts, demonstrated that infrastructure could be disrupted by state action and was not simply a neutral technical resource. Cybercrime, surveillance and misinformation affected trust, but the wider cultural history of digital communication belongs chiefly to Chapter 108. Here the issue is how communication capacity changed the economic organization of daily life.

By 2026 the mobile phone had become basic infrastructure for many forms of economic participation. It connected remittance recipients, platform workers, street vendors, students and small firms in ways not available three decades earlier. Nevertheless, digital opportunity depended on complementary institutions: reliable power, affordable data, financial accounts, transport and the skills to evaluate information. The same technology could lower entry barriers for one entrepreneur and create a new dependency on platform rules for another. Its historical impact must be measured by changed relationships among people, firms and institutions, not by device adoption alone.

Section 106.10

Digital Payments, Banking, and Financial Inclusion

For much of the twentieth century many South Asian households held savings in cash, jewelry, livestock or informal financial arrangements rather than in accounts at regulated banks. A branch could be distant, identification requirements difficult, and minimum balances or travel costs prohibitive. Merchants often relied on cash, trade credit and personal networks, and migrant families received money through formal transfers alongside informal channels. Expansion of bank branches, cooperative finance and microcredit had already begun to change the landscape before smartphones. Digital systems accelerated the process but did not invent financial inclusion from nothing.

In India, expanded bank-account programs, the Aadhaar biometric identity infrastructure and the Unified Payments Interface helped form a distinctive digital payment ecosystem. UPI was introduced in 2016 and permitted payment instructions to move between participating bank accounts through interoperable applications. Merchants could receive small transfers through QR codes without the equipment required by older card terminals. These systems depended on institutions including the Reserve Bank of India, the National Payments Corporation of India, regulated banks and software developers. The result was not a single state application but an infrastructure serving multiple providers and users, with continuing questions about fraud, authentication and competition.

Bangladesh followed a different path. Mobile financial services such as bKash, launched in 2011, used agent networks and mobile interfaces to enable transfers, bill payments and other services for customers with limited access to conventional branches. Pakistan's Easypaisa and JazzCash developed comparable but institutionally distinct mobile-money networks. Their expansion reflected the practical advantages of using widely available phones and local agents in economies with substantial informal work and remittance flows. Sri Lanka, Nepal and other countries also developed electronic payments and digital banking, but differences in regulation, network infrastructure and customer habits shaped adoption. Describing all regional payment systems as copies of India's UPI would miss these historical differences.

Financial inclusion is not adequately measured by the existence of an account. An account might be dormant, controlled by another household member, or too costly to use regularly. Digital balances could make welfare transfers more direct and reduce some delays, yet authentication failures, mistaken transfers, fraud and inaccessible complaint systems posed serious risks. Older users and people without literacy or smartphones could require assistance, sometimes paying intermediaries or surrendering privacy. The difference between access and control was especially important for women whose formal accounts did not necessarily give them independent authority over household spending.

Credit created further complexity. Digital transaction histories and mobile platforms offered alternative ways to assess borrowers, and small firms could receive payments and loans more quickly. But fast credit could also carry opaque fees, aggressive collection practices and new opportunities for predatory lending. Financial education, consumer protection and supervision became more important as services accelerated. A borrower might appear newly included because credit was available through an application while facing greater financial fragility because repayment obligations exceeded unstable earnings. Historians should therefore resist equating transaction growth or app downloads with secure household wealth.

The systems also redistributed economic power. Banks and payment operators gained data about commercial activity, while platform companies competed to control customer relationships. Public authorities could improve tax administration and benefit delivery but also raised questions about surveillance, consent and the uses of personal information. Local merchants benefited from quick settlement while sometimes facing network failures, charge disputes or changing commercial rules. Since the infrastructure depended on internet access and electricity, regional inequalities affected the convenience and reliability of digital transactions. Economic inclusion required legal recourse and trust, not only technological capacity.

By 2026 digital payments had become an ordinary feature of commerce across parts of South Asia, especially in India and rapidly expanding mobile-money markets. The change mattered because it connected previously separate worlds of cash trade, public transfers, banks and mobile communication. It did not abolish cash or informal credit, nor did it resolve the causes of low income. The most historically grounded conclusion is that digital finance reorganized how money moved and could lower barriers for many users, while new forms of dependency and risk emerged alongside the efficiencies.

Section 106.11

E-Commerce, Digital Platforms, and New Forms of Enterprise

South Asian commercial life had long been organized through bazaars, neighborhood shops, wholesalers, weekly markets and personal credit. The growth of digital commerce did not replace these institutions wholesale; instead, it created new channels through which sellers, transport services and consumers could connect. Online marketplaces allowed a merchant to display goods beyond a local catchment area, while digital advertising and messaging enabled home-based enterprises to reach customers directly. Payment systems, logistics firms and warehouses were necessary intermediaries. A website alone could not complete an order if an address was inaccessible or delivery unreliable.

E-commerce became particularly visible in metropolitan consumer markets, but its economic significance extended into smaller towns where customers had previously faced limited product choice. Digital ordering sometimes brought cheaper goods or more transparent comparisons, while posing competitive challenges to neighborhood retailers. Traditional shops responded by offering local delivery, joining digital marketplaces or emphasizing personal service and credit relationships that large platforms struggled to replicate. The outcome depended on retail density, household income, trust in electronic payments and the cost of returns. Expansion was uneven, and consumer convenience did not settle the question of whether local employment improved.

Platform labor created new opportunities and new insecurities. Ride-hailing and food-delivery services connected customers with workers through applications that assigned jobs, recorded ratings and calculated payments. Workers might value the ability to enter a market without conventional recruitment or to schedule work around other obligations. But earnings often depended on fuel costs, vehicle maintenance, commissions, incentives and unpredictable customer demand. Workers could face sudden account restrictions or changes to pay formulas without a formal employment contract. Ownership of a motorcycle or phone shifted some investment costs from firms onto individuals.

Remote freelancing offered another model. Designers, programmers, translators and administrative assistants in Pakistan, Bangladesh, India and elsewhere could compete for international contracts without physically emigrating. Successful workers earned foreign currency and gained experience in cross-border business practices. At the same time, the work was often intermittent, dependent on ratings and platform policies, and shaped by competition from workers across the world. Access to good connectivity, professional English, payment channels and dependable electricity remained prerequisites. The image of universal opportunity obscured the educational advantages required to participate effectively.

Digital logistics connected platforms to older physical economies. Warehouses, couriers, delivery riders, call centers and packaging suppliers operated in neighborhoods and industrial districts. A small enterprise might increase orders online but struggle with inventory financing or transport damage. Platform firms invested heavily in attracting customers, sometimes using discounts whose long-term sustainability was uncertain. Questions about market concentration emerged where a few providers controlled search placement, delivery access or customer data. Economic historians should follow who owns the infrastructure, who bears the costs and who can negotiate prices.

An everyday-life comparison makes the changes visible. An illustrative lower-middle-income household in 1991 might have traveled to a market, used cash, waited for letters or landline calls and relied on family connections to find work. By 2026 members of a comparable household might message a seller, receive wages through an account, consult an online timetable and send a remittance electronically. These examples are composites, not documentary portraits of a single family, and should not suggest that every household experienced the same transition. Many still purchased goods in cash or lacked reliable internet access, while some mixed old and new systems according to convenience.

The history of platform enterprise therefore belongs between telecommunications and labor history. Digital systems made markets larger and faster for some actors, but they also altered the terms of dependence and competition. Employment classifications, consumer protections, transport safety and data rights became relevant economic institutions. As with earlier changes in industry, technological innovation created value without determining how that value would be distributed. A full account examines workers, consumers, small firms and platform owners together rather than presenting the new economy entirely from the standpoint of the successful application or the professional entrepreneur.

Section 106.12

Digital Inequality, Data Governance, and Emerging Technologies

The expansion of digital infrastructure created a new form of inequality even as it reduced some older barriers. A student with stable electricity, a private computer, fast connectivity and trained teachers could use online services very differently from a student sharing a phone on an unreliable network. Location, disability, income, language and household bargaining all influenced access. Digital inclusion was therefore a question of capabilities as well as connections. Public programs that reported growing network coverage could still leave residents unable to complete forms, evaluate information or resolve a failed transaction.

Gender differences were especially consequential. In many areas women had less independent access to phones, fewer opportunities for paid digital work and greater exposure to monitoring by family members. Online harassment and safety concerns could discourage participation, while domestic care responsibilities limited time for training or paid employment. Some women used digital commerce to develop home enterprises and professional networks, but these gains were neither universal nor automatic. The history of technology intersects with labor and social institutions without substituting for the detailed treatment of gender and rights reserved for Chapter 107.

Language also shaped participation. Interfaces designed primarily for English-speaking urban professionals excluded many potential users unless localized and made accessible. Voice interfaces and local-language content expanded possibilities, but they raised questions about reliability and the treatment of minority languages. Digital literacy involved recognizing scams, managing passwords, understanding permissions and exercising judgment about financial claims. Neither school enrollment nor smartphone ownership measured these skills directly. Where governments increasingly delivered services online, poor digital design could convert an intended efficiency reform into a barrier to citizenship.

Digital identity systems created opportunities and risks. More consistent identification could facilitate welfare payments, account opening and service delivery, reducing some forms of duplication or administrative delay. Yet biometric or database errors could deny an eligible person access, and large-scale collection of personal data raised concerns about privacy, consent and misuse. Different South Asian states adopted different approaches to data protection and cybersecurity. Private platforms collected transaction and location information that could improve services while also conferring considerable market and informational power. Legal institutions and accessible remedies mattered to whether the benefits were distributed fairly.

Cybersecurity became an economic concern as banks, hospitals, transport networks and governments relied more heavily on interconnected systems. Fraud, account takeovers, ransomware and interruptions to essential services could damage trust and impose losses disproportionate to household income. Internationally connected firms faced data-security requirements imposed by clients and regulators abroad. Small businesses often lacked capacity to manage these risks independently. Economic development therefore required institutions capable of enforcing standards and investigating wrongdoing, not simply investment in digital hardware.

Artificial intelligence entered the regional policy debate more prominently in the 2020s. The World Bank's October 2026 South Asia Economic Update, titled Adopting AI for Growth, described opportunities for productivity, new services and improved public administration, while emphasizing skills, infrastructure and business conditions. These were analyses and projections at the time of writing, not proof that AI had already transformed employment across the region. Firms could use automation to assist programmers, process documents or improve logistics; the same technologies might displace tasks and change entry-level hiring. The distribution of gains would depend on ownership, training and the demand for new services.

The historical comparison with earlier technologies is instructive. Railways and ports changed trade without making every settlement prosperous; electricity raised productivity without guaranteeing equal access; mobile networks expanded communication without eliminating informational asymmetries. The digital economy followed the same broad pattern. Its infrastructure could unlock remarkable opportunities, but complementary social and public institutions determined who captured them. By 2026 the central question was no longer simply whether South Asians could acquire devices. It was whether digital tools increased autonomy, productive opportunity and secure access to services across the region's diverse populations.

Section 106.13

Rural Transformation and Internal Migration

The countryside remained central to South Asian economic life after 1991 even as agriculture's share of national output and employment generally declined. Farming changed through irrigation, mechanization, commercial crops, improved roads, input markets and new connections to urban consumers. Some households produced grain for their own use while selling vegetables, milk or other goods; others depended increasingly on wages earned outside agriculture. Changes were regionally uneven. Irrigated and commercially connected areas often attracted investment, while drought-prone, mountainous or remote districts faced higher transport costs and limited access to services. The rural economy was neither static nor uniformly transformed.

Landholding patterns influenced choices. Families with sufficient land and capital could purchase machines or expand production, whereas tenants and laborers often lacked access to formal credit and bore the risk of crop failure. The subdivision of holdings among heirs made farming alone insufficient for many younger adults. Village enterprises, transport, shops and construction provided alternative income, but work was frequently seasonal. Agricultural prices and input costs exposed cultivators to both domestic policy and international conditions. State procurement, subsidies and irrigation investments shaped profitability, and the results varied sharply among countries and districts.

Internal migration became a major way to manage these pressures. Workers moved temporarily to construction sites, factories, mines, brick kilns, farms and urban services. Many retained a home village, returning for harvests, festivals, marriages or periods without work. Circular movement made the division between rural and urban misleading: a worker could spend most of the year in a city while remaining counted as part of a rural household. Conventional surveys sometimes struggled to capture these arrangements, especially where migrants lacked formal rental contracts or their employment changed rapidly. The migrant household was a network of locations rather than a single address.

Migration responded to demand as well as rural distress. Expanding metropolitan construction created jobs, while industrial zones recruited workers from distant districts. Families with established relatives in a destination had advantages in finding accommodation and employment. Recruitment agents and labor contractors sometimes organized transport and wages but could also withhold pay or create dependency. Workers unfamiliar with a city's language or administrative procedures faced additional barriers. Migrants' access to food assistance, health services and schooling for children was often constrained when entitlements were tied to a home district or proof of residence.

Women's work could change as men migrated. In some areas women undertook more farming, financial management and community responsibilities while husbands or sons earned wages elsewhere. Such changes did not automatically confer land rights or decision-making authority. Women also migrated for factory, domestic or service work, sometimes gaining independent earnings and sometimes encountering unsafe conditions and restricted housing options. Household strategies often combined several insecure income streams; remittances from an internal migrant could pay debts, sustain children's education or purchase agricultural inputs. The history of rural transformation therefore requires attention to gender, property and family obligations as well as output statistics.

The COVID-19 crisis in 2020 exposed the fragility of these arrangements. Lockdowns and sudden interruptions to transport and employment left many internal migrants unable to continue working or return home easily. Large numbers traveled from Indian cities toward their home districts under difficult conditions, while similar economic disruptions affected workers elsewhere in the region. The episode demonstrated how much urban production depended on mobile labor and how limited formal protection was for many people. It also revealed the social function of rural households as a last resort when metropolitan earnings collapsed.

Rural development and migration should not be treated as opposites. Better roads could enable commuting and profitable farming; education could encourage departure while raising remittance income; and migration earnings could finance improvements to a family home or village enterprise. Yet long-term outmigration could leave older residents with fewer nearby caregivers or reduce local skilled labor. By 2026 the rural-urban relationship was increasingly one of circulation of money, labor, information and expectations. Understanding this circulation helps explain both the growth of cities and the continuing political and economic importance of rural South Asia.

Section 106.14

Overseas Migration, Gulf Labor Markets, and Remittance Economies

Overseas labor migration connected South Asian households to the economic geography of the Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia, Europe, North America and other regions. Movement toward Gulf construction sites and service industries expanded after the oil boom of the 1970s and remained important throughout the decades covered here. Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Nepali and Sri Lankan workers filled roles in building, transportation, domestic service, retail, health care and other activities. Their journeys differed by income and qualification: some professionals entered internationally mobile careers, while many lower-paid workers accepted fixed contracts mediated by recruiters. Migrants maintained social and financial connections with families who remained at home.

Remittances were the most visible economic result. A household receiving overseas earnings could purchase food, fund schooling, pay medical bills, improve housing, buy land or repay the debts incurred to finance migration. At the national level, transfers provided foreign exchange that helped pay for imported goods and stabilize balance-of-payments pressures. Nepal became especially notable for the role of remittances in household incomes and national accounts; Bangladesh and Pakistan also received large inflows; India received very large absolute sums through its global diaspora. A country with high remittances was not necessarily a country with a strong domestic labor market, however. The transfers could reveal the limited opportunities that pushed workers to leave.

Recruitment systems carried unequal bargaining power. Prospective migrants sometimes borrowed to pay placement fees, transport, documentation and training costs, making them vulnerable if an employer changed conditions or a promised job failed to materialize. Sponsorship arrangements in destination countries could restrict the ability to switch employers, and domestic workers or workers in isolated compounds faced particular difficulties obtaining assistance. Governments negotiated labor arrangements and established support mechanisms, but enforcement varied. Not every migrant experienced exploitation, and many used overseas employment strategically; nonetheless, the risk was a structural part of the migration economy rather than an incidental exception.

Families also absorbed social costs. Parents and children could live apart for years, spouses managed households across time zones, and elderly relatives depended on remittances while lacking day-to-day care. Migration altered marriage choices, education investment, consumer aspirations and sometimes local land prices. Some workers returned with skills and savings and established businesses; others returned with illness, debt or little accumulated capital. Measuring national remittance totals alone obscures these contrasting biographies. Oral histories, labor contracts and household surveys are needed to reconstruct the lived experience behind financial statistics.

The informal transfer systems and formal banking channels used by migrants changed over time. Money-transfer firms, banks and later mobile platforms offered faster transfers and different fee structures. Digitalization could lower costs and make funds more readily available, but fees, exchange-rate margins and access to identity documents continued to matter. IFAD's September 2026 report, Sending Money Home 2026, analyzed ten-year remittance trends for low- and middle-income countries globally. Its global figures should not be relabeled as South Asian totals; researchers must identify the relevant destination or origin group and the measurement convention before comparing countries.

Dependence on overseas work created vulnerability to events abroad. Oil-price cycles, host-country economic slowdowns, public-health restrictions, migration policies and regional conflicts could reduce recruitment or interrupt contracts. Currency depreciation at home sometimes increased the local purchasing power of remittances but also raised import prices, complicating assessments of benefit. Workers in certain sectors were especially exposed to layoffs and wage arrears. At the same time, established migrant networks could provide information and assistance that reduced risks for new arrivals. Migration thus developed as a sustained institution linking labor markets, foreign policy, financial services and family strategies.

The longer historical significance is that the national economy could no longer be understood solely within national borders. A village's consumption might be financed in Doha or Dubai; a family's education choices might be shaped by demand for nurses or engineers overseas; and the government might depend on the foreign exchange generated by citizens living abroad. Remittances brought resources and sometimes mobility, but they also revealed global hierarchies of wages and rights. The resulting economy was transnational in practice, even where everyday identities and obligations remained deeply local.

Section 106.15

Education, Skills, Youth Employment, and the Demographic Challenge

South Asia's educational expansion after 1991 transformed the expectations of young people and their families. Primary enrollment grew, secondary schooling reached more districts and universities multiplied, including private institutions specializing in business, engineering, medicine and professional credentials. Increased access allowed many first-generation students to imagine careers previously out of reach. Yet schooling was not uniform in quality. Differences in teacher availability, language of instruction, rural facilities and household capacity to pay for tutoring influenced learning outcomes. A rise in years of schooling did not necessarily produce a comparable rise in basic competencies or marketable skills.

The expansion of educated cohorts collided with limited demand for secure salaried employment. Public-sector jobs remained attractive because they offered predictable pay, status and, where available, pensions. Competitive examinations drew many applicants, while vacancies often represented only a small fraction of those seeking work. Professional industries recruited graduates with particular skills, but employers sometimes complained of poor preparation even as young people reported the absence of suitable opportunities. These apparent contradictions were part of a labor market segmented by occupation, location and educational background, not proof that education had no value.

Technical and vocational training occupied an uneasy position in many societies. Manufacturing employers needed machine operators, electricians, technicians and supervisors, but students and parents could prefer university credentials or government careers. Training institutions varied in equipment, industry links and the recognition of qualifications. Where apprenticeships were informal, workers learned through relatives or employers without acquiring portable certificates. Policy campaigns promoting skills sometimes counted enrollment more readily than employment outcomes. The question was whether training connected to actual labor demand and whether firms would invest in workers after recruitment.

Young women faced additional barriers. Even where girls' school attendance and examination performance improved, mobility restrictions, unsafe commutes, childcare burdens and workplace discrimination could limit participation in paid employment. Professional sectors opened new career paths for some women, and garment factories provided wages for others, but these paths differed in income and security. Unpaid domestic and care work remained economically essential although often excluded from conventional employment indicators. Assessing the demographic dividend without attention to these conditions would mistake a statistical working-age population for an effective and equally accessible labor market.

Class and location also shaped the transition from education to work. A graduate from a well-connected urban institution might obtain internships and professional references, while a first-generation graduate in a remote district had fewer networks and higher relocation costs. Digital job platforms broadened applications but could intensify competition and enable deceptive recruitment. Some graduates pursued overseas study or work; others entered family businesses or informal self-employment. Underemployment could be as consequential as open unemployment: people might work for few hours, receive low wages or perform tasks far below their training without appearing in headline unemployment rates.

The pandemic widened existing differences. School closures disrupted learning, particularly where pupils lacked devices or quiet study space. Families facing financial hardship might withdraw children from education or postpone vocational training. Recovery required more than reopening classrooms: gaps in foundational skills could persist into later schooling and employment. Government and private initiatives to provide digital lessons helped some students but did not reach all equally. Historians should attend to this interruption because its effects on a cohort's skills and prospects may unfold long after the public-health emergency itself.

By 2026 the connection between population, schooling and jobs had become central to regional development debates. The World Bank's demographic study warned that favorable age structures were not being converted fully into productive employment. The history of education in this chapter is therefore also a history of unmet and changing expectations. More schooling brought real gains in literacy, mobility and professional opportunity, but employment depended on the organization of production and public institutions. A successful demographic transition required creating work that used skills productively, not simply supplying credentials to ever larger cohorts.

Section 106.16

Informal Employment, Women's Work, and Labor Protection

The majority of South Asian workers did not hold employment contracts resembling the standard salaried arrangements common in wealthier countries. They farmed small plots, sold goods, drove vehicles, repaired equipment, worked in family shops, cleaned households, performed construction labor, stitched garments at home or accepted tasks through digital platforms. Informality could refer to an enterprise outside formal registration or to a job lacking the protections associated with regulated employment; these are related but not identical concepts. Some informal workers were successful entrepreneurs, while many faced unstable earnings and limited legal protection. National employment statistics therefore require careful definitions rather than a simple division between people working and people unemployed.

The World Bank's November 3, 2026 report, Demographic Change in South Asia: From Tailwinds to Headwinds for Development, estimated that only about 60 percent of the region's working-age population was employed and that 88 percent of employed workers worked informally. These figures are reported as regional estimates based on statistical definitions and available surveys. They should not be treated as precise measures of each country's experience or as proof that all informal workers receive low incomes. Nevertheless, the scale makes informality indispensable to any explanation of regional economic growth and the limited reach of contributory social insurance.

Measured unemployment was only one aspect of labor insecurity. A person who worked intermittently or for very low wages could be classified as employed while remaining economically vulnerable. A family worker might contribute substantial labor without direct pay. Seasonal agricultural employment created months of intense activity alongside periods with little income. Workers lacking written contracts could find it difficult to recover unpaid wages or challenge unsafe conditions. The historical issue is not simply how many jobs existed, but whether those jobs supported predictable livelihoods, productivity and protection against illness, disability and old age.

Women faced specific forms of exclusion that were not captured adequately by a single labor-force participation rate. Women often performed unpaid care, household production, family farming and home-based piecework. Some work was undercounted or difficult to classify, and surveys could produce different results depending on how questions were asked. Social expectations, physical safety, childcare and lack of nearby jobs also limited paid employment. At the same time, the expansion of garment production, education, professional services and small enterprise created important new opportunities. The history cannot be reduced either to a narrative of permanent exclusion or to the assumption that rising female education automatically yielded secure work.

Labor organizations and public policy shaped conditions. Unions negotiated wages and safety in some factories; legal protections established minimum standards, but enforcement depended on inspectors, courts and workers' ability to make claims. Informal vendors, domestic workers and migrants often lacked collective representation. Social-insurance schemes and public transfers expanded in some countries, but coverage was incomplete and portability across regions uncertain. During crises, such as the pandemic, the absence of paid leave or unemployment insurance could force people to keep working despite health risks or to abandon urban employment abruptly.

Digital labor markets complicated classification. A delivery rider could operate as an independent contractor in a legal sense while depending on one platform for most income. Firms emphasized flexibility, but the effective freedom to refuse tasks or bargain over rates could be limited by algorithmic allocation and ratings. Policymakers debated appropriate contributions to insurance, obligations for workplace injuries and workers' access to information about pay decisions. Older informal practices did not vanish when work became mediated by an application. Rather, digital systems sometimes combined familiar insecurity with new forms of monitoring and centralized control.

The historical significance of informality extends beyond labor welfare. A government collecting limited revenue from precarious jobs may struggle to finance public services, while firms depending on low-paid work may have weak incentives to invest in skills and equipment. Households without stable wages save defensively and may restrict spending on education or health. Yet informal networks can also offer flexibility, low entry costs and resilience when formal institutions fail. The policy challenge is not merely to register workers on paper; it is to expand productive opportunities and meaningful protections. That distinction is essential to understanding why strong regional output growth could coexist with widespread economic insecurity through 2026.

Section 106.17

Poverty Reduction, Human Development, and Changes in Living Standards

Economic growth matters historically not only because national production rises but because it may alter ordinary lives. Across much of South Asia since 1991, longer lifespans, expanded schooling, improved vaccination, greater access to electricity and better communications changed what households could expect. Bangladesh's progress in child survival and education attracted attention beyond its income level; Sri Lanka retained a long record of comparatively strong human-development achievements despite economic and political shocks; and Indian states exhibited substantial differences in health, education and public provision. Nepal and Bhutan also made major gains in access to basic services, while the Maldives developed infrastructure adapted to an island geography. None of these achievements eliminated deprivation or unequal access.

Poverty is harder to measure than it first appears. National poverty lines reflect domestic prices and social judgments, whereas international lines permit comparisons by converting purchasing power rather than using market exchange rates alone. Household surveys ask about consumption or income and must account for changes in prices, household size, the time period respondents remember and the availability of samples. Some countries conduct surveys regularly; others have long gaps. Rural and urban costs differ, and public services alter actual welfare without always appearing in cash income. A claim about the number of people lifted out of poverty is therefore meaningful only if the poverty line, statistical year and methodology are stated.

The World Bank's June 2025 revision illustrates the issue. The international extreme-poverty threshold changed from $2.15 per person per day in 2017 purchasing-power terms to $3.00 in 2021 purchasing-power terms. Revised survey methods and prices altered reported poverty levels, including South Asian estimates. This does not mean millions suddenly became richer or poorer in June 2025. It means the measurement framework changed, and historical comparisons must use a consistent series or explain the break. School textbooks that combine incompatible poverty figures from different vintages risk giving a false impression of precision.

Material living standards included more than a single income measure. Electricity enabled evening study and refrigeration; roads improved access to markets and emergency care; mobile communication reduced some barriers to information. Sanitation, safe drinking water and maternal health services could raise welfare even where cash earnings remained modest. Schooling offered individual benefits but also changed expectations concerning family size, children's employment and migration. These improvements were uneven by gender, location and community. A village with a new clinic might still lack doctors, while a household counted as electrified might endure regular outages. The difference between physical access and reliable service remained important.

Nutrition and educational quality revealed persistent weaknesses. Children could attend school while failing to acquire expected competencies, and households above a narrowly defined poverty threshold could remain vulnerable to illness or lost work. Food prices, seasonal unemployment and medical expenses could drive a family into debt. Malnutrition and anemia affected work capacity and long-term health. Social programs, school meals, employment schemes and cash transfers attempted to reduce such risks, with different designs and results across national and subnational governments. Historians should assess not only the existence of a program but also its coverage, funding and practical accessibility.

Large shocks exposed the fragility of progress. A pandemic, flood, crop failure, energy-price increase or currency crisis could quickly reduce purchasing power for households without savings or formal protection. Sri Lanka's 2022 shortages and inflation illustrated how macroeconomic instability could affect access to fuel, food and medicines even in a country with relatively strong historical health and education outcomes. The pandemic caused disruptions to work and schooling across the region, with unequal capacity to recover. Economic resilience was therefore a separate dimension of development from a measured decline in poverty during favorable years.

A historical synthesis must hold achievement and vulnerability together. Incomes and public services improved in many places, and opportunities available to ordinary families in 2026 differed considerably from those of 1991. But improvements were distributed through unequal landholding, labor markets, gender relations and government institutions. Some families accumulated savings and professional qualifications; others remained one illness or lost wage from hardship. Development is best understood as a changing set of capabilities and securities, not simply a rising national output figure or an abstract claim that poverty has ended.

Section 106.18

Wealth, Regional Inequality, and Uneven Development

Economic growth did not unfold uniformly across South Asia, either between countries or within them. Industrial corridors, capital cities and coastal districts often attracted investment, while remote highland areas, drought-prone regions and less connected interior districts developed more slowly. India's southern and western urban-industrial centers accumulated advantages that differed from the challenges faced by parts of the eastern Gangetic plain. Bangladesh's commercial concentration around Dhaka and Chattogram coexisted with varied rural conditions. Pakistan's major metropolitan areas offered opportunities unavailable in many peripheral districts. Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan and Maldives similarly displayed differences between central commercial locations and more distant communities. Such disparities emerged from history as well as geography.

The concentration of wealth should be distinguished from income inequality. A professional salary measures a flow over time; ownership of urban land, housing, business equity or financial assets represents accumulated wealth. Rising property prices could enrich owners while making housing less affordable for younger workers and renters. Inherited education, business networks and the ability to borrow against collateral shaped access to profitable opportunities. Statistics based on household expenditure may show a different degree of inequality from surveys of income or estimates of asset ownership. Comparisons therefore need to state which distribution they describe and how reliably it is measured.

Regional concentration could reinforce itself. A district with good transport, skilled labor, universities and dependable electricity was attractive to firms; incoming investment then supported more services and training, encouraging further expansion. Other districts faced a self-reinforcing shortage of capital, public facilities and professional opportunities. Government fiscal transfers, rural roads, education investment and decentralized administration attempted to counter these patterns. However, local capacity determined whether resources translated into effective services. The same national economic policy could produce different outcomes when implemented in places with unequal infrastructure and institutional experience.

Income and opportunity also varied among households living in the same city or village. Formal professionals benefited from stable wages, employee benefits and access to credit; many informal workers depended on unpredictable daily earnings. The cost of water, transport and housing could absorb a large share of a low-income household's budget. Children attending well-resourced schools had different prospects from those whose education was interrupted by seasonal migration or unpaid household work. The detailed political and historical treatment of caste, gender and religion belongs to Chapter 107, but their influence on economic opportunity cannot be ignored in a chapter about the distribution of growth.

Public investment could reduce or deepen disparities. A well-placed transport link might connect a remote district to markets and schools; an expensive corridor serving only already prosperous firms might concentrate benefits further. Digital payments could lower transaction costs for small businesses, but the advantages were greater where reliable network coverage and financial literacy already existed. Export industries could generate substantial foreign exchange while relying on low-paid labor or imported inputs. Government budgets therefore mediated who gained from growth. The key question was how private investment, public services and social protection interacted, not which sector expanded fastest.

The scale of international migration introduced another layer. Families with the resources and networks to send a member abroad might receive transfers that allowed investments in housing or schooling; families unable to finance migration could be excluded from those opportunities. Yet migrant households could also accumulate debt or lose income when contracts ended. Tourism communities in the Maldives and Sri Lanka benefited from overseas visitors while facing sudden downturns; Bhutan's hydropower communities experienced different opportunities from those dependent on subsistence agriculture or public employment. Uneven development was not simply a ranking of countries by national income but a pattern of specialized and interdependent local economies.

Economic historians therefore need several measures at once: output per person, real household consumption, access to services, labor earnings, ownership and exposure to shocks. A country can grow rapidly while wealth remains concentrated, and a country can improve health or education despite slower expansion of gross domestic product. Neither observation invalidates the other. The new South Asia combined remarkable economic dynamism with persistent inequalities rooted in location, labor organization and the accumulation of assets. That tension should govern the interpretation of the final section rather than be treated as an afterthought.

Section 106.19

Growth, Economic Disruption, and Development through 2026

The first decades of the twenty-first century brought strong economic expansion to parts of South Asia, followed by shocks that revealed the limits of integration and the costs of weak buffers. International demand supported garment production and technology services; domestic consumption, construction and public infrastructure investment drove activity in other sectors. Governments sought manufacturing exports, foreign investment and modern services, but the mix of growth differed among countries. The region did not experience one continuous boom. Global financial instability, commodity-price cycles, political disruptions, severe weather and domestic policy mistakes periodically interrupted development.

The COVID-19 pandemic was an especially consequential turning point. Restrictions on movement and commerce interrupted factories, construction, tourism and retail activity, and millions of migrants lost income or were forced to return home. Schools closed, disrupting the accumulation of human capital, while small enterprises faced falling demand and limited cash reserves. Digital services helped some businesses continue operating and enabled households to communicate across distance, but workers whose livelihoods depended on physical presence had fewer alternatives. The economic recovery that followed was uneven, and the social costs were not captured fully by the rebound of annual output statistics.

Sri Lanka's economic collapse in 2022 demonstrated the interaction of external vulnerability and domestic policy. A severe foreign-exchange shortage, debt distress, inflation and interruptions to fuel and medicine supplies undermined living standards. Subsequent stabilization and debt restructuring generated a recovery, but hardship and policy debate persisted. IMF reporting in 2026 described substantial progress alongside continuing risks. Chapter 103 provides the detailed political history; here Sri Lanka serves as evidence that public services and earlier human-development achievements do not eliminate vulnerability to a balance-of-payments crisis or unsustainable financing arrangements.

Pakistan faced recurring external financing constraints, inflation and the challenge of maintaining investment and energy supplies while promoting exports. Bangladesh, long celebrated for garment-led development, experienced slower growth after 2023 amid financial-sector weaknesses, energy issues and investment constraints. The World Bank's October 2026 country update described stalled job creation and pressures on women's employment. India's economy continued to expand strongly, with the World Bank's October 2026 India update projecting growth of 7.1 percent in fiscal year 2026–27, a forecast rather than a completed outcome. National trajectories were distinct, and even strong output performance left unresolved the challenge of creating enough productive work.

The smaller economies likewise followed different paths. Nepal's dependence on labor migration and remittances brought foreign exchange but exposed households to labor conditions abroad. Bhutan invested in hydropower and related infrastructure, while confronting youth employment challenges and migration by educated workers. The Maldives depended heavily on tourism and infrastructure spending; the World Bank's June 2026 development update estimated growth in 2025 while emphasizing high risks from public debt. These comparisons show why international tourism, energy exports and migrant transfers mattered to national development in ways that a generic regional manufacturing story would miss.

The October 6, 2026 World Bank South Asia Economic Update, Adopting AI for Growth, projected 6.9 percent regional growth for calendar year 2026. This was a dated projection made before the year had ended, and not an observed final annual result. Other institutions used different country groupings, reporting periods and assumptions, sometimes producing different figures. Its significance for this chapter lies less in the exact decimal than in the tension emphasized by contemporary analysis: the region could display rapid aggregate growth while employment ratios, informality, inequality and vulnerability remained pressing concerns. Future outcomes of artificial intelligence, industrial policies and global trade realignments were uncertain at the research cutoff.

The longer historical balance is therefore mixed but substantial. New industries, mobile networks, roads, urban institutions and international labor connections transformed opportunities that were scarce in 1991. Yet infrastructure gaps, inadequate social protection, uneven education, gender barriers and weak job creation limited the reach of growth. By November 2026 the South Asian economy was more connected, more urban and more technologically capable, but it was also exposed to shocks in foreign markets, energy costs and domestic financial systems. The unresolved question was not whether development had occurred. It was whether further gains could be translated into broad, secure and productive livelihoods.

Section 106.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The economic history of South Asia from 1991 to 2026 is a history of transformation without uniform convergence. Population structures changed, cities expanded, and manufacturing and services joined previously distant locations to international markets. The telephone became a mobile device, the mobile device became a payment and work platform, and families increasingly organized their livelihoods across cities and national borders. These developments did not displace agriculture, informal employment or older social networks. Instead, they were layered upon them. The same household might cultivate land, receive a Gulf remittance, send a daughter to a garment factory and make purchases with a digital wallet. Such combinations are a defining feature of the region's economic history.

The historical causes were multiple. Governments altered the rules governing trade and investment; firms built productive networks and developed new skills; consumers adopted communications and payment technologies; workers moved to places where wages or opportunities seemed more favorable; and households invested in education and health. Geography, historical institutions and political decisions shaped the results. A port with reliable transport could support an export industry, a university city could attract professional services, and a rural district lacking infrastructure could remain dependent on migration. Technology expanded options but did not remove differences in bargaining power or the costs of obtaining capital and training.

The comparative histories demonstrate that there was no single route to development. India's information-technology services and diversified manufacturing were important but employed different segments of the workforce. Bangladesh's garment industry combined extraordinary export expansion with difficult labor and safety questions. Pakistan's textiles and urban commerce developed under recurrent macroeconomic constraints. Sri Lanka joined strong human-development traditions with severe financial vulnerability. Nepal's remittance networks, Bhutan's hydropower investments and the Maldives' tourism economy each offered resources and distinctive risks. Differences in national scale mattered, but institutional quality, sectoral specialization and the distribution of opportunity mattered as well.

A central distinction runs through the chapter: growth is an increase in economic output, employment describes the utilization of labor, and development concerns the expansion of people's capabilities and security. These outcomes can reinforce one another, but they are not identical. Strong exports need not create sufficient jobs; more accounts need not guarantee safe savings or credit; and higher education levels need not deliver professional employment. A reduction in extreme poverty is important even when many households remain vulnerable to shocks. Historians should evaluate each claim with the appropriate evidence, rather than use gross domestic product as a universal measure of social change.

The contemporary record also illustrates the difficulty of writing history close to the events. The World Bank and other institutions revised statistics, issued forecasts and documented changing economic conditions through November 2026. A prediction of annual growth cannot be treated as a final observation, and a revised poverty line cannot be read as a sudden change in families' daily lives. Census records, household surveys, policy documents, factory investigations, labor reports and oral testimony each reveal different dimensions. Together, they support a more careful account than either celebration of a technology revolution or blanket condemnation of global markets would provide.

The region's challenges were connected. Employment policy could not be separated from schooling, transport or women's access to work. Digital payments depended on financial regulation and reliable electricity. Industrialization required trade routes and skilled workers. Migrant remittances sustained local demand but exposed families to external conditions. Housing and public health determined whether urban workers could enjoy the productivity gains of growing cities. The historical question is not which single reform produced progress, but how multiple institutions combined to distribute opportunity and risk.

Placed within the textbook's longer sequence, the post-1991 era both extended and transformed earlier South Asian development. Colonial commercial networks, independence-era public investment, agrarian change and postcolonial institutions all shaped the choices available to later generations. Liberalization did not begin economic exchange, and digitalization did not begin entrepreneurship. What changed was the scale, speed and organization of connections across markets, households and states. Chapter 107 will examine the social institutions that shaped access to these opportunities, Chapter 108 the cultural and media environments in which they were understood, Chapter 109 the environmental constraints on future development, and Chapter 110 the region's broader place in the world. The economic history of the new South Asia remains a story of substantial achievement and persistent distributional questions, rather than a completed march toward a single model of prosperity.

Chapter Summary

The transformation of South Asia after 1991 rested on older patterns of agriculture, state-led industrialization, trade and population change. Fertility and mortality declined at different rates, leaving some societies with large youthful working-age populations and others confronting aging. These trends affected labor supply, household obligations and demand for public services, but demographic opportunity depended on education, employment and institutions.

Megacities and industrial corridors expanded through migration, natural population growth and the reclassification of settlements. Their economies drew on manufacturing, commerce, services and construction. Housing shortages, unequal infrastructure and long commutes constrained the benefits of urbanization for low-paid workers, while roads, electricity and transport linked cities more closely to rural districts.

Economic liberalization altered incentives for production and foreign investment without ending government involvement in industry. Bangladesh built an unusually influential garment-export sector; Pakistan and Sri Lanka developed distinctive textile and apparel industries; India combined diversified manufacturing with strong professional services. International supply chains offered markets while exposing firms and workers to pressure from buyers and external shocks.

Information technology and outsourcing expanded highly skilled employment in selected metropolitan regions. Mobile communications and later smartphones connected traders, migrants and households, while digital payment systems transformed financial transfers. Such infrastructure lowered some barriers to commerce but raised new concerns over privacy, fraud, market power and exclusion from services.

Migration linked villages, cities and overseas destinations. Internal migrants worked in construction, factories and services, often returning periodically to rural homes. Overseas workers sent remittances that financed consumption, schooling and investment, but recruitment costs, separation and dependence on foreign labor markets produced vulnerabilities.

Schooling and technical training expanded while many educated young people struggled to find suitable work. The World Bank’s November 2026 study estimated that only about 60 percent of the working-age population was employed and that about 88 percent of employed workers worked informally. These regional estimates require careful interpretation and conceal variation among countries and occupations.

Poverty measures, human-development indicators and employment statistics illuminate different aspects of change. Improvements in education, health, sanitation and communication were substantial, but many households remained exposed to illness, inflation, job losses and other shocks. Wealth and opportunity were unevenly distributed among regions and social groups.

The 2020 pandemic, Sri Lanka’s 2022 crisis and continuing macroeconomic difficulties in other countries showed that economic progress did not produce automatic resilience. As of November 2026, official growth forecasts described continuing opportunities alongside unresolved employment, inequality and financing challenges. The historical conclusion is that output, employment and human development are connected but distinct, and the institutions governing their distribution remain central to South Asia’s future.

Key Terms

Definitions are arranged for review rather than repeated as an unannotated list.

Demographic transition
Long-term movement from high birth and death rates toward lower ones, with country-specific timing.
Demographic dividend
Possible economic gain when people of working age become a larger share of the population, conditional on productive employment and social investment.
Dependency ratio
A comparison of populations conventionally considered outside working ages with those inside working ages; it is not a direct measure of actual dependence.
Urbanization
The expansion of populations and activities classified as urban; national definitions and boundaries differ.
Peri-urbanization
The emergence of mixed rural-urban settlements and activities around metropolitan areas.
Informal settlement
A residential area with some insecure tenure or nonstandard planning and service arrangements; conditions vary.
Liberalization
Reduction or alteration of certain state restrictions on trade, investment or enterprise; not a complete withdrawal of public policy.
Import substitution
Policy encouraging domestic production of goods formerly imported, often through protection and public investment.
Industrial policy
Public measures intended to influence which productive activities develop and where.
Export-processing zone
Designated area offering infrastructure or regulatory advantages to firms producing for export.
Global value chain
Production, design, logistics and distribution organized across multiple enterprises and places.
Ready-made garments
Clothing manufactured in standardized form for domestic or foreign buyers, an important Bangladeshi export sector.
Outsourcing
Contracting defined services or production tasks to another firm, sometimes in another country.
Remittances
Money transferred by migrants or other earners to people or accounts in their home or origin country.
Circular migration
Repeated movement between a home location and destination for work or other purposes.
Informal employment
Employment lacking specified formal arrangements or protections under the applicable statistical standard.
Underemployment
Work insufficient in hours, earnings or effective use of skills relative to what the worker seeks or can supply.
Labor-force participation
The share of a defined population that is employed or actively seeking and available for work under a specified survey definition.
Financial inclusion
Usable access to suitable financial services such as payments, saving and credit, without implying financial security.
Digital public infrastructure
Shared digital systems supporting public or private services, such as identity, payments or data exchange.
Unified Payments Interface (UPI)
India’s interoperable account-to-account payments infrastructure, introduced in 2016.
Platform economy
Economic activity coordinated through digital intermediaries that match buyers, sellers and workers.
Purchasing-power parity (PPP)
Method of comparing currency purchasing power using prices of comparable goods and services.
International poverty line
A monetary threshold used for global poverty comparison, periodically revised with PPP benchmarks.
Human development
Expansion of capabilities and conditions of life, including health, education and material welfare.
Social protection
Institutions and programs intended to reduce vulnerability to poverty, illness, unemployment or old age.
Labor productivity
Output per unit of labor input, a measure distinct from wages or employment levels.
Debt distress
Severe difficulty meeting debt obligations without damaging financial stability or essential services.

Chapter Reflection

The transformation described in this chapter matters because it reveals how historical change is lived across households and institutions, not merely recorded in national income tables. A new export factory may change the choices available to a young woman; a telephone can connect a distant family; a road can alter the future of a market town. These developments arise from the decisions and labor of ordinary people as well as the strategies of governments and corporations. Their consequences are not uniform, and the inequalities surrounding them are themselves part of the historical record.

In the long history of South Asia, the period from 1991 to 2026 did not erase older patterns of agricultural dependence, social stratification or regional diversity. Instead, commercial and technological networks changed the relationships among those patterns. A growing city continued to depend on distant farms and migrant labor; a technology firm required infrastructure and educational investments built over decades; a household receiving an overseas wage remained affected by conditions abroad. The interaction of continuity and change makes economic transformation a history of institutions, migration and social power rather than a simple narrative of modernization.

The chapter also demonstrates why historians must practice caution with contemporary evidence. Forecasts are not completed outcomes; a revised poverty line does not immediately alter household life; and a company’s account of its achievements cannot substitute for workers’ experiences. Reliable interpretation requires comparison among statistical records, policy documents and social testimony. The chapters that follow will show that the opportunities created by economic change were filtered through caste, gender, religion, media, environmental pressures and international relationships. Chapter 106 provides the material and demographic foundations for understanding those later discussions.
