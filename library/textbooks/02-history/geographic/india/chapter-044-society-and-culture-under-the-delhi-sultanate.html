Society and Culture under the Delhi Sultanate

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the early fourteenth century, visitors to a hospice at Ghiyaspur, on the outskirts of Delhi, could find a shaykh named Nizamuddin Auliya receiving courtiers, merchants, poets, and the poor alike, while a communal kitchen fed those who came. A few miles away, bazaars sold grain, cloth, and horses, masons worked on mosques and tanks, and officials in Persian dress met village headmen who spoke regional languages. These scenes belong to the same world, and this chapter tries to understand it.

Chapters 40 through 43 followed the political history of the Delhi Sultanate from Aibak and Iltutmish through the Khaljis, the Tughlaqs, the Sayyids, and the Lodis. They explained how a military regime became a durable state, expanded, fragmented, and fell. This chapter changes the angle of view. It asks how the existence of the Sultanate altered social, economic, religious, and cultural life across northern India, and how existing institutions and communities, in return, shaped what the Sultanate became.

The central argument is that the Sultanate did not simply replace an earlier society with a new one. Political change brought new elites, migration networks, religious institutions, and languages into contact with older systems of village organization, jati hierarchy, temple patronage, Sanskrit learning, and regional commerce. The results were uneven: they varied by region, community, occupation, and period.

Two oversimplifications must be avoided. One imagines two separate communities locked in permanent conflict; the other imagines a seamless, harmonious composite culture. The evidence shows conflict, accommodation, indifference, competition, and cooperation, often in the same place at the same time.

The chapter is organized in six divisions: society and community; agriculture, production, commerce, and cities; religion; language and learning; architecture and the arts; and a concluding synthesis. Its sources range from Persian chronicles and the travel narrative of Ibn Battuta to Sufi conversation records, inscriptions, coins, monuments, and regional literature, and it attends throughout to who wrote, who is missing, and what survives.

The chapter prepares the way for the regional states of Chapters 45 to 47, for the devotional history of Chapter 48, and for the Mughal period beginning with Chapter 50. It should not project Mughal institutions or the later separation of Hindi and Urdu backward onto the Sultanate centuries.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Describe the composition of the Sultanate elite and the bases of social standing.
  3. Explain how rural intermediaries, cultivators, and revenue demands shaped village society.
  4. Distinguish among varna, jati, and occupational status, and explain social mobility.
  5. Analyze slavery, dependency, and women's lives without treating them as uniform.
  6. Trace the development of agriculture, crafts, markets, credit, and trade networks.
  7. Compare Chishti, Suhrawardi, and other Sufi traditions and their institutions.
  8. Assess interpretations of conversion, coexistence, and religious boundaries.
  9. Contextualize Persian, Sanskrit, Hindavi, and regional literary traditions.
  10. Interpret architecture, music, and material culture as historical evidence.
  11. Connect the social and cultural history of this chapter to the political narrative of Chapters 40 to 43.

Historical Context

The Delhi Sultanate (c. 1206 to 1526) governed varying portions of northern India and at times parts of the Deccan, drawing on Turkic, Afghan, and Persianate traditions. It inherited a South Asia already shaped by regional kingdoms, temple institutions, agrarian expansion, and merchant networks (Chapters 31 to 37), and by earlier Muslim presence in Sindh and the Indian Ocean ports (Chapter 38) and the Ghaznavid and Ghurid conquests (Chapter 39).

The Sultanate is also part of a wider Islamic and Eurasian world. Mongol expansion pushed scholars and soldiers toward India, and connections with Central Asia, Iran, and the Indian Ocean brought people, goods, and ideas. At the same time, the Sultanate's direct authority was uneven, and regional powers such as Vijayanagara, the Bahmani state, and the sultanates of Bengal and Gujarat developed their own institutions (Chapters 45 to 47).

Readers should keep three cautions in mind: Persian sources dominate the record; the experiences of ordinary people are poorly documented; and later religious, linguistic, and political categories must not be applied automatically to medieval communities.

Section 44.1

Studying Society under the Delhi Sultanate: Sources and Historical Problems

Chapters 40 through 43 explained how the Delhi Sultanate was founded, expanded, governed, fragmented, and finally overthrown at Panipat in 1526. This chapter asks a different question: how did people live, work, worship, learn, and create during those three centuries? The question is harder to answer than it first appears, because the surviving evidence is unevenly distributed. It speaks loudly about sultans, nobles, and saints, and quietly, when at all, about cultivators, weavers, porters, domestic servants, and most women.

The best-known sources are Persian chronicles written by court-connected authors, among them Minhaj-i Siraj (writing c. 1260), Ziya al-Din Barani (c. 1357), Shams-i Siraj Afif (late fourteenth century), and Isami, whose verse history of 1350 was written in the Deccan. These works are invaluable for political events and for the ideals of the educated Muslim elite, but their authors wrote for patrons, drew on Persianate models of kingship, and rarely described village life directly. The Arabic travel narrative of Ibn Battuta, who was in South Asia from about 1333 to 1347 and served for a time as a judge in Delhi, offers an outsider's observations on cities, food, slavery, and customs, though it was dictated years afterward and mixes observation, hearsay, and stylization. Sufi conversation records (malfuzat) such as the Fawa'id al-Fu'ad preserve the social world of devotional communities. Inscriptions in Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit, and regional languages record mosques, wells, endowments, and local grants; coins show monetary policy and royal self-presentation; and standing monuments and excavated remains supply material evidence.

Historians therefore read these sources against one another. They also ask whose voices are missing. A claim in a chronicle about the "obedience" of peasants, or a juridical text about women's duties, tells us what an author thought should be true. Where the evidence is thin, this chapter says so, and it distinguishes documented practice from normative prescription throughout.

Source Criticism Workshop

What Do Chronicles Reveal, and What Do They Conceal?

Consider four kinds of evidence for a single subject, such as the life of a Delhi neighborhood: a Persian chronicle, Ibn Battuta's account, a Sufi conversation record, and an inscription recording the construction of a well or stepwell. Each answers different questions.

Persian chronicle: strong on rulers, campaigns, and elite ideals; written for a literate Persian-reading audience; often silent on ordinary labor. Travel narrative: valuable for what a newcomer found remarkable, but shaped by the traveler's expectations and by later composition. Malfuzat: record talk in a devotional community, compiled by disciples with devotional aims. Inscriptions: contemporary and datable, but brief and shaped by the patrons who commissioned them.

Source analysis questions:

  1. Which of the four source types is most likely to mention a weaver or a water-carrier, and why?
  2. How might a disciple's purpose in compiling a conversation record shape what it preserves?
  3. Why is a dated inscription sometimes more reliable than a chronicle for establishing when something happened, and what can it not tell us?
Section 44.2

The Ruling Elite, Military Households, and Social Hierarchy

The Sultanate's governing class was not a single ethnic or religious bloc. In the thirteenth century it was dominated by Turkic military commanders, many of them freed slaves or the descendants of slaves, together with Persian-speaking scribes, jurists, and scholars who had migrated to Delhi as Mongol expansion disrupted Iran and Central Asia. The Khaljis brought Turko-Afghan military families; the Tughlaqs relied on frontier commanders; and the Lodis, as Chapter 43 showed, drew on Afghan tribal networks. Alongside these immigrant groups stood Indian Muslims, converts and their descendants, and a significant number of Hindu officials, accountants, soldiers, and local chiefs who participated in governance at different levels.

Social standing rested on several overlapping bases: military rank and titles; office and access to the sultan; landed or revenue assignments; wealth; claimed descent; learning; and patronage ties. Persian authors often wrote in a vocabulary of hierarchy that separated "well-born" (ashraf) from "low-born" (ajlaf). Barani, for example, complained that Muhammad bin Tughlaq elevated men from humble trades to high office. Historians read such passages cautiously: they reveal an educated aristocrat's anxieties and a real, if limited, degree of upward mobility through royal favor, rather than a systematic social policy. Elite households were large, combining kin, clients, retainers, and enslaved dependents, and they depended on revenue assignments whose insecurity meant that status could be won and lost within a generation.

The composition of the elite changed over time. In the thirteenth century, the Turkic slave-commanders who surrounded Iltutmish and Balban (Chapter 40) dominated military and political life, and they were wary of newcomers. When Jalal al-Din Khalji took the throne in 1290, Barani reports that many established nobles saw the Khaljis, whose origins lay in the Khalj region of Afghanistan, as outsiders. Under the Tughlaqs, frontier commanders, Persian administrators, and Indian-born officers gained prominence, and Sultan Firuz Shah's minister Khan-i Jahan Maqbul, whom sources describe as a convert of Telugu origin from the Warangal region, shows how a captive or newcomer could reach the highest civil office. The Sayyids claimed descent from the Prophet, and the Lodis relied on Afghan tribal followings (Chapter 43), whose ideas of kinship and shared rule differed from earlier Turkic models. The sources on origin and lineage are often later or partisan, so claims about ancestry need careful testing.

Persian-speaking scribes, jurists, and bureaucrats formed a separate stratum within the elite. Wazirs and revenue officials, such as the ministers who served Iltutmish and later sultans, handled correspondence, accounts, and revenue assessment, while judges, religious officers, and scholars staffed the legal and religious establishment. Hindu clerks, accountants, and revenue specialists, from communities such as the Kayasthas and Khatris, worked in administration in many regions, although the evidence for individuals is scattered and often indirect. Hindu soldiers served in Sultanate armies and in the forces of subordinate chiefs, and local rulers who acknowledged Delhi's overlordship held their own positions of rank. The elite as a whole was therefore multilingual and multiconfessional in practice, even though the vocabulary of official culture was Persian and Islamic.

Status was displayed and maintained through ceremony and material signs. Titles such as malik, khan, and amir marked ranks; robes of honor (khilat), insignia, seating at court, and grants of revenue assignments communicated favor. Balban's court ceremonial, including prostration and kissing the ruler's feet, as Persian sources describe it, aimed to emphasize the distance between ruler and nobles. Yet no rank was secure. Revenue assignments (iqta) were not hereditary rights, officeholders could be transferred or dismissed, and rulers such as Alauddin Khalji confiscated wealth and curtailed noble households. Nobles therefore invested in marriage alliances, patronage, and clients, and they supported large households whose size and dependents signaled standing as well as providing security.

Modern historians have long examined this ruling class, from K. M. Ashraf's classic study of the lives of the people of Hindustan to later work by Irfan Habib and others on revenue and the military. They differ over how far the elite functioned as a cohesive class and how much its composition reflected royal strategy rather than social change. This chapter treats the elite as a shifting, competitive group whose members shared a political environment but not a single origin or interest, and it asks students to compare the experience of an elite courtier with those of the cultivators, artisans, and others whose labor supported the elite.

Section 44.3

Village Society: Cultivators, Landholders, and Local Intermediaries

Most people under Sultanate rule lived in villages, and the Sultanate's fiscal system reached them through intermediaries who were already powerful before the Delhi sultans arrived. Persian sources mention khuts, muqaddams, and chaudhuris, together with rais, ranas, and other local chiefs. These figures collected revenue, mobilized labor, held hereditary rights or privileges, and often controlled land, grain, and armed followers. The Sultanate, which could not administer every village directly, relied on negotiation, coercion, and periodic military expeditions to secure their cooperation.

Below the intermediaries were cultivators of varying status, from substantial landholders to smallholders, tenants, and landless laborers, many of whom were bound by debt or by caste-based occupation to specific local arrangements. Surviving evidence rarely allows precise counts, and practice differed among the Punjab, the Ganga-Yamuna Doab, Bengal, Gujarat, and Malwa. Historians such as Irfan Habib have stressed the pressure that revenue demands placed on peasants, while others have emphasized negotiation and local power. This chapter treats the village as a site where older institutions and new demands met, not as a passive object of royal policy.

Village life was organized by more than revenue. Households of cultivators lived alongside potters, carpenters, barbers, leatherworkers, and other service families, who were commonly paid with shares of grain or other customary dues; the best documentation for such arrangements comes from later periods, so for the Sultanate centuries historians use it as a plausible pattern rather than a proven rule. Disputes within communities were often handled by caste or village councils, and religious specialists, whether Brahmins, Muslim prayer leaders, or Sufi-linked holy men, performed rites and mediated local quarrels. Villages were therefore social units with their own authorities, and the Sultanate's revenue officers had to work through them.

Barani describes the khuts and muqaddams as holders of comfortable estates who rode good horses, wore fine cloth, and were reluctant to pay what they owed, and he presents Alauddin Khalji's measures as a deliberate effort to humble them. The account is partisan, but it hints at rural power that could resist the state. Some chiefs fortified their villages or retreated into forests and hills, as the Mewatis near Delhi did (Chapter 40), and peasants could flee, hide grain, or join rebellions. The Sultanate's control of the countryside thus depended on repeated negotiation and force, and its limits are visible in the recurrence of expeditions against local strongholds.

Historical Vocabulary

Khut, Muqaddam, and Chaudhuri

These Persian-language administrative terms describe rural intermediaries, though their exact meaning varied by region and period. Khut and muqaddam usually denote local landholders or village headmen with hereditary standing; chaudhuri denotes a headman of a larger unit. Barani's accounts show that such figures often possessed privileges and wealth beyond those of ordinary cultivators.

Why it matters: the terms remind students that Sultanate revenue extraction passed through existing rural elites. Equating them with later Mughal zamindars would project a later system backward.

Section 44.4

Caste, Jati, Occupational Communities, and Social Mobility

Brahmanical texts classified society into four varnas, but the lived world of medieval South Asia was organized chiefly through jatis: numerous endogamous communities linked to occupation, region, kinship, and ritual standing. These categories overlapped but were not identical, and neither was identical to the later colonial and modern concept of "caste" (see Chapters 73 and 107). Under the Sultanate, jatis continued to organize artisans, merchants, farmers, pastoralists, service communities, and communities regarded as ritually impure, who were often excluded from temples and water sources and assigned burdens such as sanitation and leatherwork.

Change occurred inside these structures. Political service created new routes to status: Hindu clerks and accountants rose in the administrative world, military recruits could earn rank, and merchants and bankers accumulated wealth. Muslim communities were not outside hierarchy either: ideals of equality before God coexisted with distinctions of descent, occupation, and region, and converts frequently carried older occupational identities into new communities. Normative texts present rigid orders; evidence of practice shows negotiation, regional variation, and gradual change.

Texts and inscriptions show how complex the social order was. A Maithili prose work of the early fourteenth century, the Varnaratnakara of Jyotirishvara Thakkura, offers long conventional lists of occupations, kinds of people, and urban scenes, and it is useful for suggesting how contemporaries imagined a varied society, even though its literary conventions limit its value as a census. Merchants such as Jain Oswal and other trading families, scribal groups such as the Kayasthas and Khatris, Brahmin scholars and ritual specialists, warrior lineages claiming Kshatriya status, and artisan and service communities all appear in different sources. Political change allowed some local chiefs to advance claims to higher ritual rank, a process that historians relate to the growth of Rajput identities discussed in Chapter 35.

Muslim society was also stratified. Immigrant families claiming descent from the Prophet or from Arab, Persian, or Turkic origins enjoyed prestige, while communities of converts and artisans, such as weavers, butchers, and cotton carders, formed groups that often married within their own occupations. The Persian pair ashraf and ajlaf expressed an ideal of high and low birth, though the fully developed system of Muslim status groups in northern India took shape more clearly in later centuries. Scholars therefore describe the Sultanate period as one in which several hierarchical systems overlapped, sometimes reinforcing one another and sometimes competing, and in which political service, wealth, and religious learning could change a family's position within a lifetime.

Historical Vocabulary

Varna, Jati, and Social Status

Varna is a fourfold ideal classification (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) found in Brahmanical texts. Jati refers to the many local endogamous groups through which social life was actually organized. Occupation, ritual rank, wealth, and political power did not always align.

Students should avoid assuming that every occupation was permanently hereditary or that every region applied the same hierarchy.

Section 44.5

Migration, Settlement, and the Formation of New Communities

The Sultanate period saw continuous movement of people. Soldiers, administrators, jurists, poets, Sufis, merchants, and craftsmen came from Central Asia, Iran, and Afghanistan; the Mongol invasions of the early thirteenth century pushed many learned and skilled families toward Delhi, which Persian authors praised as a refuge. Movement also occurred within South Asia: troops, officials, and artisans were relocated, and Muhammad bin Tughlaq's forced transfer of Delhi's elite to Daulatabad (Chapter 42) was only the most dramatic example. Timur's invasion of 1398 to 1399 disrupted the capital and, according to later sources, carried skilled artisans away.

Settlement produced mixed urban communities. Delhi, Multan, Lahore, Jaunpur, Gulbarga, and other towns contained immigrants and local residents, speakers of Persian, Turkic languages, Sanskrit-derived vernaculars, and others. Intermarriage, shared markets, and shared patronage gradually produced new identities, though not a uniform fusion: immigrant groups often preserved distinctions of origin, and ties of descent and language remained socially significant. Students should therefore see identity as a process rather than a fixed label.

Migration followed political shocks. Scholars and officials from Ghur, Khwarazm, and Central Asia arrived in the 1220s as Mongol armies destroyed major cities, and Minhaj-i Siraj, who came from the Ghurid world, is himself an example of a learned migrant who found a career at Delhi. Mongols also entered the Sultanate in other ways: some settled and converted to Islam and were described in Persian chronicles as 'new Muslims.' Barani attributes to Alauddin Khalji a mass killing of Mongol converts following an alleged conspiracy, generally dated to about 1311 (Chapter 41); the chronicler's reported numbers are difficult to verify. This later massacre should not be confused with the revolt of Mongol soldiers during the Gujarat campaign of 1299. Migrant communities could be both valued and vulnerable.

Settlement created new kinds of places. Garrison posts (thanas) and market towns (qasbas) gave soldiers, officials, and traders footholds in the countryside, while Sufi lodges drew followers from surrounding villages. Forced and voluntary movement within the Sultanate also changed regional populations: the transfer of people to Daulatabad in the 1320s, though many later returned, left a Muslim population in the Deccan that contributed to the later Bahmani state (Chapter 46). Because most evidence comes from elite sources, ordinary migrants, whether artisans, soldiers, or laborers, appear only in passing, and historians infer their presence from place names, shrines, and the later makeup of communities.

Section 44.6

Slavery, Dependency, Labor, and Manumission

Slavery was a prominent institution under the Sultanate and took several forms. Captives of war, purchased slaves, and the children of enslaved women were employed as domestic servants, concubines, artisans, soldiers, administrators, and royal attendants. The best-known case is the elite military slave: Iltutmish and Balban had themselves been slaves before rising to the sultanate (Chapter 40). These powerful figures should not be taken as typical. The great majority of enslaved people were laborers, domestic workers, and women and children whose lives were governed by owners and rarely recorded.

Islamic law regulated slavery and treated manumission as a pious act, while Brahmanical and customary traditions had their own categories of bondage and servitude, so legal and customary status varied. Chroniclers report very large royal slave establishments under Firuz Shah Tughluq, with figures that should be treated cautiously. Ibn Battuta describes female slaves in elite households and the circulation of slaves as gifts. Slavery cannot be treated as one uniform condition: an elite slave-commander, an artisan slave in a royal workshop, and a domestic slave child faced very different circumstances. Debt bondage and dependent agricultural labor also shaded into servitude in ways that sources only partly reveal.

People entered slavery in several ways. Warfare was the most visible: campaigns in the Doab, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the Deccan produced captives, and Persian chronicles mention large numbers taken in raids and sieges. Others were bought in markets, received as tribute or gifts, born to enslaved mothers, or sold in times of famine and debt. Barani's account of Alauddin Khalji's market regulations lists fixed prices for enslaved boys and girls alongside horses and cloth, which shows that slave sales were routine enough to be regulated, although the figures reflect a controlled-price setting and should not be generalized. Ibn Battuta, for his part, describes buying enslaved people and receiving them as gifts, and he treats such transactions as ordinary features of elite life.

Elite slaves are better documented than most. The early sultans' bandagan (household slaves) rose through military service, and later examples include Malik Kafur, the commander of Alauddin's Deccan campaigns, who according to the sources had been a captive, and Khusrau Khan, a favorite of Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah who briefly seized the throne in 1320 (Chapter 41). Barani and other elite writers often resented such men as upstarts, and their hostility colors the record. Firuz Shah Tughluq is said by Afif to have collected an enormous number of slaves and organized their training and maintenance through a dedicated department; the figures Afif gives should be handled with caution, but they point to the use of enslaved people as soldiers, guards, attendants, and craftsmen at court.

For most enslaved people the evidence is thin and indirect. Women served as domestic workers and concubines, and enslaved eunuchs held positions in royal and noble households. Children raised in a household might be educated for service, and some artisan slaves worked in royal workshops (karkhanas), where skill could improve a person's treatment without ending their dependence. Islamic jurists treated the enslavement of non-Muslim captives as lawful in certain circumstances and encouraged freeing slaves as an act of piety; a woman who bore her owner's child gained protections, and the child was generally regarded as free. In practice, such provisions depended on owners' choices and on enforcement that we can seldom observe. Brahmanical legal texts recognized categories of bondage as well, and local custom governed labor obligations in the countryside.

Historians therefore avoid two errors. One presents Sultanate slavery as nothing but the rise of military slave-commanders; the other treats it as a uniform system of plantation-style bondage. The evidence shows a range of coerced and dependent conditions, from the exercise of power by a few enslaved nobles to the vulnerability of captives and servants who left almost no testimony. Since such sources mostly record the owners' point of view, the experiences of the enslaved must be inferred carefully from laws, prices, administrative practice, and the occasional offhand remark.

Section 44.7

Women, Family, Marriage, Property, and Gender Relations

Evidence about women is uneven. Royal women such as Raziyya, queen mothers, and noble ladies appear in chronicles and inscriptions, and some exercised political or patronage influence. Most women, however, worked in the household and in agricultural and craft production: processing grain, spinning, tending animals, hauling water, and contributing to artisan and trading households. Their labor is largely invisible in court-centered texts but central to the economy.

Law and custom framed marriage, inheritance, and property differently across communities. Islamic legal principles gave women defined shares of inheritance and rights over their dower, while Brahmanical legal traditions recognized forms of women's property (stridhana) and prescribed wifely duties; regional custom modified both. Evidence for veiling and seclusion (purdah) points mainly to elite households, and cannot be generalized to rural women. Ibn Battuta reports having observed a widow's sati, a practice recorded for particular regions and groups rather than a universal rule. Sources also mention women as devotees at shrines, patrons of religious foundations, and members of Sufi households. A fully historical account must avoid inferring all women's lives from prescriptive texts or from queens.

Royal and noble women nevertheless illustrate how gender and power interacted. Raziyya, who ruled in Delhi from 1236 to 1240 (Chapter 40), remains the only woman to have held the Sultanate throne in her own name, and the chroniclers' mixed reactions show how contested the idea was. Queen mothers and senior wives could shape succession, household finances, and patronage; Persian and Arabic sources, including Ibn Battuta, describe Makhduma-i Jahan, the mother of Muhammad bin Tughlaq, as a person of wealth and influence, and Alauddin Khalji's household included queens whose names survive in the literature. Elite women endowed mosques, tombs, and charities, and women of rank were often educated in religion and household management. These women belonged to a thin social layer, and their prominence cannot stand for women generally.

Marriage and property show the interplay of legal systems. For Muslims, marriage was a contract in which the husband owed a bridal gift (mahr) to the wife, and inheritance rules gave daughters, wives, and mothers fixed shares, usually smaller than those of male heirs. For Hindus and Jains, regional legal commentaries such as the Mitakshara and the Dayabhaga treated a woman's own property (stridhana), widows' claims, and the authority of male heads of household in different ways, and local custom often decided disputes. Such rules shaped what women could hold and bequeath, but evidence of how often women actually claimed their entitlements is scarce, and the courts that heard such claims left few records for this period.

Outside the elite, women's work was fundamental. Rural women hauled water, ground grain on hand mills, tended livestock, helped with planting and harvest, and prepared food. In towns, women worked as domestic servants, cotton spinners, entertainers, vendors, and dependents of artisan families; historians who date the spread of the spinning wheel to this era have drawn attention to the likely effect on household textile work, although the evidence remains debated. Enslaved women (§44.6) were an important part of the female workforce. Religious life also drew women in: they visited shrines, sought healing and blessings, and appear in Sufi tradition as devoted mothers and disciples, as in the accounts of Nizamuddin Auliya's mother, though these are partly hagiographic. Jain traditions had communities of nuns, and women patrons supported Jain and Hindu temples.

Normative texts and travelers' reports must be read critically. Legal and ethical writings describe duties and ideals, travelers record what struck them, and chroniclers rarely mention women who were not queens or scandals. Ibn Battuta's description of a sati he witnessed shows that the practice existed in some communities, but it does not show how common it was or how voluntary individual cases were. Historians therefore approach women's history by combining legal sources, inscriptions of women's endowments, Sufi and devotional literature, and what can be inferred from labor patterns, while saying plainly where the evidence stops.

Division II — Agriculture, Production, Commerce, and Urban Life

The material foundations of society; economic history is more than royal taxation.

Section 44.8

Agriculture, Irrigation, and the Material Foundations of Society

Agriculture supported the Sultanate and its cities. In the north, the two harvest seasons described by Ibn Battuta and by revenue practice, autumn (kharif) and spring (rabi), produced grains such as wheat, barley, rice, millets, and pulses, alongside sugarcane, cotton, oilseeds, indigo, and vegetables. Rainfall, river floods, and wells determined what could be grown. Irrigation by wells and tanks was widespread; the rope-and-bucket lift remained common, and historians such as Irfan Habib have argued that the geared Persian wheel spread across northern India during the Sultanate period, though the chronology and the evidence remain debated.

The Sultanate also shaped the landscape. Rulers such as Firuz Shah Tughluq built canals, notably from the Yamuna toward Hisar, to extend cultivation and supply new towns, and he sponsored orchards around Delhi. Agrarian expansion could draw cultivators into new settlements, but warfare, revenue pressure, and weather could reverse it. Ibn Battuta reported serious famine in the mid-1330s, and Barani linked agrarian distress to policy failure and rebellion. The material foundation of society, in other words, was both productive and fragile, dependent on rainfall, rivers, labor, and political stability.

Ibn Battuta described the two main seasons of cultivation and listed the crops of each. Autumn crops included rice, sorghum, pulses, and sesame, and spring crops included wheat, barley, chickpeas, and lentils, with sugarcane, cotton, and indigo grown for sale and processing. The mix varied: rice and fish defined the economy of the eastern deltas, millets and wheat the drier west and northwest, and cotton and oilseeds supplied the textile and oil trades of towns. Orchards and gardens produced fruit, and herding provided draft animals, milk, and transport. Farmers combined crops and animals according to soil, water, and market access, a pattern that differed from village to village.

State policy sometimes tried to enlarge the cultivated area. Barani reports that Muhammad bin Tughlaq created an agricultural department in the 1330s to extend cultivation by lending resources and supervising the planting of better crops, a scheme that Barani considers a failure because of poor administration and the harsh conditions of the period. Firuz Shah Tughluq's canals took water from the Yamuna and other rivers toward Hisar, and such works are described by Afif as increasing cultivation and revenue. Droughts and famines, which Ibn Battuta and others mention, show how closely agriculture depended on rainfall, and the movement of people in search of food could empty villages as easily as the new canals filled others.

Section 44.9

Revenue Extraction, Rural Inequality, and the Agrarian Economy

Chapters 41 and 42 examined the revenue reforms of Alauddin Khalji and Muhammad bin Tughlaq; this section examines their social consequences. Revenue was assessed on cultivated land and collected in cash and in kind, often through local intermediaries. Because the Sultanate needed money for armies and for the support of its elite, revenue demands could be heavy, and officials and assignment-holders (iqta holders) sometimes pressed collection hard. The pressure fell unequally: powerful local landholders could resist, evade, or shift burdens, while ordinary cultivators had fewer options.

Cash payment tied rural households to markets. A cultivator who had to pay in coin sold grain or cash crops to traders, often at disadvantageous prices, and borrowed from moneylenders when harvests failed. Grain merchants and carriers moved supplies to cities. These patterns increased commercial exchange but also deepened rural indebtedness and inequality. Interpretations differ: some scholars emphasize the extractive power of the state, others the room for local bargaining and the persistence of local custom. The evidence supports both a picture of real pressure and one of regional variation.

Barani reports that Alauddin Khalji's officials measured land and demanded a very high share of the produce in the Doab, a figure he gives as half. How fully these demands were collected, and for how long, is uncertain, and scholars such as Irfan Habib have used the evidence to argue that the burden on cultivators was heavy, while others point out that local intermediaries often absorbed or deflected the pressure. What the sources do show is that the state tried to reduce the share kept by village headmen and to hold them to the same liability as other cultivators, an approach Barani presents as a sharp break with earlier practice.

Peasants had few options other than payment, evasion, or flight. Revenue officials were also notorious for demanding extra payments, and rulers sometimes tried to restrain them. A short text attributed to Firuz Shah Tughluq, the Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi, lists taxes and cesses that he says he abolished as unlawful, and chroniclers credit him with lightening some burdens, although the claims serve to present him as a pious ruler and cannot be taken at face value. Rebellions in the Doab during Muhammad bin Tughlaq's reign, as Barani describes them, show how revenue pressure, famine, and political weakness could combine. The picture is of a revenue system whose harshness varied with the ruler and the season and whose operation depended on bargaining at the village level.

Section 44.10

Artisans, Workshops, Textiles, and Specialized Production

Craft production flourished in towns and in many villages. Weavers produced cotton cloth of many grades; dyers worked with indigo and other pigments; metalworkers made tools, weapons, and vessels; leatherworkers, potters, masons, carpenters, and stonecutters served both rural and urban demand. Royal workshops (karkhanas) manufactured goods for the court and the army, while private craftsmen sold in markets or worked on commission. Construction projects, from mosques to fortifications, depended on large numbers of masons, carvers, and laborers.

New techniques and materials appear in the evidence. Paper, which began to spread more widely in this period, supported record keeping and manuscript culture. Historians such as Irfan Habib have argued that the spinning wheel (charkha) became common in India during or around the Sultanate period, a suggestion that remains debated because of limited early evidence. Artisans were typically organized by jati or occupation, and their skills were transmitted within families. Many were low in the ritual hierarchy despite their economic importance. Patterns of demand, including elite consumption, military needs, and urban growth, influenced production, and the movement of artisans from place to place could spread techniques.

The royal workshops (karkhanas) were large organizations. Afif says Firuz Shah Tughluq maintained dozens of them, producing goods for the court, army, and household, and enslaved and salaried craftsmen worked alongside hired artisans (see §44.6). Textiles were especially important: Gujarat, Bengal, the Deccan, and the Delhi region all produced cloth, and Ibn Battuta remarks on the quality and cheapness of cotton goods in Bengal. Dyers, printers, and embroiderers supplied both everyday and luxury markets, and robes of honor distributed by the court created steady demand for fine fabrics.

Artisans were integrated into social hierarchies. Weavers, potters, smiths, leatherworkers, and masons formed communities defined by occupation, and some of these groups were ranked low in ritual terms despite their economic importance. In northern India many weaver communities became Muslim over time, and later tradition associates the poet Kabir with a weaver community, although the details of his life are uncertain. Construction relied on skilled masons, who sometimes moved with patrons from region to region; Timur is said to have taken masons from Delhi after his 1398 to 1399 invasion, though the details come from later narratives. Little documentary evidence survives about wages, apprenticeship, or conditions of work, so historians reconstruct these from chronicles, monuments, and later parallels.

Section 44.11

Markets, Money, Credit, and Merchant Communities

Markets ranged from village periodic fairs to the great bazaars of Delhi. The Sultanate issued silver tankas and smaller copper or billon jitals, standardizing coinage over a wide area and encouraging monetized exchange. Money changers, bankers, and moneylenders provided currency conversion and credit, and traveling merchants carried goods, bills, and information. Grain carriers (banjaras) linked agricultural regions with cities and armies.

Merchant communities were diverse. Some were Hindu or Jain trading castes, including long-established Gujarati and Marwari merchant networks; others were Muslim traders from Central Asia and the Indian Ocean world; Multanis were notable as long-distance traders and financiers. Wealthy merchants could be influential patrons, but small traders, peddlers, and market laborers lived with far more insecurity. State regulation, tolls, and the demands of military supply all affected commerce. Credit was both an engine of exchange and a source of dependency, particularly for cultivators and small producers.

Delhi's markets had specialized zones, and Barani's account of Alauddin's market regulations (Chapter 41) shows the range of goods sold: grain, cloth, horses, slaves, and everyday items. The sultan's price controls attracted attention because they were unusual, and they imply a normal market in which prices were unregulated and subject to hoarding and shortages. Traveling traders, grain carriers, and local shopkeepers made up a graded commercial world in which the wealthy dealt in horses, textiles, and credit and the poor sold vegetables or small wares.

Credit connected producers and traders. Moneylenders advanced funds to cultivators, artisans, and merchants, and money changers assessed coin quality and exchanged currencies. Multani financiers appear in sources as influential lenders, and Jain and other mercantile communities financed trade and religious endowments. Long-distance transfers by written orders, the later hundi, are better documented for later centuries, and the evidence for the Sultanate period is limited. Tolls and cesses affected costs, and rulers sometimes abolished or modified them. Merchants valued stable coinage and secure roads, which tied their interests to political order, though they also traded across political boundaries and could shift their business if conditions changed.

Section 44.12

Inland Trade, Maritime Connections, and Commercial Networks

Delhi was connected by overland routes to Multan and Lahore in the northwest, to the Doab and Bengal in the east, to Gujarat and the Arabian Sea coast in the west, and, beyond the frontier, to Afghanistan and Central Asia. Horses were among the most important imports, vital to cavalry warfare, and arrived through northwestern routes. Textiles, indigo, sugar, grain, and other goods moved in many directions, and ports in Gujarat, Sindh, Bengal, and the south connected Sultanate markets to the Indian Ocean. Regional maritime histories belong to later chapters (see Chapters 47 and 49); here the point is how such networks affected society in the Sultanate heartland.

These networks brought revenue, luxury goods, and new settlers, and they tied the court's military needs to commercial supply. They also exposed the Sultanate to external shocks: Mongol disruption, Timur's invasion, and political fragmentation after the Tughlaqs reduced the security of long-distance routes. The Sultanate's power rested partly on its ability to control roads, markets, and key towns, but trade also continued through regional centers independent of Delhi.

Horses make the strongest case for the political importance of trade. Sultanate cavalry depended on horses brought from Central Asia and Iran through Afghan and Punjab routes, and Simon Digby's study of the military supply of horses and elephants shows how much the state relied on dealers, tolls, and frontier markets to maintain its forces. War elephants came from eastern and southern forests. This dependence helps to explain the Sultanate's interest in the northwest and in routes that Mongol campaigns or local rivals could close.

Goods traveled in many directions. Textiles, sugar, indigo, and grain moved from the Indian plains to markets in Central Asia and the Indian Ocean, while imports included horses, dried fruit, metalwork, and luxury goods, and Gujarat's ports, particularly Cambay, and Bengal's river ports linked the interior to maritime commerce. The records, however, rarely give volumes, prices, or merchant names, and many assumptions about trade rely on travelers' descriptions and later evidence. Roads and rest houses encouraged movement, but invasions, famine, and the dispersal of political authority could interrupt it, and traders adapted by shifting routes and partners.

Section 44.13

Cities, Neighborhoods, Food, Dress, and Everyday Urban Life

Delhi changed over time. Successive rulers founded or expanded distinct urban centers, from the early fortified area near the Qutb complex to Siri under Alauddin, Tughluqabad, Jahanpanah, and Firuzabad, so that Delhi was a cluster of settlements rather than a single compact city. Ibn Battuta, who knew it well, described it as a vast and populous city, though his enthusiasm and imprecision mean we cannot derive exact population figures. Cities contained palaces, mosques, markets, Sufi hospices, and neighborhoods organized by occupation, language, and community; courtyards and lanes, wells and tanks, and the markets for grain, cloth, and metalwork defined daily life.

Food reflected regional agriculture and wider exchange: wheat, rice, pulses, meat, milk products, sugar, spices, and betel (pan), which Ibn Battuta described in detail and which became an important feature of hospitality. Dress varied with rank, region, and religion; courtly clothing reflected Persianate and Turkic fashions, while ordinary dress remained simpler and often cotton. Social contrasts were sharp. Elite households enjoyed gardens, servants, and imported goods; artisans, servants, and the urban poor lived in crowded and often precarious circumstances. Entertainment included poetry gatherings, music, wrestling, festivals, and shrine visits, which people of different communities might attend.

Ibn Battuta described Delhi as consisting of several adjoining towns, and the Delhi region does include the older fort area near Mehrauli, Alauddin's Siri, Tughluqabad, and Muhammad bin Tughlaq's Jahanpanah, with Firuzabad added later. Each center had its own function and population, and the sultan's changing preferences affected where people lived and worked. Dwellings ranged from stone and brick mansions to mud-brick and thatched houses, and courtyards, lanes, and shared wells organized neighborhoods. Surviving monuments mostly represent elite and religious buildings, so the housing of ordinary people is poorly preserved.

Urban life centered on markets, mosques, shrines, and festivals. Court sources describe celebrations such as Nauroz and Islamic holidays, and crowds gathered at the death anniversaries of saints and at royal processions, while evidence for popular festivals in Delhi is thin. City residents depended on rural supply of grain and fuel and on water from wells and reservoirs, so floods, drought, and war affected them directly. The urban poor included day laborers, servants, porters, and entertainers, who appear in sources mainly when famine or violence made them noticeable.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village, a Market, and an Urban Neighborhood

The village: days shaped by agricultural seasons, wells and tanks, grain storage, and negotiations with intermediaries; women's labor in processing food and spinning, and men's in plowing and harvest. The market: the meeting place of cultivators selling grain, weavers selling cloth, money changers weighing coin, and carriers arriving with goods; prices and credit tied town and countryside together. The urban neighborhood: mixed households of artisans, clerks, servants, and traders around a mosque, temple, or shrine, where religious and social life overlapped.

Each of these scenes is reconstructed from fragmentary evidence. The reconstruction is plausible, but it should not be mistaken for a detailed documentary record of any particular person.

Division III — Religious Communities, Sufism, Bhakti, and Religious Change

Institutions, devotion, coexistence, and the long processes through which communities formed.

Section 44.14

Islamic Institutions: Mosques, Ulama, Law, and Religious Education

Muslim religious life was organized around mosques, congregational prayer, schools, charitable endowments (waqf), and the authority of scholars (ulama) and judges (qadis). The Sultanate generally followed the Hanafi legal tradition, and royal officials such as the chief judge and the officer responsible for religious endowments and charity linked religious institutions to the state. Madrasas, including those associated with Delhi rulers such as Iltutmish and Firuz Shah Tughluq, trained scholars and jurists. Ulama advised, legitimated, and sometimes criticized rulers.

The relationship between religion and power was complex. Sultans claimed to uphold Islam and to protect the community, while ruling a largely non-Muslim population and relying on practical accommodation. Barani and other writers called for rulers to implement religious law strictly, but actual policy depended on circumstances. Muslim society was also diverse: Sunni traditions dominated official culture, Shia and Ismaili communities existed, and ordinary Muslims often practiced in ways shaped by local customs and shrine devotion. The ulama and the Sufis represented different, sometimes competing, sources of religious authority.

Religious offices linked state and community. The Sultanate had a chief judge, a senior officer responsible for charity and endowments, and local qadis in towns, and a market inspector (muhtasib) supervised weights, prices, and public morality. The Friday sermon (khutba) was delivered in the ruler's name, which made it a public sign of sovereignty, and a large congregational mosque in a town marked its inclusion in the Muslim polity. The legal environment was mostly Hanafi in the north, though Muslim communities along the western coast followed the Shafi'i school, a difference reflecting separate migration histories.

Relations between rulers and scholars were negotiated. Barani presents a conversation between Alauddin Khalji and Qadi Mughis al-Din of Bayana about the treatment of non-Muslim subjects, fiscal policy, punishment, and the relationship between religious law and royal authority. Historians question whether Barani's reported dialogue preserves an actual exchange or reconstructs his own political argument; it cannot be treated as a verbatim transcript. In Barani's telling, the sultan defended decisions made for perceived political necessity even when they did not conform to the qadi's legal advice. The episode illustrates contemporary debates about the relationship of jurists to royal government without proving precisely what the two men said. Sufis and ulama also differed: Sufi tradition recounts that jurists challenged Nizamuddin Auliya's defense of sama, and the dispute is recorded as a lively exchange that was never resolved by a single authority. Muslim religious life, in short, had debate and competing leadership within it.

Section 44.15

The Chishti Sufis: Spiritual Networks and Community Life

The Chishti order became central to the religious life of northern India. Mu'in al-Din Chishti (d. c. 1236) is traditionally associated with Ajmer; Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. c. 1235) worked in Delhi; Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar, known as Baba Farid (d. 1265), established a center at Ajodhan in the Punjab; Nizamuddin Auliya (d. 1325) led the Delhi community from Ghiyaspur, and his disciple Nasir al-Din Chiragh-i Dihli (d. 1356) continued it. Dates and biographical details for the earlier figures rest partly on later hagiography and should be treated with caution.

The khanqah (hospice) was both a spiritual and a social institution. A shaykh trained disciples (murids), appointed deputies (khalifas) who carried the tradition to other towns, and offered hospitality, food, and counsel to visitors from all ranks. Charitable giving, fasting, prayer, meditation, and the devotional practice of sama structured community life. Chishti shaykhs typically kept a deliberate distance from the court, avoiding royal grants and presenting themselves as dependent on God rather than on the sultan, though rulers and nobles sought their blessings and patronage was never absent. The network of khalifas made the order a form of social organization that linked towns across northern India.

Nizamuddin Auliya, who was born in Badaun and later became a disciple of Baba Farid, settled in the Delhi area and made his hospice at Ghiyaspur a center for the whole city. His successors carried the Chishti tradition across the north: Burhan al-Din Gharib, one of his deputies, went to the Deccan, and the tradition continued there into the fifteenth century (Chapter 46). The Chishti attitude toward state service is often described as one of reluctance; Chishti shaykhs generally avoided formal office, and Nizamuddin is said to have distributed gifts rather than hoarding them, though as Simon Digby and others have noted, practice and ideal did not always match.

Most of what is known about Chishti saints comes from the malfuzat and from later biographical collections, including the Siyar al-Awliya composed in the fourteenth century and the later Akhbar al-Akhyar. These works tell us how disciples remembered their masters and emphasize miracles and moral examples. Stories about dramatic confrontations between saints and sultans, such as the legend associated with Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, should be treated as traditions, not as reliable reports. Carl Ernst and other scholars have also shown that the genre of malfuzat is shaped by its devotional purpose, which affects how historians use it as evidence.

Historical Vocabulary

Malfuzat and Sama

Malfuzat (singular malfuz) are records of a Sufi shaykh's conversations compiled by disciples. Sama means "audition": the devotional listening to poetry and music in Sufi gatherings.

Both terms matter because they point to evidence and practice that court chronicles rarely preserve. Malfuzat are devotional texts and must be read critically; sama was debated among Muslim scholars.

Section 44.16

Suhrawardi and Other Sufi Traditions

The Suhrawardi order, associated in South Asia with Baha al-Din Zakariya of Multan (d. c. 1262) and his successors, built a center in the Punjab that combined spiritual leadership with considerable wealth and connections to rulers and merchants. Traditional accounts present the Suhrawardis as comfortable with property and political engagement, in contrast with the Chishtis' emphasis on poverty, but this contrast should not be drawn too sharply. Both orders accepted gifts and received visitors, and individual shaykhs varied widely in outlook.

The Suhrawardi shaykhs of Multan show how a Sufi house could function as a regional institution. Multan was a commercial crossroads on routes to Sindh, the Punjab, and Central Asia, and a shaykh there stood among merchants, officials, and migrant scholars. The family's standing passed to successors, notably Baha al-Din Zakariya's grandson Rukn-i Alam, whose tomb in Multan, built in brick with glazed tilework, is among the best-known early Sultanate monuments outside Delhi. The tomb is usually dated to the early fourteenth century, and it ties religious authority, family succession, and architectural patronage together.

Comparison with the Chishtis is useful provided it is made carefully. Later hagiographers sometimes magnified differences, presenting Chishtis as ascetics distant from rulers and Suhrawardis as wealthy and politically engaged, and these portraits served the interests of later communities. Contemporary evidence shows overlap: Chishti shaykhs received gifts and visitors, and Suhrawardi shaykhs taught devotion and discipline. Students should treat the contrast as a pattern of emphasis, not a rigid division.

Other Sufi traditions operated in Bihar, Bengal, Gujarat, Kashmir, and the Deccan, where local institutions and languages shaped practice. In Bihar, for example, Sharaf al-Din Maneri (d. 1381) of the Firdausi order produced letters and teachings that remain influential. Because the Sultanate's political authority was uneven, Sufi networks often provided continuity across political boundaries.

Section 44.17

Shrines, Pilgrimage, Sama, and Shared Religious Spaces

After a saint's death, his tomb often became a shrine, a destination for pilgrims seeking blessings, healing, and community. Shrines attracted devotees from many backgrounds, including Hindus, Jains, and Muslims, and they served as centers for charity, hospitality, and religious festivals (urs). They were also economically significant, drawing offerings and sustaining local trades. Not all shrines functioned in the same way: some were closely linked to ruling patrons, others to local kin groups, others to pastoral or agrarian communities.

Debate surrounded sama. Many Chishtis defended it as a legitimate aid to devotion, while some jurists and ulama objected, citing limits on music in religious law. The disagreement shows that Muslim religious life contained serious internal arguments. Shared spaces such as shrines did not erase religious boundaries, but they allowed encounters and shared practices, which scholars interpret differently. They should be understood as regionally and historically specific rather than as evidence of a uniform harmony.

Shrines also had an economic and institutional life. They received gifts, endowments, and sometimes land grants, supported kitchens and attendants, and were managed by descendants or disciples who held the position of custodian (sajjada nashin). Hereditary custodianship helped some shrines survive political change but also tied them to family interests. Ajmer's dargah, associated with Mu'in al-Din Chishti, became far more prominent after the Sultanate, especially under later patronage, while other shrines remained mainly local. The prominence of any given shrine therefore reflects a long history of patronage and not only the original saint's reputation.

Religious authorities did not agree about shrine practices. In the Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi, Firuz Shah Tughluq reports forbidding women to visit tombs on holy days, citing concerns about improper conduct; this is a royal claim of prohibition, not proof that the restriction was universally obeyed. Devotees came with vows, requests for healing or children, and offerings, and women were among the visitors, though evidence for their experience is limited. Shrines sometimes stood on sites already regarded as sacred, which may have helped them attract people of different backgrounds. These patterns show real overlap in practice, but historians stress that participation did not remove differences in belief or social standing.

Section 44.18

Hindu, Jain, and Other Religious Communities under Sultanate Rule

Most people under Sultanate rule were not Muslims. Temples, Brahmanical scholarship, ritual specialists, Jain monasteries and merchants, and local religious authorities continued, supported by local patrons, merchants, and chiefs. Jain communities were prominent among traders and scholars; the Jain monk Jinaprabhasuri of the Kharatara tradition wrote on pilgrimage sites and is reported to have visited Muhammad bin Tughlaq's court. In regions such as Mithila, Varanasi, Gujarat, and parts of Rajasthan and Malwa, Sanskrit learning and temple institutions persisted.

The relationship with the Sultanate was neither uniformly peaceful nor uniformly violent. Conquest and political rivalry led to the destruction or appropriation of some temples, especially those with political significance, as at the Qutb complex in Delhi, where earlier building materials were reused for the Quwwat al-Islam mosque. Richard Eaton's work on temple destruction has argued that such acts were often tied to political symbolism and to specific royal temples, not to a general program against all temples. At the same time, non-Muslims paid special taxes under Islamic law in some periods, with the application of the jizya varying by ruler; Afif reports that Firuz Shah extended it to Brahmins. Temples were also repaired, built, and patronized in many places, including under local rulers who were subordinate to Delhi. Historians stress that policy depended on time, place, and ruler, and that neither "uninterrupted tolerance" nor "uninterrupted persecution" fits the evidence.

The violence of conquest was real and is well attested. When the Ghurid forces defeated the Gahadavala ruler Jayachandra in 1194, Varanasi, a major center of Brahmanical learning, suffered destruction and the displacement of religious institutions, and the early construction at Delhi and Ajmer reused material from earlier temples and other buildings. Later Khalji expeditions into Gujarat and the south, including the raids that Malik Kafur led in 1310 to 1311, struck wealthy temples, which held treasure and carried political prestige. Eaton has argued that such attacks on temples were mainly directed at sites tied to defeated kings and royal legitimacy, and that temples outside the main lines of conflict were frequently left alone. Not every historian accepts the framework in the same degree, and the evidence from chronicles, which sometimes celebrated destruction for religious or rhetorical reasons, requires cautious reading.

Policy toward non-Muslim subjects was governed by a mixture of religious law, political needs, and local practice. Islamic jurists classified non-Muslims who accepted Muslim rule as protected subjects who paid a tax, the jizya, but the Sultanate's revenue system rested largely on land taxes collected from villages, and the amount and the exemptions varied. Afif reports that Firuz Shah Tughluq collected jizya from Brahmins, who had previously been exempt, and that he punished a Brahmin accused of leading Muslims to idol worship, while chroniclers of the Lodi period report that Sikandar Lodi restricted ritual bathing at Mathura and destroyed temples there. Such accounts show that rulers sometimes enforced stricter policies, usually under religious advice, but they also reveal that such measures were noteworthy precisely because they were not the norm.

Existing institutions continued in many places. Brahmin families kept up Sanskrit scholarship at Varanasi, Mithila, and elsewhere, and legal and ritual commentaries continued to be composed. Hindu merchants, local chiefs, and families sustained temples, and Hindu communities used pilgrimage routes and festivals. The Jain tradition was especially resilient: merchants endowed temples and the great manuscript libraries of western India, Jain monks wrote in Sanskrit, Prakrit, and regional languages, and Jinaprabhasuri's pilgrimage texts show a Jain scholar who could move in the political world of Delhi. The Dilwara temples at Mount Abu, built earlier under Chaulukya patronage, and later Jain foundations in Rajasthan and Gwalior show the strength of patronage networks that did not depend on Delhi.

Delhi's direct rule extended unevenly, and many Hindu chiefs and rulers, whether subordinate, tributary, or independent, supported temples in their territories. Smaller religious communities, such as Christians and Jews on the western coast and Zoroastrians in Gujarat, also lived within the broader Sultanate world or on its edges, though the documentation is sparse. Students should treat temples and monasteries as institutions with their own economies, landholdings, and relationships to political power, not simply as passive victims or symbols. The history of religious communities under Sultanate rule is a history of adaptation, negotiation, loss, and persistence, differing among regions and across the three centuries.

Section 44.19

Bhakti Traditions and Vernacular Religious Expression

Devotional (bhakti) movements flourished during the Sultanate centuries, expressed largely in regional languages. In Maharashtra, Jnaneshwar and Namdev composed devotional poetry; in northern India, teachers such as Ramananda, Kabir, and Ravidas are associated with devotion that criticized or reinterpreted ritual and caste distinctions, though dates and biographies are uncertain and often derive from later tradition. Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539) was born in the Lodi period and later formed the Sikh community (see Chapter 48). In Kashmir, Lal Ded and Nund Rishi shaped vernacular religious expression.

Bhakti was not a single movement and cannot be explained as a mere reaction to Islam. Its roots included older southern devotional traditions (Chapter 48), regional literary developments, and local social tensions. Different poets took different positions on caste, ritual, scripture, and religious boundaries; some used language that engaged Sufi and Hindu idioms alike. Historians stress the diversity of these voices and caution against assuming that all low-caste poets rejected hierarchy in the same way or that their later reputations match their own words.

Marathi devotional writing is an example of devotion in a regional language. Jnaneshwar, who is associated with a commentary on the Gita in Marathi around 1290, and Namdev belong to the early Varkari tradition centered on the god Vitthala at Pandharpur, a tradition that welcomed participants from many social groups. In eastern India, poets such as Vidyapati in Mithila and Chandidas in Bengal wrote devotional and romantic verse in regional languages, and in Kashmir Lal Ded and Nund Rishi composed religious poetry that crossed communal lines. These are separate traditions, and treating them as a single movement would flatten their differences.

Kabir and Ravidas illustrate the problems of evidence. Verses attributed to them survive in several collections, including the Sikh scripture and other later anthologies, and the texts differ, so historians cannot be sure which lines go back to the poets themselves. Their dates are placed in the fifteenth century, though scholars debate details. Their poems criticize priestly authority and ritual and invoke a God beyond sectarian labels, but they also vary in tone and message. Sufi writing and devotional poetry shared images and genres, as in the vernacular romances such as the Chandayan, and this indicates exchange rather than a simple reaction of one tradition to another. Chapter 48 returns to these figures in greater depth.

Section 44.20

Islamization, Conversion, Coexistence, and Religious Boundaries

How did Muslim communities grow in South Asia? The term Islamization describes varied processes by which Muslim communities and religious practices developed, not a single government campaign. Older narratives often posited either forced conversion by rulers or mass conversion by lower castes seeking equality. Modern scholarship generally treats both as inadequate. Conversion occurred through varied processes over generations: the migration and settlement of Muslims; marriage; the work of Sufis and other religious figures; the patronage of local leaders; the incorporation of communities into new economic and political networks; and the gradual adaptation of local religious practice. Richard Eaton, in studies of Punjab and Bengal, has argued that long-term social and agrarian change, rather than political coercion alone, shaped religious transformation, and has emphasized that processes took place largely over centuries and sometimes after the Delhi Sultanate. Evidence for the Sultanate period itself is limited, and later developments should not be projected backward.

Several models help organize the evidence. In an influential 1985 essay on conversion, Eaton distinguished processes he called accretion, identification, and reform. In accretion, groups outside established religious orders were gradually absorbed into a new community; in identification, a new religious figure or practice was integrated with existing ones; reform referred to movements that insisted on stricter norms. These are analytical tools, not stages every community passed through, and historians debate how far they travel beyond the regions Eaton studied.

The regional cases differ. In the western Punjab, Eaton linked shrine networks, such as that of Baba Farid, with pastoral and agrarian communities settling and shifting toward cultivation, a process that unfolded largely between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries. In Bengal, his study The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier (1993) connected Muslim growth with the opening of delta forest to rice cultivation and with local religious leaders, again over a long period that mostly followed the Delhi Sultanate's authority there. Students should therefore treat these studies as evidence about long-term patterns, not as direct accounts of conversion in Delhi, the Doab, or Gujarat during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Political incentives mattered, but they were uneven. Rulers granted land, offices, and stipends to Muslim scholars and holy men, and Muslims could receive advantages in some administrative and military settings. Yet the Sultanate drew its revenue mainly from non-Muslim cultivators and traders, and chroniclers record no sustained state program of mass conversion. Individual episodes, such as the conversion of war captives, enslaved people, or members of defeated ruling families, did occur and are better documented than village-level change, which can bias the record toward dramatic cases.

Conversion also did not mean abrupt cultural change. Converted households often retained marriage customs, occupational identities, festivals, and kin networks, and newer Muslim communities developed distinct local expressions of religion that scholars and reformers sometimes criticized. At the same time, non-Muslim neighbors adopted practices such as visits to shrines. The boundaries between communities could be firm in some contexts and permeable in others, which is why historians prefer to speak of long processes of community formation rather than of single moments of conversion.

Coexistence is also complex. Muslims and non-Muslims shared markets, villages, and sometimes shrines, while rulers, ulama, and local elites drew boundaries at various times. The textual record is dominated by elite voices, so the experiences of ordinary converts and neighbors are poorly documented. Students should avoid framing the period as a permanent civilizational conflict or as seamless harmony.

Historical Debate

Section 44.21

Persian as a Language of Government, Scholarship, and Literature

Persian was the language of Sultanate administration, court literature, and learned culture, brought to India through the earlier Ghaznavid and Ghurid states and reinforced by migrant scholars and officials. Minhaj-i Siraj wrote the Tabaqat-i Nasiri around 1260; Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) composed poetry, historical verse, and prose; Ziya al-Din Barani wrote the Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi around 1357; Afif wrote a history of Firuz Shah; and Isami composed the Futuh al-Salatin in 1350. Each work served a purpose: to legitimate a ruler, instruct a prince, preserve a lineage, or advance the author's views.

Persian literary culture also shaped political ideals, including the model of justice and kingship drawn from Iranian traditions. But it was a language of a specific educated class. Its use in administration spread Persian vocabulary and bureaucratic practice, yet it did not displace local languages in everyday life. Historians must read these texts as products of literate elites with their own anxieties and ambitions.

The major Persian works differ in purpose. Minhaj-i Siraj's Tabaqat-i Nasiri is a universal history arranged in generations (tabaqat), with extended treatment of the Ghurids and the early Delhi sultans, and it was written for the ruler Nasir al-Din Mahmud by an author who had himself been a judge and teacher. Amir Khusrau's Khazain al-Futuh (1311) praises Alauddin Khalji's campaigns in ornate prose, and his masnavis tell historical and romantic stories in verse. Barani wrote after losing his position and used his history to argue for a moral order, while Afif's later work celebrates Firuz Shah, and Isami's verse chronicle, written in the Deccan, is sharply critical of Muhammad bin Tughlaq. Each text therefore advances a view that must be weighed.

Persian was also the language of practical administration. Collections of letters and model documents, such as the letters of Ain al-Mulk Multani, a fourteenth-century official, illustrate how correspondence, grants, and orders were drafted, and surviving inscriptions and decrees supplement the chronicles. Few original administrative records from the Sultanate survive, so historians rely on such collections and on later references. The prestige of Persian also shaped careers: knowledge of the language and its literary forms opened doors to posts and patronage, and communities that learned it, including Hindu scribes, gained access to administrative life.

Section 44.22

Sanskrit, Prakrit, Apabhramsha, and Regional Literary Traditions

Sanskrit remained vital. Scholars in Kashmir, Mithila, Varanasi, Gujarat, and elsewhere produced works on grammar, law, philosophy, poetry, and religion; Jain authors wrote in Sanskrit, Prakrit, and Apabhramsha; and courts, monasteries, and merchant patrons supported composition. A Sanskrit inscription of 1276 at Palam near Delhi, recording the building of a stepwell, shows the language in use within the Sultanate's capital region and refers to the sultan's rule. In Kashmir, Jonaraja continued the Sanskrit chronicle tradition of the Rajatarangini.

Regional languages such as Marathi, Bengali, Maithili, Gujarati, Punjabi, Kannada, and Telugu gained literary expression, often in devotional contexts and at regional courts, including courts outside Delhi's control. Vidyapati in Mithila wrote in Sanskrit, Maithili, and an eastern literary idiom. The picture is of multiple literary worlds, in which Persian did not displace Sanskrit or the regional languages.

Sanskrit and regional writing also recorded the political events of the period from non-Delhi viewpoints. Nayachandra's Sanskrit Hammira-mahakavya (early fifteenth century) recounts the Chahamana king Hammira's struggle against Alauddin Khalji at Ranthambore, and the later Kanhadade Prabandha (1455), in an early Rajasthani-Gujarati idiom, narrates the Jalor ruler's conflict with the same sultan. The Jain scholar Merutunga's Prabandhachintamani (1305) gathers stories about kings and ministers of western India. These works offer valuable regional perspectives, though they are literary and often idealize their heroes.

Vernacular literature expanded in several regions. In Maharashtra, Mahanubhava writers produced early Marathi prose, and Jnaneshwar and Namdev wrote devotional verse; in the Telugu country, poets such as Tikkana worked under Kakatiya patronage; and in Bengal, the Bengali Ramayana attributed to Krittivasa is conventionally assigned to the fifteenth century, although surviving versions were extensively reworked in subsequent centuries. Maladhar Basu's Krishna Vijaya belongs to the later fifteenth century and received patronage connected to the Bengal Sultanate (Chapter 47). These examples show that the Sultanate itself was only one of several patrons of literature, and that political fragmentation sometimes encouraged regional courts to support local languages.

Section 44.23

Hindavi, Multilingualism, and Linguistic Exchange

Language contact was constant. Persian and Arabic vocabulary entered the speech of northern India, while Persian-literate poets and Sufis used local languages. Amir Khusrau wrote verses and riddles in an idiom he and others called Hindavi, though the attribution of some Hindavi works to him is uncertain. Mulla Daud's Chandayan (1379), a romance in an eastern Hindi idiom (Awadhi), shows a Muslim poet working in a vernacular tradition with Sufi themes.

Hindavi was a flexible term for the spoken languages of northern India, not the standardized Hindi or Urdu of later centuries. Modern language identities, including the later separation of Hindi and Urdu by script and religion, were not fully formed in the Sultanate period, and students should avoid projecting them backward. Bilingualism was common among administrators, merchants, and religious figures. In the Deccan, an early form that later became Dakhni developed (see Chapter 46).

Amir Khusrau's own writings provide valuable evidence. In his Persian poem Nuh Sipihr (1318), he lists the languages of India, including forms used in Delhi, Punjab and Sindh, Kashmir, Gujarat, Bengal, and the Deccan, and he praises Sanskrit as more refined than the vernaculars. This shows that a Persian-writing poet in Delhi recognized a diverse linguistic landscape and valued Indian learned traditions. His Hindavi verses, riddles, and songs have been transmitted orally and in later collections, so some attributions may be later.

Other terms were used for the language of everyday life: Hindavi, Dehlavi, and later Hindi, Rekhta, and Dakhni, which referred to related but changing varieties. The term Urdu, as a name for a distinct literary language, is later. Verses attributed to Baba Farid, written in a western Punjabi speech and preserved in the Sikh scripture, show Sufi use of regional languages, and the Chandayan shows the same in an eastern idiom. In administration and trade, bilingual or multilingual individuals interpreted between Persian and vernacular speakers. The result was a layered linguistic world in which script, religion, and language did not align in the modern way.

Section 44.24

Education, Libraries, Manuscripts, and the Circulation of Knowledge

Education took several forms. Maktabs taught reading, writing, and basic religious knowledge; madrasas offered advanced training in law, theology, and grammar; Sanskrit schools and teachers transmitted grammar, philosophy, law, and medicine; and private teachers and Sufi hospices passed on specialized knowledge. Paper manuscripts became increasingly important for preserving texts, and libraries existed at courts and some religious centers.

Knowledge moved through several channels. Translations occurred: Ziya al-Din Nakhshabi adapted Sanskrit narrative material into the Persian Tuti-nama in the 1330s, and later chroniclers report that Firuz Shah ordered translation of Sanskrit works acquired at Nagarkot, though the details are debated. Historians distinguish documented transmission from broad claims that scientific knowledge flowed freely between traditions. Medicine, astronomy, and mathematics were practiced in Sanskrit, Arabic, and Persian traditions, and more evidence exists for coexistence and occasional exchange than for systematic synthesis.

Muslim education began with the maktab, where children learned the Quran, Persian reading, and elementary writing. Advanced study in madrasas covered Quranic exegesis, Hadith, Hanafi jurisprudence, grammar, logic, and rhetoric, and legal handbooks such as the Hidaya were central to training judges and jurists. Minhaj-i Siraj identifies himself with the Delhi madrasa called the Nasiriyya, and Firuz Shah's madrasa at Hauz-i Khas became one of the best-known institutions of the later Sultanate. Teaching also occurred privately in the homes of scholars and in Sufi hospices, where disciples learned devotional texts and ethics under the guidance of a shaykh. Support came largely through endowments (waqf), royal grants, and the generosity of nobles.

Sanskrit education depended on teachers (gurus), who trained students in households, temples, and monasteries rather than in a single central institution. Varanasi remained a major center, and in Mithila scholars developed the school of new logic (navya-nyaya) associated with Gangesha, whose treatise on the means of knowledge is usually placed in the thirteenth or fourteenth century. Kashmir, Gujarat, and the Deccan had their own learned communities, and Jain monks and scholars maintained libraries and wrote on logic, grammar, and narrative. These traditions mostly operated under local patronage and were not controlled by the Sultanate, although the political climate affected their security and funding.

The material base of learning changed as paper became more common. Paper was used for Persian chancery documents and books, and Jain communities in western India adopted paper for manuscripts, alongside palm leaf, in the centuries around 1200. Copying was usually done by professional scribes or by students and disciples, and the survival of manuscripts depended on the stability of the institutions that held them. Libraries existed in royal households, noble residences, madrasas, Jain repositories, and some Sufi centers, but wars, sacks, and neglect destroyed much, and the Timurid sack of Delhi in 1398 to 1399 is often cited as a disruptive moment, even though we lack a full inventory of what was lost.

Documented cases of cross-tradition scholarship are valuable because they show what actually occurred. In 1370, the Jain astronomer Mahendra Suri, who was connected to the court of Firuz Shah Tughluq, composed a Sanskrit treatise on the astrolabe, the Yantraraja, drawing on instruments and knowledge from the Arabic and Persian astronomical tradition. In medicine, Sanskrit Ayurvedic learning and the Greek-Arabic tradition practiced by Muslim physicians coexisted, and in 1512 a Persian medical compendium, the Ma'dan al-Shifa-yi Sikandar Shahi, was prepared in the Lodi period and drew on Sanskrit sources. Ziya al-Din Nakhshabi's Tuti-nama adapted Sanskrit storytelling for Persian readers. These examples demonstrate intellectual exchange, but they are specific and limited; they do not prove that the two traditions regularly merged. For most students and scholars, formal education remained within their own community and language, and the movement of knowledge across those lines depended on particular patrons, translators, and bilingual intermediaries.

Section 44.25

Architecture and the Transformation of Building Traditions

Sultanate architecture combined imported forms and local craft. The Quwwat al-Islam mosque in Delhi, begun about 1193, reused materials from earlier buildings and incorporated carved screens by local masons; the Qutb Minar, begun around 1199 and completed under Iltutmish, carries inscriptions in Arabic. Iltutmish's tomb and the early tomb known as Sultan Ghari illustrate the development of funerary architecture. The Alai Darwaza (1311) is a refined gateway with a dome and horseshoe arches, built in the Khalji period. Tughlaq architecture, including Tughluqabad and the tomb of Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, is characterized by sloping walls and austere, massive forms. Firuz Shah's building projects included Firozabad and the Hauz-i Khas complex, and the Sayyid and Lodi periods produced distinctive tombs, including octagonal and square types set in gardens.

Technical features included true arches and domes, lime mortar, stone and brick construction, calligraphic ornament, and geometric design, executed by local artisans. Early arches at Delhi show corbelled construction, suggesting masons adapting familiar techniques to new forms. Scholars, including Catherine Asher in her studies of Indo-Islamic architecture, emphasize diversity and regional adaptation rather than a single imported style. Firuz Shah also moved Ashokan pillars to Delhi, an act that shows both interest in antiquities and the Sultanate's inability to read their inscriptions.

A short architectural chronology helps to organize the monuments. Aibak and Iltutmish enlarged the Delhi mosque, whose columns and other reused materials (spolia) came from earlier buildings, and built the Qutb Minar, whose tapering shaft carries bands of inscriptions; Iltutmish's tomb and the Sultan Ghari show early funerary design. Alauddin's mosque gateway introduced a refined dome and horseshoe arches, and his unfinished Alai Minar suggests an ambition to surpass the earlier tower. Tughlaq builders favored massive sloping walls of rubble faced with stone, as at Tughluqabad and the tomb of Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, while Firuz Shah's works used plainer forms; the Khirki mosque built under his minister Khan-i Jahan Junan Shah has largely roofed prayer spaces surrounding four smaller open courtyards. Lodi tombs include octagonal and square types set in gardens, with some experimenting with double-shell dome construction.

Outside Delhi, regional sultanates and local rulers developed their own styles: the Rukn-i Alam tomb at Multan in brick and tile, Bengal's Adina mosque, and the Atala mosque at Jaunpur (1408) with its massive entrance, all of which are examined in Chapter 47. Construction depended on organized labor and masons whose names are rarely recorded, although some inscriptions name builders or patrons. Modern historians, including Catherine Asher, caution against dividing buildings into purely 'Hindu' and 'Islamic' elements, since workshops used techniques from several traditions and patrons selected forms to convey particular messages. The resulting buildings are best read as products of negotiation between patrons, craftsmen, and available materials.

Section 44.26

Music, Poetry, Performance, and the Cultural World of Amir Khusrau

Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325), born in northern India to a Turkic father and an Indian mother, served successive sultans while remaining a disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya. He wrote Persian lyric poetry, a five-poem cycle in imitation of earlier Persian masters, historical verse, and prose, and also verses in Hindavi. His writings reveal a cultured, multilingual elite world and show admiration for Indian music and poetry. Sama gatherings in Sufi hospices combined poetry, music, and devotion, and courts supported musicians and poets.

Later tradition credits Khusrau with inventing instruments such as the sitar and tabla and genres such as qawwali and khayal. Historians generally treat such claims skeptically: the contemporary evidence supports his poetry, patronage, and involvement in Sufi musical life, but not specific inventions, which appear in later traditions.

The Chishti defense of sama took place in the open. The Fawa'id al-Fu'ad records Nizamuddin Auliya's views on when listening to poetry and music is permitted, and conditions such as the listener's spiritual state and the avoidance of musical instruments in some settings are mentioned in Chishti tradition. Qawwali as a genre is usually traced to later centuries, so students should avoid reading it directly into Khusrau's time. The earlier practice combined recitation, sung poetry, and the setting of a Sufi gathering, and it drew both scholarly criticism and popular devotion.

Courtly music also flourished. Ibn Battuta describes musicians and singers at court festivals, and Sanskrit musical theory continued: Sharngadeva's Sangitaratnakara, composed at the Yadava court in the Deccan in the thirteenth century, systematized earlier Indian musical thought. Khusrau's prose and verse show that Persian and Indian musical vocabularies met, but later legends, such as the tale of a famous musician brought from the Deccan to Delhi, mix history and tradition. Music thus offers an example of cultural exchange that is real but difficult to document precisely.

Section 44.27

Urban Landscapes, Waterworks, Gardens, and Public Spaces

Water shaped cities. Reservoirs such as the Hauz-i Shamsi built under Iltutmish and the large Hauz-i Alai (later Hauz-i Khas) supplied Delhi; wells and stepwells served neighborhoods; and Firuz Shah's canals and orchards extended water and shade. Afif credits Firuz with establishing many gardens, though numbers in chronicles should be treated cautiously.

Mosques, tombs, markets, and shrines created public spaces for prayer, trade, and sociability. A stepwell like the one recorded at Palam served both practical and social needs. Built environments connected patrons to public life, and ordinary users, including women collecting water, shaped how spaces worked in practice. Construction also required labor, often compulsory or poorly paid, whose experience is rarely recorded.

Firuz Shah Tughluq built a new city, Firuzabad, beside the Yamuna in the 1350s, and its citadel complex (the Kotla) contained a mosque, palace buildings, and a pillar of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka that he had moved from Topra in the Ambala region. Another Ashokan pillar from Meerut was set up on the Delhi Ridge. Afif describes the careful transport of the pillars, and the project shows the sultan's interest in monuments that no one in his court could read, since the Brahmi script of Ashoka's edicts had been forgotten (Chapter 16). He also founded or expanded towns such as Jaunpur and Hisar, which linked canals, markets, and mosques in planned settlements.

Water systems were central to urban life. Ibn Battuta describes the large Hauz-i Shamsi as a reservoir that held rainwater and attracted people to its edges, and stepwells such as those in Mehrauli served as sources of water and gathering places. Gardens surrounded tombs and some royal buildings, and gardens, wells, and tanks influenced neighborhood life, health, and the use of land. Roads and rest houses (sarais) served travelers and merchants. These works were built by labor of whose conditions we know little, and their maintenance depended on patronage, so some fell into disrepair when political conditions changed. Public spaces such as mosque courtyards, shrine precincts, and markets formed the setting for much of the social life described in this chapter.

Section 44.28

Decorative Arts, Material Culture, and Regional Adaptation

Surviving objects include coins with Arabic and Persian inscriptions, carved stone, calligraphic ornament, glazed tilework (as at Multan and at Delhi monuments such as Shish Gumbad and Sikandar Lodi's tomb), metalwork, textiles, and manuscripts. Illustrated manuscripts from the late Sultanate period survive in small numbers, and their dating and attribution are debated. Craftsmen adapted techniques and motifs from South Asian, Central Asian, and Iranian traditions according to the setting, whether courtly, commercial, religious, or domestic.

The archaeological and museum record is thin for many categories, especially perishable goods such as textiles and everyday wood and leather objects. Historians therefore reason cautiously, avoiding claims about overall styles based on a few survivors. The surviving record nevertheless shows how patrons and artisans negotiated tradition and novelty across regions.

Coins are among the best-dated objects. Early issues in the name of the Ghurid ruler Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad ibn Sam retained the bull-and-horseman type familiar from earlier northern Indian coinage, including Chahamana issues; the jital continued a small-denomination monetary tradition that predated the Delhi Sultanate (Chapter 39). Iltutmish established silver tankas bearing Arabic inscriptions. He received formal investiture in 1229 from the Abbasid caliph al-Mustansir, whose name appears on surviving later coins; references to Abbasid caliphs had also appeared on some of Iltutmish's earlier coin issues, so 1229 should not be treated as the first such reference. Alauddin Khalji's coins used the grand title "Second Alexander," and Muhammad bin Tughlaq's experiments with gold, silver, and token currency (Chapter 42) generated coins with unusually varied legends. Taken together, the coins illustrate how rulers adapted existing monetary forms and combined them with Islamic and Persianate claims to legitimacy.

Carved stone shows collaboration between patrons and artisans. The screen of the Quwwat al-Islam mosque combines Arabic calligraphy with flowing vegetal scrolls and motifs that resemble earlier Indian carving, and masons trained in temple building contributed methods of cutting, stacking, and finishing stone. Glazed tilework appears on the tomb of Rukn-i Alam at Multan and survives in blue-tile decoration at Delhi's Shish Gumbad and tomb of Sikandar Lodi; the distribution of these monuments shows that patrons and craftsmen adapted glazed decoration in distinct regional settings. The Iron Pillar of the Gupta period still stood in the courtyard of the Delhi mosque, a reminder that older objects were incorporated into new settings with meanings that we cannot always recover.

Textiles, metalwork, and books are harder to trace because so few examples survive from before the sixteenth century. Chroniclers and Ibn Battuta mention robes of honor, silk and cotton goods, and royal workshops for clothing and other luxury goods, and metalworkers produced weapons, vessels, lamps, and inlaid objects, but written descriptions outnumber surviving pieces. Manuscript production left a firmer trail: Qur'an manuscripts from roughly the late fourteenth to the mid-sixteenth centuries preserve a distinctive calligraphic hand conventionally called Bihari, although its exact place of development is uncertain. Qur'an manuscripts and Persian texts were prepared for patrons, and a small number of illustrated manuscripts from the late Sultanate period survive. Jain communities in western India commissioned painted paper manuscripts of religious works, which preserve visual conventions independent of the courts of Delhi. The dating and attribution of many items remain disputed.

Domestic and everyday objects are as important as courtly pieces, although they have received less attention. Excavations and surface finds at settlements around Delhi have produced pottery, bangles, beads, tools, and imported glazed wares, which show regional supply and household consumption in ways chronicles do not. Because such objects are often poorly dated or published, historians use them cautiously. The labels "Indo-Islamic" or "Sultanate style" are convenient but can mislead if they imply a single aesthetic: different patrons, communities, regions, and workshops made distinct choices, and the same craftsman could produce a mosque ornament, a temple carving, and a household object.

Division VI — Historical Synthesis

Bringing the threads together.

Section 44.29

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that the Delhi Sultanate transformed South Asian society through new political and cultural connections, but that the direction and extent of change depended on local institutions, regional histories, and the choices of communities. Five connected processes organize the synthesis.

Social hierarchy and mobility. A mixed elite of Turkic, Afghan, Persian, Indian Muslim, and Hindu participants governed alongside long-established jati and local power structures. Mobility existed through service, trade, and royal favor, but status remained heavily shaped by descent, wealth, and office. Slavery and dependent labor were integral to the economy and household life.

Agrarian and commercial development. Villages supplied revenue and grain through local intermediaries; cash payment, coinage, and credit tied rural producers to markets. Artisans, merchants, and cities expanded, linked by inland and maritime networks that exposed the Sultanate to external shocks.

Religious continuity and change. Sufi orders, ulama, temples, Jain institutions, and devotional poets coexisted and sometimes competed. Conversion was a long, regionally varied process, and coexistence was neither uniformly harmonious nor uniformly hostile.

Multilingual literary production. Persian dominated administration and court literature, yet Sanskrit, Prakrit, Apabhramsha, Hindavi, and regional languages continued to flourish, producing a multilingual cultural world.

Architecture and cultural practice. Builders and artisans combined forms from several traditions, producing monuments, waterworks, and objects that reveal both patronage and local craft. Music and poetry crossed court, shrine, and street.

Students should compare the experience of an elite courtier, a village cultivator, an artisan, a merchant, and a participant in a devotional community. Each lived within the same political framework but encountered it differently; each had different sources of security and vulnerability. Chapter 45 turns to Vijayanagara, a southern imperial system with its own institutional and cultural history. Chapter 48 returns to Bhakti, Sufism, and Sikhism in greater depth, and Chapter 50 begins the story of the Mughals, who inherited many Sultanate practices while creating their own.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Society and Culture across Three Centuries

What changed? New elites, Persian administration, a monetized economy, Sufi networks, new architectural forms, and spreading Muslim communities.

What remained continuous? Village structures, jati organization, Sanskrit learning, temple institutions, regional languages, and agricultural rhythms.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban elites, immigrants, artisans in royal service, and communities near Sufi centers or administrative towns.

Who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers, whose lives continued to be organized by local intermediaries and seasonal labor.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that political change did not transform every community equally.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined society and culture under the Delhi Sultanate between c. 1206 and 1526, arguing that the Sultanate's political establishment interacted with existing institutions to produce varied and uneven change.

Division I examined the evidence and the social structure. Court-centered Persian sources, travel accounts, Sufi conversations, inscriptions, and monuments must be read against one another. The elite combined Turkic, Afghan, Persian, Indian Muslim, and Hindu participants; villages were mediated by khuts, muqaddams, and chaudhuris; jati organization persisted and allowed some mobility; migration produced mixed communities; slavery took many forms; and women's lives were diverse but poorly recorded.

Division II explored the economy. Agriculture, helped by wells and in places by canals, supported the Sultanate, while revenue demands, indebtedness, and famine created vulnerability. Artisans and workshops produced textiles, metalwork, and construction labor; coinage, credit, and merchant communities supported markets; and inland and maritime networks tied the Sultanate to Central Asia and the Indian Ocean. Delhi, a cluster of successive cities, offered sharp social contrasts.

Division III addressed religion. Mosques, ulama, qadis, madrasas, and waqf endowments organized Muslim institutional life. The Chishti and Suhrawardi Sufi orders built networks of hospices and shrines. Hindu and Jain communities maintained temples, scholarship, and merchant networks, with relations to the state varying by place and period. Bhakti poets used regional languages in varied ways.

The debate over Islamization showed why neither coercion nor social liberation alone explains the growth of Muslim communities; long, regionally varied processes of migration, settlement, networks, and local adaptation were at work, and the evidence for the Sultanate period itself is limited.

Division IV considered language and learning. Persian dominated administration and court literature, but Sanskrit, Prakrit, Apabhramsha, Hindavi, and regional languages continued to flourish. Education occurred in maktabs, madrasas, Sanskrit schools, and Sufi hospices, and some translation occurred, though claims of broad scientific exchange require care.

Division V turned to architecture and the arts. Builders adapted arch and dome technology to local craft, producing the Qutb complex, the Alai Darwaza, Tughlaq monuments, and Lodi tombs. Music and poetry flourished at court and in Sufi settings, with Amir Khusrau a central figure whose later legend must be distinguished from contemporary evidence. Waterworks, gardens, and objects reveal social life and patronage.

The chapter's overall conclusion is that the Sultanate transformed South Asian society through new connections, but that local institutions, regional histories, and community choices shaped the direction and extent of change. Many of the communities, institutions, and cultural forms described here outlasted the Sultanate and shaped the Mughal and regional worlds that followed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the history of the Delhi Sultanate beyond sultans and battles. The institutions that outlasted political regimes, including villages, jati communities, Sufi networks, markets, shrines, schools, and languages, are central to understanding how South Asia changed and how it did not.

It also shows how historians work with uneven evidence. The Persian chronicle, the Sufi conversation record, the inscription, and the standing monument each give partial access to the past. Learning to ask who wrote, who is absent, and what a source can and cannot establish is a skill this series uses repeatedly.

The chapter's themes recur later. Sufi shrines, Persianate culture, and multilingual literary life continue into the Mughal period; agrarian intermediaries and revenue systems evolve; devotional poetry is examined further in Chapter 48; and debates about religious change and historical memory return in Chapter 111.

Placed within the six-volume series, the chapter completes the political-cultural arc of Part VIII and prepares the regional histories of Part IX. It reminds students that the Sultanate was one part of a larger, diverse South Asian world rather than a synonym for it.
