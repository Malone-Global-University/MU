South Asia and the First World War

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the last days of September 1914, troop transports carrying the Lahore and Meerut Divisions of the Indian Army reached Marseilles. Men recruited in the Punjab, the Himalayan foothills, and the plains of Hindustan walked down the gangways into a French port that almost none of them had ever imagined seeing, and within weeks they were in the trenches of Flanders and Artois, facing an industrial war whose scale and technology bore no resemblance to the frontier campaigns for which many of them had been trained.

Their arrival was one of the least expected events of the early war. Britain's army in 1914 was small, and the Indian Army was one of the few trained forces the Empire could deploy quickly beyond Europe. The Indian Corps that took its place in the line near La Bassée in the autumn of 1914 helped to hold a thin British front during a critical period. Soldiers who had grown up in irrigated Punjab villages, Garhwal hill settlements, and the wheat-and-millet districts of the Ganges plain now experienced winter mud, artillery barrages, and eventually poison gas. Their experience is one of the central subjects of this chapter, but it is only one part of it.

The scale of South Asia's involvement was very large. Government recruitment returns counted 1,440,437 Indian combatant and noncombatant recruits between August 1914 and December 1919, beyond an estimated prewar Indian establishment of 239,561. A separate, conservative estimate places approximately 1,096,013 Indian personnel overseas, in France and Belgium, Mesopotamia, East Africa, Gallipoli, Egypt, Palestine, and other theaters. These are not interchangeable measures: the first is a recruitment total extending beyond the 1918 armistice, the second estimates overseas service, and neither counts every category in precisely the same way. Casualty figures likewise depend on definitions and incomplete records. The chapter therefore treats the scale of mobilization as both a major historical fact and a statistical question that requires explicit denominators.

The war was not experienced only by men in uniform. Villages in the Punjab and elsewhere supplied recruits, and some districts felt official pressure to supply more of them. Families depended on remitted pay or lost an adult worker. The Government of India raised money through taxation, borrowing, and an enormous financial contribution to Britain, while prices rose, shipping was disrupted, and a few industries, including cotton textiles, jute, and steel, found wartime demand for their products. In 1918 and 1919 an influenza pandemic killed millions of people across the subcontinent, with mortality figures that remain uncertain but were catastrophic by any measure. Military service, the economy, and public health all shaped the political atmosphere in which nationalist leaders worked.

Politics itself moved in several directions at once. Some constitutional leaders offered cooperation in the expectation that loyalty would be rewarded with reform. Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant founded Home Rule Leagues that turned the demand for self-government into a sustained campaign. The Indian National Congress and the All-India Muslim League negotiated the Lucknow Pact of December 1916. At the same time, migrants in North America, conspirators in Berlin, and mutineers in Singapore pursued very different strategies, aiming to overthrow British rule by force. The government answered with new emergency powers, and in August 1917 the British Cabinet announced a constitutional goal of responsible government for India. Each of these developments is treated here separately, because they should not be conflated.

The chapter also takes seriously the South Asian regions outside the constitutional politics of British India. Nepal's Rana government placed Gurkha recruitment at Britain's disposal. Burma, then administered as part of British India, supplied rice, labor, and transport connections. In 1915 Ceylon experienced anti-Muslim riots and a harsh declaration of martial law, an episode whose causes lay largely in local religious, social, and commercial tensions rather than in the European war itself. Princely states offered troops, money, and medical assistance. The wartime history of South Asia, in other words, was plural from the beginning.

The evidence for this history is rich and uneven. Soldiers' letters survive largely through the reports of the colonial Censor of Indian Mails, which means they come to historians already selected, translated, and filtered. Revolutionary conspiracies are known chiefly through prosecution records and intelligence reports, and these were produced by the authorities who suppressed them. Congress and League resolutions, Home Rule publications, and parliamentary declarations record constitutional politics more directly, but they reflect the voices of organized, mostly educated, and largely male political elites. Reading these sources critically is part of the chapter's historical purpose.

The chapter's central question can be put simply. How did South Asia's contribution to the First World War alter the relationship between colonial subjects and imperial power, and why did wartime sacrifice produce both demands for constitutional reform and more radical resistance? Chapter 77 ended with a nationalist movement divided over methods, communal representation, and the limits of reform. Chapter 78 shows how global war deepened those divisions and also created unexpected opportunities for cooperation. It ends in the winter of 1918–1919, on the threshold of the postwar movements, the Rowlatt legislation, and the Gandhi-led campaigns that Chapter 79 examines.

Learning Objectives

  2. Describe the organization of the British Indian Army in 1914 and analyze how the colonial ideology of "martial races" shaped recruitment.
  3. Distinguish among combatants, noncombatant workers, and military followers, and explain why statistics about South Asian war service depend on definition.
  4. Compare the conditions, objectives, and outcomes of Indian military service on the Western Front, in Mesopotamia, and in other theaters.
  5. Evaluate soldiers' letters as historical evidence, taking into account censorship, translation, dictation, and selection.

Historical Context

By 1914 the British Raj, whose institutions Chapter 71 described, had governed most of the subcontinent under direct Crown authority for more than half a century. The Secretary of State for India in London, advised by the India Council, stood above the Viceroy and the Government of India. The Viceroy in 1914 was Lord Hardinge, whose term began in 1910 and whose capital had been moved from Calcutta to Delhi after the Durbar of 1911. Provincial governments, the Indian Civil Service, district officers, and a large army sustained colonial authority. Representative institutions existed, but they were limited: the Indian Councils Act of 1909 had enlarged the legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims, but it left executive authority in official hands.

The nationalist movement that Chapters 76 and 77 traced was divided in 1914. The Congress had split at Surat in 1907 between Moderates and those then called Extremists. Tilak, the most prominent of the latter, had been sentenced in 1908 to six years' imprisonment in Mandalay and was released only in June 1914. The Muslim League, founded in 1906, was a separate organization with its own political concerns. Revolutionary networks in Bengal, Maharashtra, and the Punjab, and among Indian migrants abroad, pursued violent methods, and the colonial government treated them as a serious threat. The reunification of Bengal in 1911 had removed one grievance but had left the political atmosphere unsettled.

The Indian Army was the military instrument of this system. Reorganized under Lord Kitchener between 1903 and 1909 into a force of divisions meant to defend the northwest frontier and support imperial operations elsewhere, it recruited chiefly from groups the colonial state classified as "martial races" (Chapter 73, §73.4). British officers held the commissioned ranks, while Indian soldiers rose at most to junior Indian officer positions. The army had experience of campaigns in China, Africa, and on the frontier, but not of a European war against a first-rate industrial power.

The imperial financial and constitutional relationship also mattered. Indian revenues paid for the Indian Army, but its deployment was decided in London and Delhi, and a long-running dispute existed over which government should bear the cost of Indian troops used outside India. Nationalists since the 1880s had argued, as Chapter 76 discussed in connection with the "drain" theory, that India was made to pay for imperial purposes that served British rather than Indian interests. The war gave those arguments new urgency.

The wider global context also shaped events. The Ottoman Empire, whose sultan was widely regarded by Sunni Muslims as caliph, entered the war against Britain at the end of October 1914. This created acute difficulties for a government that relied on Muslim soldiers and needed Muslim political goodwill. Germany sought to exploit British imperial vulnerabilities, including through contact with South Asian revolutionaries abroad. Afghanistan remained neutral but occupied a strategically sensitive position on the northwestern frontier. Finally, the war's ideological language, which stressed the defense of small nations and the principle of self-determination, gave Indian nationalists a vocabulary with which to criticize colonial rule.

Section 78.1

The Outbreak of War in 1914 and India's Imperial Position

When Britain declared war on Germany on August 4, 1914, India was at war from the same moment. This followed from the constitutional structure of the Empire. India was governed through the Secretary of State and the Viceroy, not by a responsible Indian government, and neither the Imperial Legislative Council nor any other representative Indian body was consulted about whether to enter the conflict. The Viceroy, Lord Hardinge, announced the situation, and the Government of India began to place its military and financial resources at the disposal of the imperial war effort. The Dominions, by contrast, had self-governing parliaments, although their formal position was also in practice bound to Britain's declaration. India's absence from any decision-making role became a point of grievance and, in time, of political argument.

India's strategic position made it a vital reserve. The Indian Army existed partly to defend the northwest frontier and partly to serve as an imperial force that could be sent to the Persian Gulf, East Africa, China, and elsewhere. A long-standing provision of the Government of India Act of 1858 limited the use of Indian revenues for military operations beyond India's external frontiers without the consent of Parliament, and the question of who should pay for Indian troops employed overseas had been contested before the war. Wartime financial arrangements between London and Delhi were negotiated under pressure and later became the subject of nationalist criticism, a theme taken up in §78.6.

Public reaction among politically organized Indians was, at first, largely supportive. The princes, who ruled about two-fifths of the subcontinent's territory under British paramountcy, rapidly offered troops, funds, and personal service. The Congress session at Madras in December 1914 expressed loyalty and appeal for cooperation. Many Moderates reasoned that Britain was fighting for principles of liberty and the rights of small nations, and that India's contribution would strengthen the argument for self-government. Gandhi, then in Britain after leaving South Africa, helped to organize an ambulance unit of Indians resident in London, an episode that belongs more fully to the account of his career in Chapter 79.

Muslim opinion required special attention. The Ottoman Empire entered the war on Germany's side at the end of October 1914, and the Ottoman sultan was widely recognized by Sunni Muslims as caliph. The British government feared that fighting him would alienate Muslim soldiers and politicians. Hardinge and other officials therefore offered public assurances that Britain would respect the holy places of Islam in the Ottoman territories its forces might enter. Politically active Muslims such as Maulana Muhammad Ali and Abul Kalam Azad, whose newspapers criticized British policy toward the Ottoman Empire, were treated with suspicion, and some would later be interned under wartime emergency powers. The Muslim League's leadership included a range of positions, and it would be a mistake to describe Muslim attitudes as uniform.

Other groups were openly hostile or skeptical. Revolutionary organizations saw the war as an opportunity. Indian migrants abroad, as Section 78.9 shows, had experienced racial exclusion within the Empire and had little reason to feel loyalty. Within India, many peasants and laborers were scarcely able to understand the causes of a European war, and for them the first sign of the conflict was often a recruiter's visit or a rise in prices. The statement that "India" supported the war therefore needs qualification at every step: it was the declared position of the government, the princes, and the leaders of the main constitutional organizations, but it was not a popular referendum.

Wartime loyalty could coexist with criticism of colonial government. Leaders who offered help did not abandon the demand for reform; they connected the two. The expectation that sacrifices would be rewarded with greater self-government became a recurring political theme, reflected in the speeches of Moderates, in the language of the Home Rule Leagues, and later in the Congress–League reform scheme. This expectation, and its eventual partial disappointment, helps to explain why the political atmosphere of 1918 and 1919 was so different from that of 1914.

Section 78.2

The Indian Army: Recruitment, Organization, and Imperial Military Hierarchy

The Indian Army of 1914 was a professional volunteer force organized around regiments recruited from specific communities, commanded by British officers, and placed under the Commander-in-Chief in India. Its Indian soldiers, called sepoys in the infantry and sowars in the cavalry, served under Viceroy's commissioned officers, such as subadars and jemadars, who held a rank above the sepoys but below even the most junior British commissioned officer. Indian officers in the combatant regiments of the Indian Army could not hold the King's commission at the start of the war; that barrier should not be generalized without qualification to every distinct imperial medical or ancillary service. This hierarchy, in which authority depended on race as much as on competence, shaped relationships throughout the conflict and was one of the grievances that postwar reformers would address.

Recruitment depended on the ideology of "martial races," whose development and consequences Chapter 73 examined. After 1857 and especially after the 1880s, British military administrators concluded that certain communities, notably Sikhs, Punjabi Muslims, Dogras, Jats, Pathans, Rajputs, Garhwalis, Gurkhas and other groups, were more naturally suited to soldiering than most Bengalis, Tamils, or other populations. The theory flattered the groups it favored, tied army service to land and village-based loyalty, and excluded large parts of the subcontinent from military employment. It was also self-reinforcing: the army recruited from the same districts and families, and military families developed their own traditions of service.

The consequence was a pronounced regional concentration. The Punjab, a comparatively small area of the subcontinent, supplied a very large proportion of the army's combatants, and the northwest frontier and Nepal were also important sources of recruits. Many soldiers came from small and middling cultivating families and from pastoral communities. Pay was modest, but it was cash income in a rural economy where cash was scarce, and service carried the possibility of a pension, land concessions in some districts, and social standing. These incentives, together with family tradition and the honor attached to service, help to explain why many men enlisted.

The army also relied heavily on noncombatants. Medical personnel, muleteers, camel drivers, boatmen, cooks, water-carriers, sweepers, and many kinds of laborers accompanied the fighting units. As the war lengthened, the Government of India raised additional labor and transport units, including labor corps sent to France and to the Middle East. Statistical calculations that include these workers produce larger totals than those that count only fighting men, which is why this chapter specifies what each number counts. The noncombatants were often drawn from groups outside the martial classes, and their wartime service is less visible in regimental histories.

Recruitment was officially voluntary, and India had no system of general conscription comparable to those adopted in Britain in 1916 or in other European states. But "voluntary" did not mean uniformly free. As manpower demands grew, officials in the Punjab and elsewhere set recruiting targets, relied on village headmen and landowners to produce recruits, and used the weight of local authority in ways that some contemporaries and later historians regarded as coercive. Michael O'Dwyer, the Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab, was especially associated with the effort to raise men from his province. Historians continue to discuss how far enlistment reflected consent, economic need, honor, or pressure, and the answer varied across districts and years.

The social composition of the army also changed. Wartime losses and the creation of new units led to the recruitment of classes previously passed over, and to the broadening of recruiting areas beyond the traditional heartlands. The issue of commissions also became an explicit wartime political demand. On August 20, 1917, Secretary of State Edwin Montagu told the House of Commons that the formal bar on King's commissions for Indians was to be removed and announced steps to commission nine Indian officers recommended for their field service. The announcement did not dismantle the racial command structure overnight: selection, training, regimental appointments, and the scope of future admissions remained contested. It marked a qualified change in policy, not equality of command throughout the army. The army that emerged in 1918 was larger and more diverse than that of 1914, but it remained a hierarchical colonial institution, and the contradiction between large Indian contributions and the denial of equal authority fed directly into the political debates later in the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Martial Races

The term describes a colonial theory, developed by British military officials chiefly after 1857, that certain Indian communities possessed inherited fighting qualities and were therefore better recruits than others. It guided the regional and communal composition of the Indian Army and was reinforced by official handbooks and recruiting practice.

The idea mattered to the First World War because it determined who was asked to fight and who was not, and it concentrated the burden of military service on particular districts. It should not be mistaken for an objective description of any community's character. Modern equivalents such as "natural warriors" repeat colonial assumptions, and the theory is better understood as an instrument of colonial control that shaped identities as well as recruitment (see Chapter 73).

Primary Source Window

British Indian Army Wartime Reports and Recruitment Records

Source type: Official statistical and administrative compilations · Creator: Government of India and the Army Department; summary volume published as India's Contribution to the Great War (Calcutta, 1923) · Date: compiled 1914–1923

Context: After the war the Government of India published a compilation of its military effort, drawing on wartime returns of recruitment by class and province, casualty records, and theater-by-theater summaries. Related files in the India Office Records in the British Library document recruiting directives and the changing class composition of the army. These are official records produced to account for, and to justify, India's contribution.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Official returns divide the men raised into combatant and noncombatant categories, break recruitment down by province and by class, and list casualties by theater. The compilation emphasizes loyalty, steady expansion of recruitment, and the range of theaters in which Indian units served.

Analysis: These records provide the framework for any quantitative discussion and show how the government classified people. For example, the often-cited 1,440,437 is a total of recruits to the Indian ranks recorded through December 1919, divided into 877,068 combatants and 563,369 noncombatants; it should not be substituted for the smaller number sent overseas or for the prewar standing establishment. They are indispensable for tracing the regional weighting of recruitment and for comparing theaters. They also present the war from the standpoint of administrators. Categories such as "class" reflect colonial assumptions; totals vary according to whether followers and reservists are counted; and the records say little about the reasons individual men enlisted or about the experience of recruiting pressure in villages. A historian must read the tables alongside letters, memoirs, and local records.

Questions for students:

  1. What kinds of information do these records provide, and how are people classified?
  2. Who produced them and for what purpose?
  3. What can historians learn about the regional concentration of recruitment?
  4. What cannot be established from official statistics alone?
Section 78.3

Indian Soldiers on the Western Front, 1914–1915

The Lahore and Meerut Divisions landed at Marseilles in September and early October 1914, and the Indian Corps went into the line in the La Bassée and Armentières sector in late October. The transfer was improvised. British commanders needed reinforcements immediately, but Indian units had been organized for different tasks, with different weapons, supplies, and expectations. Many soldiers reached the front after a long sea voyage and a short period of acclimatization, in cold and wet conditions entirely unlike the dry heat and open country of the Indian frontier.

The first months were costly. Indian units fought in the battles around La Bassée and Armentières in late 1914 and held difficult sectors, including at Givenchy and Festubert, during the winter. On October 31, 1914, Sepoy Khudadad Khan of the 129th Duke of Connaught's Own Baluchis became the first Indian recipient of the Victoria Cross for holding his machine-gun position after the rest of his team had been killed or wounded. Casualties, particularly among British officers who could not easily be replaced, left units short of leadership, a problem that was more serious in regiments in which British officers and Indian soldiers depended on each other for communication.

In March 1915 the Indian Corps played a major role in the battle of Neuve Chapelle, an attack in which the Meerut Division, including Garhwali and other units, suffered heavy losses in return for limited gains. In April and May the Lahore Division was committed during the Second Battle of Ypres, after German forces used chlorine gas, and Indian units were also engaged at Aubers Ridge and Festubert. Soldiers faced artillery, machine guns, and gas in conditions that no previous colonial campaign had prepared them for. Equipment problems, including shortages of rifles of the standard pattern, and dependence on British supply systems, compounded the difficulties.

Command relationships and racial attitudes also shaped the experience. British commanders sometimes regarded Indian soldiers as less suitable for sustained trench warfare in northern Europe and as needing a distinctive form of supervision. Indian soldiers observed that they were treated differently from white troops in matters of leave, medical treatment, and promotion, even when they fought in the same battles. There were also reports of cases of self-inflicted wounds and of men who deserted or refused orders under strain, and some were punished severely. These episodes should be placed within the general context of the extreme pressure of trench warfare rather than treated as evidence of an Indian lack of courage, a stereotype the sources themselves sometimes reproduce.

Wounded Indian soldiers were evacuated to hospitals in southern England, including facilities in and around Brighton, where officials arranged food, religious accommodations, and supervised visits in line with perceived caste and religious requirements. These hospitals attracted public attention, and the correspondence of patients provides some of the best evidence of how Indians interpreted Britain and the war. Their letters, which §78.5 examines, describe unfamiliar landscapes, machinery, and the scale of killing, and some express admiration for British hospital care while others reveal homesickness, fear, and anxiety about the family left behind.

In late 1915 the Indian infantry divisions were withdrawn from the Western Front and redeployed to other theaters, chiefly Mesopotamia and Egypt. The reasons included the heavy casualties, the difficulty of replacing Indian reinforcements, concerns about morale in winter conditions, and the strategic pull of the Middle East. The Indian cavalry and many support and labor units continued to serve in France, and in 1917 Indian labor corps were sent to the Western Front. The Indian infantry's departure should not be read as the end of Indian participation in Europe, but it marks a shift in the war's geography for South Asia.

Section 78.4

Mesopotamia, East Africa, Gallipoli, Egypt, and Palestine

The Mesopotamian campaign was the most Indian of the war's major theaters. Concerned to protect British oil interests and the Persian Gulf, the Government of India dispatched Indian Expeditionary Force D to the head of the Gulf in late 1914, and its troops occupied Basra on November 22, 1914. The objective was limited, but success encouraged an advance up the Tigris. Under the command of Major-General Charles Townshend, a British and Indian force moved toward Baghdad in 1915, and an engagement at Ctesiphon in late November was followed by a retreat to the town of Kut al-Amara, where Ottoman forces besieged it from December 1915.

The siege of Kut lasted until April 29, 1916, when the garrison of about 13,000 British and Indian troops surrendered after relief attempts failed. Disease, hunger, and exhaustion had weakened the defenders. The relief columns, advancing along a river system poorly served by transport, suffered heavy losses. Many of the captured men died in captivity or on marches into Anatolia, a fact that shaped how Indian and British publics remembered the campaign. The surrender damaged British prestige, particularly in the Middle East, and it had political effects in India as well because the army's Indian soldiers had paid much of the cost of the failure.

The deeper causes lay in logistics and administration. The expedition had been launched with inadequate river transport, insufficient medical facilities, and poor planning for the climate and terrain. Wounded men were carried back in crowded boats, often with limited medical care, and the shortage of hospital ships and of medical staff was acute. A parliamentary commission of inquiry, the Mesopotamia Commission, investigated these failures and published a severe report in 1917, and the ensuing political crisis contributed to the resignation of Austen Chamberlain as Secretary of State for India in July 1917, a change that brought Edwin Montagu to the India Office (§78.17). Administrative reforms followed, and under General Frederick Maude the campaign was reorganized, with improved supply and medical arrangements. British and Indian forces captured Baghdad on March 11, 1917.

Other theaters involved Indian soldiers in different ways. In East Africa, Indian units took part in the failed British landing at Tanga in November 1914 and in the long, disease-ridden campaign against German forces under Lettow-Vorbeck, where transport, supply, and climate again determined outcomes. Indian troops and mountain batteries, including Gurkha units, fought at Gallipoli in 1915, and Indian formations defended the Suez Canal against Ottoman attack in early 1915. In Palestine the Indian cavalry and infantry formed an important element of General Allenby's army in 1917 and 1918, and in September 1918, during the advance culminating in the battle of Megiddo, Indian cavalry units, including the Jodhpur and Mysore Lancers, took part in the capture of Haifa.

The comparison among theaters is instructive. On the Western Front, Indian soldiers served in an industrial war within a European alliance and under British command and supply. In Mesopotamia they formed the bulk of a force whose success or failure depended on Indian administrative capacity, and the shortcomings of the Government of India became publicly visible. In East Africa and Palestine, they served in climates and terrains that tested health and transport. In each case Indian men and followers bore most of the physical risk, while command, strategy, and interpretation of the war remained largely British.

These campaigns also show why the war cannot be understood as an exclusively European conflict. For South Asians, it was fought along the Tigris, in the East African bush, in the Sinai, and in the Suez Canal, and it was connected to the Indian Ocean and Middle East in ways that transformed regional politics. It also placed Muslim soldiers in conflict with the Ottoman Empire, which created tension, and it reinforced the strategic importance of the Persian Gulf for the Government of India, a link that would endure after the war.

Section 78.5

Soldiers' Letters, Families, Hospitals, and the Experience of War

Soldiers' correspondence is the most intimate evidence of how South Asians experienced the war, and also the most carefully mediated. Many sepoys were literate in no language, or only partly so, and relied on regimental scribes, comrades, or letter-writers to compose or read their letters. Letters were written in Urdu, Hindi, Gurmukhi (Punjabi), Gurkhali, and other languages, then dispatched through the military postal system. Dictation shaped what could be said: a man who spoke through a scribe could not always be sure of privacy, and conventional greetings, blessings, and formulas of respect often occupied much of a letter's space.

The central institution for historians is the censorship system. From late 1914 the Government of India and the British authorities in France monitored the mails of Indian troops. Officials read letters in the original languages, assessed morale, and prepared periodic summaries that included extracts in English translation. The reports of the Censor of Indian Mails in France, catalogued in the British Library's India Office Records, preserve thousands of such extracts. David Omissi's edition of selected letters made this archive widely accessible. The censor's purpose was surveillance and intelligence, not historical preservation, and letters considered seditious or damaging to morale could be withheld.

This has fundamental consequences for interpretation. The surviving extracts are not an unfiltered archive of soldiers' thoughts. They were selected by officers with institutional aims, translated into English with all the distortions that translation brings, and sometimes reproduced in part. Soldiers also knew, or suspected, that their letters were read, and they sometimes used euphemism, coded phrases, or conventional language to evade censorship or to express criticism indirectly. Silence in the archive may mean agreement, self-censorship, or the censor's decision to omit. Historians must therefore treat the letters as testimony shaped by a system of control, not as transparent windows into private feeling.

Within these limits, the letters reveal a great deal. Soldiers wrote of the cold and mud, the noise of artillery, the unfamiliar machinery of war, and the sight of enormous casualties. They described European cities, farms, and people with curiosity and sometimes with surprise at encountering white women and at what they saw as the apparent equality with which they were treated by civilians. They also wrote about comradeship, fear, religion, and anxiety about death in a foreign land, and about how their families were managing at home. Some letters conveyed enthusiasm for the war or pride in service, others expressed fatalism, and a few expressed bitterness or an inclination to resist. No single attitude predominates.

Families were deeply involved. Soldiers sent part of their pay home through allotments and money orders, and families used these remittances for subsistence, debt repayment, marriage expenses, and agricultural investment. Wives, parents, and village elders sometimes dictated replies pleading for news, requesting leave, or reporting births, illnesses, and deaths. The difficulty of communication across continents, and the long delays between letters, created chronic anxiety. For wounded men, hospital records and letters show what care was available. Hospitals in England and France, and hospital ships and base hospitals in the Middle East, treated large numbers of Indian patients, though the quality of medical provision varied sharply between theaters, as the Mesopotamian experience demonstrated.

Religious observance and caste rules created practical problems for the authorities and for the men themselves. Hospital administrators in Britain arranged separate kitchens, supplies, and ritual spaces, and officials debated how to treat the cremation of Hindu and Sikh soldiers and the burial of Muslims. These arrangements were carried out with varying success and attracted public interest in Britain, which sometimes treated the soldiers as exotic subjects. For the soldiers themselves, the experience of being served by white nurses and of living in a European country modified assumptions about racial hierarchy and left an ambiguous legacy, a theme that recurs in postwar political arguments about rights and treatment within the Empire.

Section 78.6

War Finance, Inflation, Industry, and the Indian Economy

The war's financial burden fell heavily on the Government of India. It paid for the maintenance and equipment of troops, shipped supplies and manufactured goods for imperial use, and covered part of the costs of expeditions beyond India. Revenue came from taxes, from borrowing, and from currency and exchange measures. Customs duties were raised during the war, including a significant increase in import duties on many goods in 1916 and 1917, and income tax and a super-tax on higher incomes were increased. The government floated war loans in India, and in 1917 it announced an extraordinary contribution of about £100 million to Britain's war costs, a large sum that nationalist critics saw as a burden placed on a poor country without its representatives' consent.

Currency and exchange policy also had consequences. Wartime movements in the price of silver, the metal behind the rupee, and disruptions to trade and shipping created difficulties for the government's management of the currency. The authorities increased the issue of paper currency, and the value of the rupee against sterling was adjusted. These technical measures contributed to uncertainty and, together with the large military expenditure and the withdrawal of goods for imperial use, to price rises. The financial relationship between London and Delhi, and in particular the question of how much India should pay, remained controversial and became part of the nationalist critique of imperial economics inherited from the "drain" arguments of Dadabhai Naoroji and others (Chapter 76).

For some industries, the war created opportunities. The cotton mills of Bombay and Ahmedabad, jute mills near Calcutta, and the steel works of the Tata Iron and Steel Company at Jamshedpur found strong demand, particularly for military goods such as jute sandbags, cloth, and steel rails. The disruption of imports from Britain and other belligerent countries stimulated local production in some sectors. Indian business groups, many of them already politically attentive, expanded profits, and the government appointed an Indian Industrial Commission in 1916 to consider how industry might be developed and supported. The experience encouraged leading industrialists to argue for tariff protection and greater state support after the war.

These opportunities were limited by structural constraints. India lacked a developed capital-goods sector, and wartime shortages of machinery and shipping restricted expansion. Industrial growth remained concentrated in a few regions and sectors, and most of the population continued to depend on agriculture. The wartime advantage enjoyed by some manufacturers was partly a short-term effect of disrupted imports and military demand, not a lasting change in the economy's structure, and British commercial and government interests continued to shape trade policy. Historians therefore distinguish between genuine industrial stimulation and the unevenness and fragility of that stimulation.

Inflation was the war's most widespread economic burden. Prices of food grains, cloth, kerosene, and many other necessities rose substantially, particularly from 1916 and 1917 onward, while shipping shortages, military requisitions, and exports reduced the supply available in local markets. Wages rose in some urban and industrial occupations but generally not enough to keep pace with prices, and wage earners, small cultivators dependent on purchased goods, and fixed-income households were hurt. Cultivators who sold grain could sometimes benefit from higher prices, though debts, rents, and the costs of cloth and other purchases limited the gain. Industrial employers, workers, cultivators, and consumers therefore experienced the same economy very differently, a point that helps explain the growth of labor unrest after the war (Chapter 80).

The economic effects reached politics in several ways. Nationalists criticized the war contribution and the absence of Indian control over fiscal policy. Business leaders sought tariff protection and a bigger voice in economic policy. Urban consumers and wage earners grew increasingly restive as prices rose. And in the countryside, the combination of recruitment, rising costs, and, by 1918, food shortages and disease created discontent that could be turned toward political organization. The wartime economy thus linked the experience of ordinary households to the arguments of political leaders.

Section 78.7

Villages, Recruitment Campaigns, Women, and Princely-State Contributions

The rural Punjab bore a particularly heavy share of the recruitment burden. Districts that had long supplied soldiers were asked to produce more, and provincial officials organized recruiting through local networks: district officers, landed notables, village headmen, retired soldiers, and military pensioners. Authorities offered pay, pensions, and in some places land grants or promises of favor, while social and official pressure could be applied to families and communities. Quotas and competition among districts influenced local practice, and by 1917 and 1918 the intensifying demand led to complaints, in the sources and in later scholarship, about heavy-handed recruiting. Historians differ on how widespread coercion was, and local variation was significant.

The effect on village life was considerable. When adult men enlisted or were sent to labor corps, their households lost agricultural labor, sometimes at critical seasons. Families made decisions about which sons would serve, which would stay on the land, and how remittances would be used. Landholding families with a tradition of service often saw military income as complementary to farming, and pensions and enhanced status could improve their position. Poorer households, and those lacking male labor, faced greater difficulty. Differences between irrigated canal colonies, where farms were larger and more commercial, and barani (rain-fed) areas, where cultivation was more precarious and military service especially important, shaped patterns of recruitment and its effects.

Women's responsibilities expanded when men departed. Wives, mothers, and daughters managed fields, livestock, and household finances, often within extended families. They dealt with officials over allotments and pensions, negotiated with moneylenders, and bore the emotional burden of uncertainty. The sources, which are predominantly official or male-authored, record women's experience indirectly, through remittance and pension files, petitions, and later oral testimony. A few women, such as nurses and some members of philanthropic organizations, were directly involved in war work, but for the great majority the war was lived at home. The separation of spouses, the deaths of sons, and the shift in household authority were important social consequences.

The princely states made substantial contributions. Rulers offered personal military service, Imperial Service Troops (state forces under British inspection), hospital ships, ambulances, funds, horses, and supplies. Some princes, such as the Maharaja of Bikaner and the Maharaja Pratap Singh of Idar and Jodhpur, served with imperial forces, and princely cavalry regiments fought in Egypt and Palestine. The princes' donations to war funds were publicized widely and strengthened their political standing with the British government. Their wartime loyalty also reinforced the imperial strategy of treating the princely order as a conservative partner of the Raj, a policy examined in Chapter 71.

Public declarations of loyalty should be compared with the pressures borne by local populations. A ruler's donation was a political act that enhanced his relationship with the paramount power, and in some states the cost was ultimately met by subjects through taxation and local requisitions. Villagers experienced recruitment, prices, and the shortage of goods in ways that were not mirrored in speeches. At the same time, military service could bring tangible benefits to some families, and the social prestige of service was real. The history of recruitment therefore requires attention to both coercion and aspiration.

Section 78.8

South Asia beyond British India: Nepal, Ceylon, Burma, and Wartime Rule

The Kingdom of Nepal, never formally colonized, was ruled by the hereditary Rana prime ministers, of whom Chandra Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana held office in 1914. At the outbreak of war he placed Nepal's resources at Britain's disposal. Gurkha regiments of the Indian Army, already recruited in Nepal under arrangements dating from the nineteenth century, expanded, and Nepal permitted recruitment on a much larger scale, with estimates that well over 100,000 Gurkhas served in various capacities and that a significant number were killed or wounded. Nepal also sent troops of its own to garrison parts of northern India, freeing Indian units for overseas service, and provided financial gifts. The Rana government's motives combined strategic calculation, dynastic security, and the aim of securing British support; after the war Britain recognized Nepal's independence in the Treaty of 1923, a development Chapter 91 returns to.

Burma was administered as a province of British India until 1937, and its wartime role was largely economic and logistical. The delta's rice economy supplied exports at a time when food supplies mattered to the imperial war effort, and transport systems, including the Irrawaddy river network, carried goods and labor. Burma was not a major recruiting ground for the Indian Army, which drew on a different set of communities, though labor units and military police drew on Burma's population and Indian migrants there. Political life, centered on organizations such as the Young Men's Buddhist Association, was beginning to develop its own grievances about colonial administration, and wartime conditions contributed to these concerns. The history of Burma is not simply an extension of Indian politics and requires separate attention.

Ceylon, a Crown colony distinct from British India, experienced one of the war's most serious internal crises. In late May 1915, disputes over the conduct of Muslim and Buddhist religious processions near Kandy, in the context of competition between Sinhalese and Muslim (Moor) traders and of Buddhist revivalist activity, led to violence. Over the following days, riots spread through parts of the Central and other provinces, directed chiefly against Muslim communities, whose shops and homes were attacked and looted. The disturbances had local religious, social, and commercial causes, and the European war does not explain them, although wartime anxiety and the departure of some British forces may have influenced the colonial authorities' response.

The governor, Sir Robert Chalmers, declared martial law in June 1915, and military commanders, acting with extensive powers, suppressed the disorder with considerable force. They also targeted prominent Sinhalese figures, including leaders of the temperance and Buddhist revival movements, whom the authorities suspected of fomenting unrest or disloyalty. Sinhalese political and professional figures such as the Senanayake brothers and D. B. Jayatilaka were imprisoned, and the Buddhist reformer Anagarika Dharmapala was confined in India. Troops and police shot civilians and used summary punishments, and the death toll in the repression, by some accounts, far exceeded that in the original violence. Martial law was lifted in the late summer, and the affair prompted an inquiry, the replacement of the governor, and a significant shift in Ceylonese politics.

It is important to separate the two phases. The riots were violence against Muslim communities, with attacks on persons and property. The colonial government's response was a separate set of actions, much of which was directed at Sinhalese political figures and the Buddhist majority, and which later gave Sinhalese nationalists a grievance against British rule. The Sri Lanka National Archives and specialist scholarship, including the work of K. M. de Silva and Michael Roberts, show how these elements fit together. Politicians such as Ponnambalam Ramanathan, who traveled to London to challenge the repression, helped to publicize what had occurred. The affair contributed to the development of the Ceylon National Congress in 1919 and to later constitutional change, themes treated in Chapter 91.

For the Maldives and Bhutan, the war's effects were slight, though both were integrated into the wider imperial strategic and commercial order. The importance of these varied cases is that the chapter's central question, how war altered the relationship between subjects and imperial power, had different answers in different places. In Nepal, the war reinforced a partnership between a ruling house and the British. In Ceylon, martial law created a political grievance. In Burma, war added to a developing set of tensions. Treating these regions alongside the better-known history of British India prevents the history of the war in South Asia from being reduced to its Indian constitutional politics.

Section 78.9

The Komagata Maru Incident and the Politics of Imperial Citizenship

The story of the Komagata Maru begins before the war, with the restrictions that Canada placed on Asian immigration. South Asian migrants, most of them Punjabi Sikhs, had begun arriving in British Columbia in the early twentieth century, seeking work in lumber mills, railways, and agriculture. They were British subjects, traveling within the Empire, but Canadian authorities and many white residents regarded them as undesirable. In 1908 the Canadian government adopted a "continuous journey" requirement, in effect that immigrants must arrive by uninterrupted passage from their country of origin. Since no direct steamship route then ran from India to Canada, the rule effectively excluded most Indian migrants while avoiding any explicit mention of race.

In 1914 Gurdit Singh, a Punjabi businessman living in Singapore and Hong Kong, chartered a Japanese steamer, the Komagata Maru, to carry Indian passengers from Hong Kong toward Vancouver and contest the racial exclusions embodied in Canadian immigration rules. The ship left Hong Kong in April 1914, traveled by way of East Asian ports including Japan, and reached Vancouver on May 23 carrying 376 passengers, most of them Sikhs, with a smaller number of Muslims and Hindus. It had not sailed nonstop from the passengers' country of origin, India, as Canada's continuous-journey regulation required; the rule had been designed for a route that was effectively unavailable to those travelers. Canadian immigration officials refused to let most of them land. Only a small number, those who could establish prior Canadian residence, were admitted. The remaining passengers were confined to the ship in Burrard Inlet for two months while legal challenges and political negotiations proceeded.

South Asian residents of Vancouver organized a Shore Committee, raised money for legal fees and supplies, and held meetings in support of the passengers. Community leaders and sympathetic lawyers challenged the exclusion in court, and the Court of Appeal of British Columbia eventually upheld the government's position. In July 1914 an effort to force the ship out of the harbor, involving a Canadian vessel and armed police, was resisted by the passengers. Canadian naval involvement, in the form of a warship, ultimately compelled the Komagata Maru to depart on July 23, 1914, as war was approaching. The ship's return voyage coincided with the outbreak of the war in Europe.

The vessel eventually reached Budge Budge, near Calcutta, on September 29, 1914. The Government of India, apprehensive about radical agitation and by then wary of returning migrants from North America, had issued new regulations, the Ingress into India Ordinance of 1914, to control the movement of such people. Authorities attempted to detain the passengers and to send them by train to the Punjab. Many resisted, a dispute with police and soldiers developed, and shots were fired. About twenty people were killed, including passengers and others, and many were arrested or went into hiding; Gurdit Singh escaped and remained a fugitive for years. The incident, widely reported, became a symbol of official brutality.

Its significance lies in the connection it exposed between imperial citizenship and race. The passengers were British subjects, but the Empire's laws treated them as inferior and excludable, and the Government of India treated their return as a security threat. The affair strengthened anticolonial feeling among migrants and in the Punjab, where the incident was associated with the sufferings of Sikh families. It also formed a bridge between the diaspora's grievances and revolutionary politics. The incident's origins lay in prewar racial policy, whereas its consequences unfolded during the war, an important distinction. Chapter 75 examined the broader history of Indian migration and the diaspora, and §78.10 shows how that experience fed into the Ghadar movement.

Section 78.10

The Ghadar Movement: Origins, Organization, and the Attempted Uprising of 1915

The Ghadar movement arose among Indian migrants on the Pacific coast of North America, mainly Punjabi laborers in British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and California, together with students and intellectuals. In 1913 activists founded the Hindustan Association of the Pacific Coast, commonly known through its newspaper as the Ghadar (meaning "revolt" or "mutiny") Party. The paper, published first from San Francisco in Urdu and Gurmukhi and later in other languages, circulated widely among migrants and abroad. Sohan Singh Bhakna was its first president, Lala Har Dayal, a former Oxford scholar and nationalist intellectual, a prominent organizer and propagandist, and Kartar Singh Sarabha a young activist who became one of its best-known figures.

The movement's ideology combined anticolonial nationalism, anger at racial discrimination, and a commitment to armed rebellion. Its publications, including poems and songs in Punjabi and Urdu, denounced British rule, recalled the Rebellion of 1857, and called on migrants to return and incite the army to revolt. While most of its members were Punjabi Sikhs, the party sought to be cross-religious, and its leadership and rhetoric included Hindus and Muslims. It was thus a transnational movement, rooted in the experience of discrimination overseas and connected to politics in India, not merely a local Punjabi insurrection.

The outbreak of war in August 1914 gave the movement its opportunity. Ghadar leaders issued calls for return and revolution, and thousands of migrants, perhaps several thousand, traveled back to India via the Far East in the following months, some inspired by the movement, some because of the war and economic conditions, and some motivated by the Komagata Maru's fate. The Government of India, alerted by intelligence, used the Ingress into India Ordinance to screen and intern returnees, and many were arrested at the ports or restricted to their villages. Those who evaded detention made contact with sympathetic soldiers and revolutionaries in the Punjab and with figures such as Rash Behari Bose, an experienced Bengali revolutionary.

The plan for an uprising centered on the military garrisons of the Punjab. Conspirators hoped that soldiers in cantonments, including Lahore, Ferozepore, and Rawalpindi, would mutiny on a fixed date, originally in late February 1915, and that the rebellion would spread. But informants had penetrated the network, and the government learned of the plot. When the planned date was advanced, the authorities moved against units and individuals, arrested conspirators, and disarmed or transferred suspect regiments. The attempted uprising collapsed with little actual fighting. The failure reflected not only informers but the limited readiness of the army, most of whose soldiers did not join.

Prosecutions followed. Special tribunals created under wartime powers tried large numbers of defendants in a series of Lahore Conspiracy Cases, and many were sentenced to death, transportation, or imprisonment. Kartar Singh Sarabha, still very young, was hanged in November 1915 together with other leading figures, and he became a heroic symbol in later Punjabi nationalist memory. Hundreds were convicted overall, and others were interned or restricted. In the United States, a separate trial in San Francisco in 1917 and 1918 prosecuted Indian activists and Germans under American neutrality laws. These proceedings are an important source, though they should be read critically because prosecution evidence reflects official claims.

The movement's importance lies in several areas. It showed that colonial exclusion abroad could produce revolutionary politics, that anticolonial activity was transnational, and that the government considered the army's loyalty a critical issue. It also had limits: its operational capacity was small, its plans were penetrated, and its influence within the army was restricted. Ghadar should not be confused with the Home Rule Leagues, which sought self-government through open constitutional agitation. The contrast between revolutionary and constitutional strategies is a recurring theme of the chapter.

Section 78.11

The Hindu–German Conspiracy, Berlin Networks, and Afghanistan

Revolutionary activity during the war was linked to the strategic interests of Britain's enemies. In Berlin, Indian exiles, including Virendranath Chattopadhyaya and Champakaraman Pillai, formed the Indian Independence Committee (also called the Berlin India Committee) with the support of the German Foreign Office. Germany saw Indian unrest as a way to divert British resources and disrupt the empire, and it financed propaganda, supported attempts to contact prisoners of war, and explored the possibility of supplying arms. The Ottoman Empire, having entered the war, also had an interest in anticolonial movements across Muslim regions and in the northern frontier of India.

Efforts to obtain weapons illustrate the limits of the networks. German agents in the United States, working with Ghadar activists, attempted in 1915 to ship arms to India, including through an elaborate scheme involving the vessels Annie Larsen and Maverick that failed because of poor coordination and surveillance. British intelligence, with American cooperation, monitored these activities, and the later San Francisco "Hindu–German Conspiracy" trial prosecuted both Germans and Indians. The label reflects the prosecution's framing; it does not mean that every Indian revolutionary was a German agent or that the Indian networks were controlled from Berlin. Indian activists pursued their own goals and used German assistance opportunistically, while Germany pursued its strategic interests.

Afghanistan occupied a special place in these plans. The country's ruler, Amir Habibullah Khan, was formally neutral, but his state lay on India's northwestern frontier and had a Muslim population sensitive to the Ottoman caliphate. A German-Turkish mission, led by Oskar von Niedermayer and Werner Otto von Hentig, reached Kabul in 1915, accompanied by Indian revolutionaries. In December 1915, Raja Mahendra Pratap, a prince of Hathras with long experience of nationalist politics, was proclaimed president of a Provisional Government of India in Kabul, with Maulana Barkatullah as prime minister. The government claimed to represent a free India and sought recognition from Afghanistan and other powers.

Another network operated among Muslim religious scholars and activists. Maulana Mahmud Hasan of the Deoband seminary and his associate Ubaidullah Sindhi planned to organize a movement involving Afghanistan and the frontier tribes. In 1916 British intelligence intercepted messages written on silk cloth, which led to the discovery of what became known as the "Silk Letters Conspiracy." The affair led to the arrest of Mahmud Hasan in the Hijaz and his internment in Malta, and it shaped official suspicions of Muslim activism for years to come. It also demonstrates that revolutionary networks were not confined to a single community.

The results were limited. Habibullah Khan, who was dependent on a British subsidy and cautious about the risks, kept Afghanistan neutral and did not allow the mission to launch a major attack on India. The provisional government remained a symbolic body without territory or army. British authorities, helped by intelligence and by diplomatic pressure, contained the threat. The significance of the Kabul mission was therefore chiefly political: it demonstrated that Indian nationalists could organize at an international level, claimed sovereign status, and caused anxiety among British officials. Historians distinguish between the revolutionaries' aspirations, German and Ottoman diplomatic interests, and the networks' demonstrated operational capacity, which was slight.

The legacy of these networks was nevertheless significant. Some participants, including Mahendra Pratap and Barkatullah, later pursued contacts with revolutionary Russia and other states. The wartime links between exiled Indians, German and Ottoman officials, and Afghan politics left marks on frontier policy, and Britain's fears of such conspiracies informed its legislative response in India, a subject treated in §78.12.

Section 78.12

The Singapore Mutiny, the Defence of India Act, and Wartime Surveillance

On February 15, 1915, soldiers of the 5th Light Infantry, an Indian regiment stationed in Singapore, mutinied. The regiment, largely composed of Muslims from northern India, had been stationed there for some time, and it was under suspicion and strain: rumors circulated that it would be sent to fight the Ottoman Empire, which many Muslim soldiers regarded with religious sympathy. Some of the men also had grievances over discipline and promotion. The mutineers killed British officers and civilians, and the disturbance continued for several days before being suppressed with the help of ships and troops from Britain's allies, including Japanese and Russian forces. About forty-seven mutineers were executed, and others were transported or imprisoned.

Explanations of the mutiny vary. Military grievances and personal conflicts with officers probably contributed. The rumor of service against the Ottoman Empire, in the context of Muslim religious feeling and the caliphate, was clearly important. Ghadar and other revolutionary propaganda had circulated in Singapore, and some contact between agitators and soldiers is documented, though its role is debated. Official inquiries tended to emphasize conspiratorial influence, but later historians have stressed the interaction of local grievances, rumor, religious sentiment, and revolutionary contacts, and caution against attributing the revolt to a single cause. It remains an example of how the loyalty of Indian troops could not be taken for granted.

The government's response to this and other perceived threats was legislative. The Defence of India (Criminal Law Amendment) Act, passed in March 1915 and commonly called the Defence of India Act, provided the executive with extensive powers during the emergency. It authorized special tribunals to try offenses connected with the war and public safety, with limited rights of appeal, and permitted the restriction or internment of individuals without ordinary trial under the Defence of India Regulations. Censorship of the press and correspondence tightened, and the government used the powers against revolutionaries, returned migrants, and political activists. The Ali brothers and Abul Kalam Azad were among Muslim journalists affected, and numerous others were interned or externed from particular provinces.

Not all of those targeted were conspirators. Colonial officials sometimes interpreted political activism, journalism, and religious networks as part of a single plot, and the broad definitions in emergency regulations allowed arrests on suspicion. At the same time, real conspiracies existed, as the Ghadar uprising attempt and the Kabul mission demonstrate. The challenge for historians is to distinguish proven plots from allegations, and to recognize how emergency powers expanded the reach of the state into ordinary political life. The distinction matters for assessing how fairly the legislation was applied and how far it fostered resentment.

The wartime emergency powers changed the relationship between the colonial government and political organizations. Constitutional politicians who supported the war found their activities constrained by censorship and regulations, and the knowledge that detention without trial was possible shaped political tactics. When the war ended, the government sought to retain some of these powers, a decision that led to a committee under Justice Sidney Rowlatt, appointed in December 1917, and to the legislation of 1919. The Rowlatt Acts and the movement against them are introduced here only as consequences of wartime repression; their detailed treatment belongs to Chapter 79.

Section 78.13

Wartime Congress Politics: Loyalty, Disappointment, and Renewed Organization

The Indian National Congress entered the war in a weakened and divided condition. Since the Surat split of 1907, Moderates had dominated the organization, and its annual sessions focused on constitutional petitioning. At its Madras session in December 1914, the Congress expressed loyalty to the Crown and support for the war effort, and many of its leaders, such as Surendranath Banerjea and Pherozeshah Mehta, argued that India should demonstrate its worthiness for self-government by cooperating. The calculation was political: leaders believed that Britain, defending democratic principles and dependent on Indian resources, would be obliged to reward India's contribution.

The political landscape changed with the loss of senior leaders. Gopal Krishna Gokhale, the most respected Moderate and a mentor to younger politicians, died in February 1915, and Pherozeshah Mehta died that November. Their deaths removed figures who had held the Moderate camp together and eased the way for a reconsideration of Congress's strategy. At the same time, Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in January 1915, and he spent his first period at home observing the country and working with Gokhale's Servants of India Society; his later influence belongs to Chapter 79 and is mentioned here only as context.

Tilak's release in June 1914 reintroduced a leader of the more assertive nationalist tendency. He supported the war effort in public statements, which reassured authorities, while seeking to rebuild his political base. Negotiations with Moderates over his readmission to Congress, delayed by distrust on both sides, led to his return to the organization's structures at the Lucknow session of 1916. The reconciliation was driven by the shared wish to present a united front on constitutional demands, and by the willingness of Moderates to cooperate in new circumstances. Their reunion also reflected the increasing salience of questions about representation and self-government raised by the war.

Wartime restrictions limited political activity. The Defence of India Regulations, press censorship, and the surveillance of public meetings meant that organizing required care, and the Congress continued to meet annually while avoiding open confrontation. The rhetoric of the war, which emphasized the defense of liberty and the rights of nations, provided arguments that Indian leaders used. They asked why the principles for which Britain claimed to be fighting should not apply to India, and they stressed that a country supplying hundreds of thousands of men and substantial sums deserved a voice in its government. The argument that sacrifice should be rewarded became central to nationalist rhetoric.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a Bombay lawyer who belonged to both the Congress and, since 1913, the Muslim League, became a prominent advocate of cooperation across organizations. He worked with Moderates and with Tilak, assisted in building a joint platform, and participated in the preparations for the Lucknow sessions. Other leaders, including the League's Wazir Hasan and the Raja of Mahmudabad, were also involved. This convergence, in which Congress and League leaders sought to agree on constitutional demands, was an important wartime development and the subject of §78.16.

Historical Debate

Was Wartime Imperial Loyalty a Strategy, a Conviction, or a Misjudgment?

The question: When Congress leaders, princes, and other political figures declared loyalty to Britain in 1914, did they reflect genuine commitment to the imperial cause, a calculated bargain for constitutional rewards, or a reading of British intentions that proved mistaken?

Interpretation A — Loyalty as political bargaining: Leaders offered cooperation because they expected concessions. The language of loyalty was combined, even in 1914, with references to India's claim to self-government, and by 1916 the same leaders were pressing publicly for reform. On this view, loyalty was a strategy that carried a price, and the later disappointment with the pace of reform shows that its assumptions were conditional.
Interpretation B — Loyalty as sincere but limited: Many Moderates did believe in the connection with Britain and in gradual constitutional progress within the Empire, and their support was not purely tactical. For these leaders, the war was a test of the imperial relationship, and wartime service was a matter of principle as well as calculation. The shift toward more assertive demands reflected changing circumstances, not insincerity.

Evidence: Historians draw on Congress resolutions, speeches, private correspondence, newspapers, and the later political trajectories of individuals. They also consider the very different attitudes of groups outside the constitutional mainstream, such as revolutionaries and some Muslim politicians, who did not share this loyalty.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the origins of postwar mass nationalism. If loyalty was a bargain, then the postwar disillusionment follows logically from broken expectations. If it was sincere, the change in political mood was a more profound transformation. Most scholars see a combination, with variation among individuals and over time.

Organizational continuity also had a practical meaning between annual sessions. Provincial associations maintained correspondence, newspapers circulated interpretations of British promises, and local leaders had to decide how to convert an abstract demand for responsible government into campaigns that could be explained to supporters. The difference between a Congress resolution and a movement capable of maintaining local committees was especially significant under wartime emergency rules. A campaign might have an impressive public meeting and still lack the subscriptions, volunteers, and regional infrastructure needed for sustained influence. The Home Rule Leagues addressed this institutional weakness, but they did not eliminate it; their different geographical bases and varied political constituencies were part of the story. These changes help explain why the Lucknow Pact was more than an exchange of speeches. It represented negotiations among organizations that had regained the capacity to bargain, discipline internal disagreement, and agree on at least some constitutional objectives. Whether such elite negotiations could claim to represent people outside their membership remained an unresolved question.

Section 78.14

Bal Gangadhar Tilak and the Indian Home Rule League, 1916

Bal Gangadhar Tilak returned to public life in 1914 after six years in Mandalay. He had been imprisoned following the 1908 sedition conviction connected with his writings in the newspaper Kesari, and the experience made him a symbol of resistance to the colonial state. On his release he was cautious, but he soon resumed political work, and by early 1916 he had concluded that the moment was right to organize a campaign for self-government. His approach differed from the boycott-centered tactics associated with the Swadeshi period (Chapter 77): he emphasized constitutional agitation, political education, and organization.

The Indian Home Rule League, which Tilak established in April 1916 at a conference in Belgaum, took its name from the Irish campaign for self-government within the Empire. Its structure was more formal than the loose networks of the earlier period. It organized branches in towns and districts, particularly in Maharashtra, the Central Provinces, and Karnataka, and its leaders delivered public lectures, distributed pamphlets, and held meetings. Tilak's speeches and the newspapers Kesari (in Marathi) and Mahratta (in English) carried its message. The League's formal demand was self-government within the Empire, not independence, a phrase that fitted legal limits and the political atmosphere.

The use of Indian languages was crucial. Political communication through vernacular newspapers, speeches, and songs allowed the League to reach audiences who did not read English, including small-town professionals, traders, and some cultivators. This was not a new technique, but it was applied systematically to a program of self-government and political education. The League sought to explain what Home Rule meant in terms of everyday administration, taxation, and representation, and to build a sense of participation in constitutional demands. Tilak's own reputation as a leader associated with the Marathi-speaking public and with earlier patriotic festivals gave the League a regional base.

The authorities were wary. The provincial government of Bombay and the Government of India monitored the League and in 1916 initiated proceedings against Tilak for sedition, in connection with his speeches; the case was ultimately dismissed on appeal, but he was subjected to a bond and was barred for a time from entering the Punjab and Delhi. The experience underlines the constrained conditions in which constitutional agitation took place during the emergency. Tilak's strategy combined a firm demand with careful adherence to legal limits, as he knew that the authorities were ready to act.

Assessing the League's reach requires caution. Its membership, in the tens of thousands at most, remained modest relative to the population of the subcontinent, and its strength was concentrated in specific regions. It was largely a movement of the urban and small-town educated, and of some landholding and commercial groups, not a mass peasant movement. Its limitations included regional concentration, the fear of repression, and rivalry with other organizations. Yet its achievement was real: it created organizational networks, trained activists, and popularized the idea of self-government as a practical political demand. Its relationship with Besant's league, treated next, was one of cooperation and some rivalry.

Section 78.15

Annie Besant and the All-India Home Rule Movement

Annie Besant, born in London in 1847, had a long career as a social reformer, freethinker, and Theosophist before settling in India, where she became president of the Theosophical Society in 1907 and based herself at Adyar in Madras. She was closely associated with educational work and the Central Hindu College in Benares, and in 1914 she launched the weekly Commonweal and took over a Madras daily that she renamed New India, newspapers that became vehicles for her political views. By 1915 she had begun to call for self-government for India, and in September 1916 she established the All-India Home Rule League, with its main base in Madras and the south, but with branches in other regions.

Besant's league complemented Tilak's. His organization concentrated on western and central India, while hers worked in Madras, parts of the south, Bombay, and elsewhere through local branches, including in places where Tilak was less well known. The two leagues divided the country, in effect, and cooperated at the national level, though differences of style and tension over leadership persisted. Besant's organization was more explicitly linked to a program of political education, and her speeches and publications explained the case for self-government and criticized official policies. She was a powerful speaker and journalist, and her British background gave her a distinctive position as an advocate of Indian self-government.

The League's supporters included students, lawyers, teachers, journalists, women, and regional organizers. Women's participation was notable: Besant encouraged women to take part in public life, and women such as Sarojini Naidu were drawn into political activity. Local branches arranged lectures and discussions, circulated pamphlets, and organized meetings. The league's use of newspapers and short political tracts made its arguments accessible, and Home Rule became a familiar slogan in many towns.

The government's response transformed the movement. In June 1917 the Governor of Madras, Lord Pentland, ordered the internment of Besant and two associates, B. P. Wadia and George Arundale, in a hill station. The action, taken under the Defence of India Regulations, provoked intense public reaction. Protest meetings were held across the country, prominent figures returned honors, including the Madras leader Sir S. Subramania Iyer, who renounced his knighthood, and many people who had not previously joined the League enrolled in it. The internment made Besant a martyr and demonstrated to many politicians the arbitrary character of emergency powers. The government released her in September 1917, partly in response to the controversy and to the change of policy that accompanied Montagu's arrival at the India Office.

In December 1917, at its session in Calcutta, the Congress elected Besant as its president, an indication of the influence Home Rule had acquired within the organization. Home Rule changed political practice by turning the demand for self-government into a wider and more sustained campaign, with organization, local branches, and constant publicity. Yet it must not be treated as equivalent to the mass movement that would follow. Its membership remained limited, its social base was predominantly educated and urban, and it did not yet involve the sustained participation of peasants and workers that Gandhi's campaigns would later achieve (Chapter 79). It was a major step toward, but not the same as, mass nationalism.

Section 78.16

The Lucknow Pact of 1916: Congress–League Cooperation and Its Limits

The Lucknow Pact was the product of negotiations in 1915 and 1916. Several developments encouraged cooperation. The Muslim League, since 1913 formally committed to a goal of self-government appropriate to India, was no longer simply a body of loyalists. Its younger leaders were increasingly concerned about British policy toward the Ottoman Empire and the treatment of Muslim journalists and activists under wartime powers. The Congress, for its part, was reuniting Moderates and Tilak's followers and was seeking a joint platform. Both organizations held their annual sessions in Lucknow in December 1916, and the sessions were coordinated.

Tilak, Jinnah, and other leaders played central roles. Jinnah, who presided over the League session, was a link between the two organizations. Tilak's willingness to accept separate Muslim representation was important for the Congress, and Moderate leaders joined the negotiations, although some Congress figures had reservations about the principle. The drafts were prepared by a joint committee. In October 1916, nineteen elected members of the Imperial Legislative Council had already submitted a memorandum on reforms, and its principles shaped the pact. The agreed Congress–League scheme called for an elected majority in provincial and imperial legislatures, an enlarged franchise, control over finance for the legislatures, and the inclusion of Indians in the executive councils.

The most significant and contested element concerned representation. The Congress accepted the principle of separate electorates for Muslims, introduced in 1909, and agreed on specific proportions of seats for Muslims in the councils. Under the scheme, Muslims would hold a third of the elected Indian seats in the Imperial Legislative Council. In the provincial councils, the allocations varied by province: in provinces where Muslims were a minority, such as the United Provinces, Bihar, and Madras, they were granted a larger share of seats than their population proportion ("weightage"), while in Punjab and Bengal, where they were a majority or a near-majority, they accepted a smaller proportion than numbers alone would have given them. The pact thus embodied a negotiated balance among regions with different demographic circumstances.

Contemporaries valued the agreement as a landmark of cooperation. It brought together the two main political organizations for the first time in a joint constitutional demand and strengthened the argument that Indians could agree on a program. British officials were obliged to take notice of an agreed scheme backed by both bodies, and the pact contributed to the pressure that produced the Montagu Declaration. Nationalist writers presented it as evidence of Hindu–Muslim unity and an example of political maturity.

Critics, then and since, have questioned both its legitimacy and its long-term implications. The pact was negotiated by organizations whose leaders did not represent the whole population, and many groups were not party to it. Sikhs in the Punjab, whose political significance exceeded their demographic size, were not included in the arrangements, and the formula reduced the representation of Muslim majorities in Punjab and Bengal while giving minorities elsewhere weightage, creating disagreements within the Muslim community and among Hindus. Some leaders feared that religiously differentiated electorates would entrench communal divisions, while others saw them as necessary guarantees. The pact must be understood as a negotiated agreement among political organizations, not a comprehensive agreement among India's religious communities.

Scholars emphasize the regional character of its consequences. Research by historians such as Mushirul Hasan and Ayesha Jalal has examined how the arrangement affected provincial politics, and how its terms were interpreted in different ways in later decades. It did not prevent later communal conflict, and its provisions were revised in the 1919 reforms, but it established a pattern of negotiated political cooperation and of separate representation that shaped constitutional debates through the 1920s and 1930s. The question of how much weight to give its achievements and its limitations is addressed in the following debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Separate Electorates

A system in which voters belonging to a defined community, such as Muslims, vote only for candidates reserved for that community, in constituencies composed of that community's voters. Separate electorates for Muslims were introduced in the legislative councils by the Indian Councils Act of 1909 (Chapter 77, §77.6), and the Lucknow Pact confirmed Congress's acceptance of the principle.

The term should be distinguished from reserved seats within a common electorate and from the later system of weightage, which gave a community more seats than its proportion of the population. Its supporters argued that it protected minorities; its critics argued that it encouraged communal identity in politics. It is not the same as the modern reservation of seats for scheduled castes and tribes, a system with different origins (see Chapters 81 and 88).

Historical Debate

The Lucknow Pact: Landmark of Unity or Foundation of Communal Politics?

The question: How should historians assess the significance of the Lucknow Pact: as a genuine achievement of cooperation, or as an agreement whose acceptance of separate electorates entrenched religious differentiation in politics?

Interpretation A — A genuine achievement of cooperation: The pact brought the Congress and the League into a joint constitutional demand at a critical moment, defused immediate disagreements, and strengthened India's case for reform. Its compromises reflected practical political bargaining among leaders working within the existing system, which already included separate electorates. On this reading, the pact is an example of what negotiation could achieve.
Interpretation B — A limited and consequential compromise: By accepting separate electorates and fixed shares, the agreement institutionalized religion as a basis of representation and left unresolved the question of how to reconcile different regional interests. It was negotiated by elites, excluded groups such as Sikhs, and created arrangements that later fed communal competition. On this reading, its short-term success had long-term costs.

Evidence: Historians use the Congress and League resolutions, the memorandum of October 1916, press reaction, the later working of the 1919 reforms, and provincial studies of electoral outcomes. They also examine the role of individual leaders and the contrast between the pact's short-term effect on British policy and its longer legacy.

Why it matters: The debate affects how the origins of communal politics, and the later relationship between Congress and the League, are understood. Many scholars argue that both interpretations contain truth: the pact was a significant achievement of its moment and also embodied tensions that later became more severe. It should not be read as the inevitable cause of later events.

Section 78.17

The Montagu Declaration of August 20, 1917

By 1917 the political situation had changed. The Mesopotamia Commission's report of 1917 criticized the Government of India and the India Office, and Austen Chamberlain, the Secretary of State for India, resigned in July. His successor, Edwin Montagu, was a Liberal politician with a reformist outlook and a conviction that the war had altered the terms of the imperial relationship. Montagu had previously served as Under-Secretary of State for India and was familiar with the constitutional questions. Several pressures shaped the new Cabinet's attitude: the war's demand for imperial unity and manpower, the Russian revolution of 1917, the Home Rule agitation and the Congress–League scheme, and the need to demonstrate that Britain's stated war aims applied to its own empire.

On August 20, 1917, Montagu announced in the House of Commons the government's policy. The statement declared that the British government's policy was the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration, and the gradual development of self-governing institutions, with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire. It added that substantial steps should be taken as soon as possible, that the British government and the Government of India must be the judges of the time and manner of each advance, and that progress would depend on the cooperation received from those on whom new opportunities of service would be conferred.

The significance of the declaration lay in its explicit commitment to responsible government, a goal that no previous official statement had set so clearly. For the first time, the British government stated that its objective was a form of self-government in India, within the Empire. Yet its limits were equally apparent. It specified no timetable and no definite constitutional mechanism. It reserved to the British government and the Government of India the right to decide the pace of advance, and it described progress as conditional on Indian cooperation. The language, careful and general, left considerable discretion to British authorities, and it did not promise independence or even dominion status.

Reactions varied. Moderates welcomed the declaration as a major advance and as vindication of their strategy, and many saw it as a basis for future reform. Home Rule advocates and Congress leaders accepted it as a significant statement while insisting on more rapid and definite steps. Muslim League leaders also welcomed the statement but wanted guarantees for their community's representation. More assertive nationalists regarded the declaration as insufficient or as a device to blunt the agitation. The government, for its part, hoped that the announcement would reassure moderate opinion and isolate the more radical elements.

The declaration should be seen as the outcome of several forces rather than a gift from British goodwill. Wartime service gave Indians a strong moral claim, organized political pressure had made the case for reform visible, and British officials were concerned about imperial stability and the risk of unrest. At the same time, the decision to concede a principle while retaining control of its implementation shows how British authorities sought to manage the pace of change, a tension that dominated the next phase of constitutional politics.

The Montagu–Chelmsford Report of 1918 and the Limits of Imperial Reform

Montagu traveled to India in the winter of 1917–1918 to consult officials and Indian leaders, an unusual step for a Secretary of State. He and the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford, together with a small group of advisers, conducted interviews, heard deputations from many organizations, and considered a range of schemes, including the Congress–League scheme. The consultations were not free of controversy: officials, Indian political groups, and European and Anglo-Indian interests presented competing views. In April 1918, a War Conference in Delhi gathered representatives to discuss India's contribution; Gandhi attended and supported recruitment, a position that shows his stance at that moment, to be discussed in Chapter 79.

The Report on Indian Constitutional Reforms, signed in April 1918 and published in July, set out a framework. Its central principle was the transfer of some responsibilities in the provinces to Indian ministers accountable to elected legislators, while retaining critical functions under the control of the governor and his executive council. Subjects were to be divided between "transferred" and "reserved" categories. Education, local self-government, and some other areas were to be placed in Indian hands, and finance, law and order, and other major subjects remained with the official side. This division is the system later known as dyarchy, a term commonly applied to the arrangement even though the 1918 report itself discussed the principle in somewhat different language.

The report also proposed an enlarged franchise, larger elected majorities in the legislative councils, and modifications to the central government. It continued the principle of separate electorates, extending it to Sikhs in the Punjab and to other minority groups, and it did not accept the Lucknow Pact's allocation in every detail. At the imperial level, the Government of India retained substantial authority, and the Viceroy's executive council remained responsible to the Secretary of State and Parliament, not to the Indian legislature. The authors presented these as the first stage of a progressive system, to be reviewed after a period of ten years.

It is important to distinguish the 1918 report from the Government of India Act that Parliament passed in December 1919. The report was a set of recommendations, which were modified in the process of legislation, with the help of a Joint Select Committee and further consultation. The Act implemented the main structure of provincial dyarchy and the other changes, and its working in the 1920s belongs to later chapters. The present chapter is concerned with the report's content and with the reaction to it.

Nationalist reaction was mixed and, among many, critical. A special Congress session held in Bombay in August and September 1918 condemned the proposals as inadequate, and the Congress rejected the notion that the British would determine the pace of reform. Some Moderates, however, thought the proposals deserved a trial and, led by Surendranath Banerjea, eventually broke away to form the National Liberal Federation in late 1918. The division within Congress thus reappeared, and it separated those who accepted the proposals as a basis for cooperation from those who considered them too limited.

The central contradiction was visible. India had provided men, money, and supplies on an unprecedented scale, and politicians had organized campaigns and negotiated agreements, yet the British government proposed to share limited responsibility in the provinces while retaining decisive power at the center. Reformers saw the proposals as an advance, and critics saw them as a means of controlling the pace of change. The disparity between enlarged wartime responsibilities and limited political authority fed the sense of grievance that, together with other factors, shaped the postwar atmosphere.

Historical Vocabulary
Dyarchy

From Greek roots meaning "double rule," the term describes the system, associated with the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms, in which provincial government was divided into "transferred" subjects, administered by Indian ministers responsible to elected legislatures, and "reserved" subjects, which remained under the governor and his executive council. Responsibility was thus shared between elected Indian politicians and British-controlled officials within the same provincial government.

The term should not be applied to the central government, which retained its existing structure, nor treated as a complete system of self-government. It was an experiment in partial transfer of power and is best understood as a compromise between the demand for responsible government and British reluctance to relinquish control. Its working under the Act of 1919 is examined in later chapters.

Section 78.19

The War's End, Demobilization, Food Shortages, and the Influenza Pandemic

The final year of the war made heavy demands on South Asia. The German spring offensive of 1918 led Britain to seek additional manpower, and the Government of India intensified recruitment under the slogans of imperial emergency. The Delhi War Conference of April 1918 formalized the effort, and many provinces were set larger recruitment targets. At the same time the Palestine campaign, culminating in the advance of September and October 1918, drew on Indian cavalry and infantry. The Armistice of November 11, 1918 ended fighting on the Western Front, and the Ottoman Empire had already signed a separate armistice in October, but the consequences of the war for South Asian society were only beginning to appear.

Demobilization created immediate problems. Soldiers and laborers returned to a country where prices were high and employment uncertain, and the government faced questions about pensions, disability payments, and the resettlement of veterans. Disabled soldiers and the families of the dead depended on pensions that were often modest, and some returned to find their land holdings or family circumstances changed. Many men had seen other societies and had been treated, in some respects, differently from colonial expectations, and some returned with new political awareness. Others came back in bad health. The government promised rewards, including grants of land in some regions, but implementation was uneven, and promises raised expectations that were not always satisfied.

Economic distress deepened in 1918. Poor monsoon rains in several regions reduced harvests, and food prices, already elevated by wartime demand and export pressures, rose further. Shortages of grain and cloth, disrupted commerce, and the continuing weight of taxation produced hardship, especially for the urban poor and agricultural laborers. Local food riots and price protests occurred in some areas. The government introduced some controls, but their effectiveness was limited, and the administrative capacity to manage a crisis of this scale was strained.

Into this situation came the influenza pandemic. The disease reached India in mid-1918, probably carried by ships and troop movements to Bombay, and a more lethal wave swept the subcontinent in the autumn, spreading along railways and through towns and villages. Mortality was extraordinary. It struck hard in the Bombay Presidency, the Central Provinces, the United Provinces, and the Punjab, among other regions, and it killed adults in their prime in unusual numbers. Women appear to have suffered high mortality, possibly because of their caring roles and poor nutrition, and the poor and lower castes, who lived in crowded conditions and lacked care, were especially vulnerable. Hospitals and dispensaries were overwhelmed, and the government's public health system, small and poorly funded, could do little.

The total death toll remains uncertain. Registration of deaths was incomplete, many rural deaths went unrecorded, and contemporary diagnosis was imprecise. Historian David Arnold estimates at least 12 million deaths in India, and other studies have produced higher totals through demographic reconstruction from census data, with estimates ranging up to nearly 20 million. The 1921 Census recorded exceptionally low population growth over the decade, reflecting the combined effect of epidemic mortality, famine, and other factors. Whatever the exact figure, the pandemic was among the deadliest events in modern South Asian history, and it deserves to be treated as a major event in its own right, not as an appendix to the war.

Public health failures and economic hardship intensified dissatisfaction with colonial administration. Many Indians believed that the government had been unprepared and had given too little attention to the welfare of its subjects. Critics drew attention to the contrast between the resources devoted to war and those available for health. The social consequences, including orphaned children, widowed families, and loss of labor, were profound and long-lasting. Literary and personal accounts, including those of the Hindi poet Suryakant Tripathi "Nirala," who lost family members, record the trauma. These experiences, together with the political developments traced in earlier sections, helped to create an atmosphere in which, by the end of 1918, many Indians were ready to question the terms of imperial rule.

Section 78.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: How the Great War Changed South Asia

The preceding nineteen sections described a war experienced in many ways. More than 1.4 million men were recruited into combatant and noncombatant Indian ranks by the end of 1919, while roughly 1.1 million Indian personnel are estimated to have served overseas, in theaters stretching from the Flanders mud to the Tigris, the East African bush, and the Sinai. They experienced the opportunities and risks of military service, the hierarchy of a colonial army, and in many cases the rigors of illness and injury. Their families experienced separation, remittances, anxiety, and sometimes loss. Recruitment, taxation, inflation, and, in 1918, epidemic disease affected villages and towns throughout the subcontinent, though unevenly, and in the princely states and the regions beyond British India the war had its own varied consequences.

Imperial racial hierarchy shaped both the battlefield and the home front. Indian combatant units remained overwhelmingly commanded by British officers, and Indian officers faced the King's-commission barrier until the limited policy change announced in 1917; Indian migrants were excluded from Canada under rules designed to avoid naming race; and colonial officials interpreted political activism through the lens of security. Yet the war also disrupted these hierarchies. Indian troops demonstrated their importance to the imperial war effort, and soldiers who had served overseas had seen European society and had been affected by the contrasts. The war's rhetoric of liberty and self-determination offered a language that nationalists could use against the colonial order.

Wartime political strategies diverged. Constitutional cooperation, expressed in the loyalty resolutions of 1914, expected reward for service. Home Rule transformed that expectation into an organized campaign for self-government within the Empire, led by Tilak and Besant. The Lucknow Pact represented an unprecedented negotiated agreement between Congress and the League, with the limitations and consequences discussed above. Revolutionaries, including the Ghadar movement, the Berlin and Kabul networks, and the Singapore mutineers, sought to overthrow British rule by force, but their organizations were penetrated, and their results were limited. These strategies should not be conflated, and each had a different relationship to violence, to the colonial state, and to the idea of the nation.

The colonial state responded with both repression and concession. The Defence of India Act expanded executive power and generated lasting resentment, while the Montagu Declaration and the 1918 reform proposals acknowledged the need for constitutional change. The two responses were linked. British officials wanted to maintain control and order while offering enough reform to conciliate moderate opinion, and the combination produced a dynamic of both cooperation and conflict. By 1918, the Raj remained powerful, with its army, bureaucracy, and finances intact, but the conditions of its authority had altered.

Political expectations had changed. In 1914 many leaders had been willing to defer demands in the name of loyalty. By the end of 1918, self-government was the declared goal of British policy, Congress and the League had negotiated a joint scheme, Home Rule had created national organizations, and the experience of repression and hardship had broadened political awareness. The gap between promise and practice, the increased burdens on ordinary people, the return of soldiers, and the devastation of the pandemic made the postwar period more volatile. The nationalist movement was larger, more organized, and more confident, though still divided.

Several unresolved questions remained at the end of 1918. Would wartime sacrifices be followed by meaningful self-government, or by continued imperial restrictions? Would the emergency powers be withdrawn? Would the reforms satisfy a politically mobilized public, or would they intensify frustration? How would the unresolved issues of communal representation, labor, and rural distress be addressed? These questions framed the next phase of South Asian history.

The next chapter addresses that phase. Chapter 79 examines Gandhi and the transformation of Indian nationalism: his experience in South Africa, his early campaigns at Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda, the Rowlatt Acts that extended wartime repressive powers, the massacre at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar in April 1919, the Khilafat movement, and the Non-Cooperation campaign that turned the organized politics of 1916–1918 into a mass movement. The history traced in this chapter, of soldiers and families, of revolutionaries and constitutionalists, of promises and hardships, is the essential background to that transformation.

Cause and Effect

From War Mobilization to Political Demands

Long-term causes

A colonial army organized around martial-race recruitment, an imperial fiscal system criticized by nationalists, a divided but growing nationalist movement, and racial hierarchy within the Empire created the conditions into which the war arrived.

Short-term causes

The war's manpower and financial demands, military setbacks such as Kut, the Mesopotamia Commission, and wartime emergency powers pushed the government and Indian political groups into new positions.

Event or transformation

Mobilization of soldiers, labor, and resources, along with inflation, food shortages, and the influenza pandemic, produced economic and social pressures that touched villages, cities, and families across South Asia.

Immediate effects

Home Rule agitation, the Lucknow Pact, revolutionary conspiracies, repressive legislation, and the Montagu Declaration reshaped constitutional politics between 1916 and 1918.

Long-term consequences

A politically organized public with higher expectations, a state determined to control the pace of reform, and the unresolved questions of repression and representation set the stage for postwar mass movements.

This textbook's response

Historians should resist attributing postwar politics to any single cause: military service, economic hardship, ideology, repression, and constitutional promises all contributed.

Historical complexity: Military service was not political consent, hardship did not automatically produce nationalism, and different regions and groups experienced the war differently. The pathway from mobilization to political demand ran through organizations, leaders, and local circumstances.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indian Nationalism before 1914 and at the End of 1918

What Changed

  • Moderates and Tilak's followers were reunited within Congress, and Congress and the League negotiated a joint constitutional scheme
  • Self-government became the declared goal of British policy, rather than only an Indian demand
  • Organized campaigns, notably the Home Rule Leagues, used branches, newspapers, and vernacular speech on a wider scale
  • Wartime hardship, recruitment, and epidemic disease affected far larger numbers of people than earlier political agitation had reached
  • Emergency powers expanded the state's reach into political life

What Stayed the Same

  • Constitutional leaders were still overwhelmingly educated, urban, and male
  • The British government retained control of the pace of reform and of the army, finances, and security
  • Separate electorates and communal representation remained part of the constitutional framework
  • The Indian Army's racial hierarchy and martial-race recruitment persisted
  • Revolutionary movements remained small and were suppressed

Who experienced change most directly: Soldiers, their families, and the urban political classes experienced the most visible changes; many cultivators and laborers experienced the war chiefly through prices, recruitment, and disease, while political structures changed less for them until the postwar movements. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents the mistake of treating 1918 as the beginning of mass nationalism or as merely the continuation of earlier politics.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of South Asia during the First World War, from India's entry into the conflict as part of the British Empire in August 1914 to the armistice and the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919. Its central argument is that the war transformed the relationship between colonial subjects and imperial power by combining intensive mobilization, economic strain, repression, and political organization, and thereby created the conditions for a new phase of mass nationalism.

India entered the war without a decision by representative Indian institutions. The Viceroy, Lord Hardinge, committed the country, and princes, Congress leaders, and many other political figures offered support, expecting that service would be rewarded. This loyalty was not uniform: Muslim politicians were concerned about the Ottoman Empire, revolutionaries saw an opportunity, and peasants often experienced the war through recruiters and prices.

The Indian Army, organized through colonial martial-race classifications and commanded principally by British officers, recruited more than 1.4 million Indian combatants and noncombatants from 1914 through 1919, while the estimated overseas total was around 1.1 million. Indian troops served on the Western Front in 1914 and 1915, in Mesopotamia, where the siege of Kut and the Mesopotamia Commission revealed serious administrative failure, and in East Africa, Gallipoli, Egypt, and Palestine. Statistics depend on definitions, and recruitment, though officially voluntary, involved pressure in some districts.

The home front was shaped by finance, inflation, industry, and social change. The Government of India raised taxes and loans and made a large contribution to Britain, while some industries profited and many households suffered from rising prices. Villages, particularly in the Punjab, bore the burden of recruitment, women assumed new responsibilities, and princely states contributed troops and funds. Soldiers' letters, filtered by censorship, are a vital but mediated source of their experience.

The chapter also examined South Asia beyond British India. Nepal's Rana government supported Gurkha recruitment on a large scale; Burma contributed through its economy and transport; and Ceylon experienced the 1915 anti-Muslim riots and a harsh martial law whose targets included prominent Sinhalese figures. These cases show that the war's effects varied by region.

Revolutionary nationalism was transnational. The Komagata Maru incident exposed racial exclusion within the Empire and the violence at Budge Budge. The Ghadar movement among Indian migrants sought an uprising in 1915 that failed through informers and limited preparation. Revolutionaries in Berlin and Kabul pursued German and Ottoman support and proclaimed a provisional government, but their operational capacity was slight. The Singapore Mutiny showed that sepoy loyalty could not be taken for granted. The Defence of India Act expanded executive detention, special tribunals, and censorship.

Constitutional politics also changed. Congress reunited Moderates and Tilak's followers, and Tilak and Annie Besant founded the Home Rule Leagues in 1916, turning demands for self-government into organized campaigns. Besant's internment in 1917 strengthened the movement and led to her election as Congress president. The Lucknow Pact of December 1916 created agreement between Congress and the League on reform and separate electorates, a landmark that was also limited and contested.

The British government responded with the Montagu Declaration of August 20, 1917, which committed it to the progressive realisation of responsible government within the Empire but retained control of the pace of change. The Montagu–Chelmsford Report of 1918 proposed limited transfer of provincial responsibilities, later known as dyarchy, and drew divided reactions from Indian politicians.

The war's end brought demobilization, food shortages, and the influenza pandemic, which killed at least 12 million people in India by one estimate and possibly considerably more. Public health failures and economic hardship intensified dissatisfaction with colonial administration. By the end of 1918, the Raj remained powerful, but Indian political expectations had changed, and the unresolved question was whether wartime sacrifice would be followed by meaningful self-government or by continued restriction.

The chapter has emphasized that military service is not political consent, that Home Rule and Ghadar must not be conflated, that the Lucknow Pact was significant but not universally representative, and that South Asian wartime history cannot be reduced to the constitutional politics of British India. These cautions will remain important in the chapters that follow.

Voices and Records of South Asia in the First World War

This project asks you to compare how different kinds of sources record the experience of the war and to assess their reliability.

  1. Choose a theme. Examples include soldiers' letters from France, the Mesopotamian campaign, recruitment in a Punjab district, the Ceylon riots of 1915, the Komagata Maru, the Ghadar trials, or the influenza pandemic.
  2. Locate sources. Using the MGU Library and the Source Register, identify at least two kinds of evidence, such as an official report, a published collection of letters, a newspaper, and a scholarly study.
  3. Analyze each source. Identify its creator, date, purpose, and audience, and note what it can and cannot establish.
  4. Compare. Explain where the sources agree and differ, and what the differences reveal about perspective, censorship, or bias.
  5. Write. Produce a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, using careful language such as "the evidence suggests" or "this remains debated."

Library resources to use: published collections of soldiers' letters, national archives guides, histories of the First World War in South Asia, historical atlases, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on colonial South Asia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a global event reshaped a colonial society from the village to the capital. The First World War connected a Punjab farm to a Flanders trench, a Tigris river boat, a Vancouver harbor, and a Berlin committee room. It reminds us that South Asian history cannot be understood only within the subcontinent's boundaries, and that the people who lived it were involved in global processes as soldiers, laborers, migrants, taxpayers, and political actors.

The chapter also reveals how historical change can occur without a simple turning point. In 1914 many leaders pledged loyalty, and in 1918 the same political world had been altered by organization, repression, promises, and hardship. The movements discussed here, from Home Rule to Ghadar, followed different paths, and the war did not create a single nationalism. It produced a more complex political landscape in which constitutional bargaining, revolutionary conspiracy, and mass suffering coexisted.

Several developments shaped what followed. The wartime emergency powers led to the Rowlatt legislation, the Lucknow arrangements influenced debates about representation, the reform proposals framed the politics of the 1920s, and the experience of soldiers and families entered family and regional memory. The pandemic's toll and the economic strain of 1918 left deep marks on ordinary life. In the larger six-volume sequence, the chapter forms the bridge between the organized but still limited nationalism of the early twentieth century and the mass politics of the 1920s and 1930s.

Chapter 79 turns to the figure who would transform that politics. Students who understand the conditions described here, including the unfulfilled expectations, the repression, the economic hardship, and the organizational base, are better prepared to ask why Gandhi's methods found a response in 1919 and 1920, and to resist the idea that the later movement arose simply from one leader's personality.

Source Register

Primary documents and archival collections

  • British Library, India Office Records: Reports of the Censor of Indian Mails in France, IOR/L/MIL/5/825–828.
  • Government of India, India's Contribution to the Great War (Calcutta, 1923); related India Office Records on recruitment and class composition.
  • Report of the Mesopotamia Commission (British Parliamentary Papers, 1917).
  • Canadian Order-in-Council of 1908 establishing the continuous-journey requirement and Library and Archives Canada immigration files on the Komagata Maru.
  • Report of the Committee Appointed to Investigate Revolutionary Conspiracies in India (Sedition Committee, 1918); Ghadar newspaper and poem collections; U.S. diplomatic and trial records on the Hindu–German conspiracy.
  • Indian National Congress and All-India Muslim League proceedings, December 1916; Memorandum of the Nineteen (October 1916); Home Rule League newspapers.
  • Hansard, House of Commons, August 20, 1917 (Montagu Declaration); Report on Indian Constitutional Reforms (1918).
  • Census of India, 1921; provincial sanitary commissioners' reports, 1918–1919.
  • Sri Lanka National Archives materials on the 1915 disturbances.

Verification and documentary locators

  • Army personnel categories: Santanu Das, “Indians at Home, Mesopotamia and France, 1914–1918: Towards an Intimate History,” Race, Empire and First World War Writing; Cambridge text and summary. Cites 1,440,437 new combatant/noncombatant recruits through December 1919, 239,561 Indian ranks in 1914, and roughly 1,096,013 overseas personnel.
  • Commissions: Edwin Montagu's reply, House of Commons, August 20, 1917, Hansard: “Army Commissions to Indians”; announcement concerning nine recommended officers.
  • Trans-Pacific migration: Parks Canada, The Komagata Maru Incident of 1914; identifies the 376 passengers, route via Hong Kong and Japan, and Canadian continuous-journey restrictions.
  • British Library, India Office Records catalogue for Reports of the Censor of Indian Mails in France, IOR/L/MIL/5/825–828. These are archival finding aids; the chapter's source-window prose is editorial reconstruction, not facsimile-collated transcription.
