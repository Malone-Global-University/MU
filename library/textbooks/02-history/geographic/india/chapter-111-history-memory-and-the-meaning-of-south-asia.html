History, Memory, and the Meaning of South Asia

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Concluding Chapter · Research cutoff: November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

In the early 1920s, Indian archaeologists working for the Archaeological Survey of India dug into mounds at two sites on the plains of the Indus system, Harappa in the Punjab and Mohenjo-daro in Sindh, and found baked-brick buildings, drains, weights, and small stamp seals bearing signs no one could read. On September 20, 1924, the Survey's Director-General, John Marshall, announced to readers of a London illustrated newspaper that these finds belonged to a hitherto unknown civilization. Almost overnight, the documented urban history of the subcontinent was pushed back by well over a thousand years. The discovery has never stopped producing arguments about what it means.

The Indus Civilization is where this textbook began, in Chapters 2 through 4, and it is a fitting place to begin its last chapter. Each of the preceding chapters has tried to tell what happened in South Asia's past and why. This chapter asks a different question: how do we know, who has told the story, and what have they wanted it to do? The 1924 announcement shows the problem in miniature. The evidence was real, a set of excavated objects, structures, and seals. The interpretations that followed were not all evidence-based. Some were careful, some were tentative, some were driven by colonial assumptions about Indian antiquity, and some, in the century since, have been driven by the needs of modern nations, religious communities, and political movements.

Chapter 111 therefore stands apart from Chapters 1 through 110. Chapter 110 completed the chronological narrative by examining South Asia's position in the world in 2026. This chapter does not extend the chronology. It steps back to examine the relationship between the past and the present: how historians build knowledge from evidence, how communities hold memories that may differ from the historians' conclusions, how states and institutions shape public understanding of history, and how all of this bears on the question of whether South Asia can be understood as a single region. Its central question is the one set out in the Master Plan for this chapter: what does the history of South Asia reveal about the development of its civilizations, societies, religions, cultures, and states, and why do the peoples of the region continue to interpret their shared and separate histories differently?

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I returns to the ancient period and to the evidence and debates surrounding the Indus Civilization, Indo-Aryan origins, and the use of antiquity in modern national identity. Division II examines the regional, religious, and colonial interpretation of precolonial South Asia, including the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal Empire, sacred places, and the colonial reorganization of historical knowledge. Division III turns to colonialism, nationalism, independence, and Partition as remembered rather than only as narrated. Division IV considers postcolonial conflicts and the institutions, monuments, and media through which historical claims enter public life. Division V steps back to ask what unites and differentiates the region, whose histories have been neglected, how historical practice is changing, and what the whole sequence of 111 chapters allows us to conclude.

Three cautions shape the whole chapter. First, historical uncertainty remains visible. The Indus script is undeciphered, the languages of the Indus cities are unknown, and the relationship between ancient population movements and the spread of Indo-Aryan languages remains an active research field. Where evidence runs out, this chapter says so. Second, ancient peoples are not treated as the ancestors of modern nations in any simple way. Political borders, religious communities, and ethnic identities have all changed, and modern states did not exist in antiquity. Third, scholarly disagreement is distinguished from claims that lack evidence. Honest disagreement among historians about how to interpret sources is a normal part of the discipline. Fabricated evidence, or claims that no responsible scholar could defend on the available record, are a different matter, and treating the two as equivalent would be a distortion of its own kind.

The chapter covers all seven countries of the region. India receives substantial attention because of the scale of its historiography and public debate, but Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives each have their own historical traditions, their own memories of conflict and conquest, and their own relationships to the shared past. A recurring comparative question is why communities that share elements of the same history often build quite different modern narratives from it.

The kinds of evidence examined here are unusually varied. They include excavation reports and radiocarbon dates, comparative linguistics and ancient DNA, royal inscriptions and monastic chronicles, Persian court histories and colonial gazetteers, school textbooks and museum labels, speeches in constituent assemblies, oral testimony recorded decades after the events it describes, films, novels, and social media posts. Each tells something real, each has limits, and each was produced by someone for some purpose. Learning to ask of every source what it can and cannot establish is the chapter's main practical lesson.

This is also the point at which the series closes the loop on its own structure. Chapter 1 established South Asia as a historical region and warned against projecting modern borders backward. Chapter 2 traced the long preparation for urban civilization. This chapter returns to both themes with the benefit of everything in between. It ends not with a verdict on the meaning of South Asian history but with a synthesis of what the preceding chapters show, an argument about how the region is best understood, and an invitation to students to test that argument against the evidence themselves.

  6. Assess how colonial knowledge systems classified South Asian societies and how those classifications shaped later narratives and identities.

Historical Context

This chapter draws on every previous chapter but repeats none of them. Its context is not a period of the past but an intellectual and institutional landscape that took shape over roughly two and a half centuries. Before the late eighteenth century, South Asian societies had rich and varied ways of recording and remembering the past: Sanskrit and Prakrit genealogies and royal eulogies, Pali monastic chronicles in Sri Lanka, Kalhana's twelfth-century verse chronicle of Kashmir, Persian court histories under the sultanates and the Mughals, Tamil inscriptions and poetry, Marathi bakhars, Assamese buranjis, and the oral traditions of countless communities. None of these was history in the modern disciplinary sense, but many were sophisticated forms of historical thought with their own rules of evidence and their own purposes (see Chapters 24, 36, 44, 52, and 58).

From about 1784, when the Asiatic Society of Bengal was founded, European scholars and officials began to create a new body of historical knowledge about South Asia, built on the study of Sanskrit, Persian, and Pali texts, the decipherment of ancient scripts, the survey of monuments, and the administrative collection of data in censuses and gazetteers (see Chapters 67 and 73). This work produced real discoveries, among them the decipherment of Brahmi, the identification of Ashoka, and the recovery of Buddhist history in the land of its origin. It also embedded assumptions about race, religion, and civilization that shaped colonial rule and left a long legacy. South Asian scholars, from the nineteenth century onward, took up, criticized, and rebuilt these frameworks.

The political upheavals of the twentieth century, nationalism, independence, Partition, and the creation of several new states (see Chapters 76 through 92), then gave history an urgent public role. Every new state needed an account of where it came from. National historians, school curricula, museums, and monuments became instruments of nation-building, and historical disputes acquired direct political stakes. Since the 1990s, as Chapters 99 through 108 explained, mass media, electoral competition, religious mobilization, and then digital communication have made historical argument a routine part of public life, for better and for worse.

Students should therefore read this chapter as an investigation of the conditions under which historical knowledge is produced and used. The evidence base has also changed, with new tools such as ancient DNA analysis, high-precision radiocarbon dating, satellite and LiDAR survey, and digitized archives joining older methods. The chapter's account of scientific results is current to its research cutoff of November 20, 2026, but findings in fast-moving fields such as ancient genomics are always provisional, and students should treat any single published study as one contribution to a continuing conversation, not a final word.

Section 111.1

History, Memory, Evidence, and the Interpretation of South Asia's Past

History, in the sense used throughout this textbook, is a disciplined effort to reconstruct and explain the past from surviving evidence. It proceeds by asking questions of sources, testing them against one another, and stating conclusions with a confidence proportionate to the evidence. Memory is something different. It is the way individuals, families, and communities carry a sense of the past in the present, through stories, rituals, anniversaries, place names, monuments, and songs. Memory is often emotionally powerful and socially vital. It is also selective, shaped by present concerns, and sometimes in conflict with what the evidence supports. The two are not enemies. Historians study memory as a historical phenomenon, and memory is often a source of evidence about how people experienced events. But a community's strong conviction about its past is not, by itself, evidence that the past happened that way.

South Asia offers an unusually rich field for thinking about this distinction because its historical sources are so varied. For the Indus Civilization and the farming villages of Chapter 2, the evidence is overwhelmingly archaeological: excavated architecture, seals, tools, plant and animal remains, and human skeletons. For the Vedic world, it is a body of orally composed and transmitted texts, later written down, that cannot be dated precisely and do not describe events in the manner of a chronicle. From the third century BCE, inscriptions on rock and stone, and later on copper plates, supply datable royal statements and records of land grants. Coins preserve names, titles, and images. Foreign travelers such as Megasthenes, Faxian, Xuanzang, Al-Biruni, and Ibn Battuta left outsider accounts. Court chronicles in Persian and Sanskrit, vernacular literatures, devotional poetry, administrative archives, and, in the modern period, newspapers, census returns, parliamentary debates, photographs, film, and oral testimony all add to the record.

Each type of source carries built-in limits. An inscription records what a ruler wished to proclaim, not necessarily what happened. A court chronicle flatters its patron. A monastic chronicle serves the monastery's view of the past. A colonial census reflects the categories its compilers thought important. A survivor's testimony recorded seventy years after an event may express what the survivor has come to understand rather than what they understood at the time. The historian's first task is therefore source criticism: asking who made a source, when, for whom, and for what purpose, and what it leaves out. Chapters throughout this textbook have asked students to do this, and the exercise becomes the central subject of this final chapter.

Historians also practice historiography, the study of how earlier historians have understood the past. Historiography reveals that every generation asks different questions. Nineteenth-century European scholars were preoccupied with language families and race. Colonial administrators wanted legal and revenue precedents. Nationalist historians in the early twentieth century wanted to show that their societies had ancient, creditable histories and the capacity for self-rule. Marxist historians in the mid-twentieth century concentrated on land, class, and economic change. Since the 1980s, social historians, feminist scholars, Dalit and Adivasi writers, environmental historians, and others have widened the field. Recognizing these shifts does not make history arbitrary. It reminds students that historical knowledge is built cumulatively, revised as evidence and methods improve, and always produced by people in particular circumstances.

One of the most important distinctions this chapter draws is between reasonable scholarly disagreement and claims unsupported by evidence. Historians may legitimately disagree about how much weight to give a particular source, how to explain the decline of a state, or how to interpret an ambiguous archaeological pattern. They do not legitimately disagree about whether a documented event occurred, whether an inscription says what it says, or whether an artifact was forged. A claim does not become a respectable interpretation merely because it is passionately held or politically useful. Equally, a historian's confident reading of the evidence does not deserve automatic deference merely because it comes from an authority. The aim of this chapter is to give students tools to tell the difference.

Historical Vocabulary
Historiography, Historical Memory, and Collective Memory

Historiography is the study of how history has been written: which questions historians have asked, which sources they have used, and what assumptions shaped their conclusions. Historical memory and collective memory refer to the ways groups remember and represent the past, usually through commemoration, storytelling, ritual, and education, and often with a present purpose. Historical evidence means the surviving material, textual, oral, and visual traces from which the past can be reconstructed.

These terms matter because a historian may study one without endorsing the other. The Mahavamsa, for instance, is excellent evidence for how Sri Lankan Buddhist monks understood their island's history in the first millennium CE and a powerful source of later collective memory, while its account of early events cannot be taken as a straightforward record of what occurred. The modern habit of using "history" to mean any account of the past blurs these distinctions.

Section 111.2

The Indus Civilization: Rediscovery, Archaeology, and Modern Historical Memory

The story of the Indus Civilization's rediscovery, introduced in this chapter's opening, is a case study in how archaeological knowledge is made. Chapters 3 and 4 examined the civilization itself. Here the subject is how modern scholarship recovered it. Hints appeared before 1920. In the nineteenth century, Alexander Cunningham, the first head of the Archaeological Survey of India, had noted an unusual stamp seal from Harappa, but its significance was not understood, and Harappa's ancient brick was being plundered by railway contractors who used it as ballast on the Lahore–Multan line. Only in the early twentieth century, under Marshall, who directed the Survey from 1902 to 1928 and professionalized Indian archaeology and monument conservation, did the scale of the buried sites become evident.

Indian archaeologists did much of the decisive fieldwork. Daya Ram Sahni began excavations at Harappa in 1920–21 and found seals and other material of a kind not previously recognized. Rakhaldas Banerji, working at Mohenjo-daro from about 1919 to 1922, recognized that the site's structures and seals belonged to a very early date and noted resemblances to the Harappa finds. Marshall and his colleagues brought these threads together, and the 1924 announcement made clear that two sites roughly six hundred kilometers apart belonged to a single material culture. Soon afterward, specialists in Mesopotamian archaeology pointed to similar seals in Mesopotamian contexts, which helped anchor the civilization chronologically in the third millennium BCE. Mortimer Wheeler directed the Survey in the 1940s and brought more systematic stratigraphic methods, and excavation at other sites, including Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi in India, and a growing number in Pakistan, has transformed knowledge of the civilization's extent.

Partition in 1947 divided this heritage. The two most famous sites, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, lay in Pakistan, while many others lay in India. The Archaeological Survey's collections and staff were divided, and some of the best-known objects ended up on one side of the new border or the other. The "Dancing Girl" bronze from Mohenjo-daro is held in New Delhi, while the sculpture commonly called the "Priest-King" is held in Karachi. Excavation access, publication, and international collaboration were all affected. Indian archaeologists have since concentrated on sites in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana, and Punjab, while Pakistani archaeology has had to maintain Mohenjo-daro, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980, under conditions of flooding, salinity, and limited funding. Dholavira in Gujarat was inscribed in 2021. The civilization was divided between two states that had no shared archaeological tradition and were often at odds.

Much about the civilization remains uncertain, and responsible history must say so. The Indus script, found on thousands of seals and other objects, remains undeciphered, as Chapter 4 explained. The inscriptions are short, no bilingual text has been found, and the language or languages of the Indus people are unknown. Scholars have proposed Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda, and other language affiliations, as well as the possibility that the signs do not encode a language in the usual sense, but none of these proposals has achieved general acceptance. The civilization's political organization is also unclear, since no palace or royal inscription has been identified, and its relationship to later cultures, including those of the Vedic period, remains debated (see Chapters 3 through 5).

Because the evidence is incomplete, the Indus Civilization has been claimed by many modern constituencies. Pakistani national narratives sometimes present it as a heritage of the Indus valley, or occasionally as an ancestor of the Muslim homeland. Some Indian narratives treat it as the origin of Hindu civilization, pointing to seals with possible ritual imagery. Some Tamil and other Dravidian-language nationalists see it as a Dravidian civilization. Each claim goes beyond the evidence. A civilization that flourished about 2600 to 1900 BCE, before Islam, before Hinduism in any later sense, and before the languages and nations of modern South Asia existed, cannot be assigned to one modern religion, ethnicity, or state. It is better understood as one of the early urban civilizations of the world, whose cultural inheritance may be shared in complex and not fully traceable ways by many later populations.

John Marshall’s “First Light on a Long Forgotten Civilization,” 1924

Source type: Illustrated newspaper article by an archaeological official · Created: September 20, 1924, in the Illustrated London News · Author: Sir John Marshall, Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India

Context: By 1924 the excavations at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro had produced seals, pottery, and structures that appeared to Marshall to belong to a single, hitherto unknown civilization. He chose to announce the discovery in a London popular weekly, addressed to a broad English-speaking readership, rather than waiting for a formal excavation report. The article was accompanied by photographs of the sites and finds. The article, titled “First Light on a Long Forgotten Civilization,” is available in full through the Harappa.com archive, alongside period illustrations; students should read its original wording rather than treat the paraphrase below as a quotation.

Original primary document: Marshall, First Light on a Long Forgotten Civilization (20 September 1924); archive context and illustrations.

Paraphrase of the source's main claims: Marshall presents the finds from the two sites as evidence of a previously unrecognized civilization, links the seals and other objects to material known from Mesopotamia, and suggests that Indian antiquity extends much deeper than the textual and monumental record had indicated. He invites specialists to contribute to its interpretation. (This is a paraphrase, not a quotation. Students should consult the original for exact wording.)

Analysis: The article is evidence of how the discovery was first understood and presented. It shows the confidence and the caution of an official archaeologist addressing a public audience. It also shows his assumptions, notably that the key to dating the finds lay in comparison with Mesopotamia, and that the significance of the discovery would be judged partly by its relation to the Western Asian civilizations European scholars already knew. The article does not mention the Indian excavators' roles with the prominence modern readers might expect, which reflects the hierarchy of the colonial Survey and later led historians to restore Sahni and Banerji to the narrative. It cannot tell us what the civilization's language was, who ruled it, or what its people believed, and Marshall did not claim to know.

Section 111.3

Indo-Aryan Origins, Language, Migration, and Historical Debate

Few topics in South Asian history carry as much political weight as the origins of the Indo-Aryan languages, and few require as much care to separate evidence from ideology. Chapter 5 examined the early Vedic world; this section examines how scholarship about it developed and why it became so contested. The scholarship began in 1786, when William Jones, a British judge in Calcutta, observed in a lecture that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin shared structural similarities so strong that they seemed to share a common source. Comparative linguistics grew from this insight and eventually established the Indo-European language family, which includes Sanskrit and most languages of Europe, Iran, and northern South Asia, as one of the best-established findings of historical linguistics. The Indo-Iranian branch, which includes Sanskrit and Avestan, shows that speakers of Indo-Aryan languages and the ancestors of Iranian speakers were once linguistically close.

Nineteenth-century scholars, however, fused linguistic findings with racial thought. Max Müller, who did much to popularize Vedic study in Europe, and later writers such as Herbert Risley, who used anthropometry in the colonial census, treated "Aryan" as a race as well as a language group. They imagined fair-skinned Aryan invaders conquering darker indigenous peoples and used this picture to explain caste, language divisions between north and south, and the supposed unity of European and Indian elites. This theory, whose intellectual history Thomas Trautmann has studied, had troubling uses. It served British rulers who could claim kinship with high-caste Indians while justifying colonial hierarchy, and it fed European racial ideologies, including National Socialism's appropriation of the "Aryan." In South Asia, it was both adopted and resisted: some reformers and lower-caste thinkers used it to argue that Brahmins were foreign conquerors, while some Hindu nationalists rejected it and insisted on indigenous Aryan origins.

Archaeology contributed its own interpretations. In the 1940s, Mortimer Wheeler suggested that the decline of Mohenjo-daro might be linked to the invasion of Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples and cited skeletal remains and Rigvedic references to fortresses. By the 1960s, scholars such as George Dales had shown that the skeletal evidence did not support a massacre, and the idea of an organized "Aryan invasion" was abandoned by serious scholarship. Chapter 4 showed that the decline of the Indus cities was more gradual and tied to environmental and economic change. The invasion hypothesis is therefore not a viable modern position. Yet its legacy persists, in political debates that treat any discussion of migration as a revival of the discredited invasion theory.

Contemporary scholarship works with the idea of migration and cultural transformation, supported by several lines of evidence that must be integrated rather than treated separately. Linguistic reconstruction shows that Indo-Aryan languages entered South Asia from the northwest and that the earliest Indo-Aryan Vedic Sanskrit has features, including loanwords from other languages, indicating contact with pre-existing populations. Archaeological evidence shows cultural interaction across Central Asia and the northwestern borderlands in the second millennium BCE, but it has not identified a single archaeological "Indo-Aryan culture" and the connections remain debated. Ancient DNA has added a new kind of evidence. Studies published in 2019, including Narasimhan and colleagues in Science and Shinde and colleagues in Cell, analyzed genomes from the Indus periphery and neighboring regions, and showed that South Asian populations descend from a mixture of ancestry components that included, by the second millennium BCE, ancestry related to Bronze Age steppe pastoralists, along with older South Asian hunter-gatherer ancestry and ancestry related to Iranian-plateau farmers. A woman's genome from the Indus site of Rakhigarhi analyzed in the Cell study lacked steppe-related ancestry, which has been read as evidence that such ancestry was not a feature of the civilization's population as it existed there.

What genetics can and cannot establish must be stated clearly. Ancient DNA shows population ancestry and movement. It does not, on its own, show what language people spoke, what they believed, or how they identified themselves. Languages can spread without large population replacement, and populations can change languages. Interpreting genetic results therefore depends on integrating them with linguistic and archaeological evidence, and most researchers who work in this area emphasize that the results are consistent with the movement of populations carrying steppe-related ancestry into South Asia, but that the exact routes, timing, and relationship to the spread of Indo-Aryan languages are still being worked out. Some advocates of an "Out of India" theory, which holds that Indo-European languages spread from South Asia outward, argue that the genetic evidence is consistent with their view. Most historical linguists and geneticists reject this argument on linguistic grounds, since the language evidence points toward an origin outside the subcontinent, and the idea has not gained acceptance in mainstream scholarship.

A responsible treatment of these debates must distinguish disagreement about evidence from disagreement driven by modern identity. Scholars differ about the chronology and mechanisms of migration, the role of other populations, and the relationship between the Indus world and the Vedic one. These are real scholarly questions. Racial claims about the superiority of "Aryans," or the claim that all Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples are foreign invaders in their own land, are not evidence-based interpretations at all. Neither are claims that deny any movement of people or language into South Asia in order to protect a vision of unbroken indigenous continuity. The honest summary is that the evidence points to complex population mixture over millennia, that language spread by processes that are still debated, and that "Aryan" is best used as a term for a linguistic and cultural-historical category, as Chapter 5 insisted, not as a biological race.

Historical Vocabulary
Linguistic Reconstruction, Migration, Ancestry, Ethnicity, and Population Genetics

Linguistic reconstruction is the method by which historical linguists infer features of ancestral languages, such as Proto-Indo-European, from systematic correspondences among related languages. Migration is the movement of people, which may be large or small, rapid or gradual, and may or may not lead to language change. Ancestry in population genetics refers to statistical genetic relationships among individuals and populations, not to cultural or political identity. Ethnicity is a social identity based on perceived common descent, culture, language, or history, and it can change within a lifetime. Population genetics analyzes patterns of genetic variation to infer how populations are related and have moved.

These terms must be kept distinct. A person's genetic ancestry does not determine their language or identity, and a language community is not a biological unit. Misuse of these distinctions underlies both the older racial theories and some modern genetic claims about "pure" lineages.

Section 111.4

Ancient India and the Construction of Modern National Identity

Modern states and movements have turned to the ancient past because it offers symbols of antiquity, unity, and moral purpose. The Indian National Congress and other anticolonial organizations used images from ancient history to answer colonial claims that Indians were incapable of self-government. Nehru's The Discovery of India, written in prison in the 1940s, presented a long civilization of unity in diversity. Historians such as K. P. Jayaswal argued that ancient India had republican institutions, and others emphasized the achievements of the Mauryas and the Guptas as proof of political and cultural accomplishment. These efforts were understandable responses to colonial denigration, but they also set patterns later critics would question, among them the tendency to read ancient states as precursors of the modern nation.

Ashoka offers a clear example. Chapter 16 showed that Ashoka was the third Mauryan emperor, reigned in the mid-third century BCE, and left a set of rock and pillar edicts that survive. But by the Middle Ages he had been largely forgotten in India outside Buddhist traditions, which remembered him in texts such as the Sri Lankan chronicles and the Ashokavadana. His modern reputation began when James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in the 1830s and scholars linked the king named "Piyadasi" in the inscriptions to the Ashoka of Buddhist texts. The twentieth century made him an icon. The Sarnath lion capital, which comes from an Ashokan pillar, became the emblem of the Republic of India in 1950, and the wheel from the same monument was placed on the national flag. Ashoka's attempt to rule by dhamma and his remorse after Kalinga have been invoked as a model of nonviolent, tolerant statecraft by Nehru and many others.

Historians have noted several problems with such uses. Ashoka ruled an empire that did not correspond to modern India. It included parts of present-day Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nepal, and Bangladesh, and probably did not include much of the far south. His dhamma was a royal ethical program, not a modern ideology of secularism or democracy. And the image of a tolerant emperor rests on his own inscriptions, which are propaganda, so we cannot easily test their claims against other evidence. Ashoka also belongs to several traditions. Sri Lanka remembers him as the patron through whom Buddhism reached the island; Nepal's Lumbini, where an Ashokan pillar marks the Buddha's birthplace, is a major pilgrimage center; and Pakistan has Ashokan inscriptions at Mansehra and Shahbazgarhi and the Buddhist remains of Taxila and Gandhara. A figure of this kind belongs to the heritage of many modern states.

Buddhist heritage more generally has played a special role. The rediscovery of Buddhism's Indian origins in the nineteenth century, through archaeology at Sarnath, Sanchi, Bodh Gaya, and Lumbini, led to international Buddhist revival movements, such as the work of the Sri Lankan Anagarika Dharmapala to reclaim the Mahabodhi temple. B. R. Ambedkar's conversion to Buddhism in 1956 with hundreds of thousands of followers (see Chapter 80) gave Buddhism new significance as a religion of social emancipation, and his reading of Buddhist history differed from both colonial and Hindu-nationalist ones. Nalanda, the great monastic university of Chapters 34 and 39, became a symbol again with the modern revival project in Bihar, in which several countries of Asia have taken part.

The Vedic texts and the epics have been used in similar ways. For some, the Vedas represent the foundation of an unbroken Indian civilization; others point out that the Vedic corpus belongs to a specific time and region and that the Rigveda's world was not that of the later epics, nor of the Tamil south, nor of the Indus Civilization. The Ramayana and Mahabharata, as literature and as living traditions, are among the most influential texts in South Asian and Southeast Asian cultural history, yet they are not eyewitness accounts of historical events, and attempts to date the "events" they describe by astronomical calculation or to locate their battles precisely have not produced results acceptable to historians. The Gupta period has been described by some historians as a "golden age," but Chapter 29 noted that the phrase is a modern judgment that obscures regional variation and the achievements of other periods.

The point is not that cultural continuities do not exist. They obviously do: Sanskrit literature, the cults of Vishnu, Shiva, and the Goddess, Buddhist and Jain institutions, and the diverse traditions of the south have all persisted in changing forms. The point is that continuity of cultural tradition is not the same as the continuity of a nation or state. The ancient past of South Asia belongs to several overlapping traditions: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Muslim, Sikh, Christian, secular, regional, and national. It also belongs to historians' evidence-based reconstruction, which may support or challenge the meanings communities assign. Treating antiquity as the property of a single modern community, or as the origin of a single modern state, requires ignoring much of what the evidence shows.

Division II · Sections 111.5–111.8

Regional Traditions, Religious Histories, and the Interpretation of Precolonial South Asia

Section 111.5

Regional Kingdoms, Languages, and Competing Civilizational Narratives

Much popular history of South Asia is told from the viewpoint of a political center in the north, with Pataliputra, Delhi, and Agra as the repeated capitals. This textbook has argued otherwise. Chapters 8, 22, 23, 31 through 37, and 45 through 47 showed that the Deccan, Tamilakam, Bengal, Kashmir, Gujarat, and the Himalayan and island regions had their own political and cultural trajectories, and that large empires such as the Mauryas, Guptas, Cholas, and Mughals were exceptional episodes against a background of regional states. Regional societies also wrote and remembered their own pasts, in different languages and for different purposes, and these traditions often fit uneasily into national narratives.

Tamil historical memory is one of the oldest and best documented. The Sangam poems, composed in the early centuries CE, and the extensive inscriptions of the Pallava, Chola, and Pandya kingdoms (see Chapters 23, 32, and 33) provide a continuous record of kingship, temple patronage, and local assemblies. Tamil literary culture developed independently of Sanskrit even while interacting with it, and modern Tamil political movements, from the Self-Respect Movement of Periyar (Chapter 80) to the Dravidian parties, have drawn on this history to argue for the distinctiveness of Tamil civilization and sometimes for its priority over a Sanskrit-based north Indian narrative. Recent archaeological work at sites such as Keeladi near Madurai, where excavations have uncovered an urban settlement with early Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions on pottery, has drawn wide public interest, and the dating and interpretation of the finds have been argued over in both scholarly and political terms. This is a clear example of how a regional discovery can enter public debate before the evidence has been fully published and assessed.

Sri Lanka's historical tradition is anchored in Pali chronicles, particularly the Dipavamsa and the Mahavamsa (see Chapter 24). These were composed by Buddhist monks and present the history of the island as the history of Buddhism's arrival and royal protection, with the legendary king Dutugemunu's defeat of a Tamil ruler a celebrated episode. Modern Sinhala nationalists have drawn on this account to argue that Sri Lanka is the special guardian of Theravada Buddhism and that the Sinhalese are its principal inheritors, while Tamil historians point to the long Tamil presence on the island, Hindu and Buddhist Tamil patronage, and the Chola occupation (Chapter 36) to argue for a plural pre-modern history. Historians note that the chronicles are valuable but religious in purpose, that their account of ethnic conflict reflects their authors' concerns, and that the ethnic categories of the modern civil war cannot be projected onto the ancient period without distortion. Chapter 103 examined how these narratives shaped the conflict.

Kashmir produced one of the few Sanskrit works in South Asia that approximates critical history, Kalhana's Rajatarangini, completed around 1148 CE (Chapter 36). Kalhana explicitly sought to evaluate earlier sources and to write without partiality, and his work has been treasured in Kashmir ever since. Bengal's historical consciousness drew on the Pala and Sena dynasties, the Sultanate of Bengal (Chapter 47), and the modern Bengali Renaissance, and Rakhaldas Banerji, whom we met in the Indus story, also wrote a history of Bengal that helped shape regional identity. In Nepal, the Gorkha unification under Prithvi Narayan Shah in the 1760s became the foundation of a historical narrative of the Himalayan kingdom, which in turn was revised after the end of the monarchy in 2008. Bhutan's Buddhist state tradition grew from the unification under the seventeenth-century lama Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal and is remembered through the monastic and monarchical institutions that followed.

Maldives belongs to the Indian Ocean as much as to the subcontinent. Its pre-Islamic past was Buddhist, as archaeology of the atolls shows, and its conversion to Islam, traditionally dated to the middle of the twelfth century, is central to the national narrative of a sovereign Muslim state. Travelers such as Ibn Battuta, who spent time there in the fourteenth century, provide early outside evidence. The relationship between the country's Islamic identity and its Buddhist archaeological heritage has been a delicate subject in modern politics: in February 2012, during political turmoil in Male, a crowd reportedly damaged pre-Islamic Buddhist artifacts in the national museum, an episode that revealed how contested a society's sense of its pre-Islamic past can become (see Chapter 104).

These regional examples show why a single national story cannot contain South Asian history. They also show that regional traditions are not always opposed to national ones. Tamil, Bengali, Sinhala, Kashmiri, and other regional pasts have been recruited to support national narratives, as when a state portrays its regional heritage as part of a larger civilizational unity, and have challenged them, as when regional movements argue for autonomy on the basis of a distinct historical identity. The task of the historian is to take regional sources seriously on their own terms, ask what each was written to accomplish, and resist both the dissolution of regions into nations and the opposite assumption that regional identities are timeless.

Section 111.6

The Delhi Sultanate, Mughal Empire, and the Interpretation of Muslim Rule

No part of precolonial South Asian history has been more controversial than the centuries of rule by Muslim dynasties. Chapters 38 through 44 and 50 through 56 told this history in detail. Here the subject is how it has been interpreted. The first systematic framework was colonial. James Mill's History of British India (1817) divided Indian history into Hindu, Muslim, and British periods, a scheme later reinforced by compilations such as Elliot and Dowson's History of India as Told by Its Own Historians, which selected Persian passages in a way that emphasized conquest and religious conflict. This framework served colonial interests, since it portrayed the pre-British period as an age of religious despotism and conflict from which British rule offered relief.

Nationalist and later historians took up, modified, and contested this picture. Some Hindu nationalist writers adopted the idea of a long era of Muslim domination and foreign rule, portraying it as a period of oppression of Hindus. Some Muslim writers, in turn, celebrated Muslim rule as a golden age of Islamic civilization in India. Other scholars, including Mohammad Habib, Satish Chandra, Irfan Habib, and Athar Ali, argued that medieval states should be analyzed through their administrative, economic, and social structures, not through the religion of their rulers. More recent historians such as Richard Eaton, Cynthia Talbot, Muzaffar Alam, and Sanjay Subrahmanyam have examined local sources, regional contexts, and Persianate culture to produce more differentiated accounts. The result is a scholarly consensus that the old religious periodization oversimplifies, even if there is continuing disagreement about specific issues.

What the evidence supports is a picture of great variety. The Delhi sultans were Turkic and Afghan military elites who ruled through a combination of conquest, iqta assignments, and alliances with local elites (Chapters 40 through 43). Their states fought each other as readily as they fought Hindu kingdoms, and they were frequently threatened by the Mongols. The Mughals, Chaghatai Turks with a Central Asian and Persianate culture, built a large empire whose nobility included Rajputs and other Hindus, Iranians, Central Asians, and Indian Muslims (Chapters 51 through 54). Muslims themselves were diverse: Sunni and Shia, Sufi and orthodox, descended from migrants and from local converts. Most Muslims in South Asia were not the descendants of rulers but of local populations whose conversion over centuries was gradual and varied (Chapter 44). Hindu and other non-Muslim communities participated in these states as soldiers, administrators, bankers, and zamindars, and also experienced taxation, warfare, and at times persecution.

Individual rulers' reputations show how interpretation changes. Akbar, who abolished the jizya, patronized translation, and brought Rajput nobles into the Mughal elite, has been praised as a model of tolerance, and some historians now caution that his policy of sulh-i kul was also an imperial strategy of governing a diverse realm (Chapter 52). Aurangzeb, who reimposed the jizya in 1679, fought long wars in the Deccan, and ordered the destruction of certain temples, has been cast as a bigot by many writers, and defended as a pious, conventional ruler who also patronized temples and employed Hindu officials by others (Chapter 54). Historians generally agree on the major documented facts and disagree about motives, proportion, and significance. A fair account distinguishes criticism of specific rulers' policies from sweeping statements about all rulers of a religion, and distinguishes the documented political context of any ruler's actions from the retrospective assumption that religion explained everything.

The relationship among empire, taxation, warfare, and religion was in fact complex. Temple destruction in the medieval period, studied by Eaton, was often directed at temples associated with rival royal authority and was part of political conflict, while many other temples were protected, endowed, or ignored, and Hindu kings also plundered and desecrated temples of their rivals. Patronage of religious institutions, including Hindu temples and Jain communities by Muslim rulers and mosques and dargahs by Hindu rulers, was widespread. This does not make the period harmonious: there was real violence and discrimination. It makes the period impossible to summarize in one word.

Historical Debate

Is It Useful to Describe South Asian History through Religious Periods?

The question: Should historians continue to divide the past into "Hindu," "Muslim," and "British" periods, or does the scheme distort more than it clarifies?

Interpretation A — Religious periodization is misleading: Critics argue that the scheme, invented by colonial writers, treats the religion of a ruling dynasty as the defining characteristic of an entire society. It makes the majority population invisible, ignores the Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs, Christians, and others, assumes that each religious community was a single political bloc, and implies that Muslim rulers were foreigners while Hindu rulers were not, even when dynasties such as the Mughals were rooted in the subcontinent for centuries. They propose instead neutral labels such as early medieval, medieval, and early modern, or divisions based on political economy.
Interpretation B — Religion cannot be dismissed as a category: Other historians note that rulers did use religious language to legitimate rule, that religion affected law, taxation, patronage, and conflict, and that contemporaries themselves used religious categories. They caution that abandoning the vocabulary may understate real episodes of discrimination or violence, and they point out that the neutral labels have their own problems, since "medieval" and "early modern" are borrowed from European history. On this view, the task is to study the role of religion carefully, not to deny it.

Evidence: Both sides draw on inscriptions, chronicles, administrative records, and monuments. The evidence shows that rulers drew on religion in some contexts and on administrative, dynastic, or ethnic loyalties in others, and that the religious composition of ruling elites differed widely.

Why it matters: The periodization shapes public understanding of whether the premodern past was an era of continuous religious conflict or of varied coexistence. Most scholars now treat the two extreme readings, uninterrupted communal conflict and uninterrupted harmony, as unsupported by the evidence.

Section 111.7

Temples, Mosques, Shrines, Literature, and Shared Cultural Heritage

Sacred buildings and the literatures and practices associated with them are among the most visible legacies of South Asia's past. The Buddhist monastic complexes of Sanchi, Nalanda, and Ajanta, the temples of Khajuraho, Thanjavur, and Hampi, the Jain temples of Mount Abu and Shravanabelagola, the great mosques of Delhi, Jaunpur, and Bijapur, the shrines of Sufi saints at Ajmer, Delhi, and Multan, and the Sikh gurdwaras of Amritsar and Nankana Sahib are the material expression of the religious history in Chapters 28, 44, 48, and 56. Sacred sites often accumulated meaning across centuries, sometimes serving more than one community, and their histories are frequently more layered than later claims suggest.

Sufi shrines show how sacred places could draw worshippers across religious boundaries. The dargah at Ajmer, associated with Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti, was patronized by the Mughals, including Akbar, and has long attracted Hindu and Muslim pilgrims. Many shrines in Punjab and Sindh similarly host devotees from various communities, and Bhakti poets such as Kabir and the Sikh Gurus drew on devotional ideas that circulated across religious lines (Chapter 48). Mughal architecture combined Persianate and regional Indian styles, and temple, mosque, and palace architecture in Rajasthan, Bengal, and the Deccan displayed mutual borrowing. These patterns support the argument that South Asian religious life was often porous. But they do not support a claim of seamless harmony, and the textbook's earlier chapters have documented conflict, persecution, and competition over patronage.

Documented episodes of destruction and conversion of sacred sites need to be recorded accurately. Medieval rulers of various faiths seized and sometimes damaged religious sites in the course of war; Buddhist monasteries in the Gangetic plain suffered in the political upheavals of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries; and Mahmud of Ghazni's raid on Somnath around 1025–1026 became an enduring symbol. Romila Thapar's study of Somnath showed how differently the event was recorded in Persian sources, Sanskrit inscriptions, and later colonial and nationalist writing, and how the memory of a single raid was reshaped over nine centuries into a master narrative of the relation between Hindus and Muslims. The rebuilding of the Somnath temple after independence, in the early 1950s, itself became a political and historical symbol. Sources such as these demonstrate that the same event can be recorded in several ways, depending on who writes and why.

Sacred places also became sites of modern dispute and of modern cooperation. The Golden Temple at Amritsar was the scene of the 1984 military operation (Chapter 94). The Buddhas of Bamiyan in Afghanistan, destroyed in 2001, showed how heritage could be targeted for ideological reasons. The Kartarpur Corridor, opened in November 2019 to allow Indian Sikh pilgrims to visit the shrine of Guru Nanak in Pakistan, is an example of a heritage site becoming a channel for contact between rival states. Hindu temples and Sikh gurdwaras survive and are in use in Pakistan, and Muslim shrines and mosques in India, yet minorities in each country have also reported neglect, encroachment, or violence at sites of their religion. The Ayodhya dispute, discussed in §111.15, is the best-known contemporary case.

Preservation, pilgrimage, and tourism shape how the past is presented. State agencies restore selectively, tourism boards prefer certain narratives, and international organizations such as UNESCO introduce their own criteria of "outstanding universal value." Pilgrims often care less about archaeological chronology than about devotional significance, and a site's meaning to its worshippers may legitimately differ from what excavation reveals. Historians should neither dismiss devotion nor allow it to override evidence. The aim of this section is to show that South Asia's religious past was both shared and contested, and that understanding it requires holding both facts together, without sentimentalizing coexistence or reducing the record to conflict.

Manuscripts and translation were another meeting ground. Akbar's court sponsored Persian translations of the Mahabharata (as the Razmnama) and other Sanskrit works, and in the seventeenth century the Mughal prince Dara Shukoh arranged a Persian translation of the Upanishads (Chapter 52 and Chapter 56). Such projects show that elite Muslim patrons took Sanskrit learning seriously, and that Brahmin scholars and Persian secretaries collaborated. They also remind historians that the survival of South Asia's literary heritage depended on libraries, monasteries, courts, and private families, many of which were later damaged by war, neglect, or climate, and that contemporary manuscript conservation is itself a form of historical responsibility.

Section 111.8

Colonial Knowledge, Historical Classification, and the Writing of South Asian History

European colonial rule transformed not only the political but the intellectual landscape of South Asia. As Chapter 67 showed, the East India Company's officials and scholars, beginning with William Jones and the Asiatic Society, studied Sanskrit, Persian, and Arabic texts partly out of curiosity and partly to govern more effectively. This body of work is often called Orientalist, and Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) argued that such scholarship was bound up with imperial power. Subsequent scholars have complicated that thesis, noting that colonial scholars depended on Indian pandits and munshis, and that Orientalist work recovered texts and histories that might otherwise have been lost. The most useful view is that colonial knowledge was both genuinely informative and shaped by the aims of the colonial state.

The institutions of colonial knowledge multiplied through the nineteenth century. The Archaeological Survey of India (1861) surveyed and conserved monuments, a task formalized by the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act of 1904 under Viceroy Curzon. The Linguistic Survey of India, directed by George Grierson and published between 1898 and 1928, mapped the region's languages. The census, begun on a regional basis in the 1870s and made an all-India exercise in 1881, enumerated religion, caste, and occupation (Chapter 73). Ethnographic surveys, gazetteers, and the "martial races" theory of military recruitment classified populations. Colonial historians also wrote the first comprehensive histories. In Sri Lanka, officials such as George Turnour translated the Mahavamsa into English in 1837, and Wilhelm Geiger's later translations influenced both scholarship and Sinhala nationalist readings. Colonial Ceylon also established an Archaeological Survey in 1890. Nepal and Bhutan were never colonized, but British Residents, notably Brian Hodgson in Kathmandu, collected Buddhist manuscripts that shaped Western knowledge of Himalayan Buddhism.

Experiences varied among territories. British India was directly governed in the provinces and indirectly through princely states, so its knowledge systems differed from those of Ceylon, a Crown colony after 1802 whose administration was organized differently. Nepal retained independence under treaties after 1816, and Bhutan developed a distinctive treaty relationship with Britain, while Maldives became a British protectorate with internal autonomy in 1887. These different relationships to imperial power influenced how each society later remembered the colonial period and shaped its institutions of historical research.

One enduring colonial framework was the periodization into Hindu, Muslim, and British periods, discussed in §111.6. Another was the classification of society into fixed castes, tribes, and religious communities. Historians such as Bernard Cohn and Nicholas Dirks argued that colonial censuses and ethnography reified caste, turning flexible local hierarchies into rigid categories used for administrative and political purposes. Other scholars, including Susan Bayly and C. A. Bayly, emphasized that caste had a long precolonial history of identity and conflict and that colonial classification built on existing practices while changing them. The most defensible position, supported by the evidence in Chapter 73, is that colonial categories neither invented caste out of nothing nor merely recorded what already existed: they hardened, standardized, and politicized identities that had been more fluid and locally varied.

South Asian intellectuals were not passive recipients. Rammohan Roy, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, R. G. Bhandarkar, and others engaged with Orientalist scholarship, sometimes drawing on it to support reform or nationalism. Later historians, including Jadunath Sarkar, who combined rigorous archival method with strong judgments on the Mughals and the Marathas, and D. D. Kosambi, who brought Marxist methods to ancient Indian history, created distinctive scholarly traditions. The Subaltern Studies group, discussed in §111.18, directly challenged both colonial and nationalist historiography. The colonial period thus established a framework that later scholars have worked with, against, and beyond.

The section's argument is balanced on two cautions. It would be wrong to treat every colonial classification as wholly invented, since many reflected real social structures. It would equally be wrong to treat older identities as fixed and unchanged, since religious, caste, and ethnic identities in the precolonial period were more varied and negotiable than the enumerated categories allowed. The task for students is to trace how each category was created, used, contested, and sometimes adopted by those it described.

Cause and Effect

How Colonial Knowledge Systems Shaped Later Historical Narratives

Long-term causes

European commercial and territorial expansion; the Company's need to understand law, revenue, and local society; Enlightenment interest in language and civilization; existing South Asian scholarly traditions on which colonial scholars relied.

Short-term causes

The founding of the Asiatic Society (1784), the Archaeological Survey (1861), the census (1871–1881), and the Linguistic Survey; administrative demands after 1858 for a stable, classified society.

Event or transformation

Creation of standardized categories (caste, tribe, religion, language) and historical frameworks (Hindu–Muslim–British periods, "Aryan" race) used in administration, education, and public discourse.

Immediate effects

Recovery of lost texts and monuments; politicization of enumerated communities; new educated classes who learned and then contested colonial historical frameworks.

Long-term consequences

Persistence of colonial periodizations and categories in textbooks, law, politics, and popular memory; counter-traditions of nationalist, Marxist, subaltern, and Dalit historiography.

Historical complexity

Colonial knowledge was dependent on South Asian collaborators, built on precolonial categories, and used by nationalists as well as imperialists. No single cause explains later identity politics.
Division III · Sections 111.9–111.12

Colonialism, Nationalism, Independence, and Partition Memory

Section 111.9

Colonialism and Its Legacies in South Asian Historical Memory

Few topics in modern South Asian history are argued over more persistently than the effects of British rule. The evidence examined in Chapters 62 through 75 is extensive, and much of it is not in dispute. The East India Company acquired political power in Bengal after Plassey and Buxar and the grant of the diwani in 1765. Land-revenue systems, from the Permanent Settlement to ryotwari and mahalwari arrangements, reorganized rural society. Railways, beginning with the first passenger line in 1853, telegraphs, ports, and irrigation works changed the economy. Serious famines occurred repeatedly, including the Bengal famine of 1943, which killed an estimated two to three million people. English-language education, a modern legal system, and a bureaucratic state were created. Racial hierarchy structured colonial society, and violence was used to suppress resistance in 1857 and later. The question is how to weigh and interpret these facts.

One set of interpretations stresses extraction and underdevelopment. Nineteenth-century nationalists such as Dadabhai Naoroji and R. C. Dutt argued that colonial taxation and trade policy transferred wealth from India to Britain, the "drain" (Chapter 76). Later historians, including Irfan Habib, and more recently Utsa Patnaik, have developed quantitative versions of such arguments, though specific estimates of the total transfer are contested. Another set of interpretations, associated with some British and Indian economic historians, notes that India's economy grew under colonial rule in certain sectors, that railways and irrigation improved some regions, that colonial rule coexisted with growing Indian industrial entrepreneurship, and that the counterfactual, what would have happened without colonialism, cannot be known. Historians such as Tirthankar Roy have urged analysis of specific mechanisms rather than sweeping verdicts. Most scholars agree that colonial rule was designed to serve British imperial interests, that the benefits and costs were unevenly distributed across regions and classes, and that claims that colonialism was a gift of modernization neglect the coercion and unequal power on which it rested.

It is important to distinguish what historians can establish from what political judgment adds. Whether the Bengal famine of 1943 resulted from wartime British policy, such as the diversion of shipping and food and the refusal of relief, or from a mixture of causes is a matter of evidence on which historians have written extensively, and many emphasize that policy decisions in London and Delhi worsened a crisis that had other causes (Chapter 84). Whether the colonial legal system promoted equality or entrenched hierarchy depends on which cases and periods are examined. Statements such as "colonialism was entirely beneficial" or "colonialism caused all later poverty" are retrospective political judgments, and the historian's work is to test them against evidence about how institutions actually operated, for whom, and with what consequences.

Memories of colonial rule differ by country. In India, memory is dominated by the nationalist narrative of a long freedom struggle, with 1857, the Salt March, and Quit India as touchstones. Pakistan commemorates the colonial period within a narrative that emphasizes the loss of Muslim political power after 1857 and the movement for a separate homeland. Bangladesh's memory of the British period is overshadowed by later conflict with Pakistan, though Bengal's history of the 1943 famine and the 1905 partition of Bengal are central. In Sri Lanka, the Portuguese, Dutch, and British periods are treated in Sinhala nationalist writing as a sequence of foreign domination, and the late colonial period is also associated with the Buddhist revival and with the plantation economy that brought Tamil laborers from South India (Chapter 75). Nepal and Bhutan, though never colonized, were deeply affected by British power in the region, including the Anglo-Nepalese War of 1814–1816 and the treaties that followed, and they remember colonial influence in terms of independence preserved. Maldives, as a protectorate from 1887 until independence in 1965, preserved its internal sultanate while accepting British control of its external affairs.

Debates about colonialism have also entered modern politics well beyond the academy. Popular books and speeches have called for reparations or for recognition of imperial atrocities, while others have defended the imperial record. For students, the lesson is that any account of colonialism should specify what is claimed, what evidence supports it, and what alternative explanations exist. Powerful emotions about colonial humiliation or colonial achievement are part of historical memory, and historians study them, but they do not replace evidence.

Section 111.10

Competing Memories of Anticolonial Nationalism

Nationalist movements in South Asia are remembered through commemorations, school lessons, statues, public holidays, and the stories families tell. These practices select and simplify. Indian official memory has long emphasized the Congress-led movement and the central role of Gandhi, with Nehru, Patel, and others as principal figures, a narrative reinforced by Independence Day ceremonies and by monuments such as Rajghat and, since 2018, the Statue of Unity dedicated to Vallabhbhai Patel. Pakistani national memory centers on the Muslim League, the Lahore Resolution of 1940, and Muhammad Ali Jinnah, honored as Quaid-i-Azam, with the creation of Pakistan presented as the culmination of a distinct Muslim national movement. In Bangladesh, the Language Movement of 1952 and the Liberation War of 1971 take precedence, with the pre-1947 movement remembered in the context of Bengal's own politics, as Chapter 90 showed.

Within each narrative, leaders are interpreted differently. Gandhi's reputation as the architect of nonviolent mass mobilization (Chapter 79) has been criticized from several directions. Ambedkar and Dalit thinkers have argued that his approach to caste was paternalistic and that the Poona Pact of 1932 limited Dalit political autonomy (Chapter 81), while some radical nationalists said he restrained more militant struggle and some Hindu nationalists blamed him for concessions to Muslims. Nehru is celebrated as a builder of democratic institutions and criticized for economic and Kashmir policy. Jinnah, regarded in Pakistan as founder, has in Indian popular memory been blamed for Partition, while historians such as Ayesha Jalal have argued that his goals and methods were more complex than either picture allows, and that his demand for Pakistan was partly a bargaining position within negotiations. Subhas Chandra Bose, the leader of the Indian National Army (Chapter 84), is remembered in Bengal with particular fervor and has been invoked by multiple political currents. Ambedkar himself, chief architect of the Constitution (Chapter 88), moved in the late twentieth century from relative marginality in national narratives to wide public veneration, particularly among Dalits, an example of how memory can be reshaped by political mobilization.

Leader-centered histories are complicated by those that attend to other actors. Peasant movements, labor strikes, communist and socialist organizations, and revolutionary groups pursued goals that sometimes differed from the Congress program (Chapter 80). Dalit political movements, including those led by Phule, Periyar, and Ambedkar, regarded caste hierarchy as an equal or greater enemy than colonial rule, and in some cases saw British rule as offering opportunities denied by caste society. Women participated in the Civil Disobedience movement, revolutionary groups, and the Quit India movement, yet standard narratives gave them little space for decades. Regional nationalists in Bengal, Punjab, Assam, Tamil Nadu, and elsewhere developed their own visions of freedom. Muslim politicians such as Maulana Azad opposed Partition, while others supported it. Even Sikh and princely-state politics produced distinct programs. The simple picture of a single nation rising against a single colonial power omits the plurality of aims the movements contained.

Public commemoration reinforces some narratives. Independence anniversaries, flag ceremonies, textbooks, film, and statues repeat a short list of heroes and events, while others are forgotten. In recent decades, governments and movements have promoted the recovery of previously neglected figures and events, for instance tribal leaders such as Birsa Munda, and revolutionary martyrs, and have sometimes pressed rival interpretations of established heroes. Such expansions can bring neglected stories to light, and they can also be used for political advantage, a distinction students must evaluate in each case.

The section's essential point is that anticolonial nationalism was not a single movement with one shared objective. It included demands for self-rule within the empire, for complete independence, for a unified India, for a separate Pakistan, for the end of caste, for land redistribution, for regional autonomy, and for varied conceptions of religion's place in the state. The unity that emerged in national memory after independence was in part constructed, and recognizing this does not diminish the achievements of those involved but makes them more historically comprehensible.

Section 111.11

Independence, Partition, and Competing Visions of Nationhood

The partition of British India and the independence of India and Pakistan in August 1947 (Chapters 85 through 87) resulted from negotiations among the British government, the Indian National Congress, the Muslim League, and other actors, and from violence on the ground. The political question underlying the negotiations was how to organize a diverse society after the end of British rule. Congress leaders, committed to a single Indian nation with a secular state and universal citizenship, wanted a strong center. The Muslim League, led by Jinnah, argued that Muslims were a separate nation whose political safeguards could not be assured in a Congress-dominated India, and eventually demanded a separate state. The Cabinet Mission plan of 1946 had tried to preserve unity with a loose federation, but it collapsed, and the British decision to withdraw in 1947 compressed the timetable for partition into months.

Each of the new states proclaimed its purposes at its birth. Jawaharlal Nehru, speaking to the Indian Constituent Assembly at midnight on August 14–15, 1947, described independence as a moment of fulfillment of a pledge, the "tryst with destiny," and committed the new nation to service to the poor and to ending poverty, ignorance, and inequality of opportunity. Muhammad Ali Jinnah, addressing the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan in Karachi on August 11, 1947, envisioned a state in which citizens of all religions would be free to worship and in which religion would have, in his words, nothing to do with the business of the state. These speeches show that both leaders articulated inclusive civic ideals at the moment of founding.

They must be read with care. A speech states aspirations and positions for a particular audience. It is not a description of subsequent governmental practice. India's Constitution adopted in 1950 guaranteed equality and religious freedom, and the state has also experienced communal violence and discrimination against minorities. Pakistan's early constitutional debates, as Chapter 90 showed, soon turned on the role of Islam in the state, and Jinnah's speech has been debated by Pakistanis ever since: some read it as a blueprint for a secular state, others as a statement of minority protection within an Islamic polity. Historians therefore use such speeches as evidence of declared principles and political rhetoric, then compare them with the laws, institutions, and events that followed.

Partition itself shaped the visions of nationhood. India defined itself as the heir to the whole of British India's secular institutions and as a state for all its citizens; Pakistan defined itself as a homeland for the Muslims of the subcontinent, though with large non-Muslim minorities and, in its eastern wing, a population whose language and political culture differed from those of the west. The result was two states with quite different founding ideas and with unresolved territorial disputes, above all over Kashmir (Chapters 87 and 105). Among South Asia's other territories, Sri Lanka (then Ceylon) became independent in February 1948 with a negotiated transfer of power, Nepal and Bhutan maintained their sovereignty in the new regional order, and Maldives remained a British protectorate until 1965 (Chapter 91). The wider dissolution of empire gave each of these states different relations to the new India and Pakistan.

The constitutional debates that followed show the difference between founding rhetoric and institutional choice. India's Constituent Assembly, in which Ambedkar played a central part (Chapter 88), abolished separate electorates, guaranteed freedom of religion and the cultural and educational rights of minorities in the Constitution of 1950, and declared the state a republic with universal adult franchise. Pakistan's Constituent Assembly adopted the Objectives Resolution in March 1949, which affirmed that sovereignty belonged to God and also promised protections for minorities, and the interpretation of that document has been contested ever since (Chapter 90). Comparing the two sets of founding texts, and the practice that followed, shows how similar language of rights could coexist with quite different institutional arrangements.

Section 111.12

Partition, Displacement, Violence, and Intergenerational Memory

Partition, examined as a political event in Chapter 86, has also been one of the most intensively remembered events in South Asian history. Between 1947 and the early 1950s, somewhere between twelve and twenty million people crossed the new borders, among the largest forced migrations of the twentieth century. Estimates of those killed range widely, from about two hundred thousand to as many as two million, with one million a commonly cited figure, and none of these estimates can be treated as precise. Women suffered abduction and sexual violence on a large scale, and both governments later participated in recovery operations whose methods and consequences have been critically assessed. Families lost homes, land, and livelihoods, and cities such as Delhi, Lahore, Karachi, and Amritsar were transformed by the exodus and arrival of refugees. Bengal's partition was less violent in 1947 but produced a continuing movement of people across a border that never stabilized.

For many decades, Partition was underrepresented in scholarly history, which focused on high politics and the transfer of power. Beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, scholars turned to the experiences of ordinary people through oral history, interviews, memoirs, and literature. Urvashi Butalia's The Other Side of Silence and Gyanendra Pandey's Remembering Partition, along with Ritu Menon and Kamla Bhasin's work on women, showed how much remained hidden in official records and family silences. Writers, including Saadat Hasan Manto, Bhisham Sahni, and Qurratulain Hyder, had earlier captured the experience in fiction. Partition memory is now carried by diaspora communities in Britain, North America, and elsewhere, by museums such as the Partition Museum in Amritsar, opened in 2017, and by projects of digitized testimony.

Oral history is a powerful but demanding source. It records experiences, emotions, and details that official documents omit, particularly those of women, the poor, and minorities. It also has limitations. Memory changes with time, is influenced by later events and by subsequent retellings, and is shaped by the interview setting. Survivors may emphasize certain experiences and omit others, and some may have been children at the time. Testimony must therefore be corroborated against other evidence where possible, and treated as evidence for how the past was experienced and remembered as much as for what happened. The 1947 Partition Archive, founded in 2010, has collected thousands of recorded testimonies from people across South Asia and the diaspora and provides a major public resource for this work.

The collection of testimony about violence carries ethical responsibilities. Interviewers must obtain informed consent, respect privacy and the wishes of interviewees about publication, be aware that recalling trauma can distress a person again, and take care with details that might identify living relatives. Historians must also think about silence. Many survivors did not speak for decades, and some never did; their silence is itself part of the historical record. Treating silence as an absence of experience would be a mistake, and so would pressing people to speak against their wishes.

Partition memory is also political. In India and Pakistan, official and popular narratives have each emphasized the suffering of their own community and the responsibility of others, and Partition has been invoked in current disputes over citizenship and minority rights. Chapter 86 and this section's argument both reject descriptions of Partition violence as the product of ancient, unchanging hatred, since the evidence shows that it arose from specific political mobilization, administrative collapse, and local conflicts, and that many people also protected neighbors. They likewise reject collective guilt for entire communities, while holding that specific actors and institutions bear responsibility where evidence allows.

One of the most closely studied episodes concerns women who were abducted during the violence. Both governments agreed on a program for the recovery of abducted women, and India passed the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act in 1949. Estimates of the numbers involved run to tens of thousands, and historians such as Urvashi Butalia and Ritu Menon and Kamla Bhasin have shown that the recovery operations often disregarded the wishes of the women themselves, particularly those who had formed new families and were returned to their original communities. The episode is evidence of how states claimed authority over women's bodies and of how official records, which count recoveries, differ from testimony, which records the experience.

Everyday Life and Social History

Photographs, Keys, and Household Objects: Remembering Displacement in Families

For many families, the history of Partition is carried less in books than in objects: a photograph of a house that no longer exists, a key to a door in a city now in another country, a utensil carried across a border, a letter from a relative who stayed behind. Such objects have been collected in museums and in family archives, and they allow historians and descendants to approach the past through ordinary things. A key can stand for an expectation of return; a photograph can show how people dressed, lived, and presented themselves before the upheaval.

Memory is also passed on through food, language, songs, recipes, and the names of lost villages and neighborhoods. Second and third generations, including those in diaspora communities, often learn about Partition through the stories, and the silences, of parents and grandparents, and they sometimes travel to the places of origin. The historian's task is to treat these practices as evidence of how a past event continues to shape identity, while recognizing that family narratives, like all memory, are selective and may differ among relatives who experienced the same events.

Section 111.13

Postcolonial Conflict and the Construction of National Memory

The wars and civil conflicts of the postcolonial period produced memories that differ sharply across and within countries. Chapters 92, 95, 103, 104, and 105 told these histories. Here the focus is on how they are remembered and contested. A central point is the difference between the historical study of events and three other things: official commemoration, political rhetoric, and personal testimony. All are valuable sources for understanding memory, and none can substitute for the careful reconstruction of what happened.

Bangladesh's Liberation War of 1971 is the founding event of the state. The Language Movement of 1952, commemorated each February 21, and the war that followed the Pakistani military crackdown of March 1971 and ended with the surrender of Pakistani forces in Dhaka on December 16 are central to Bangladeshi identity. Memory of the war has been politically divisive within Bangladesh, as competing parties have claimed the legacy of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and of the freedom fighters and have disagreed about the role of collaborators. The scale of civilian deaths is also disputed: the figure of three million is Bangladesh’s official number, while some international and Pakistani-commissioned estimates are substantially lower. Scholars disagree about methods and coverage: some estimates count direct violent deaths, while others attempt to include deaths from illness, deprivation, and displacement, including among refugees in India. These are not interchangeable measures, and no universally accepted comprehensive count exists. In Pakistan, official memory long minimized the atrocities committed in East Pakistan and framed the war as the result of Indian intervention, while Pakistani critics and historians have argued for acknowledgment of the army's conduct. The political changes in Bangladesh after 2024 (Chapter 102) have reopened questions about how the history of 1971 and of later governments should be told.

In Sri Lanka, the civil war that ended in May 2009 (Chapter 103) left a deeply divided memory. Many Sinhala Buddhists commemorate the military victory over the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) as the end of terrorism. Many Tamils recall the anti-Tamil violence of July 1983, the destruction of the Jaffna library in 1981, and the heavy civilian casualties in the war’s final phase. A 2011 United Nations panel of experts reported that a range reaching as many as 40,000 civilian deaths could not be ruled out, citing credible sources; it did not verify 40,000 as an established total and emphasized that only a proper investigation could produce an accurate count. The Sri Lankan government disputed allegations of casualties on that scale. The panel’s report is a source to evaluate, not a substitute for a definitive mortality register. Muslim communities, expelled from the north by the LTTE in 1990 and caught up in later violence, have their own memories. Public remembrance of the dead in Tamil-majority areas has faced official restrictions at various times, while government commemorations emphasize the war victory. The question of accountability remains unresolved.

In Nepal, the history of the Rana period, the 1950–51 revolution, the Panchayat system, the people's movements of 1990 and 2006, the Maoist insurgency from 1996 to 2006, for which approximately seventeen thousand deaths are commonly cited as an estimate rather than an exact verified total, and the abolition of the monarchy in 2008 (Chapter 104) are each remembered differently by supporters of the monarchy, democrats, Maoists, and marginalized groups such as Madhesis and Janajatis who contest the state's historical narrative. Bhutan's memory of its modern state formation emphasizes monarchy and Buddhist heritage, while the expulsion or departure of many ethnic Nepali-speaking residents (Lhotshampa) in the early 1990s remains disputed between Bhutan and refugee communities. In Kashmir and in India and Pakistan, memories of the wars of 1947–48, 1965, and 1971, of the Kargil conflict, and of the insurgency that began around 1989 are inseparable from the political dispute itself (Chapter 105).

Official inquiries and transitional-justice bodies are additional sources, with their own limits. Pakistan's Hamoodur Rahman Commission, appointed after the war of 1971, produced a report that was withheld for decades and published only in part. Sri Lanka's Lessons Learnt and Reconciliation Commission reported in 2011, and a United Nations expert panel the same year produced a different assessment. Nepal established a Truth and Reconciliation Commission and a commission on enforced disappearances in 2015, whose work has been slow and contested. Historians use such reports as evidence of what governments were prepared to acknowledge and what victims' groups demanded, and they note that the mandate, independence, and access of each body shaped its findings.

Section 111.14

History in Schools, Museums, Archives, and Public Institutions

Most people encounter history not through monographs but through schools, museums, public monuments, and commemorations. These institutions decide which past is presented. School textbooks in particular are an instrument by which states transmit a national narrative, and their content has been contested throughout the postcolonial period. In India, the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) produced widely used textbooks in the 1970s by leading historians such as Romila Thapar and Bipan Chandra; their approach was criticized in the 1990s and 2000s by Hindu nationalist groups, and in periods of Bharatiya Janata Party government the curriculum has been revised, as in the 2002–2004 and 2017–2023 rounds. An NCERT curriculum “rationalisation” announced in 2022, with revised books reaching students for the 2023–24 school year, removed or shortened selected coverage of Mughal history and the 2002 Gujarat violence. Revisions also cut particular passages on Hindu extremists’ hostility to Gandhi, the circumstances of his assassination, and the subsequent ban on the RSS; they did not eliminate every reference to Gandhi’s assassination from all textbooks. NCERT cited the need to reduce learning loads after pandemic disruption, while critics questioned the historical balance and transparency of the omissions. These claims should be evaluated against specific textbook editions rather than conflated into one undifferentiated change. Students should look at the specific revisions themselves rather than rely on either characterization.

In Pakistan, studies of school textbooks, including a notable report issued in 2003 by the Sustainable Development Policy Institute, found that Pakistan Studies and history books often presented the nation's history as one of an inevitable Muslim homeland, minimized the role of non-Muslims, and portrayed neighbors, especially India, in hostile terms. Bangladeshi textbooks have changed with successive governments, reflecting the parties' competing narratives of 1971. Sri Lankan textbooks have been criticized for giving Sinhala Buddhist history primacy and for neglecting Tamil and Muslim contributions. In Nepal, the end of the monarchy required revisions of narratives that had celebrated the Shah dynasty. Bhutan's curriculum emphasizes national heritage and values. In every case, governments and educational authorities choose which periods, rulers, religions, and movements to emphasize.

Museums and memorials make similar choices. The National Museum in New Delhi, the National Museum of Pakistan in Karachi, the Liberation War Museum in Dhaka, which began as a citizen initiative in 1996, and the Partition Museum in Amritsar represent different versions of history for different publics. Archives preserve the documentary record: the National Archives of India, provincial and state archives, the Pakistan and Bangladesh national archives, and the vast colonial records in the British Library, along with local and community archives. Access to records, including the declassification of government files, is often limited, and gaps in the record reflect political decisions about what to preserve. Archaeological collections, heritage boards, and university departments conduct the research from which the public narrative is, in principle, drawn.

Public disagreement over curricula is not in itself a sign of manipulation. Any selection of topics reflects judgment, and communities legitimately differ about what to teach. The standards that matter are whether what is taught is accurate, whether significant evidence is omitted in ways that mislead, and whether the process permits scholarly scrutiny. Educational simplification, such as compressing a complex subject for younger students, is different from demonstrable factual distortion, such as stating that something occurred that did not, or presenting a disputed claim as settled. Government-approved narratives must remain open to scholarly criticism, and independent historians, archives, and a free press are the institutions by which that scrutiny occurs. Where those institutions are weak or constrained, the public record is less reliable.

Professional bodies also shape what counts as established history. The Indian History Congress, founded in 1935, the Pakistan Historical Society, founded in 1950, and university departments throughout the region organize conferences, peer review, and the publication of research, and they have at times served as centers of resistance to political pressure on the curriculum. Their independence varies with funding, legal protection, and the climate of public debate. Where historians face harassment, litigation, or the withdrawal of institutional support for studying sensitive subjects, public knowledge is narrowed. A student evaluating a claim about the past should therefore ask not only who made it but also whether the institutions that would normally test it are free to do so.

Section 111.15

Archaeology, Monuments, Historical Claims, and Contemporary Politics

Archaeology occupies a delicate position in public life because it produces physical evidence that appears to settle historical arguments. In practice, it settles fewer than people expect. Archaeologists establish chronology through stratigraphy, the layered sequence of deposits, and through scientific dating such as radiocarbon analysis. They establish provenance, meaning exactly where and in what context an object was found, which is crucial because an object without recorded context loses most of its interpretive value. They reconstruct construction sequences, determining what was built first and what was later added or altered. They also recognize the limits of what survives. A structure can be dated and described, but the intentions of its builders, the beliefs of its users, and the circumstances of its destruction or abandonment often cannot be recovered with certainty. Chapter 2's discussion of Mehrgarh, where a 2025 radiocarbon study of tooth enamel from burials proposed a substantially later date for the excavated aceramic Neolithic cemetery than earlier conventional chronologies, is a reminder that even well-established chronologies can change when new methods are applied.

Archaeology becomes political when claims about the past are tied to claims about the present: ownership of land, the right to build or worship, national origin, or the legitimacy of a political movement. Several kinds of problems then arise. One is the demand for certainty that the evidence cannot provide. Another is the selective use of findings, in which results supporting a position are emphasized and those against it ignored. A third is outright fabrication, as when forged inscriptions or manipulated images are circulated. Such cases have occurred in South Asia and elsewhere, and they are distinct from legitimate interpretive disagreement. A fourth is the assertion of unbroken ownership or identity across thousands of years, which no archaeological evidence can demonstrate, since populations, religions, and states change.

Debates over preservation, restoration, and ownership of monuments are widespread. The Taj Mahal, the Qutb complex in Delhi, the Buddhist ruins in Pakistan's Swat valley and Taxila, and the Sri Lankan sacred city of Anuradhapura are all objects of conservation efforts and sometimes of conflicting claims. Cultural property also circulates internationally: colonial-era removal of objects, such as the Amaravati sculptures in the British Museum, has raised calls for repatriation, and illicit trade in antiquities continues. Heritage agencies must weigh scientific conservation against public use and devotional practice.

The most widely known recent case is Ayodhya, where the sixteenth-century Babri Masjid was demolished by a crowd on December 6, 1992, at a site that many Hindus revere as the birthplace of Rama (Chapter 99). Archaeology entered the dispute repeatedly. B. B. Lal’s excavations in the 1970s, and his later public interpretations of features he associated with pillar bases, became part of the controversy; the Archaeological Survey of India conducted court-ordered excavations in 2003 and identified remains of a large pre-existing structure. Scholars disagreed about how to interpret these results: some argued that the evidence pointed to a temple, others that the pillar bases and other finds were not clearly part of a temple and that the excavation's methods and conclusions were flawed. The Supreme Court of India, in its judgment of November 9, 2019, awarded the disputed land to a trust for building a Ram temple and directed that alternative land be allotted for a mosque. The Court treated the ASI findings as evidence of a pre-existing, non-Islamic structure of Hindu religious origin but explained that those findings did not establish that a temple had been demolished for the purpose of constructing the mosque. It condemned the unlawful 1992 demolition and held that ownership had to be determined from legally admissible evidence of possession rather than archaeology alone. A new temple was consecrated in January 2024. The case is instructive because it shows a court deciding a property dispute on legal grounds, using but not resting on historical-archaeological claims, and because it left continued disagreement about what the evidence showed. Related disputes at other sites, and the Places of Worship Act of 1991, which sought to freeze the status of religious sites as of August 15, 1947 (with the Ayodhya case excepted), remain subjects of litigation and political argument.

Keeladi, near Madurai, offers a different kind of dispute. Archaeological occupation deposits, calibrated radiocarbon samples, inscribed potsherds, and claims about the date of Tamil-Brahmi writing must be evaluated as related but distinct classes of evidence. A radiocarbon result dates the sampled organic material and its archaeological context; it does not automatically date every nearby pot inscription or prove the earliest possible appearance of a script. The Tamil Nadu archaeology department’s interpretation of the site has attracted interest in the antiquity of Tamil urban culture. In May 2025, the Archaeological Survey of India requested that the excavator K. Amarnath Ramakrishna revise the report on the 2014–16 seasons, seeking clearer chronological and classificatory justification; Ramakrishna defended the evidentiary basis of his analysis. The request is evidence of an ongoing methodological and institutional dispute, not by itself proof that the site’s discoveries were invalid or that every proposed date was established. Students should distinguish published excavation context, scientific dating, interpretation, and political claims before drawing conclusions.

The section's conclusion is that responsible scholarship evaluates evidence on its terms, states its limits, and does not claim to resolve every present-day dispute. Historians and archaeologists can say what was found, how it was dated, and what interpretations are consistent with it. They cannot say who ought to own a site or what a society should do about a historical grievance, which are questions of law, ethics, and politics. A scholarly profession that over-claims for politics, or that is made to produce preferred answers, loses the credibility on which its contribution depends.

Historical Vocabulary
Archaeological Provenance, Heritage Preservation, Historical Authenticity, and Historical Interpretation

Provenance is the documented origin and find context of an object or the chain of custody of a document. Heritage preservation refers to the conservation, protection, and presentation of historic buildings, sites, objects, and traditions. Historical authenticity concerns whether an object or source is what it purports to be and whether it dates from the time and place claimed. Historical interpretation is the explanation historians build on authenticated evidence.

The terms mark successive stages of argument: first establish that an object is genuine and where it came from, then preserve it, then interpret it. Disputes often arise because one stage is skipped. An object without provenance may be impossible to interpret reliably, and an interpretation that outruns the evidence is not made more authoritative by public support.

Historical Debate

Scholarly Evidence, Public Heritage, and Competing Claims about Monuments

The question: When a monument or site is claimed by more than one community, what role should archaeological and historical evidence play, and how much can it settle?

Interpretation A — Evidence should guide public decisions: Proponents argue that excavation and documentary evidence can show what stood at a site and when, that this evidence should inform courts and public bodies, and that communities have a legitimate interest in knowing whether a site was used for different purposes in the past. They note that archaeology can correct myths and can confirm traditions in some cases.
Interpretation B — Evidence cannot decide such disputes: Others argue that archaeological findings rarely resolve questions about intent, rights, or present obligations, that excavation can itself be politicized, and that claims of historical wrongs several centuries old cannot ethically determine present property or worship rights. They emphasize that communities' continuous practice and legal protections, and the principle of a shared, plural heritage, should carry weight, and caution that reopening historical grievances can be used for political mobilization.

Evidence: Both sides rely on excavation reports, dated structures, inscriptions, travelers' accounts, and records of worship and ownership. The evidence is typically partial: it can show the presence of earlier structures or reuse of materials, but it rarely shows why a building was erected, destroyed, or converted, or the nature of continuous use.

Why it matters: How the relationship is understood determines whether historical research is treated as a tool for adjudicating claims or as a source of understanding that informs, but does not determine, legal and political outcomes. Most professional historians and archaeologists hold that the evidence must be reported accurately and that its limits must be respected whichever way the argument runs.

Student task: Using the Ayodhya example in this section, distinguish three questions: What did the excavations find? What do those findings suggest about the site's history? What should be done about ownership and use of the site today? Explain which of these historians can answer and which belong to law, ethics, or politics.

Section 111.16

Literature, Film, Digital Media, and the Public Memory of History

Chapter 108 examined South Asian media as a cultural industry. This section is concerned with a narrower question: how fiction, film, television, and the internet shape what people believe about the past. For most people, a historical novel, a blockbuster, or a viral video has far greater influence than a scholarly book. This is not inherently a problem, since art has always interpreted history. It becomes one when artistic invention is mistaken for evidence or when media are used deliberately to mislead.

Literature has been among the most powerful carriers of historical memory. Partition fiction by Manto, Sahni, and Hyder, Bengali novels about the 1943 famine and the Liberation War, Sri Lankan English and Tamil literature on the civil war, and Urdu and Hindi literature about the Mughal court all offer accounts that complement or challenge official narratives. Historical films and television have likewise dramatized the Mughal period, the Maratha wars, the independence movement, Partition, and the wars of the postcolonial period. Their approach varies from careful reconstruction to romanticized spectacle. Productions that depict ancient or medieval kingdoms have at times provoked protests from groups who claimed misrepresentation of their ancestors or religion, and some have been censored, delayed, or altered in response to political or legal pressure. These controversies show how commercial incentives, audience expectations, state censorship, and political advocacy shape historical storytelling.

The key distinction for students is between artistic interpretation and documentary evidence. A film may legitimately invent dialogue, compress events, or create composite characters in order to dramatize a theme. It becomes misleading when it claims to depict what actually happened while altering the record, or when the audience takes its inventions for fact. Evaluating such works requires asking what the creators claim, which sources they used, what they changed, and why. Historical fiction can nevertheless be valuable evidence of how a society has remembered an event at the time of its making.

Diaspora communities play a distinctive role. South Asian communities in Britain, North America, the Gulf, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Caribbean preserve and reinterpret histories of migration, indentured labor, displacement, and national origin (Chapter 75). Their memories, expressed in memoirs, community museums, film, and festivals, sometimes differ from those of the societies they left, and they may be more or less nationalist, more or less critical. Since the 1990s, diaspora funding and advocacy have also influenced debates over textbooks and public commemoration in some countries of residence, adding another layer to the politics of memory.

Digital technology has multiplied both access and distortion. Digitization has opened archives, manuscripts, newspapers, and oral-history collections to anyone with an internet connection, a major democratization of historical evidence. At the same time, social media and messaging platforms spread simplified, unverified, or fabricated historical claims at great speed, often through images, memes, and short videos that strip context. Misattributed quotations, forged documents, doctored photographs, and invented "ancient" achievements circulate widely, and by 2026 synthetic images and AI-generated text and video have made fabrication cheaper and harder to detect. Students should apply the same discipline to a viral post as to any other source: identify its origin, check it against reliable independent evidence, and ask who benefits from its circulation. Digital literacy has therefore become part of historical literacy, a point developed further in §111.19.

Popular history writing and television debate add a further layer. Books addressed to general readers by journalists, politicians, and retired officials, as well as by academic historians, have large audiences and set the terms of public discussion. Some are rigorous and widely cited by specialists, while others rely on selected sources. A useful rule for students is to check whether such a work cites verifiable sources, engages with those who disagree, and distinguishes what it can show from what it suspects. Programs in which commentators argue over history in a few minutes of broadcast time seldom allow this kind of care, which is one reason why the slower disciplines of the seminar, the archive, and the peer-reviewed journal remain important.

Section 111.17

Unity, Diversity, and the Historical Idea of South Asia

Is South Asia a coherent historical region? Chapter 1 began with this question, and the preceding 110 chapters allow a better-informed answer. The geographic foundations are real. The Himalaya and the Hindu Kush, the Indian Ocean, and the deserts and forests of the east and west give the region natural boundaries, though permeable ones, as the passes of the northwest and the sea routes of the monsoon show. Two great river systems, the Indus and the Ganges–Brahmaputra, and the rhythm of the monsoon shaped agriculture, settlement, and politics from the farming villages of Chapter 2 to the climate challenges of Chapter 109. These conditions did not determine history, but they set recurring problems of water, land, and movement.

Connections across the region are equally well documented. Trade routes carried goods from the Indus cities to Mesopotamia and, later, spices, textiles, and metals across the Indian Ocean (Chapters 25 and 49). Religious traditions spread across political boundaries: Buddhism from the Gangetic plain to Sri Lanka, the Himalaya, and Southeast Asia, Hindu devotional movements across the peninsula, Islam through Sufi networks and maritime merchants, and Sikhism through the Punjab. Sanskrit and Persian served as languages of learning and administration across enormous distances, and the Tamil, Bengali, and other vernacular traditions interacted with them. Imperial expansions, from the Mauryas to the British, created, at different moments, political frameworks that linked much of the region. Migration, labor, and, more recently, remittances and digital communication have kept those links active (Chapter 106).

Against these connections stand real and persistent differences. South Asia contains languages from several families, among them Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic, and others, as well as several scripts. Its religious landscape includes Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs, Christians, and many local traditions. Its regions, from Kashmir to Kerala and Balochistan to Assam, have distinctive histories, and its political experience since 1947 has diverged greatly. Politically, the region has seldom been united. Even the largest empires, as Chapter 18 on the Mauryas and Chapter 54 on Aurangzeb showed, never ruled all of it under a single administration, and the British Raj, though larger than any predecessor, left out Nepal, Bhutan, and, as a separate colony, Ceylon. The evidence therefore supports neither the claim of an eternal political unity nor the claim that the region is merely a modern invention of area-studies departments.

Modern borders cut across older historical regions. Punjab was divided in 1947 between India and Pakistan, and Bengal between India and what became Pakistan and, after 1971, Bangladesh. Kashmir's divided territory is the subject of a continuing dispute. Himalayan and Baloch communities straddle several borders, and the Tamil and Sinhala populations of Sri Lanka have connections across the Palk Strait. Borders also divide watersheds and economic regions, as with the Indus waters treaty and the shared rivers of the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta. Such divisions have made historical regions into border regions, and they raise the problem, discussed in Chapter 1, of studying historical regions that no longer correspond to political units.

The vocabulary used for the region is itself historical. "India" derives from the Greek and Persian names for the Indus; "Hindustan" comes from Persian usage and referred to different areas at different times; "Bharat," used in the Indian Constitution alongside "India," draws on ancient Sanskrit tradition, and "Bharatavarsha" appears in the Puranas and, in a related form, in the Hathigumpha inscription of Kharavela. "The Indian subcontinent" was a colonial-era geographic phrase, "South Asia" a mid-twentieth-century term that gained currency in area studies and in regional institutions such as SAARC, and "Pakistan" was proposed as a name in 1933 by Choudhry Rahmat Ali before it became a state. Each term carries different assumptions, and none is neutral. This textbook uses "South Asia" for the region and has tried to use more specific names for particular places and times.

Section 111.18

Marginalized Histories and the Expansion of Historical Interpretation

For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, written history in and about South Asia focused on rulers, dynasties, high politics, and elite culture, because those were the subjects on which the surviving sources were most abundant and because states and nationalist movements wanted such histories. This textbook has tried, within the limits of the evidence, to include villagers, merchants, women, laborers, forest peoples, and minorities. That effort rests on several decades of changes in historical practice.

Social history, influenced by Marxist approaches and by scholars such as D. D. Kosambi, turned attention to land, class, and production. In the early 1980s, a group of historians led by Ranajit Guha launched Subaltern Studies, which argued that elite-centered histories, whether colonial or nationalist, ignored the autonomous political action of peasants, tribal communities, and workers. The project influenced scholars well beyond South Asia, and it also drew criticism, including from historians who thought it exaggerated the autonomy of subaltern consciousness or relied too much on literary theory. Feminist historians, among them Uma Chakravarti and Kumkum Sangari and Sudesh Vaid, examined women's lives, the construction of gender in law and ideology, and women's participation in reform and nationalism. Dalit intellectuals, from Jotirao Phule and Ambedkar to later scholars, wrote histories of caste exclusion and resistance that challenged both Brahmanical and nationalist narratives. Adivasi history, studied through movements such as that led by Birsa Munda and through ecological and economic analysis, showed forest and hill communities as historical actors in their own right, not as survivals from a primitive past.

Other approaches broadened the geographic frame. Studies of Northeast India, the Himalayan belt, and the highlands of Southeast Asia, including Willem van Schendel's concept of "Zomia" and James Scott's argument about state evasion in highland societies, challenged the assumption that all societies were or wished to be part of states. Work on the Ahom chronicles of Assam, the oral epics of pastoralists, and the records of Himalayan monasteries showed alternative historical traditions. Indian Ocean history, for example Sugata Bose's work on the networks linking South Asia to East Africa, Arabia, and Southeast Asia, treated the sea as a historical region in itself and gave attention to merchants, sailors, and migrants. Environmental history, addressed in Chapter 109, added another dimension by studying forests, rivers, famine, and climate. Oral history, as §111.12 described, brought testimony from those excluded from archives.

The effect of these approaches has been to change the questions asked and the sources considered, and in some cases to revise established interpretations. Historians now discuss caste as a historically evolving institution and regard women's labor and household strategies as central to economic history. They examine peasant protest as politics rather than disorder. These revisions do not necessarily invalidate earlier findings: the chronology of dynasties, the major legal and administrative changes, and the key economic trends documented by earlier historians remain the basis for much current work. The new approaches add and complicate rather than simply replace.

A caution is required. Expanding the historical record does not mean replacing one exclusive narrative with another. A history written only from the perspective of one marginalized community would be as partial as one written only from that of rulers. The aim is a fuller account in which different experiences are placed in relation to one another, and in which claims about any group, whether dominant or subordinate, are tested against evidence. Marginalized groups are also not uniform: there were hierarchies within Dalit, Adivasi, and women's communities, and different groups sometimes clashed. The best recent scholarship pays attention to such internal differences.

Language and access shape which histories can be written. Much of the evidence for marginalized communities lies in vernacular sources, in Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Punjabi, Sinhala, Assamese, and many other languages, in local records, in Dalit autobiographies, in the oral traditions of forest and pastoral communities, and in missionary and court records that mention such groups only in passing. Researchers need linguistic skills, patient fieldwork, and ethical care in working with communities. Where sources are scarce, historians sometimes read colonial and elite records "against the grain," looking for traces of people the authors did not intend to describe, and they must be careful not to claim more than such traces can support.

Section 111.19

Historical Knowledge in the Twenty-First Century: Evidence, Debate, and Responsibility

The practice of history has changed substantially in recent decades. Archaeological science, which includes high-precision radiocarbon dating, isotopic analysis of bones and teeth to trace diet and mobility, residue analysis of pottery, archaeobotany, and remote sensing by satellite and LiDAR, has made it possible to answer questions that were previously unreachable. The 2025 study proposing a later chronology for Mehrgarh’s aceramic Neolithic cemetery, discussed in Chapter 2, illustrates how a new set of dated samples can challenge established models without by itself dating every occupation across a large site. Ancient-DNA research, noted in §111.3, has opened an entirely new source on population history. Linguistic research, using computational and comparative methods, continues to refine models of language spread. Digital archives have made manuscripts, newspapers, and government records available worldwide, and oral-history collections have preserved testimony that would otherwise be lost. Machine tools for searching and transcribing texts, including large collections of handwritten documents, are beginning to change how historians work.

These advances do not replace the old disciplines of source criticism. Historians must still establish when and by whom a source was produced, test its claims against independent evidence, attend to translation and the meaning of terms in their own time, and specify the degree of certainty in their conclusions. Chronology, provenance, and corroboration remain fundamental. New techniques bring their own risks: scientific results can be over-interpreted, a single study can be treated as definitive, and technical authority can be used rhetorically. The proper response is to treat new results as evidence to be integrated with other evidence, and to be revised as further work appears.

The distinction between legitimate disagreement and misuse of evidence matters most in public debate. Historians legitimately differ about the dating of Keeladi, the role of migration in the spread of Indo-Aryan languages, the extent of the drain of wealth under colonial rule, or the motives of Aurangzeb. They do not legitimately differ about whether forged documents are authentic, whether the 1943 famine occurred, or whether violence took place in Partition. Where public arguments rest on invented evidence or on denial of established events, the correct response is to correct the record, not to treat the matter as a balanced controversy. Equally, where claims are matters of real scholarly uncertainty, pretending to certainty in either direction is a failure of honesty.

Historians carry particular responsibilities in this field. They must report evidence accurately, including evidence that complicates their own views, and disclose uncertainty. They must treat contested religious identities, communal violence, colonialism, caste, migration, and national origins with attention to the consequences their words may have for living communities, without allowing that concern to censor the evidence. They have obligations in preserving materials, including documents, oral recordings, and monuments, and in ensuring that vulnerable communities, whose members may be endangered by the publication of certain information, are represented fairly and with consent. They also have a duty to the public to explain how historical knowledge is produced so that it can be judged.

Translation raises a further problem. A great part of South Asia's historical record is in Sanskrit, Pali, Persian, Arabic, Tamil, and other languages, and its meaning depends on translators' choices. A single term, such as dharma, jihad, or kafir, can be rendered differently in different times and contexts, and popular arguments sometimes turn on a mistranslation. Machine translation and automated transcription are expanding access but can introduce errors that are not obvious to non-specialists. Responsible practice involves consulting several translations, identifying the language of the original, and relying on specialists for sensitive or contested passages.

Finally, students need to evaluate historical claims independently. A claim does not become true because it appears in a textbook, a government publication, a religious tradition, or a popular video. Useful questions include: What is the source? What kind of evidence is offered? Who produced the claim and what is their interest? Is it consistent with other evidence? Do specialists in the field accept it, and if not, why? Is the certainty claimed proportionate to the evidence? Textbooks, including this one, are also interpretations that rest on the current state of scholarship, and they should be read critically and updated as research advances.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Transformation of Historical Research Methods and the Interpretation of South Asia's Past

What changed?

  • Sources expanded from texts and monuments to include scientific dating, ancient DNA, oral testimony, digital archives, and satellite survey
  • Subjects widened from rulers and dynasties to women, laborers, Dalits, Adivasi communities, pastoralists, and borderland peoples
  • Frameworks moved from religious periodization and racial theory toward regional, social, economic, and environmental analysis
  • Practitioners shifted from largely European and elite South Asian scholars to a global community that includes many regional and local historians

What remained continuous?

  • The need for source criticism, chronology, and corroboration
  • The influence of present concerns on historical questions
  • The use of history in political and religious argument
  • Incomplete evidence, especially for ancient periods and for communities that left few records

Who experienced change most directly? Researchers with access to new technologies and archives experienced the change most directly, as did communities whose histories were newly studied. Who experienced greater continuity? Those who encounter history mainly through schools and popular media often continued to receive older frameworks, which is why public understanding frequently lags behind scholarship.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity shows that historical knowledge is cumulative and revisable, neither a fixed inheritance nor a purely subjective construction.

Section 111.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: The Meaning of South Asia's Past

The preceding 110 chapters traced a history of more than five thousand years of urban and rural life, and of tens of thousands of years of human presence, in one of the most varied regions of the world. Volume I began with the geography of South Asia and the long process, traced in Chapter 2, by which hunter-gatherers and then farming communities in the northwest and elsewhere built villages, domesticated plants and animals, developed crafts, and exchanged goods over long distances. From these foundations arose the Indus Civilization (Chapters 3 and 4), a planned urban society that left no deciphered writing, no identified rulers, and an enduring set of questions. The Vedic and early Iron Age societies (Chapters 5 through 8), the second urbanization, the new religious and philosophical movements of Buddhism, Jainism, and the Upanishads (Chapters 9 through 13), and the encounters with Persia and Alexander (Chapter 14) produced the first territorial empire of the Mauryas (Chapters 15 through 18), and the wider world of Kushans, Satavahanas, Sangam Tamilakam, and the Indian Ocean trade of Chapters 19 through 25. The Gupta age and its classical culture (Chapters 26 through 30) closed the volume with a transition rather than an end.

Volume II showed an early medieval South Asia of regional kingdoms: Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, Pallavas, Cholas, Palas, Pratiharas, and Rajput polities, with temple-centered economies, land grants, and growing regional languages (Chapters 31 through 37). Muslim communities arrived by sea and through Sindh, and Central Asian dynasties established the Delhi Sultanate (Chapters 38 through 44), while Vijayanagara, the Bahmanis and the regional sultanates, Bhakti, Sufism, and Sikhism, and the Indian Ocean world transformed politics and religion (Chapters 45 through 49). Volume III, on the Mughals, Marathas, Sikhs, Mysore, and the successor states, described a large early modern empire and its reconfiguration into regional powers, as European trading companies became participants in South Asian politics (Chapters 50 through 61). Volume IV showed the conquest of Bengal and then the subcontinent by the East India Company, the Rebellion of 1857, and the high colonial state, with its economic, social, and intellectual transformations (Chapters 62 through 75). Volume V traced nationalism in its many forms, the world wars, negotiations, Partition, and independence (Chapters 76 through 87), and Volume VI followed the postcolonial states of India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives through development, conflict, liberalization, and the economic, social, environmental, and international changes of the early twenty-first century (Chapters 88 through 110).

Several patterns run through this sequence. First, change was repeated and large: the shift from foraging to farming, the rise and transformation of cities, the formation and collapse of empires, the spread of religions, the movement of peoples, the commercialization of agriculture, colonial conquest, mass politics, partition, and economic liberalization each reshaped society. Second, continuity was real but selective. Certain cultural traditions, languages, religious institutions, village and household structures, and geographic constraints persisted over very long periods, yet they were repeatedly reinterpreted, and they did not form a single unbroken line. Third, regional diversity was a constant. The Indus plain, the Gangetic basin, the Deccan, Tamilakam, Bengal, Kashmir, Sri Lanka, and the Himalaya each had distinct trajectories, and no single center represented the whole. Fourth, the region was always connected to the wider world, through Central Asia, Iran, the Indian Ocean, East Asia, Europe, and global migration and trade, and South Asians were agents in these connections, not only recipients of external influence.

South Asian history cannot be reduced to any of three tempting simplifications. It is not a story of uninterrupted national unity, since modern nations are products of recent history and the region was politically divided for most of its past. It is not a story of permanent civilizational conflict, since the evidence shows coexistence, exchange, and cooperation alongside real episodes of violence and persecution, and since religious communities were not unchanging political blocs. And it is not a story of an inevitable progression toward modern states, since colonial conquest, Partition, and the particular forms of the postcolonial states resulted from contingent decisions and contested possibilities. Each simplification has been used in political argument, and each is contradicted by the evidence.

The chapter has also argued that how the past is known is itself part of history. The rediscovery of the Indus Civilization in 1924 changed what could be said about South Asia's antiquity and then became a subject of national claims. The Indo-Aryan debate moved from racial theory through invasion hypotheses to genetic and linguistic models, and in doing so showed how scientific, colonial, and nationalist interests interacted. Colonial classification, nationalist and religious narratives, Partition memory, and the competing memories of postcolonial conflict all demonstrate that communities build stories about the past that serve their needs. Historians study those stories as part of history and test factual claims within them against evidence. The expansion of historical scholarship to include women, Dalits, Adivasi communities, religious minorities, laborers, and borderland peoples has enriched the record without making it less demanding.

The argument of this textbook can now be stated. South Asia is best understood as a historically interconnected but internally diverse region whose peoples have continually reshaped their institutions, cultures, identities, and understandings of their past. The connections were real: shared geography and monsoon, long-distance trade, migration, religious and intellectual exchange, imperial expansion, and the shared experience of colonialism and its aftermath. The diversity was equally real, in language, religion, social structure, and political experience. A history that emphasizes only connection becomes a story of false unity, and one that emphasizes only difference ignores the interactions that tied the region together. The truth lies in holding the two together and in following the evidence where it leads.

Finally, the historian's responsibilities return. They are to investigate evidence carefully, to say plainly what is known, what is inferred, and what is uncertain, to interpret differences among communities fairly, and to recognize that knowledge remains open to refinement. Just as the announcement of 1924 transformed what scholars thought they knew, further discoveries, whether of a bilingual Indus text, new genomes, newly opened archives, or oral testimony not yet recorded, will alter the picture. Students who finish this textbook should not hold the account in it as final but should be able to ask how it was constructed, what evidence supports it, whom it includes and omits, and what might change it. That capacity to question and test, and not any single conclusion about the meaning of South Asia's past, is the textbook's main intended result.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 111 concludes the textbook by asking how South Asia's past is known, remembered, and used. It distinguishes history, an evidence-based discipline, from memory, the way communities carry the past, and shows that both are essential subjects of study. It also distinguishes legitimate scholarly disagreement from claims lacking evidentiary support.

Division I returned to antiquity. The Indus Civilization was recovered through the work of the Archaeological Survey, with Daya Ram Sahni, Rakhaldas Banerji, and John Marshall prominent, and announced in 1924. It was divided by Partition between India and Pakistan, and it remains partly mysterious: the script is undeciphered, the languages unknown, and the political organization unclear. Modern constituencies have claimed it, though a civilization that flourished in the third millennium BCE cannot be assigned to a single modern nation or religion.

The debate over Indo-Aryan origins moved from nineteenth-century racial theory and the abandoned invasion hypothesis to current models that integrate linguistics, archaeology, and ancient DNA. Genetic ancestry shows population movement and mixture but does not by itself establish language or identity. Modern uses of antiquity, including the memory of Ashoka, Buddhist heritage, and the Vedas and epics, show how ancient pasts are drawn into national identity, though cultural continuity is different from the continuity of nations.

Division II examined the precolonial period. Regional traditions, such as Tamil, Sinhala, Kashmiri, Bengali, Nepali, Bhutanese, and Maldivian historical memory, show that no single center represents South Asia. The Delhi Sultanate and Mughal Empire were interpreted through a colonial periodization into Hindu, Muslim, and British periods that most historians now regard as misleading, though religion cannot be dismissed as a factor. Sacred places reflect both shared practice and conflict, and their histories are more layered than later claims suggest. Colonial knowledge systems, including Orientalist scholarship, the Archaeological Survey, the census, and the Linguistic Survey, both recovered the past and imposed classifications that shaped later identities.

Division III turned to colonialism, nationalism, and Partition. Historians agree on much of the colonial record while disagreeing about weight and interpretation, and memories of colonial rule differ among India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives. Anticolonial nationalism was a plurality of movements with different aims. The independence-era speeches of Nehru and Jinnah express civic ideals but must be compared with later practice. Partition memory, preserved in oral history, family objects, and literature, supplements official records, but testimony has limits and raises ethical responsibilities.

Division IV considered postcolonial memory and public history. The wars and conflicts of 1971, the Sri Lankan Civil War, Nepal's Maoist conflict and transition, Bhutan's state formation, and Kashmir have produced divergent memories. Schools, museums, and archives select and shape public understanding, and curricular change is a legitimate area of debate when it remains open to scrutiny. Archaeology can establish dates, provenance, and construction sequences but cannot settle questions of right and ownership, as the Ayodhya case illustrates. Literature, film, and digital media shape popular memory, and digital fabrication makes historical literacy more important.

Division V stepped back. South Asia is neither a region of eternal unity nor a mere modern invention: shared geography, trade, religion, and migration connected it, while language, religion, region, and politics divided it, and modern borders cut across older regions. Historiography has widened through social, feminist, Subaltern, Dalit, Adivasi, borderland, and Indian Ocean scholarship without invalidating all earlier work. Historical methods have changed through scientific dating, genomics, and digital archives, yet source criticism remains fundamental, and historians bear responsibilities for accuracy, fairness, and care for communities.

The synthesis argues that South Asia is best understood as a historically interconnected but internally diverse region whose peoples have continually reshaped their institutions, cultures, identities, and understandings of their past. Its history cannot be reduced to uninterrupted national unity, permanent civilizational conflict, or an inevitable progression toward modern states.

The mid-twentieth-century idea of an organized Aryan conquest of the Indus cities, abandoned by serious scholarship.

European scholarship about Asia, and, in Edward Said's critique, a discourse linked to imperial power.

Information, classifications, and histories produced by colonial institutions such as the census and surveys, often with the help of local scholars.
Periodization
Dividing the past into named periods, an analytical convention, not a natural division.

A historiographical project begun in 1982 that sought to recover the political action of peasants, workers, and other subordinate groups.
Oral history
Evidence gathered through recorded recollections of participants and witnesses.
Heritage
Inherited sites, objects, and traditions that a community values and seeks to preserve.
Syncretism
The blending of elements from different religious or cultural traditions; used cautiously because it can imply distinct pure traditions.
Dhamma
Ashoka's ethical program of moral conduct and tolerance, proclaimed in his edicts.
Mahavamsa
Pali chronicle of Sri Lanka composed by Buddhist monks, central to Sinhala historical memory.
Rajatarangini
Kalhana's twelfth-century Sanskrit chronicle of Kashmir.
Archaeological Survey of India
The colonial-era institution founded in 1861 for archaeological survey and conservation.
Drain theory
The nationalist argument that colonial policies transferred wealth from India to Britain.
Partition
The division of British India into India and Pakistan in 1947, accompanied by mass violence and displacement.
Tryst with destiny
Phrase from Nehru's midnight speech of August 14–15, 1947, on India's independence.
Language Movement
The 1950s Bengali movement in East Pakistan for recognition of Bengali, commemorated on February 21.
Places of Worship Act, 1991
Indian law seeking to preserve the religious character of places of worship as of August 15, 1947, with the Ayodhya dispute excepted.
Zomia
A term applied to the highland borderlands of South, Southeast, and East Asia, used to study societies at the margins of states.
Digital literacy
The ability to evaluate online information, including historical claims, for origin and reliability.

MGU Library Research Project

Tracing a Contested Past: A Case Study in History and Memory

This project asks you to investigate how a single historical subject is understood by historians and remembered by communities, and to produce an evidence-based comparison.

  1. Choose a subject. Examples include the Indus script and its claimed decipherments, Ashoka, the Mahavamsa, Somnath, Akbar or Aurangzeb, the Rebellion of 1857, the Bengal famine of 1943, Partition in a single city, the war of 1971, or the Sri Lankan Civil War.
  2. Gather three kinds of source. Select at least one scholarly study, one primary source or archival record, and one example of public memory (a school text, a monument, a film, a commemoration, or a social media post).
  3. Apply source criticism. For each source, state who produced it, when, for what audience, and what it cannot show.
  4. Compare. Identify where the three kinds of source agree and diverge, and decide which disagreements concern evidence, which interpretation, and which present-day politics.
  5. Write. In 5–7 pages (or an equivalent presentation), present your findings, state what remains uncertain, and avoid claiming more than the evidence allows.

Chapter Reflection

This textbook began with the work of excavation: seeds, bricks, and bones read patiently as evidence for people who left no words we can read. It ends by returning to excavation, for the Indus discovery of 1924 is a reminder that what we know about South Asia's past was built by particular people, with particular tools and assumptions, and that it can be rebuilt. The first lesson of the final chapter is humility about what is known. The second is confidence that careful methods, honest acknowledgment of uncertainty, and openness to correction do produce reliable knowledge.

The chapter has also shown that the meaning of the past is contested because it matters. Communities draw on history to explain who they are, to claim rights, to mourn their dead, and to imagine futures. Those purposes are legitimate. They become dangerous when they demand a past that the evidence cannot support, or when invented evidence is used against neighbors. A student who understands the difference between a memory and a finding, between a source's testimony and its limits, and between a disagreement among scholars and a denial of fact has acquired a lasting civic skill.

The idea of South Asia that emerges from these 111 chapters is neither a single civilization nor a collection of unrelated national pasts. It is a region of linked and different societies, shaped by rivers, monsoons, mountains, and seas, by farming and trade, by religions that crossed boundaries, by empires and by resistance to them, and by partitions and the states that followed. Seeing this clearly makes it possible to take pride in achievements, to face violence and injustice honestly, and to understand neighbors whose memories differ.

The chapter's closing question is an invitation. When you next meet a historical claim, in a classroom, a museum, a film, a speech, or a message on your phone, ask what evidence supports it, who is making it and why, and what would change your mind. The history of South Asia, like all history, remains unfinished, and those who ask such questions are part of how it will continue to be written.
