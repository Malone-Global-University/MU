Gandhi and the Transformation of Indian Nationalism

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the afternoon of Sunday, 13 April 1919, thousands of people gathered in Jallianwala Bagh, a walled open space in the heart of Amritsar in Punjab. Many had come for Baisakhi, the spring festival and harvest-season holiday; many others came to hear speeches protesting the arrest of two local leaders and the new security legislation known as the Rowlatt Act. The Bagh had few exits. Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer led about ninety Indian soldiers to the main entrance, ordered them to open fire without warning on the crowd, and kept the firing going for roughly ten minutes. An official inquiry counted 379 dead; Indian estimates ran far higher. Within weeks the name Jallianwala Bagh was known across the subcontinent and well beyond it.

The massacre did not occur in a political vacuum, and it did not make Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi's leadership by itself. It stood at the center of a crisis that had been building since the end of the First World War: wartime sacrifice followed by inflation, epidemic disease, and poor harvests; constitutional promises that fell short of expectations; a government that wished to retain emergency powers in peacetime; and a Muslim public alarmed by the fate of the defeated Ottoman Empire. The same year that saw Amritsar also saw the first nationwide protest campaign that Gandhi directed. This chapter studies how that campaign, and the much larger Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–1922, changed the shape of Indian nationalism.

Gandhi was fifty when the Rowlatt agitation began. He had spent more than twenty years in South Africa, where he developed a method of political resistance he called satyagraha, usually translated as "holding firmly to truth." He had returned to India only in January 1915, spent his first years learning its political terrain, and organized three limited campaigns—Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda—before attempting anything on a national scale. Chapters 76 through 78 traced the Indian National Congress from a gathering of educated petitioners into a body divided between Moderates and Extremists, reunited in 1916 and radicalized by the war. Chapter 79 shows how that organization, and the wider political world around it, was rebuilt in the years from 1915 to 1924.

It is tempting to treat this story as the biography of one extraordinary man. This chapter resists that temptation. Gandhi's methods drew on older traditions of protest, boycott, and public meeting; his campaigns depended on lawyers, merchants, students, volunteers, and local organizers who often had their own aims; his alliance with the Khilafat movement brought in Muslim leaders who pursued independent theological and diplomatic goals; and the peasants, workers, and townspeople who joined his campaigns frequently understood "swaraj" in terms drawn from their own grievances. Gandhi's influence was real and large. It operated through, not over, these other political actors.

The chapter also takes seriously the tensions inside the new politics. Satyagraha demanded discipline, but mass movements are hard to discipline. The Khilafat alliance expanded the reach of nationalism but rested on a demand that many Hindu and Muslim participants understood differently. The Nagpur reforms of December 1920 opened Congress to far more members but did not erase inequalities of region, class, caste, and gender. The violence at Chauri Chaura in February 1922, and Gandhi's decision to halt the campaign in response, forced his supporters and critics alike to ask whether nonviolence was a strategy, a moral commitment, or both.

The evidence for these events is unusually rich and unusually contested. Gandhi wrote prolifically, and the Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi preserve his letters, speeches, and articles. The Hunter Committee collected testimony on the Punjab disturbances, and the Indian National Congress conducted its own inquiry. Congress constitutions, Khilafat resolutions, police and magistrates' reports, newspapers, and later memoirs add other voices. Yet each of these sources has a viewpoint, and historians have interpreted them quite differently. This chapter introduces the evidence, identifies its limits, and invites students to weigh competing interpretations.

Chapter 79 stands between Chapter 78, which examined the First World War and the political expectations it created, and Chapter 80, which examines the peasant, labor, communist, non-Brahmin, Dalit, and women's movements that cannot be reduced to Congress or Gandhi. It ends in 1924, before the Simon Commission, the Lahore Congress, and the Salt March taken up in Chapter 81. Its central question is how Gandhi transformed the methods, organization, and social reach of Indian nationalism, and what the successes and contradictions of his first mass campaigns revealed about the possibilities and limits of nonviolent resistance.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Gandhi's political formation in South Africa from 1893 to 1914 and explain why it should not be read as the finished origin of his later Indian politics.
  2. Explain satyagraha as both an ethical philosophy and a practical method of collective resistance, including its internal difficulties.
  3. Compare the Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda campaigns in terms of participants, grievances, legal framework, method, and outcome.
  4. Analyze the origins and provisions of the Rowlatt legislation and the reasons it provoked organized opposition across political camps.
  5. Reconstruct the events of April 1919 in Delhi, Punjab, and Gujarat, distinguishing the Rowlatt satyagraha from the Amritsar massacre.
  6. Assess Jallianwala Bagh and the Punjab repression as events with independent local histories and as turning points in Indian perceptions of colonial legitimacy.
  7. Distinguish the Khilafat movement's religious, diplomatic, and political objectives from those of the Muslim League and of Gandhi.
  8. Evaluate the opportunities and tensions in the Hindu–Muslim alliance of 1919–1922.
  9. Describe the Non-Cooperation program adopted at Calcutta in 1920 and the organizational reforms adopted at Nagpur.
  10. Interpret how participants in different regions and social groups understood swaraj and non-cooperation in different ways.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of Gandhi's withdrawal of the movement after Chauri Chaura in February 1922.
  12. Contextualize Gandhi's trial, the Swarajist debate, and the aftermath of 1922–1924 within the longer transformation of Indian nationalism.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Gandhi's experience of racial discrimination and political organization in South Africa shape, but not predetermine, his later methods in India?
  2. Is satyagraha best understood as a moral philosophy, a political technique, or a combination? What difficulties arise in applying it to large populations?
  3. Why did Gandhi begin his Indian career with local campaigns rather than an immediate national movement, and what did each campaign teach him?
  4. Why did legislation that was never used in practice provoke one of the largest protest movements India had yet seen?
  5. How should historians weigh the Amritsar massacre as an event in Punjab history, as an act of colonial violence, and as a turning point in nationalist politics?
  6. Why did the Ottoman caliphate matter to many Indian Muslims, and how independent was the Khilafat movement of Gandhi's leadership?
  7. How did the Nagpur reforms change the structure of the Congress, and who was still excluded or marginal?
  8. Did participants in Non-Cooperation understand swaraj as Gandhi and the Congress leadership did?
  9. What does the violence of 1921–1922, including Malabar and Chauri Chaura, reveal about the limits of nonviolent discipline in a mass movement?
  10. Was the 1922 withdrawal a defense of principle, a political error, or something more complicated?

Historical Context

The Congress before Gandhi. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, was for its first three decades an annual assembly of educated Indians—lawyers, journalists, teachers, landowners, and some merchants—who presented petitions and resolutions to the colonial government and to British opinion (Chapter 76). Its sessions were festive but its political activity between sessions was thin. After the partition of Bengal in 1905 and the Swadeshi movement (Chapter 77), boycott, national education, and mass meetings entered nationalist politics, and the Surat split of 1907 divided Moderates from Extremists. The Muslim League, founded in 1906, pursued a constitutional program centered on safeguards for Muslim representation. During the First World War (Chapter 78), the Home Rule Leagues of Annie Besant and Bal Gangadhar Tilak revived popular agitation, and the Lucknow Pact of 1916 brought Congress and the League into a limited alliance. These were important foundations. But before 1919, Congress had no permanent local organization reaching most villages, and its leadership was drawn from a narrow social range.

Gandhi's South African background. Gandhi arrived in the British colony of Natal in 1893 as a young barrister engaged by a Gujarati Muslim trading firm. South Africa's Indian population was diverse: merchants, mainly Gujarati and many of them Muslim; indentured laborers brought from 1860 mainly from the Madras Presidency and from northern India; former indentured workers who had stayed on; and a smaller group of professionals and clerks. These groups experienced discrimination differently, and Gandhi's own first concerns were closest to the merchants'. Over two decades he built institutions—a political association, a newspaper, communal settlements—and developed the method of satyagraha in campaigns against discriminatory legislation. He left South Africa in 1914 with a reputation among Indian nationalists but not yet a national political position within India.

War and its consequences. The First World War imposed heavy burdens on India. Hundreds of thousands of Indian soldiers served overseas, the government raised taxes and borrowed heavily, prices rose sharply, and the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 killed millions across the subcontinent. Poor monsoons in 1918 and again in 1920 aggravated rural distress. The British government's declaration of August 1917 promised the "gradual development of self-governing institutions" and "responsible government" for India, and the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms, enacted as the Government of India Act of 1919, introduced dyarchy in the provinces. Many Indians judged these reforms inadequate, especially while the government sought to prolong its wartime security powers. Defeat of the Ottoman Empire and the proposed peace settlement meanwhile alarmed many Muslims. The combination of economic hardship, disappointed political expectation, and fear of repression created the setting in which an all-India campaign became possible.

The colonial state and its structure. Students should recall from earlier chapters that British India was governed by a Viceroy and a bureaucracy dominated by the Indian Civil Service, supported by police and an army recruited heavily from specific regions, especially Punjab. Provincial governors and lieutenant-governors held wide discretion. Press laws, sedition laws, and wartime emergency regulations gave officials extensive power over public speech and assembly. The Government of India's anxiety about revolutionary conspiracy, rooted in Bengal and in the Ghadar movement among overseas Punjabis, shaped the security legislation of 1919. Official fears, popular grievances, and Gandhi's new methods all met in Punjab and Gujarat in the spring of 1919.

Evidence and interpretation. Historians of this period use Gandhi's own writings, Congress and Khilafat records, official inquiries, intelligence reports, newspapers in many languages, and memoirs. These sources must be read with attention to perspective. Gandhi's accounts, especially his autobiography written later, present events through his own moral framework. Official reports reflect the anxieties of administrators. Local sources, such as depositions collected after Chauri Chaura or oral memory studied by Shahid Amin, show how participants described events differently. This chapter draws on all of these, and its source windows and historical debate invite students to analyze how interpretation depends on evidence.

Gandhi's Political Formation and Arrival in India

§§79.1–79.4 · South Africa, satyagraha, return to India, and Champaran

Section 79.1

Gandhi in South Africa: Racism, Imperial Citizenship, and Political Awakening

Gandhi reached Durban in May 1893, aged twenty-three, to assist in a commercial lawsuit for the Gujarati Muslim firm of Dada Abdulla and Company. He had trained in London as a barrister and had struggled to establish a practice in Bombay and Rajkot. South Africa offered work and distance from failure. It also offered a political education he had not expected. Within weeks he experienced the racial order of the colonies directly, most famously when he was removed from a first-class railway compartment at Pietermaritzburg despite holding a ticket. Gandhi later treated this episode as a turning point, and historians agree that it was significant, but they also caution that his political outlook developed gradually over many years rather than in a single night.

The legal position of Indians in southern Africa was complex and varied by territory. Natal, a British colony, and the Transvaal, a Boer republic until the South African War of 1899–1902, imposed different restrictions on Indian residence, trade, movement, and voting. Indians had arrived in Natal in large numbers from 1860 as indentured laborers on sugar plantations, mostly from the Madras Presidency and northern India; free "passenger" Indians, mainly Gujarati merchants, followed. These groups had very different interests. Merchants were concerned with trading licenses, property rights, and the franchise. Indentured laborers faced harsh working conditions and, after their contracts expired, a punitive annual tax of three pounds imposed in 1895 on those who did not re-indenture or return to India. Former indentured workers and their families formed a third community whose concerns differed again. Gandhi's earliest campaigns spoke chiefly for the merchant and professional class, and only later did he engage extensively with the grievances of laborers.

In 1894 Gandhi helped found the Natal Indian Congress, which gave Indians in the colony a permanent political association, a subscription base, and a channel for petitions to colonial and imperial authorities. In 1903 he began to publish Indian Opinion, a multilingual weekly that circulated among Indians in the region and gave him practical experience of political journalism. His early strategy was constitutional and loyalist. Gandhi appealed to Indians' status as subjects of the British Crown and argued that imperial citizenship entailed equal rights under law. During the South African War and the Zulu uprising of 1906 he organized Indian ambulance and stretcher-bearer units to assist British forces, a decision that reflected both his faith in imperial promises at that stage and his desire to demonstrate Indian loyalty.

Historians have also drawn attention to the limits of Gandhi's early South African politics. In the 1890s and 1900s he sometimes wrote about Black Africans in ways that reflected the racial assumptions of the time and that distinguished Indians from Africans in claims for better treatment. Recent scholarship, including that of Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed, has criticized this record, while other historians emphasize that his views changed over time. A responsible account must acknowledge both the evidence and the debate. It must also resist treating Gandhi's South African years as the history of an already formed moral leader.

The turn toward direct resistance came after 1906, when the Transvaal government introduced the Asiatic Law Amendment Ordinance, which required Indians to register and carry certificates of identity. At a meeting in Johannesburg in September 1906, Indian participants pledged to resist the law and accept the penalties. This campaign, sustained for nearly eight years, involved burning registration certificates, courting imprisonment, and mass demonstrations. Gandhi established the Phoenix Settlement near Durban in 1904 and Tolstoy Farm near Johannesburg in 1910, communal settlements that housed participants and their families and that became experiments in simple living and shared labor. They also made the campaigns more dependent on a core of committed volunteers whose lives had been rearranged around the struggle.

The final and largest phase began in 1913. A South African court judgment that year denied legal validity to marriages not solemnized under Christian rites or registered under colonial law, which affected Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi marriages and offended Indian women and men alike. Indian women became participants in the campaign in unprecedented numbers. At the same time, the three-pound tax on former indentured workers became the focus of a mobilization of mineworkers and plantation laborers. In November 1913 Gandhi led a march of thousands of Indian workers and their families from Natal into the Transvaal, breaching colonial restrictions on interprovincial movement. Strikes spread through the coal mines and sugar estates, and the government responded with arrests and force. Negotiations with General Jan Smuts, then a leading South African minister, produced a settlement embodied in the Indian Relief Act of 1914, which abolished the three-pound tax and addressed the marriage question while leaving many Indian grievances unresolved.

Students should draw three cautions from this history. First, the South African campaigns were shaped by a specific setting: a small, geographically concentrated Indian population under a settler-colonial state, with limited electoral rights and dense family and commercial ties. Second, the movement relied on a coalition of merchants, laborers, and women whose interests were not identical. Third, Gandhi was in 1893 neither a mahatma nor a fully formed theorist. He became, over twenty years, a political organizer who learned from defeat as well as success. Section 79.2 turns to the method and philosophy that emerged from these experiences.

Section 79.2

Satyagraha: Truth, Nonviolence, Self-Discipline, and Political Resistance

The word satyagraha was devised in South Africa between 1906 and 1908. Gandhi had used the English phrase "passive resistance" to describe the campaign, but he came to find it misleading, since it suggested weakness, a tactic of the powerless, and even a form of hostility that merely lacked the means for violence. A contest held in Indian Opinion for a better term produced a suggestion from his associate Maganlal Gandhi, "sadagraha," meaning firmness in a good cause. Gandhi modified it to satyagraha: satya (truth) and agraha (holding firmly). The term named a method in which resisters sought to change an opponent's position by refusing to cooperate with what they judged to be unjust, while refusing also to harm the opponent.

Its ethical foundations rested on several linked ideas. Satya signified not only factual honesty but fidelity to what one believed to be right. Ahimsa, usually translated nonviolence, signified abstention from injury in act, speech, and intention; for Gandhi it was an active moral force and not mere abstention. Voluntary suffering, tapasya, was central: the resister accepted arrest, loss of property, and physical harm rather than inflicting them, in the hope that this would affect the conscience of the opponent and the public. Self-discipline, including fasting, simple living, and, in Gandhi's own practice, sexual abstinence from 1906, was intended to prepare the resister to endure these sacrifices. A satyagrahi also accepted legal penalties for civil disobedience rather than evading them, which distinguished the method from secret or violent resistance.

Gandhi's thinking drew on many sources, and historians stress that it cannot be reduced to one tradition. He was raised in a Gujarati milieu influenced by Vaishnava devotion and by the Jain emphasis on non-injury; Shrimad Rajchandra, a Jain-influenced Gujarati thinker, was an important early interlocutor. He read the Bhagavad Gita repeatedly and interpreted it as an allegory of the inner struggle. Christian sources, notably the Sermon on the Mount, affected him, as did the writings of Leo Tolstoy, with whom he corresponded in 1910. John Ruskin's Unto This Last influenced his ideas about labor and simple living, and Henry David Thoreau's essay on civil disobedience provided a precedent for refusing obedience to unjust laws. Gandhi absorbed these materials selectively and reinterpreted them, and it is a mistake either to claim that he simply imported Western ideas or to claim that he drew only on a single Indian inheritance.

In November 1909, on a ship returning from London to South Africa, Gandhi wrote Hind Swaraj ("Indian Home Rule") in Gujarati in the form of a dialogue between a Reader and an Editor. The book criticized modern industrial civilization, rejecting railways, modern medicine, and the legal profession as instruments that fastened Indians to a materialist order, and it argued that India's subjection resulted partly from Indians' own acceptance of that order. Gandhi defined real self-rule, swaraj, as first of all self-governance: mastery of oneself and a society organized around moral responsibility rather than the mere replacement of British by Indian rulers. He also presented satyagraha, which he called soul-force, as the proper means of resistance. The book was banned in Bombay Presidency in 1910, and Gandhi later stated that, while he had modified some views, he continued to hold its main arguments. Historians read it both as a revealing statement of his convictions and as a work whose program was not adopted wholesale by the Congress that he later led.

Satyagraha raised difficult practical and ethical questions that Gandhi and his critics debated. Who decides whether a law is so unjust that disobedience is justified? How can leaders ensure that thousands of participants, not all of whom accept Gandhi's philosophy, remain nonviolent? Does a leader's insistence on discipline become a form of authority that overrides participants' own judgment? And can a method developed among a few thousand committed resisters in South Africa operate in a population of hundreds of millions, differentiated by language, religion, class, and caste? Gandhi himself repeatedly acknowledged that satyagraha required training and character, and he sometimes suspended campaigns when he judged the discipline insufficient. These tensions are visible throughout this chapter.

Finally, satyagraha was not an exclusively abstract doctrine. It was a form of politics. It created dramatic confrontations that attracted press attention, forced officials to choose between concession and visible repression, and gave ordinary people a way of acting politically without weapons or secrecy. It also tied political action to constructive tasks: spinning, sanitation, education, and service. That fusion of ethics, organization, and political theater is the key to understanding why the method drew in people who had stayed outside the elite constitutional politics of the early Congress.

Historical Vocabulary
Satyagraha and "Passive Resistance"

Satyagraha is a Gujarati-Sanskrit coinage meaning, roughly, "holding firmly to truth." It names a method of resistance combining civil disobedience, voluntary suffering, and nonviolence toward the opponent. Gandhi coined it partly to distance his campaigns from "passive resistance," a phrase associated in Britain with suffragist and nonconformist protests and, in his view, connoting weakness or concealed hostility.

Modern writers sometimes use the two terms interchangeably, or equate satyagraha with all nonviolent protest. This blurs important distinctions. Satyagraha in Gandhi's usage required a specific ethical discipline, a willingness to accept penalties, and a commitment to persuade rather than coerce. Many later movements borrowed some of its techniques without sharing all its assumptions, and historians generally treat satyagraha as one tradition within a wider history of nonviolent resistance.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The Editor argues that British rule persists largely because Indians have accepted the values and institutions of modern industrial civilization, and that merely substituting Indian for British rulers would leave this civilization intact. True self-rule begins in self-control. The proper means of resistance is soul-force: refusing to obey unjust commands while accepting the penalties, rather than using violence.

Analysis: The text shows that Gandhi's concept of swaraj was broader than political independence and that he already linked political resistance to moral transformation. It is also a polemical, partly idealized argument, written in dialogue to persuade, and its rejection of railways, doctors, and lawyers was never adopted as Congress policy. Historians therefore use it carefully: as evidence of Gandhi's convictions, but not of the program of the movement he later led. Missing are the voices of laborers and women in South Africa and the perspectives of those who rejected his arguments.

Section 79.3

Gandhi Returns to India, 1915: Gokhale, the Ashram, and Political Preparation

Gandhi reached Bombay on 9 January 1915, after a stay in London that coincided with the outbreak of the First World War. He was already known in India through reports of the South African campaigns, and Indian nationalists, including the Moderate leader Gopal Krishna Gokhale, had raised money for the struggle. Gokhale had encouraged Gandhi to return and advised him to spend a year traveling and observing before taking a public political position. Gokhale died in February 1915, and Gandhi, who regarded him as a political guide, followed the advice. He traveled widely, frequently by third-class railway carriage, visiting Calcutta, Rangoon, Madras, and north Indian pilgrimage centers, and he began to see the conditions of ordinary Indians that Congress politics seldom addressed.

His public statements in these years were cautious. At the opening of Banaras Hindu University in February 1916 he gave a speech criticizing the display of wealth and the failure of the elite to address the poverty of the countryside; the speech offended some of the dignitaries present and showed his independent style. He was not an obvious candidate for national leadership. Many Congress leaders regarded him as an eccentric moralist. He did not join the Home Rule Leagues and he supported the war effort, even recruiting for the army in Kheda in 1918, a position that coexisted uneasily with his later non-cooperation. In 1916 he attended the Lucknow Congress, where he encountered peasants from Champaran, but he did not play a prominent role in the Congress–League compact described in Chapter 78.

In May 1915, Gandhi established the Satyagraha Ashram at Kochrab near Ahmedabad, on land lent by local supporters. In 1917 it moved to a larger site on the bank of the Sabarmati River, where it became the Sabarmati Ashram. Ahmedabad was a prosperous Gujarati city with a wealthy mercantile and industrial elite, many of them Jain and Vaishnava Banias who were sympathetic to Gandhi's ethical vocabulary; their financial support was crucial to his institutions. The ashram was a community of families and individuals who took vows, including truthfulness, nonviolence, celibacy for those who took the vow, control of the palate, non-stealing, non-possession, swadeshi, and the removal of untouchability. Its daily routine included prayer, manual labor, spinning, sanitation work, and instruction, and it served as a training ground for the discipline satyagraha required.

The admission of a family from a community regarded as untouchable in September 1915 tested those commitments. Dudabhai Dafda, a teacher, his wife Danibehn, and their daughter joined the ashram. Some of the ashram's supporters withdrew funds, residents of the neighborhood harassed the community, and tensions arose even within Gandhi's own household. Gandhi held his position, and a merchant sympathizer, Ambalal Sarabhai, provided funds at a critical moment. The episode shows that Gandhi's opposition to untouchability was an early and central commitment, but it also shows that his approach to caste was reformist and that he worked within, and was constrained by, the social conservatism of his supporters. Chapter 80 examines Dalit critiques, especially that of B. R. Ambedkar, which challenged Gandhi's approach from outside.

The ashram was a collective project, and students should not read its history as Gandhi's alone. Kasturba Gandhi managed the household and endured the controversies over untouchability, sometimes with open reluctance. Maganlal Gandhi, a relative who had followed Gandhi from South Africa, ran its practical affairs, including the weaving and printing work. Mahadev Desai, who joined in 1917, became Gandhi's secretary and writer, and the associates who worked alongside them in education, medicine, and spinning provided the organizational infrastructure from which Gandhi's campaigns drew volunteers. The ashram therefore functioned as both a moral experiment and a political base, and its Gujarati roots shaped the geography of his early campaigns.

By 1917 Gandhi had established a small circle of followers, a reputation for integrity, and a base among Gujarat's merchant and professional classes. He had not yet organized an Indian satyagraha. That began when a peasant from Bihar persuaded him to go to Champaran.

Section 79.4

Champaran, 1917: Indigo Cultivation and Gandhi's First Major Indian Satyagraha

Champaran, in northern Bihar, was an area of indigo cultivation organized by European planters. Under the tinkathia system, tenants were obliged to plant indigo on a fixed portion of their holdings, commonly described as three kathas in twenty, and to sell the crop to the planter at a price below its value. Planters collected additional illegal levies and fees. By the early twentieth century, the collapse of the natural indigo market following the invention of synthetic dyes led many planters to try to shift the costs of that decline onto tenants, demanding payments in exchange for release from the indigo obligation. Peasant grievances were therefore long-standing, but they were sharpened in the 1910s by these demands.

Rajkumar Shukla, a cultivator and moneylender from Champaran who had been involved in peasant complaints, sought out Gandhi at the Lucknow Congress in December 1916 and pressed him persistently to visit. Gandhi agreed, and in April 1917 traveled to Patna and then to Motihari, the district headquarters. The district administration treated his arrival as a threat and served him with an order under the Criminal Procedure Code to leave the district. Gandhi declined to obey and expressed willingness to accept the penalty. He appeared in court, where the proceedings were soon withdrawn on instructions from the provincial government, which preferred to avoid a confrontation. The result was a public demonstration that a single private person could defy an official order without violence and prevail.

Gandhi was joined by Bihar lawyers and Congress activists including Rajendra Prasad, Brajkishore Prasad, Mazharul Haq, and Anugraha Narayan Sinha, along with Mahadev Desai and other members of his circle, who collected statements from thousands of tenants. The method of the inquiry mattered: investigators recorded testimony systematically, asked witnesses to describe their grievances in detail, and sometimes checked claims against planters' records. The inquiry produced a large body of evidence that officials could not easily dismiss. It also brought educated activists into direct contact with agrarian conditions, extending the social reach of nationalist politics. Gandhi's team conducted sanitation and school work in the villages, in keeping with his belief that political action should be joined to social service.

The provincial government appointed an official Champaran Agrarian Enquiry Committee in mid-1917, chaired by a senior official, Frank Sly, with Gandhi as a member. The committee recommended abolition of the tinkathia system, reduction of the rent enhancements imposed in recent years, and partial refunds of illegal exactions. The Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918 gave legislative form to these recommendations. A negotiated compromise had planters refund a portion of the sums extracted, in a settlement that Gandhi accepted though it fell short of full restitution. In effect, the legal obligation to cultivate indigo ended.

Historians caution against reading Champaran as a straightforward victory. The indigo system was already in crisis, and planters had economic reasons to accept reform. The refund was partial. The settlement left in place the wider structure of landlord and tenant relations in Bihar, and it did not address the problems of poorer tenants and landless laborers. Local organizers like Shukla and the Bihar lawyers did much of the groundwork before and after Gandhi's visit. Later nationalist accounts presented the episode as a triumph of Gandhian method alone, simplifying a complex story. Even so, its significance is real. Champaran demonstrated a repertoire—investigation, disciplined defiance of an unjust order, negotiation with officials, and constructive social work—that Gandhi would adapt in other settings.

The campaign also brought Gandhi into sustained contact with Bihar's educated class and with a province where Congress had been weak. The relationships he formed there, notably with Rajendra Prasad, would last through his career, and they illustrate how Gandhi's national position depended on a network of regional allies recruited campaign by campaign.

Section 79.5

Ahmedabad, 1918: The Mill Workers' Strike and Industrial Satyagraha

Ahmedabad was one of India's principal centers of cotton textile manufacture, with dozens of mills owned largely by Gujarati Hindu and Jain merchant families and staffed by thousands of workers drawn from the city and nearby villages. The workforce was divided by caste and skill. Weavers, who were relatively well paid and organized, held a different position from spinners and from lower-caste and Muslim workers, and wages and conditions differed across mills. During the 1917 plague epidemic, mill owners had paid a special "plague bonus," sometimes as much as 70 to 80 percent of wages, to prevent workers from fleeing the city. When the plague subsided, the owners proposed to withdraw the bonus, while wartime inflation was raising the cost of living sharply. Workers demanded an increase of 50 percent in wages, owners offered 20 percent, and the dispute escalated in February 1918.

Gandhi's involvement arose through personal connections. Ambalal Sarabhai, the leading mill owner, was a friend and a supporter of the ashram, and his sister Anasuya Sarabhai, who had been moved by the conditions of workers, was active in welfare work and a trusted associate of Gandhi. Shankarlal Banker, a Gujarati activist, was another key participant. The workers' representatives asked Gandhi to advise them, and the owners also trusted him as a mediator. Gandhi proposed arbitration, which the owners refused. After the owners declared a lockout, the workers went on strike in March 1918, and Gandhi worked out a strategy: workers pledged to stay on strike, avoid violence, and refuse to take other employment or charity, and Gandhi urged a compromise of a 35 percent increase.

Within about two weeks, enthusiasm began to flag. Some workers returned to the mills, others complained of hardship, and the owners hardened their position. At this point Gandhi announced a fast, which he said was intended to hold the workers to their pledge. The fast was bitterly criticized then and since. Some observers, and some historians, argued that it exerted moral pressure on the mill owners, particularly Ambalal Sarabhai, who was personally close to Gandhi and Anasuya Sarabhai, and that it reduced the free choice of workers. Gandhi himself acknowledged that the fast carried this risk. Within three days the owners agreed to arbitration, and a compromise formula gave the workers a raise phased in over days, with the arbitrator ultimately awarding the 35 percent increase Gandhi had advocated.

The Ahmedabad dispute differed from Champaran in several ways. The adversaries were Indian, not European; they were bound to the leader by personal friendship and a common merchant culture; the issues were wages and bonuses, not legal exactions; and the participants were wage-earning workers in a modern industry rather than tenant cultivators. The negotiating strategy therefore relied on moral appeal to the owners, fraternal relationships, and arbitration, not on exposure of illegal practices to officials. Gandhi's outlook, which stressed harmony between labor and capital and treated owners as trustees, reflected that setting. Critics, including later labor organizers and Marxists, argued that this approach limited workers' bargaining power and subordinated labor interests to the employers' goodwill.

The institutional legacy was significant. In 1920 Anasuya Sarabhai, Gandhi, and their associates established the Textile Labour Association (Majur Mahajan) in Ahmedabad, which became a durable union following Gandhian principles of arbitration, constructive work, and avoidance of political strikes. It operated differently from many other Indian unions of the period, which were often more confrontational and communist or socialist in orientation. Chapter 80 examines industrial labor and communist organization more fully, and the contrast with Ahmedabad is instructive.

The workers themselves are less visible in the sources than the leaders. Reports record meetings of thousands under a tree on the Sabarmati riverbank and daily pledges of steadfastness, but the testimony of individual workers is scarce. What can be said is that the dispute made clear that Gandhi's methods could be applied to workers and employers who stood in unequal economic positions and who were also linked by personal and cultural ties, and that the results depended on those ties as much as on pure coercive force.

Section 79.6

Kheda, 1918: Peasant Revenue Resistance and the Limits of Negotiated Victory

Kheda (Kaira) district in central Gujarat was one of the province's more fertile areas, dominated by cultivators of the Patidar caste, many of whom held land directly from the government under the ryotwari system and paid land revenue in cash. Unlike Champaran's tenants, who paid rent to landlords, these cultivators were revenue-paying proprietors in a legal sense. They were relatively prosperous and politically conscious, with a tradition of association through the Gujarat Sabha, a regional political body of which Gandhi was president. In 1917, heavy late rains damaged the crops, and in 1918 many villages reported poor harvests while prices for food and other goods climbed.

Under the provincial land revenue code, cultivators were entitled to a suspension or remission of revenue if the crop fell below a specified fraction of normal, commonly stated as four annas in the rupee, or one quarter. Local activists, including Mohanlal Pandya and Gandhi's close associate Narhari Parikh, collected evidence that many fields fell below this level. Officials, using their own assessments, said the yield was higher and refused suspension. The dispute turned on the interpretation of a legal standard and on who had the right to determine it. Gandhi, Vallabhbhai Patel, who had until then been a successful Ahmedabad lawyer, and others organized a campaign beginning in March 1918.

Participating cultivators signed a pledge that they would refuse to pay the revenue if the government did not grant suspension, accepting the confiscation of property and other penalties. The government responded with warnings, seizure of livestock and movable goods, and threats of forfeiture of land. Volunteers, including Patel in the lead, traveled from village to village, encouraging resistance, helping peasants endure the seizures, and recording cases. This work gave Patel his first experience of organizing a mass campaign and brought a group of Gujarati lawyers and activists into politics. Gandhi's attention was divided, since in the same months he was working on wartime recruitment for the army, an effort that disappointed some of his supporters and demonstrated the still-evolving relation between his loyalty to the Empire and his resistance to particular policies.

By June 1918, the campaign ended in a settlement that was more ambiguous than a clear victory. The government issued an instruction that revenue should be collected only from those cultivators who could afford to pay, leaving the poorer ones exempt, without formally conceding the principle of suspension. Gandhi accepted this as sufficient, and the pledge was lifted. Many participants felt vindicated, while others, including Gandhi himself in later reflection, noted that the settlement was limited and that enthusiasm had begun to waver after months of pressure. The government's formulation allowed it to save face, and the effective relief went to a portion of the cultivators.

Historians, notably David Hardiman in his study of Gujarat's peasant nationalism, have emphasized the social differences within the district. The movement drew its strength and its leaders from the landholding Patidars, who had the most at stake and the most resources. Poorer cultivators, agricultural laborers, and members of the Baraiya and other lower-ranking communities participated less consistently, and their interests in debts, wages, and access to land were often different from those of the Patidars. The campaign therefore bound together a rural elite, a group of educated organizers, and wider peasant participants whose goals only partly overlapped.

Comparing the three campaigns shows how much Gandhi's practice varied. Champaran was about exposing an exploitative system run by outsiders and obtaining legislation. Ahmedabad involved industrial wages and relied heavily on mediation and personal moral pressure. Kheda was a classic tax-resistance campaign against the state itself, involving prosperous peasant proprietors and drawing on legal arguments about revenue rules. In all three, local organizers were essential, and in none was the outcome a total victory. What they gave Gandhi was practical experience in organizing, a regional following, and a group of lieutenants, such as Rajendra Prasad and Vallabhbhai Patel, who would be central in the national movement.

Section 79.7

The Rowlatt Acts: Emergency Powers, Civil Liberties, and Postwar Disillusionment

During the First World War the Government of India had used the Defence of India Act of 1915 to detain suspected revolutionaries, restrict movement, and try political cases before special tribunals. These powers were due to lapse six months after the war ended. Officials, preoccupied with the memory of revolutionary conspiracies in Bengal, with the Ghadar movement among Punjabis, and with reports of revolutionary activity linked to German intrigue, were reluctant to see them expire. In December 1917 the government appointed a Sedition Committee chaired by Justice Sidney Rowlatt, an English judge, to investigate revolutionary crime and recommend legislation. The committee, which had no Indian majority, reported in 1918 and proposed that wartime-style powers be retained in modified form.

Two bills based on these recommendations were introduced in the Imperial Legislative Council in early 1919. Indian members opposed them virtually unanimously, and the official majority nevertheless carried the main measure in March 1919 despite their objections. Muhammad Ali Jinnah resigned from the Council in protest, and other Indian members, including Madan Mohan Malaviya and Mazharul Haq, led the opposition; their critique was that the government was breaking faith with constitutional promises. The enacted law, the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act of 1919 (Act XI of 1919), became popularly known as the Rowlatt Act. A second bill, which would have amended the ordinary criminal law, was dropped.

Students should be careful about what the Act actually provided. It authorized special courts of three High Court judges to try offenses connected with sedition without a jury and with limited rights of appeal, permitted the admission of certain evidence that ordinary courts would not accept, and, in a further part, allowed provincial governments in specified circumstances to require security from suspects, restrict their residence and movement, and arrest and confine them with limited judicial review. It was designed to expire three years after the end of the war, and the government never brought its most contentious provisions into actual use before it was repealed in 1922. Yet the law's mere existence, Indians argued, reversed the principle that executive discretion should end when emergency conditions ended, and many Indians objected to its provisions as incompatible with ordinary rights to open trial and legal defense.

The contradiction was glaring. In August 1917 the Secretary of State for India, Edwin Montagu, had announced the British government's policy of "the gradual development of self-governing institutions" and "responsible government" in India, and in 1918 the Montagu–Chelmsford report had proposed a limited devolution of power to Indian ministers in the provinces. Indians who had supported the war effort and who expected political progress saw instead a government armed with repressive powers. The Rowlatt Act became a symbol: not so much of an actual regime of mass detention as of the mistrust that, in Indian eyes, underlay British intentions. That symbolic force explains why legislation that was little used provoked such a response.

The government's arguments need to be understood as well. Officials argued that revolutionary violence was real, that Bengal and Punjab had seen assassinations, robberies, and plots, and that ordinary courts could not deal with conspiracies in which witnesses were intimidated. Some British officials genuinely believed they were protecting law and order. But they underestimated the strength of the objection, the association between the law and wartime repression, and the possibility that critics from across the political spectrum, Moderates and Extremists, Hindus and Muslims, would unite against it.

Gandhi, who had been recovering from a serious illness and who had until then been a supporter of the war effort, concluded that the law could not be accepted. After failing to persuade the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford, to withdraw the bill, he announced in February 1919 that he would organize satyagraha against it. This was a major decision. For the first time he proposed to lead a campaign not limited to one locality or one grievance, in a province-wide and in effect nationwide form.

Section 79.8

The Rowlatt Satyagraha of 1919: From Local Campaigns to All-India Protest

Gandhi established the Satyagraha Sabha in Bombay in February 1919, with a membership drawn from the Home Rule League and a number of other organizations, and distributed the pledge for signature. The organization was thin. Its leaders were mainly in Bombay and a few other cities, and in much of the country the call for satyagraha was understood only vaguely. Gandhi proposed a nationwide hartal, a voluntary suspension of work and business, with fasting and prayer, as a first step. He set the date for 30 March and then, after confusion, changed it to 6 April 1919. The communication failure was typical of the campaign: Delhi observed the earlier date, and the result was violence.

On 30 March, a large procession in Delhi, led by the Arya Samaj leader Swami Shraddhanand and joined by Hindus and Muslims together, was confronted by police and troops at the railway station and in Chandni Chowk, and several people were killed. In Bombay, on 6 April, enormous crowds gathered on the Chowpatty beach, performed rituals, and took the pledge; Gandhi and others then publicly sold copies of banned writings, including his own proscribed Hind Swaraj, as an act of civil disobedience. In Ahmedabad, Madras, Calcutta, and many smaller towns, shops closed and meetings were held. The hartal was a large demonstration of mood and of the organizing reach of the Home Rule networks, Khilafat sympathizers, and local associations, though participation varied across regions and was strongest in cities and market towns.

The campaign was most intense in Punjab and Gujarat. Punjab had been a major source of wartime recruitment and requisition, with heavy burdens on rural families, and the province's Lieutenant-Governor, Sir Michael O'Dwyer, had followed a firm policy of suppressing political agitation. In Lahore and Amritsar the hartal merged with local anger. Gandhi tried to travel to Delhi and Punjab on 8 April, but the authorities served him with an order prohibiting entry to Punjab, and he was arrested at Palwal on 10 April and sent back to Bombay. His arrest, together with the arrest of local leaders in Amritsar, helped to inflame the situation.

Violence followed in several places. In Ahmedabad, on and after 10 April, crowds attacked government buildings, burned offices, and killed a number of people, and there were disturbances in Viramgam and in Nadiad in Kheda district and elsewhere in Gujarat. In Punjab, the violence was greater and was followed by the events at Amritsar, examined in Section 79.9. Gandhi, appalled, came to feel he had misjudged. He spoke of a "Himalayan miscalculation," meaning that he had called on people to disobey laws before they had been trained in the discipline of satyagraha. On 18 April he suspended the movement. The campaign had lasted barely two weeks as an organized satyagraha.

The Rowlatt satyagraha therefore ended with a mixed record. It demonstrated that a call from Gandhi could produce large, synchronized public demonstrations across the subcontinent, a feat no previous nationalist leader had achieved. It also exposed the dangers of mobilizing crowds without organization or discipline. Participants joined for diverse reasons. Some were moved by Gandhi's moral appeal, some by anger over wartime hardship, some by loyalty to local leaders, and some by pre-existing grievances against officials. In Punjab and Gujarat, local factors shaped the course of events as much as Gandhi's instructions. Gandhi concluded that satyagraha required systematic training and a more disciplined organization than existed in 1919. That conclusion would shape his later attention to the Congress's own organization, the subject of Section 79.14.

The Act that triggered the protest was never used in the way critics had feared, and it was repealed in 1922. The campaign against it nevertheless changed the political landscape. It gave Gandhi a national reputation, established the hartal as a nationalist instrument, brought Hindu and Muslim crowds into joint demonstrations at moments, and, through the repression it provoked, set the stage for the crisis of Punjab and the broader Non-Cooperation Movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Hartal

Hartal, a term from Gujarati and other north and west Indian languages, refers to a collective suspension of work and trade as a form of protest. Shops close, markets empty, and work stops for a day or a defined period. Gandhi gave it a ritual character by adding fasting and prayer and framing it as an act of self-purification. Earlier protests had used shop closures, notably during the Swadeshi agitation, so the form was not new, but the 1919 hartal was unprecedented in being coordinated on a national scale.

The term should not be confused with civil disobedience, which involves breaking a specific law, or with a labor strike, which is aimed at an employer. A hartal asked everyone in a community, including merchants and consumers, to join a symbolic withdrawal. Because it depended on community pressure as well as individual choice, it could be coercive in practice, and critics noted that those who kept shops open sometimes faced intimidation.

Section 79.9

Jallianwala Bagh, April 13, 1919: The Amritsar Massacre

Amritsar, the principal commercial city of central Punjab and the site of the Golden Temple, had a mixed population of Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims, a strong mercantile community, and an active political life. In the weeks after the Rowlatt Act, the city's leaders, including the Congress lawyer Saifuddin Kitchlew, a Muslim, and Satyapal, a Hindu physician, organized hartals and meetings. On 9 April, the festival of Ram Navami, Hindus and Muslims joined in processions that expressed an unusual degree of unity, which alarmed officials. On 10 April the Deputy Commissioner, Miles Irving, on instructions from the provincial government, arrested Kitchlew and Satyapal and sent them out of the city.

The arrest triggered a crowd march toward the Deputy Commissioner's residence. Troops fired on it at a bridge, killing several people, and the crowd turned on symbols of colonial and commercial power. Mobs attacked and set fire to banks, the Town Hall, and the railway station, and several Europeans, including bank employees, were killed. Marcella Sherwood, an English missionary teacher, was beaten and left injured and was rescued by Indian neighbors. These events must be told clearly, since they formed the background to the authorities' later actions, but it is equally necessary to distinguish them from the gathering at Jallianwala Bagh three days later. The people who assembled there on 13 April were not the people who had attacked the banks, and the meeting had no violent intention.

Control of Amritsar was transferred to the military. Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer, a brigade commander from Jalandhar with long service in India, arrived on 11 April and took charge of the city. On 12 April he ordered troops to march through the streets, and on the morning of 13 April he had a proclamation read in parts of the city forbidding gatherings and processions. Whether the proclamation reached many people is uncertain, and 13 April was Baisakhi, the festival that brought large numbers of villagers and pilgrims to the city. In the afternoon, a public meeting was called at Jallianwala Bagh, a walled enclosure of several acres bounded by houses and buildings, with a few narrow entrances and a well, near the Golden Temple. Estimates of the crowd range from several thousand to well above ten thousand.

Dyer learned of the meeting and, at about 4:30 in the afternoon, arrived at the Bagh with about ninety soldiers, drawn largely from Gurkha and Baluch units, two armored cars that could not pass through the narrow entrance, and some British officers. Positioning his riflemen on a raised platform near the entrance, he ordered them to fire at the crowd without a warning to disperse. Firing continued for about ten minutes, and about 1,650 rounds were expended. People ran toward the walls and the few exits and were shot as they ran; some jumped into the well. Dyer left without arranging aid for the wounded, and curfew prevented relatives from reaching the injured. He later explained that he regarded the firing as necessary to produce a "moral effect" throughout Punjab.

The number of casualties is a matter of evidence and dispute. The Hunter Committee, the official inquiry, accepted a figure of 379 dead, based on figures compiled by local administrators and the civil surgeon, with perhaps 1,200 wounded in some accounts. The inquiry conducted by the Indian National Congress concluded that around a thousand people had been killed. Neither figure should be treated as certain. The official count was assembled from limited records and from lists that families may have been reluctant to give to the authorities, while Congress estimates relied on compiled testimony and may have been influenced by political purposes. Historians generally agree that the toll was far higher than minor incidents of crowd control and that the official figure was probably an undercount.

Students must also distinguish individuals and decisions. Dyer was a military officer who commanded the troops in Amritsar and gave the order to fire. Michael O'Dwyer was the civilian Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab, who supported Dyer's actions, who had advocated a hard line toward agitation, and whose government declared martial law. They shared political assumptions but were different officials with different responsibilities, and later inquiries treated their roles separately. Nor should the massacre be understood merely as a prelude to national politics. It was an event in Punjab's history that killed and injured ordinary men, women, and children, many of them visiting for a festival, and whose survivors carried its memory for decades.

Section 79.10

Punjab under Repression: Martial Law, Inquiries, and the Collapse of Imperial Confidence

Martial law was proclaimed in Lahore, Amritsar, and other districts of Punjab from 15 April 1919 and lasted until June in many places. Special tribunals tried hundreds of people for involvement in the disturbances, and a number were sentenced to death, with many sentences later reduced. Officials imposed collective fines, ordered the public to salute officers, commandeered vehicles and property, and used aircraft to bomb and strafe crowds in the town of Gujranwala. In the lane in Amritsar where Marcella Sherwood had been attacked, Dyer ordered that Indians passing through must crawl on all fours, and soldiers flogged those who did not comply. Other punishments, including the public flogging of students and the closure of schools and colleges, were imposed in Lahore and elsewhere. The press was censored and Punjab was in effect sealed from outside observers, so that news of events reached the rest of India and Britain slowly and in fragments.

These measures illustrate how the colonial state conceived of order. Officials in Punjab, shaped by memories of 1857 and of the province's strategic and military importance, thought a display of force was necessary to restore prestige. The result was to destroy the credibility of the claim that British rule rested on consent or even on legality, because the violence was directed largely at unarmed civilians and carried out under emergency powers beyond ordinary law. The Government of India passed an Indemnity Act in 1919 to protect officials from legal consequences for acts under martial law, which reinforced Indian anger.

Pressure for inquiry grew. In October 1919 the government established the Disorders Inquiry Committee, known as the Hunter Committee after its chairman, Lord William Hunter, a Scottish lawyer and former Solicitor-General. It consisted of British and Indian members. Its public hearings included Dyer's own testimony, in which he acknowledged that he had given no warning and kept firing until his ammunition was nearly exhausted, and said he would have used machine guns had the armored cars entered the Bagh. The British majority, in its report in 1920, criticized Dyer's conduct as a serious error of judgment and condemned the crawling order, while handling officials more gently; the three Indian members, in a separate minority report, condemned the firing and the martial law administration more severely. The Army Council later censured Dyer, and he was compelled to retire from his post. In a House of Lords debate and in public subscriptions in Britain, many supporters, including some newspapers, praised him, which Indians took as evidence that imperial opinion approved his action.

The Indian National Congress conducted its own inquiry, appointing a committee that included Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, Chittaranjan Das, M. R. Jayakar, and Abbas Tyabji, which collected statements from hundreds of witnesses in Punjab. Its report, published in 1920, presented a much harsher account than the official one, documenting the violence of martial law and arguing that the colonial government bore responsibility for the disorders. Gandhi, who traveled to Punjab in the autumn of 1919, came to regard the Punjab events as evidence that cooperation with the government could not be maintained. Rabindranath Tagore, in a letter to the Viceroy at the end of May 1919, renounced his knighthood in protest, a gesture that gave the outrage a voice in India and abroad.

Comparing the sources illustrates the problem of historical evidence. British official accounts emphasized the earlier violence and the danger of rebellion. Dyer's testimony and the military record stressed necessity. Indian witnesses described terror, indiscriminate firing, and humiliation, and their statements were collected under political auspices by a party that intended to use them. Each source group has strengths and biases. Historians such as Nigel Collett and Kim Wagner have used the official and military records to reconstruct the sequence in detail and to argue that the massacre was not an aberration but an extreme expression of a colonial security culture; others emphasize local Punjabi sources. The resulting account is not a simple choice between a British and an Indian version, but a composite built from critical comparison.

Amritsar became a turning point not primarily because of its casualty total, large as it was, but because of what it revealed and how it was perceived. For many Indians, including those who had supported the Empire during the war, the massacre and the martial law that followed destroyed confidence in British moral authority. It strengthened the argument that constitutional appeals alone would not secure justice, and it made the notion of withdrawing cooperation more plausible. Moderate leaders who had hoped for reform through the new constitution found their position weakened, and the demand for self-government gained force.

Section 79.11

The Khilafat Movement: Ottoman Defeat and Muslim Political Mobilization

The Ottoman Sultan held the title of Caliph, the spiritual and symbolic leader of many Sunni Muslims. Although the political authority of the caliphate had long been limited, and many Muslims in India had earlier disputed the Ottoman claim, the Sultan's position acquired new emotional and political importance in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, as Muslim publics throughout the world watched European powers encroach on Muslim territories. When the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War on the side of Germany in 1914, Indian Muslims were placed in a painful position, since many served in the British Indian Army and many British officials feared disloyalty. Muslim leaders, in turn, extracted promises, including a statement by Prime Minister David Lloyd George in 1918 that Turkey would not be deprived of its homeland.

The Ottoman defeat of 1918 and the prospect of a harsh peace settlement renewed anxiety. Indian Muslims were concerned about the fate of the Sultan, about the status of the holy places of Arabia, which they wanted to remain under Muslim sovereignty, and about the dismemberment of Ottoman territory. A Khilafat Committee was formed in Bombay in 1919, and an All-India Khilafat Conference met in Delhi in November 1919, with Gandhi in the chair on one occasion, in a gesture of Hindu–Muslim cooperation. Muslim organizations sent deputations to the Viceroy and, in 1920, a delegation led by Muhammad Ali to London to appeal to the British government and to the Allied powers. The movement's methods included public meetings, newspapers, fundraising, and appeals to the ulama, the religious scholars.

The leadership was diverse. Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali, the "Ali brothers," were educated at Aligarh and, in Muhammad Ali's case, Oxford, and were prominent journalists and politicians, internees during the war, and fiery orators. Abul Kalam Azad, a scholar and journalist from Calcutta, brought religious learning and political radicalism. Hakim Ajmal Khan, a physician from Delhi, and Dr. M. A. Ansari lent respectability and organizational experience. Many ulama, including leaders of Deoband and the Farangi Mahal scholars of Lucknow, such as Abdul Bari, supported the movement, and in 1920 the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind was formed to organize their participation. Women like Abadi Bano Begum, mother of the Ali brothers and known as Bi Amman, spoke publicly in support. These leaders brought different priorities: some emphasized religious obligation, others anti-imperialism, and others negotiation with the British.

It is important not to equate Khilafat with the Muslim League's earlier program. The League, founded in 1906, had pursued constitutional safeguards for Muslim representation, and its leaders, including Jinnah, were largely lawyers who favored negotiation. Khilafat politics was more populist, religiously expressed, and international in its concern. In 1920, as the peace terms with Turkey became known, the movement turned toward more radical action. Some ulama issued legal opinions encouraging non-cooperation, and in the summer of 1920 a movement of hijrat, emigration from British India to Afghanistan, drew many thousands of Muslims, primarily from Sindh and the Northwest Frontier, many of whom suffered hardship and returned disillusioned.

Indian Muslims were not unanimous. The Aga Khan and other leaders opposed the agitation, as did many landlords, officials, and members of the Aligarh establishment who feared confrontation with the government. Others, including Jinnah, regarded the movement's religious appeals and mass methods as dangerous. Regional communities responded differently: in Punjab, many Muslims participated, but others were tied to the provincial administration; in Bengal, Muslim peasants and merchants had their own concerns; in Malabar, as Section 79.17 explains, the movement overlapped with a long agrarian conflict. The Khilafat movement should therefore be seen as the mobilization of a significant but internally diverse section of the Muslim population, rather than as the voice of all Indian Muslims.

The movement's history extends beyond the period of this chapter. The Treaty of Sèvres of August 1920 imposed severe terms on the Ottoman government, but the Turkish nationalist movement led by Mustafa Kemal later overturned it, and in 1924 the Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the caliphate, depriving the Indian movement of its central object. That later story is not told here; what matters for this chapter is that between 1919 and 1922 the Khilafat cause provided a vast reservoir of organization and energy, and that Gandhi's decision to ally with it shaped the Non-Cooperation Movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Swaraj and Khilafat

Swaraj, "self-rule," had been used by nationalists since the early twentieth century. For Dadabhai Naoroji and the Extremists it meant self-government; for Gandhi in Hind Swaraj, it meant a broader moral and social self-mastery. In 1920–1921 the word carried different meanings for different audiences: for Congress leaders, constitutional self-government; for many peasants, relief from taxes, rents, and local officials; for others, a vague but powerful ideal of an end to foreign rule.

Khilafat derives from khalifa, "successor" or caliph, the title of the leader of the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad. In the Indian movement it referred to the campaign to protect the position of the Ottoman Sultan-Caliph and the Muslim holy places. The word's meaning for participants varied from theological obligation to symbolic anticolonial protest. The caliphate was not a modern state institution, and historians stress that the movement should be understood in the context of early twentieth-century Muslim politics and not as a simple religious reaction.

Section 79.12

Gandhi, Khilafat, and Hindu–Muslim Political Cooperation

Gandhi was drawn to the Khilafat cause for several reasons. He considered the grievance of Indian Muslims just, and he believed the British government had broken wartime promises. He saw an opportunity to bind Hindus and Muslims in a common campaign, which he regarded as essential to any future self-government. And he judged that the intensity of Khilafat feeling could be channeled into a disciplined nonviolent movement. His arguments combined religious reasoning, in which he treated the obligations of Muslims as binding and deserving of Hindu support, with political calculation about the value of solidarity. He had been involved in Hindu–Muslim cooperation in South Africa, and he claimed that a friend's religious sentiment must be respected even where one did not share it.

In 1919 Gandhi began to link the two causes. He observed Khilafat Day on 17 October 1919, spoke at the Delhi conference in November, and by early 1920 was arguing that if the government did not satisfy the Muslim demand, non-cooperation would be the response. He worked out strategy with the Ali brothers and other leaders, notably at a Khilafat conference in Allahabad in June 1920, where a program of staged non-cooperation was endorsed. On 1 August 1920 he returned the medals he had received from the government for his services in South Africa, including the Kaisar-i-Hind medal, as a token launch of the campaign; the same day marked the death of Bal Gangadhar Tilak, the veteran nationalist leader.

Objections came from several directions. Some Congress members questioned whether a movement centered on the status of a foreign Muslim ruler could be a foundation for Indian nationalism, and some Hindu leaders were indifferent or hostile. Others feared that Khilafat politics, with its religious language, could strengthen communal rather than national feeling. Jinnah and others in the Muslim League and Congress who believed in constitutional methods thought the combination of religion and mass agitation was dangerous. Within Muslim circles, there were arguments over whether the movement was too subordinate to Gandhi's leadership, or too close to the Congress, and over whether non-cooperation, including the boycott of government schools, was wise for a community that already lagged in modern education.

The alliance had real strengths. It brought Muslim leaders, ulama, merchants, and townspeople into a political movement with Hindus, supplied organizational networks such as mosques, associations, and newspapers, and created scenes of Hindu–Muslim fraternity that were rare and moving. Muslim leaders were prominent at Congress sessions, and Hindu leaders spoke at Khilafat meetings. In many towns, joint committees were formed. The alliance greatly expanded the social reach of nationalist politics in the Urdu-speaking regions of north India, the Punjab, Sindh, and parts of Bengal and the south.

It also contained tensions that its participants understood differently. For many Muslim participants, the Khilafat cause was primary and non-cooperation was a means. For Gandhi and many Congress members, swaraj was primary and Khilafat was a vital but ancillary cause. For many Hindus, support for Khilafat was an act of goodwill, rarely based on any religious investment in the caliphate. For some, notably the more cautious Congress politicians, it was a tactical concession. These differing understandings meant that the alliance would be fragile if the Khilafat cause collapsed or if local conflicts arose, as indeed happened after 1922. Historians such as Gail Minault have argued that the movement had independent roots and that Gandhi succeeded not by directing it but by joining it and translating its energy into a wider program.

In demonstrating the possibilities and the problems of coalition, the Khilafat alliance became one of the most important features of Gandhi's expansion of Indian nationalism. It showed that nationalism could include religious communities on the basis of shared political aims, and it exposed unresolved disagreements over what national unity meant: whether it could rest on common action around a particular religious cause, or required agreement on a shared definition of the nation. Chapters 81 and 82 return to these questions in the changed conditions of the late 1920s and 1930s.

Section 79.13

The Non-Cooperation Movement, 1920: Strategy, Launch, and Political Objectives

The core argument behind Non-Cooperation was that British rule in India depended on the active or passive cooperation of Indians. Colonial government needed Indian clerks, policemen, soldiers, lawyers, taxpayers, schoolchildren, and councillors to function, and if these withdrew their participation, the structure would become unworkable or, at the least, would lose its claim to legitimacy. This argument transposed the logic of satyagraha, with its refusal to cooperate with injustice, from a particular law or grievance to the institutions of the state as a whole. It was not an anarchist call to destroy government but a program of staged withdrawal that Gandhi expected to compel a change of policy.

Three issues converged in 1920. The first was the Punjab wrong: the Hunter Committee's report, published in May 1920, and the House of Lords' sympathy for Dyer convinced many that the government would not provide justice. The second was the Khilafat question, which became acute with the Treaty of Sèvres in August. The third was dissatisfaction with the Government of India Act of 1919, whose dyarchy gave Indian ministers limited powers over subjects such as education and health while keeping finance and law and order in official hands. These grievances were of different kinds, and the program of non-cooperation was designed to address them all, with swaraj as the broad objective. Gandhi said that swaraj could be attained within a year if the program were adopted effectively, a claim whose meaning and sincerity historians have debated, since he qualified it elsewhere.

The program, as formulated by the Central Khilafat Committee in June and by Gandhi in August, called for stages: surrender of titles and honorary offices, refusal to attend government functions, withdrawal of children from government-controlled schools, boycott of the law courts by lawyers and litigants, boycott of the new legislative councils to be elected in November 1920, refusal to serve in Mesopotamia as clerks or soldiers, and boycott of foreign cloth. Later stages, including refusal to pay taxes, were held in reserve. Alongside these negative measures, Gandhi urged positive tasks: promotion of hand-spinning and hand-weaving, establishment of national schools and arbitration courts, and removal of untouchability.

To become a Congress program, it required approval by the party. A special session of the Congress, held at Calcutta in September 1920 under the presidency of Lala Lajpat Rai, debated Gandhi's resolution. Opposition was serious. Many established leaders, including Lajpat Rai himself, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Annie Besant, and Bipin Chandra Pal, worried about the boycott of the councils, which they regarded as useful forums, and the boycott of schools and courts, which they saw as damaging for students and for the legal profession. Chittaranjan Das, a lawyer who gave up a lucrative practice, initially resisted the council boycott. Muhammad Ali Jinnah and others warned of the danger of disorder. After intensive debate and lobbying, including the support of the Khilafat leaders and large numbers of delegates brought by Gandhi's allies, the resolution passed by a clear majority.

The Calcutta program should be distinguished from the broader activities of 1921–1922. At Calcutta, the immediate steps were limited and graded, and the resolution set out a path rather than an immediate confrontation. The mass character of the movement developed over the following year, as local activists, Khilafat committees, and volunteers interpreted and extended the program, in some districts adding boycotts of liquor shops, village courts, and in some places refusal of rent or taxes that Gandhi had not authorized. The movement of 1921 was therefore both a national campaign and a series of local movements under a shared name.

The elections to the new legislative councils in November 1920, held under the 1919 Act, tested the boycott. Many Congress candidates withdrew and turnout was low in some constituencies, but others, including Moderates and non-Congress groups, contested the seats, and the new councils functioned. The boycott demonstrated Congress's ability to influence electoral participation but not to stop the working of the constitution. That mixed result foreshadowed the later debate over council entry.

Section 79.14

The Nagpur Congress of December 1920: Building a Mass Political Organization

The annual Congress session at Nagpur in December 1920 was attended by 14,583 delegates according to the Indian National Congress institutional synopsis (other published enumerations differ by one), an unprecedented number, along with thousands of visitors. Chittaranjan Das, who had shifted from opposition to support after the Calcutta debate, moved the resolution on non-cooperation, and it was adopted with strong support. The session is also remembered as a moment when old leaders were displaced. Jinnah, who had opposed the strategy, was shouted down by sections of the audience and left the Congress, ending his membership of the party. Annie Besant and others who had been central to the earlier phase of nationalism likewise withdrew from the center of Congress politics. The Nagpur session therefore marked a shift in the party's leadership and its character.

The most lasting product of Nagpur was a new constitution. The Congress creed of 1908 had defined its goal as self-government within the British Empire by constitutional means. At Nagpur, the creed was changed to the attainment of swaraj by all legitimate and peaceful means, a formula that was deliberately ambiguous as to whether swaraj would be within or outside the Empire, and that replaced "constitutional" with "peaceful." The shift opened the party to activities such as boycott and civil disobedience that exceeded constitutional channels while committing it to nonviolence.

The more far-reaching change was administrative. Before 1920, Congress had no permanent organization between annual sessions beyond a small committee and a few provincial bodies. The new constitution created a structure of provincial committees, known as Pradesh Congress Committees, organized on a linguistic rather than a colonial administrative basis, which meant that Congress units followed the boundaries of regional language groups such as Gujarati, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, and Bengali in place of the arbitrary provinces of British India. This enabled Congress workers to conduct politics in local languages and to build on regional identity. Below the provincial committees, the constitution provided for district, taluka, and village committees, in principle reaching down to the village level.

At the center, the All-India Congress Committee, a larger body elected by the provincial committees, was given a regular role in directing policy between sessions, and a smaller Working Committee of fifteen members was established as an executive that could meet frequently and take decisions. This created, for the first time, a permanent executive structure capable of continuous political work. Membership was opened by reducing the annual subscription to four annas, a sum affordable to many rural and urban poor people, in place of the much higher fee that had limited enrolment to the educated and the well-off. Women were admitted on the same terms as men. A goal of enrolling millions of members and collecting a large fund in honor of Tilak was set in 1921, and the Tilak Swaraj Fund reportedly exceeded one crore rupees by mid-1921, a measure of the movement's reach into merchant and landowning communities.

Historians such as Judith Brown and Sumit Sarkar have emphasized that these reforms, more than any single campaign, enabled Congress to function as a mass organization. They provided a framework for local organizers, a channel for communication, and a formal role for regional activists. But the reforms had limits. Real membership was patchy; many four-anna members were nominal. Leadership remained dominated by lawyers, merchants, and landowners, and regional committees were often controlled by established local elites. Women, Dalits, and poorer peasants were formally eligible but seldom in leadership. Delegates to sessions were chosen on a population basis, favoring large provinces and urban strata. The Nagpur constitution thus opened the party without making it socially equal.

The transformation of Congress was not Gandhi's alone. Motilal Nehru, Das, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, C. Rajagopalachari, and the regional Khilafat leaders helped draft and implement the structure, and many of the provincial committees were built by local activists who adapted the plan to their conditions. The surviving Congress constitution, and the proceedings of the Nagpur session, are valuable documentary sources for tracing these changes.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Congress before Gandhi and after the Nagpur Reorganization

What Changed

  • The creed shifted from constitutional self-government within the Empire to swaraj by legitimate and peaceful means.
  • A permanent structure of village, district, provincial, and all-India committees replaced a largely episodic organization.
  • Provincial units were reorganized by language, strengthening the use of regional languages in politics.
  • Membership fees fell, enabling much broader enrolment, and mass campaigns replaced petitions as the central activity.
  • Khilafat leaders, local activists, volunteers, and some peasant and labor groups joined the political field.

What Stayed the Same

  • Leadership remained dominated by lawyers, merchants, and landowners, with many senior figures continuing from the earlier period.
  • The party continued to rely on annual sessions as major gatherings and sources of legitimacy.
  • Gender, caste, and class inequalities persisted in who held office and set policy.
  • Local committees often reflected existing local elites and factions.

Who experienced change most directly? Regional organizers, Khilafat activists, and new members enrolled in towns and some rural districts experienced the greatest change; established provincial elites and many villagers experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: The reorganization enlarged nationalism's reach without making it socially uniform, a feature that shaped later conflicts discussed in Chapter 80.

Section 79.15

Boycotts, National Schools, Khadi, and Constructive Nationalism

In everyday life, Non-Cooperation took the form of boycotts and renunciations that placed political demands on households, students, lawyers, and merchants. The boycott of government schools and colleges aimed to withdraw young people from institutions that trained loyal subjects of the Raj. The boycott of law courts asked lawyers to give up practice and litigants to turn to arbitration. The boycott of the legislative councils asked candidates and voters to abstain. Foreign cloth was to be discarded and burned, and titles and honors returned. Each demanded sacrifice from different groups, and each produced different results.

The legal boycott drew the response of a number of eminent lawyers. Chittaranjan Das in Calcutta, Motilal Nehru in Allahabad, Rajendra Prasad in Patna, and Vallabhbhai Patel in Ahmedabad were among those who gave up or sharply reduced their lucrative practices, a symbolic and economic sacrifice. But most lawyers did not, and the courts continued to function, with litigants still using them, which showed the limits of the boycott. The educational boycott attracted students in large numbers in some regions, particularly in Bengal, Punjab, and Gujarat, where thousands left government institutions, but many returned within a year when alternatives proved inadequate. Students who left faced uncertain career prospects, since government employment required certified education.

To provide alternatives, nationalist educational institutions were founded or expanded. Gujarat Vidyapith, established at Ahmedabad in 1920 under Gandhi's inspiration, combined traditional learning, vernacular education, and manual work. Kashi Vidyapith at Banaras, Bihar Vidyapith in Patna, and the Tilak Maharashtra Vidyapith were part of a wider network of national schools and colleges. Jamia Millia Islamia, established at Aligarh in October 1920 by Muslim nationalists including Muhammad Ali, Hakim Ajmal Khan, and Zakir Husain, was intended as a national Muslim university independent of government control, and it later moved to Delhi. These institutions illustrate the movement's constructive side: the creation of alternative institutions, as distinct from mere renunciation. Many struggled with funds and recognition, and some survived and grew while others faded.

The most characteristic symbol of the movement was khadi, hand-spun and hand-woven cloth, and the charkha, or spinning wheel. Gandhi argued that spinning could provide supplementary income for villagers, reduce dependence on imported cloth, and create a common discipline uniting rich and poor, and he urged all to spin. Congress adopted khadi as a form of dress, and nationalists wore the white cap that came to be associated with Gandhi. Mass bonfires of foreign cloth were held, notably in Bombay on 31 July 1921. Rabindranath Tagore and other critics questioned the burning of cloth that poor people might have used and doubted that spinning could solve India's economic problems; Gandhi replied that the symbolic value and the moral discipline justified it. In September 1921, during a tour of Madurai, Gandhi adopted the loincloth that became his usual dress, to identify with the poorest.

The constructive program also included the campaign against untouchability, the promotion of Hindu–Muslim unity, the campaign for prohibition, including the picketing of liquor shops, and the development of village industries. Women played a prominent role in picketing and spinning. The program drew on ideas from the ashram and gave volunteers meaningful, nonconfrontational work, so that the movement could sustain itself between confrontations. Gandhi considered it essential and later returned to it repeatedly.

The costs were real. Students who left school risked their careers. Lawyers lost income. Merchants who stopped importing foreign cloth faced lost business, although Indian mill owners and many merchants gained from the boycott of foreign cloth, and some mill owners profited. Households that gave up British-made goods faced higher prices or poorer quality. Historians distinguish between symbolic renunciation, such as returning titles or burning cloth, and the practical creation of alternatives, such as schools and spinning centers, and they note that the latter was harder, slower, and less visible, but ultimately may have had more lasting effect on institutions and social networks.

Section 79.16

Non-Cooperation across India: Local Movements, Women, Students, and Political Participation

Between 1920 and 1922 the movement spread across much of British India, though unevenly. In Bengal, led by Chittaranjan Das and Khilafat leaders, it drew students, lawyers, and Muslim peasants; Das's wife Basanti Devi and his sister Urmila Devi were prominent, and Basanti Devi was arrested in December 1921 while selling khadi in Calcutta. In Assam, a strike and exodus of tea-garden laborers in 1921, known as the Chargola exodus, was connected with nationalist agitation though driven by plantation conditions. In Punjab, the Khilafat and Congress movements joined with a Sikh campaign to reform gurdwaras, whose management was held by hereditary custodians; the Akali movement, including the confrontation at Nankana Sahib in 1921, was a separate but overlapping struggle. In Bombay and Gujarat, the movement was strong among merchants, students, and mill workers, and the Bombay riots at the landing of the Prince of Wales in November 1921 were a violent episode.

In the United Provinces, the Awadh peasant movement under leaders such as Baba Ramchandra, led by the Kisan Sabha, had already mobilized tenants against landlords and rent in 1920–1921. Congress leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru, who encountered the peasants in this period, learned from them. The movement had its own aims: reduction of rents, an end to illegal exactions and forced labor, and release from landlords' control. Gandhi urged peasants to avoid violence and not to refuse rent, which would alienate landlords, and the government repressed agitations, including firing on crowds at Rae Bareli in January 1921. In the Andhra region, local movements for forest rights and tax resistance, such as the Palnadu forest satyagraha, drew on the national program; in Bihar, the groundwork of Champaran and Congress organizers brought local participation.

The social range of participants was wide. Students left colleges, and volunteers, often young men, formed corps to maintain order and conduct picketing. Merchants contributed funds and joined boycotts, though their commercial interests sometimes diverged from the movement's. Muslim ulama and Khilafat committees mobilized mosque congregations. Religious organizations, including Sikh bodies and Hindu associations, joined or watched. Rural participants included peasants of different castes, some led by local notables and others by local grievances.

Women took part in new and varied ways. Kasturba Gandhi, Sarojini Naidu, Basanti Devi, and Abadi Bano Begum had different backgrounds and roles: Naidu was a poet and political orator, Basanti Devi a public figure who courted arrest, Bi Amman a Muslim matriarch whose public speaking in Khilafat meetings, from behind a veil, gave Muslim women a model of political voice, and Kasturba a participant who worked in the ashram and traveled with Gandhi. Many other women, unnamed in the sources, spun, picketed, donated ornaments to the Tilak Swaraj Fund, and attended meetings. Their participation did not necessarily challenge gender norms in the home, and it is easily overstated, but it marked a significant opening of public political space. Chapter 80 examines women's autonomous political activity.

The central analytical question is whether participants understood the movement as the national leadership did. The evidence suggests that they often did not. Gandhi and the Congress leaders described swaraj as a goal to be reached by disciplined nonviolent action. For many local participants, swaraj was bound up with immediate concerns: lower rent, freedom from forest restrictions or forced labor, ending of liquor sales, or defense of religious institutions. Gandhi's name was sometimes invoked in rumors that attributed miraculous powers to him or promised the end of taxes and the coming of a new rule, a phenomenon studied by historians such as Shahid Amin. Officials were also alarmed by such rumors, which they interpreted as signs of unrest. The movement's strength lay in its capacity to give these varied aspirations a common language, but the gap between the leaders' intentions and the participants' understandings was a source of both energy and instability.

Chapter 80 examines the peasant, labor, caste, and women's movements whose histories are not exhausted by their connection to Congress. Here the point is that Non-Cooperation worked as a broad framework within which many local conflicts found expression, and that the movement's success in becoming a mass phenomenon created the conditions for its crisis.

Section 79.17

The Crisis of Non-Cooperation, 1921: Repression, Violence, and Competing Objectives

By 1921 the confrontation between the colonial state and the nationalist organizations had become a trial of strength. Lord Reading, who succeeded Chelmsford as Viceroy in April 1921, initially favored restraint, hoping that the movement would collapse under its own contradictions, but pressure from provincial governments and British opinion grew. Officials arrested local leaders under sedition and public-order laws, restricted meetings, censored publications, and used police and magistrates to disrupt picketing and volunteer corps. In September 1921 the Ali brothers were arrested after a Khilafat conference at Karachi passed a resolution declaring it religiously unlawful for Muslims to serve in the army, and in the following months Congress leaders including Das, Motilal Nehru, and Lajpat Rai were imprisoned. By early 1922 the number of people imprisoned in connection with Non-Cooperation was commonly estimated at approximately thirty thousand; this is a rounded secondary estimate rather than an independently reconciled prison census.

At Ahmedabad in December 1921, the Congress session, held with many leaders in jail, was presided over by Hakim Ajmal Khan and gave Gandhi full executive authority. The visit of the Prince of Wales to India, which began in November 1921, was met with a boycott and hartals. In Bombay, on the day of his landing, 17 November, crowds attacked those who did not observe the hartal, including Parsis, Christians, and Anglo-Indians, and fighting with police and troops killed dozens of people over several days. Gandhi, shocked by the violence, fasted for five days, a response that again showed his belief that the leader bore responsibility for the conduct of participants.

The most serious violence occurred in Malabar, on the southwest coast, where the Mappilas, Muslim tenant cultivators and traders, rose in revolt in August 1921. Malabar had a long history of agrarian conflict: Mappila tenants were largely subject to Hindu landlords (jenmis) who held landed rights recognized by British law, and a series of outbreaks in the nineteenth century had reflected tenant insecurity, evictions, and rack-renting. Khilafat and Congress meetings in 1920–1921 raised expectations and gave the tenants an organizational and religious language. When police raided a mosque at Tirurangadi in August 1921 in search of Khilafat activists, rumors of desecration spread and an armed uprising followed, led by figures such as Ali Musaliar and Variyamkunnath Kunhahammed Haji, who set up a short-lived administration.

The rebellion involved attacks on police stations, government offices, and landlords, and acts of violence against civilians, including killings and forced conversions of some Hindus, as well as the destruction of temples. The colonial government responded with martial law and troops, and thousands of rebels were killed. In November 1921, one hundred prisoners—ninety-seven Mappilas and three Hindus, according to the subsequent inquiry—were transported from Tirur toward Coimbatore in a closed railway goods wagon. Seventy ultimately died, principally from asphyxiation, in what became known as the wagon tragedy. The inquiry identified dangerously obstructed ventilation and found serious failures in the conditions of transport and supervision. The long-term effects on relations among communities in Malabar were severe.

Historians have offered different interpretations, and students should distinguish among them. The colonial and some nationalist accounts framed the rebellion as religious fanaticism, a view that underplayed agrarian causes. Others, including K. N. Panikkar and Conrad Wood, have stressed the long background of tenancy conflict and political grievance, with religion providing the idiom of mobilization. Still others point to the interplay: the rebels had social and economic grievances and a religious vocabulary of resistance, and the violence against Hindus, though not the whole of the rebellion, was real and has been remembered with pain. The uprising was not a direct outcome of Gandhian non-cooperation. Gandhi and Khilafat leaders had no control over it; yet it arose in the same political environment, and it damaged the image of Hindu–Muslim cooperation and strengthened the arguments of critics.

These events show the main difficulty of 1921. The movement's rapid growth outran the capacity of the leadership to impose discipline, and local actors pursued objectives of their own. At the same time, the government's repression escalated. Gandhi, in early 1922, was under pressure from impatient followers to move to a higher stage of mass civil disobedience, including refusal to pay taxes, and from cautious colleagues to avoid it. He chose the district of Bardoli in Gujarat as the site of a controlled experiment and wrote to the Viceroy in early February 1922, threatening to launch it unless the government changed its policy of repression. Events in eastern Uttar Pradesh intervened.

Section 79.18

Chauri Chaura, February 1922: Violence and the Withdrawal of Non-Cooperation

On 4 February 1922, a crowd of volunteers and local people, perhaps two thousand or more, marched in a demonstration in the bazaar of Chauri Chaura, a market village in Gorakhpur district in the eastern United Provinces. Volunteers of the local Congress and Khilafat organizations had been picketing against the sale of liquor and against high prices of food in the market, and local police had harassed and beaten some of them. When the demonstrators marched past the police station, an altercation followed. The police fired at the crowd, and when their ammunition ran out, the sub-inspector and constables retreated into the building. The crowd, enraged, set fire to the station, killing the policemen who were inside or who tried to flee. Twenty-two policemen died.

The local setting matters. The district was poor and densely populated, with a mixed agricultural population in which lower-caste cultivators and weavers were prominent. The Gorakhpur Khilafat and Congress committees, led by local figures such as Bhagwan Ahir, a former soldier who had become a volunteer leader, had organized volunteers, and the bazaar was a site of tensions over prices, liquor, and police power. The police had a reputation for harassment, and several policemen were hated locally. Shahid Amin's study, Event, Metaphor, Memory, has shown that the event cannot be understood as an isolated outburst by an unruly crowd; it grew out of the particular grievances and political culture of the region, and its later memory was shaped by competing narratives from the courts, the nationalist leadership, and the community.

The legal response was severe. A sessions court in Gorakhpur sentenced 172 people to death in January 1923. In the appellate proceedings, the Allahabad High Court dealt with 170 of those death sentences: nineteen were confirmed, 110 were commuted to transportation for life, thirty-eight defendants were acquitted, and three received convictions for rioting only. Nineteen were eventually executed. The distinction between the initial sentencing and the appellate outcomes is essential to interpreting the prosecution. The harshness provoked protest; Madan Mohan Malaviya led the legal defense of the accused, while Gandhi, who regarded the killings as wrong, took a different line from those who justified them.

Gandhi, then in Gujarat preparing the Bardoli campaign, learned of the events a few days later and was shaken. He called a meeting of the Congress Working Committee at Bardoli, which passed resolutions on 12 February 1922 suspending mass civil disobedience throughout the country and directing workers to concentrate on constructive activities such as spinning, national education, untouchability removal, and the promotion of temperance. He also undertook a five-day fast as penance. In his writings in Young India he argued that the movement's nonviolence had been compromised, that the people were not yet ready for civil disobedience, and that continuing in the face of violence would be a betrayal of the principles of satyagraha. He treated the killings as a moral failing for which the movement bore responsibility.

The decision stunned many. Congress leaders in jail, including Motilal Nehru, Lajpat Rai, and Jawaharlal Nehru, and younger activists such as Subhas Chandra Bose, were dismayed. They argued that a movement that had cost so much and reached so far should not be disbanded because of an incident in a remote village, that the government was weakened and that the withdrawal would discourage supporters, who would be left to face repression. Some Khilafat leaders felt abandoned. Others, including Rajagopalachari and Rajendra Prasad, defended Gandhi's decision as necessary to avoid a violent collapse. The disagreement was not simply between those who supported and opposed nonviolence; it concerned judgment about the movement's condition and prospects.

The historical judgment on withdrawal remains disputed. Some historians hold that Gandhi acted to preserve the ethical foundation of satyagraha and to avoid a bloody confrontation that the movement could not win; others argue that the movement was already losing momentum through repression, exhaustion, and internal divisions, and that Gandhi used Chauri Chaura as an occasion to halt a campaign he feared would escape control; and a third view stresses that, whatever his motives, the decision reflected his understanding that mass movements needed organization and training that did not yet exist. The decision should not be portrayed as the inevitable result of a single episode, or as universally accepted.

Historical Debate

Was Nonviolence an Effective Strategy, and Was the 1922 Withdrawal a Mistake?

The question: Did Gandhi's decision to suspend Non-Cooperation after Chauri Chaura protect the ethical and practical basis of a mass nonviolent movement, or did it prematurely end a powerful challenge to British rule? More broadly, how effective was nonviolence as a political strategy in 1920–1922?

Interpretation A — A necessary defense of discipline: The movement had grown beyond the leadership's control, with violence in Bombay, Malabar, and now Chauri Chaura. Continuing might have led to a cycle of violence and repression that the movement, lacking arms and organization, would lose, and would have discredited nonviolence. Suspension preserved the method for later use, and the constructive work and organization built afterward laid the basis for the campaigns of the 1930s.
Interpretation B — A premature retreat: The government was under pressure, repression was producing sympathy, and the movement was at a high point of participation. A local incident, however tragic, did not justify abandoning a national campaign, particularly one that left activists and Khilafat supporters in prison and demoralized. Critics, among them leaders in jail and later Marxist and nationalist historians, argued that Gandhi's emphasis on moral purity subordinated political calculation.

Evidence: Historians draw on Gandhi's writings, letters of Congress leaders, police and intelligence reports on the movement's condition, local studies of Chauri Chaura, and statistics on arrests and participation. Evidence suggests that by early 1922 the movement was uneven: strong in some regions, weakening in others, facing severe repression, and troubled by funds and discipline. Some scholars, including Judith Brown, stress the movement's fragility; others, including Bipan Chandra, emphasize its strength and the logic of Gandhi's view that struggle must be conducted by stages.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians judge Gandhian strategy: as a coherent method tied to a theory of mass discipline, or as an improvisation shaped by personal moral commitments. It also affects interpretation of the relationship between leaders and local actors, since Amin's work shows the people of Chauri Chaura as participants with their own purposes and not merely as a test of discipline.

Section 79.19

Gandhi's Trial, Imprisonment, and the Political Aftermath, 1922–1924

The government, which had hesitated to arrest Gandhi at the height of the movement, moved against him once he had suspended the campaign and his authority seemed weakened. He was arrested at Sabarmati on 10 March 1922 and tried on 18 March in Ahmedabad on charges of sedition under section 124A of the Indian Penal Code, based on three articles published in Young India. Gandhi pleaded guilty and, in a prepared statement, accepted that the law had been broken and asked for the severest penalty. Judge Broomfield, who treated him with notable courtesy, sentenced him to six years' imprisonment, adding that if the government should reduce the term, no one would be happier. The trial became a national event, and the statement was widely circulated.

The political consequences of his imprisonment were profound. The central figure of the movement was removed, and with the Ali brothers and many leaders already in prison, the organization lacked direction. Congress leaders were divided on what to do in the absence of mass civil disobedience. Following the Gaya session in December 1922, presided over by Chittaranjan Das, the party split over the question of the legislative councils. Das and Motilal Nehru, joined by others such as Vithalbhai Patel, argued that Congress should end the boycott of the councils, contest the elections, and use the legislatures to obstruct and expose the government from within. They were known as "pro-changers." Rajagopalachari, Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, and others, the "no-changers," defended the Gandhian program of constructive work and abstention from the councils.

When Das's proposal was defeated at Gaya, he and Motilal Nehru formed the Congress–Khilafat Swarajya Party, usually called the Swaraj Party, in January 1923. A compromise at a special Congress session in Delhi in September 1923 allowed those who wished to contest elections to do so. In the November 1923 elections the Swarajists won a large number of the elected seats in the Central Legislative Assembly and a majority in the Central Provinces, and became the strongest group in Bengal's council. In the Assembly under Motilal Nehru and, in the provinces, Das, they used debate, walkouts, and the rejection of budgets to challenge dyarchy. In Bengal, Das also negotiated a pact in 1923 intended to improve Hindu–Muslim relations, though it provoked opposition.

Gandhi's imprisonment ended early. In January 1924 he fell ill with appendicitis and was operated upon in a Poona hospital, and the government, fearing the consequences if he died in custody, released him on 5 February 1924. He emerged to a changed political landscape: the Khilafat cause was ending, as in March 1924 the Turkish government abolished the caliphate; communal tensions were rising, with riots in various places in 1923 and 1924, notably at Kohat in the Northwest Frontier, which led Gandhi to undertake a twenty-one-day fast in September–October 1924 for Hindu–Muslim unity; and the Congress was split between Swarajists and no-changers. He sought a compromise, and in the following months he worked out an agreement with Das and Motilal Nehru, and he presided over the Belgaum Congress in December 1924. These developments fall near the 1924 endpoint of this chapter and are retained as a short epilogue; they show that Gandhi's priority was to prevent a lasting organizational split.

The wider problem was how to sustain a mass movement after the suspension of a major campaign. Mass mobilization had raised expectations, exhausted resources, and produced repression, and it could not be maintained indefinitely at the pitch of 1921. In the mid-1920s the Congress turned to organizational work, constructive programs, and parliamentary activity, while communal tensions, labor and peasant movements, and new political currents, discussed in Chapter 80, emerged. The long-term consequence of Non-Cooperation was therefore not the immediate achievement of swaraj but the transformation of the means by which nationalism could be organized and sustained, and the establishment of Congress as a continuing national organization with local branches across the country.

Section 79.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Gandhi's Transformation of Indian Nationalism

This chapter has traced how, between 1915 and 1924, Indian nationalism acquired new methods, new organization, and a far broader social reach. The process began outside India, in the South African campaigns where Gandhi, a barrister, moved from petitions and appeals to imperial citizenship toward satyagraha, a method of disciplined civil disobedience that combined an ethical philosophy with practical organization. Those years gave him an institutional repertoire, including a newspaper, communal settlements, and volunteers, as well as experience of the difficulties of keeping a diverse coalition disciplined. They did not give him a finished political doctrine, and when he returned to India in 1915, he spent years learning its politics.

The campaigns at Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda in 1917–1918 were local experiments in Indian conditions. Each involved different communities, grievances, and legal frameworks, and each produced limited and qualified results. Champaran combined evidence-gathering and disciplined defiance to secure reform of a tenant system; Ahmedabad tested mediation and moral pressure in a modern industry; Kheda tested tax resistance among prosperous peasant proprietors. In all three, local leaders were indispensable, and from the campaigns Gandhi acquired a regional following and trusted lieutenants such as Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, and Mahadev Desai.

The crisis of 1919 widened the stage. The Rowlatt Acts, although rarely, if ever, applied in practice, symbolized a government that was unwilling to trust the Indians it had asked to support the war. Gandhi's satyagraha, with its hartals, demonstrated the potential of nationwide protest, and its violence revealed its dangers. The Amritsar massacre and the martial law that followed in Punjab were events of independent importance, an extreme expression of colonial coercion with a lasting effect on the confidence of many Indians in British claims to legitimacy. Meanwhile, the Khilafat movement, with its own leaders and aims, brought a large Muslim constituency into politics, and Gandhi's alliance with it, with all its tensions, expanded nationalism's reach.

Non-Cooperation, launched at Calcutta in September 1920 and institutionalized at Nagpur in December, enlarged the Congress's organizational reach. The new constitution, with its linguistic provincial committees, permanent executive bodies, and low membership fee, gave the party a continuing structure, and the constructive program of khadi, national education, and social service gave volunteers sustained work. The movement enlisted students, lawyers, merchants, women, peasants, and workers, many of whom interpreted swaraj in terms of their own grievances. This breadth gave it strength and also made it hard to control.

The limits became clear in 1921–1922. Mass participation introduced tensions between national leadership and local objectives, between ethical discipline and political confrontation, and between unity and social diversity. The violence in Bombay, Malabar, and Chauri Chaura, and the repression that met the movement, tested nonviolent principles. Gandhi's withdrawal of the campaign in February 1922 exposed these contradictions, and historians still disagree about whether it should be seen as a defense of principle, a political error, or a recognition of organizational limits. His trial and imprisonment, and the Swarajist split that followed, showed the difficulty of sustaining mass politics after a campaign was suspended.

The chapter's central claim is that Gandhi transformed Indian nationalism by developing satyagraha into a method of collective political resistance, adapting it to local agrarian and industrial conflicts, and bringing mass mobilization to the center of Congress politics, but that he did so in interaction with many others. Peasants, workers, women, Khilafat leaders, ulama, local organizers, student volunteers, and rival politicians had their own purposes, and their agency shaped the movement as much as his leadership did. The institutional changes of 1920 remained after the suspension of the campaign, and the experience of 1919–1922 supplied techniques, networks, and arguments for later movements.

Chapter 80 turns to the political aspirations that were not reducible to Congress or to Gandhi's leadership: the peasant and labor movements, communist organization, non-Brahmin politics, the Self-Respect Movement of Periyar, B. R. Ambedkar and Dalit mobilization, and the political activity of women. Chapter 81 then follows the renewal of mass struggle in the late 1920s and 1930s.

Cause and Effect

From Local Satyagraha to the Postwar Crisis to National Mobilization

Long-term causes

Decades of constitutional agitation by the early Congress; Swadeshi boycott traditions; Gandhi's South African experience; growth of vernacular press and regional associations; economic strain and political disappointment under colonial rule.

Short-term causes

Wartime taxation, inflation, epidemic, and poor harvests; the Rowlatt Acts; the Amritsar massacre and martial law; the Ottoman defeat and Khilafat agitation; dissatisfaction with the 1919 reforms.

Event or transformation

The Rowlatt satyagraha, the Khilafat alliance, the Calcutta and Nagpur decisions, and the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–1922 turned Congress from an elite association into a mass organization.

Immediate and long-term effects

Immediate: enlarged membership, repression, violence, withdrawal, Gandhi's imprisonment, Swarajist split. Long-term: a permanent organizational structure, an established repertoire of mass nonviolent protest, and unresolved tensions over unity and social diversity.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the movement. Gandhi's leadership, local organizers, Khilafat politics, economic distress, and colonial repression interacted, and each shaped the others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Indian nationalism changed between 1915 and 1924 in its methods, organization, and social reach, and how Mohandas Gandhi, working with many others, brought mass nonviolent mobilization to the center of Congress politics. It has argued that Gandhi did not single-handedly create mass nationalism: he adapted older traditions of protest, relied on local organizers, and operated within a postwar crisis not of his making.

The chapter began with Gandhi's twenty years in South Africa. Arriving in Natal in 1893, he encountered racial discrimination, helped found the Natal Indian Congress, and published Indian Opinion. His early politics were loyalist and constitutional and spoke chiefly for merchants; from 1906 the campaigns against the Transvaal registration law and, in 1913–1914, against the three-pound tax and the marriage judgment drew in laborers and women and produced the method of satyagraha. Phoenix and Tolstoy Farm were experiments in communal life, and Hind Swaraj set out a critique of modern civilization and a vision of self-rule.

Satyagraha combined truth, nonviolence, voluntary suffering, and self-discipline with a practical method of civil disobedience, and it raised difficult questions about authority, discipline, and scale. After his return in 1915, Gandhi spent time learning Indian conditions and founded the Satyagraha Ashram, a collective in which Kasturba Gandhi, Maganlal Gandhi, Mahadev Desai, and others were essential and where the admission of an untouchable family showed both his commitments and the limits of his social base.

The local campaigns of 1917–1918 were different experiments. At Champaran he helped tenants challenge the tinkathia system, combining testimony, defiance of an order, and negotiation, with the Champaran Agrarian Act of 1918 as the result. At Ahmedabad he intervened in a wage dispute among mill workers, using arbitration, a strike pledge, and a contested fast. At Kheda he led prosperous Patidar peasants in withholding revenue, ending in a limited settlement. In each, local organizers were indispensable and the outcomes were partial.

The Rowlatt Act of 1919 retained wartime-style powers and, though it was seldom if ever applied, became a symbol of broken promises. Gandhi's satyagraha against it produced the first all-India hartal on 6 April 1919 but was marred by violence and suspended after two weeks. At Amritsar, following the arrest of Kitchlew and Satyapal and the riots of 10 April, General Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April; the official count was 379 dead and Indian estimates were much higher. Martial law, the Hunter Committee, and the Congress inquiry followed, and the Punjab events shook confidence in British legitimacy.

The Khilafat movement, led by the Ali brothers, Azad, Ajmal Khan, and many ulama, mobilized Muslim opinion around the fate of the Ottoman caliphate and the holy places, and had independent religious and diplomatic aims. Gandhi's decision to support it and link it to non-cooperation created a Hindu–Muslim alliance that expanded nationalism but contained differing understandings of its purposes. The Calcutta session of September 1920 adopted a graduated program of non-cooperation, and the Nagpur session of December 1920 reorganized Congress, changing its creed, creating linguistic provincial committees, a Working Committee, and a four-anna membership.

Non-cooperation operated through boycotts of schools, courts, councils, and foreign cloth, through national institutions such as Gujarat Vidyapith and Jamia Millia Islamia, and through the constructive program of khadi and the charkha. It spread unevenly across India, drawing in students, lawyers, merchants, women, peasants, and workers, many of whom interpreted swaraj in terms of local grievances. In 1921 repression grew, riots occurred in Bombay, and the Mappila rebellion in Malabar, rooted in tenancy conflict and intersecting with Khilafat agitation, involved large-scale violence and harsh repression.

On 4 February 1922 a crowd at Chauri Chaura killed twenty-two policemen, and Gandhi suspended mass civil disobedience, to the dismay of many colleagues. Historians disagree on whether this was a defense of principle, a political error, or a recognition of organizational limits. His arrest, trial, and imprisonment, the debate over council entry, and the creation of the Swaraj Party in 1923 followed; Gandhi was released in February 1924 and sought to avoid a lasting split.

The chapter concludes that the immediate aim of swaraj was not achieved, but that Congress had become a continuing national organization, mass nonviolent protest had become part of the repertoire of Indian politics, and the unresolved tensions among leadership and participants, religious communities, and social groups had become visible. These tensions frame Chapter 80 and the later history of the nationalist movement.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it marks the point at which nationalism became a mass politics. Earlier chapters showed an educated elite petitioning for reform and a series of sporadic popular movements. After 1920, the Congress had a permanent organization, a repertoire of boycott, hartal, and civil disobedience, and a social reach extending into towns and countryside. That change shaped the campaigns of the 1930s and 1940s, and the leaders and structures built in 1919–1922 would be central to the final struggle for independence.

The chapter also shows how a movement's strength and weakness can have the same source. The breadth that made Non-Cooperation powerful, with participants of many classes, communities, and regions, also meant that the movement contained aims the leadership could not control. The Khilafat alliance brought Muslims into nationalist politics while revealing unresolved questions about the basis of unity. The ethics of nonviolence gave the movement moral force and also made it vulnerable to local violence. These tensions did not disappear after 1922.

Within the six-volume sequence, this chapter links the colonial history of Volume IV to the road to Partition and independence. It shows the colonial state not only as an administrative machine but as a political actor that responded to protest with concession and coercion alike. It also introduces themes taken up in Chapters 80 through 87: the independent politics of peasants, workers, Dalits, and women; the renewed civil disobedience of the 1930s; communal politics; and the contests over what an independent India or Pakistan might be.

Finally, the chapter invites students to resist simple stories. Gandhi was extraordinarily influential, but his influence worked through other people. The decision of 1922 was neither pure triumph nor plain failure. And Amritsar, Malabar, and Chauri Chaura each have local histories that deserve attention for their own sake. Understanding mass nationalism requires attending to leaders and to the people who joined, resisted, and reinterpreted what they were asked to do.
