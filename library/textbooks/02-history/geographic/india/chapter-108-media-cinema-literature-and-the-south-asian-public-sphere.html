Part XVIII — Liberalization and Contemporary South Asia, 1991–2026

Media, Cinema, Literature, and the South Asian Public Sphere

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: 20 November 2026

Chapter Opening

On a single evening in the later 1980s, much of urban and small-town India could be found in front of one of a very small number of television sets, watching the state-run television network broadcast an epic in serial form. A little over a decade later, many households had access to dozens, and eventually hundreds, of channels in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, and other languages, many of them privately owned and financed by advertising. By 2026 a substantial share of the audience was watching neither a broadcast channel nor a cinema screen, but a phone. The distance between these two scenes is the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 106 explained the structural economic and technological transformations that remade South Asia after 1991, including the spread of mobile telephony, digital payments, and connectivity, and Chapter 107 examined the social struggles over caste, gender, religion, and rights through which South Asians contested their own societies. This chapter turns to the institutions through which those transformations and struggles were represented, communicated, argued over, and sold: newspapers and publishing houses, writers and translators, film industries, broadcasters, musicians, online platforms, and the diasporic audiences that carried South Asian culture across the world. It treats culture as a historical force in its own right, not as an ornamental afterword to economic and political history.

Three transformations organize the account. The first is the expansion of private and commercial media after liberalization, which ended or weakened state monopolies over broadcasting in several countries, multiplied newspapers and channels, and made advertising revenue the central financial fact of public communication. The second is the growing weight of regional languages and transnational audiences: the Hindi film industry, long treated as the cultural center of the region, was joined and in some respects overtaken in cultural energy by Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi, and Bengali industries, while Pakistani television drama, Bangladeshi streaming services, and diasporic markets created circuits of attention that did not run through a single capital. The third is the movement from print and broadcast media toward digital platforms, online creators, and algorithmic distribution, which allowed ordinary people to publish at scale while also creating new concentrations of technological and commercial power.

The geographic frame is deliberately South Asian rather than Indian. India's scale gives it the largest share of the narrative, but the chapter also follows Pakistan's television networks and cinema, Bangladesh's journalism and screen industries, Sri Lanka's literary and film traditions, Nepal's community radio and youth politics, Bhutan's late and carefully managed arrival of television and the internet, and Maldivian broadcasting after the democratic transition. These histories are not variations on an Indian template. Each unfolded within its own language politics, regulatory institutions, and relationship with political authority, and the differences are part of the argument.

A methodological caution governs the whole chapter. Film popularity, newspaper circulation, television ratings, and social-media engagement can establish reach. They do not, by themselves, establish a particular political or social effect, and the historian must resist the common assumption that because a medium was widely consumed it must have determined what its audience believed or how it voted. Likewise, novels and films are historically situated evidence of how writers, producers, censors, and audiences imagined their societies; they are not statistically representative accounts of how those societies actually behaved. The chapter therefore asks, throughout, who produced a work, who financed and regulated it, who could see or read it, and what contemporaries said in response.

The evidence is correspondingly varied: statutes and regulatory records, press-freedom assessments, industry reports, film and television programs themselves, published literature, journalists' own testimony, court rulings, and the digital traces of online communication. Each has limits. Industry reports are produced by organizations with commercial interests in presenting the sector as dynamic; press-freedom indices are composite judgments that compress complex realities into rankings; and platform data are largely controlled by the companies that generate them. Where figures from such sources appear, they are attributed and qualified.

The chapter ends at the research cutoff of 20 November 2026. Developments in legislation, litigation, and the media economy that were still unresolved at that date are described as unresolved. The central argument is that South Asia's public sphere expanded dramatically between 1991 and 2026, but that its transformation was shaped by competition over language, representation, commercial influence, technological access, and political authority, and that more communication did not automatically produce more equality, more reliable information, or more freedom of expression. Chapter 109 will carry the story into South Asia's environmental history, in which questions of public knowledge, journalism, and communication recur in a very different register.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how liberalization and private ownership changed the financing, ownership, and content of South Asian print and broadcast media after 1991.
  2. Distinguish among the roles of language, translation, and publishing institutions in determining which writers and ideas circulated nationally and internationally.
  3. Analyze literature as historical evidence, distinguishing literary representation from documentary evidence of social conditions.
  4. Compare the industrial histories, political relationships, and audiences of Hindi cinema, the regional cinemas of India, and the film industries of Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives.
  5. Trace the expansion of satellite, cable, and private television across the region and assess the difference between demonstrated reach and demonstrated political effect.
  6. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether commercial broadcasting and social media widened democratic participation or weakened reliable public information.
  7. Assess the roles of digital media in the Bangladeshi uprising of 2024 and the Nepali youth mobilization of 2025, while limiting claims about media to what evidence can support.
  8. Distinguish a government's stated regulatory purpose from the legal powers it acquires and from documented evidence of implementation, using the cases of India, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka.
  9. Interpret primary documents, including legislation and an industry report, with attention to authorship, purpose, perspective, and silence.
  10. Explain how streaming, diasporic audiences, and generative artificial intelligence altered the distribution and economics of South Asian cultural production through 2026.
  11. Connect the media history of this chapter to the economic and technological transformations of Chapter 106 and the social struggles of Chapter 107.

Historical Context

The media systems of 1991 had been shaped by the postcolonial settlements described in earlier chapters. In India, the newspaper press inherited from the colonial and nationalist periods operated within a constitutional guarantee of expression that was nevertheless qualified by restrictions, and it had experienced the Emergency of 1975–77, when censorship and detention curtailed public criticism (Chapter 93). Broadcasting, by contrast, was a state monopoly: All India Radio and Doordarshan were government-controlled, and the programs they carried, from the serialized Ramayan and Mahabharat of the late 1980s to national news bulletins, reflected official priorities. Hindi cinema was a vast commercial industry, but one that struggled to obtain formal financing, while regional cinemas were closely tied to their linguistic communities and, in the South, to the politics of language and identity.

The other countries of the region developed their own arrangements. Pakistan’s Pakistan Television Corporation, founded in 1964, was a state broadcaster, and military rule under Zia-ul-Haq (Chapter 95) left a legacy of censorship and Islamization of public culture. In Bangladesh, state-run Bangladesh Television and Bangladesh Betar shaped broadcasting after independence (Chapter 96), while a lively Bengali-language press and literary culture developed alongside them. Sri Lanka had state-controlled and mixed radio and television, and a press divided by language and politics. Nepal’s state-run Nepal Television dated to 1985 and the country’s media were constrained under the Panchayat system until 1990. Bhutan had radio and a government newspaper but deliberately postponed television and the internet until 1999. Maldives had state radio and television and very limited independent media before its constitutional reforms of 2008.

The economic and political transformations after 1991 changed these arrangements. India’s liberalization (Chapter 98) opened sectors to private and foreign participation and created an advertising economy that financed expansion; the collapse of Soviet-era certainties and the globalization of satellite communication reshaped the region’s broadcasting. Political conflict, including the Mandal and Ayodhya struggles of the early 1990s (Chapter 99), also made the press and television participants in, and subjects of, intense argument. Later, the connectivity revolution described in Chapter 106 produced hundreds of millions of smartphone users and shifted the center of media gravity toward digital platforms. At the same time, the contested politics of Kashmir (Chapter 105) and the changing social movements of Chapter 107 gave media institutions both stories to tell and pressures to endure.

Readers should keep in mind that the sources for this period are abundant but uneven. Statutes and official reports are retrievable; industry data are plentiful but promotional; scholarly literature on some media, such as Hindi cinema, is extensive, while research on Maldivian or Bhutanese media is limited. The chapter notes such asymmetries rather than concealing them.

Print, Literature, Language, and the Modern Public Sphere

Section 108.1

Print Culture after Independence and the Liberalization Era

South Asian print culture entered the postcolonial period with deep roots. Newspapers had been central to nationalist politics, to social reform movements, and to the formation of linguistic communities, as earlier chapters showed, and independence did not end that role. In India, the founding of the Sahitya Akademi in 1954 to promote literature in the country’s recognized languages, the growth of publishing houses in Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Malayalam, and other languages, and the development of literary magazines, small presses, and public libraries created reading communities that were organized around language more than around a single national culture. Calcutta’s book fair, begun in 1976, and Dhaka’s month-long Ekushey book fair, tied to the memory of the Bengali Language Movement, illustrate how reading and publishing could carry political and historical meaning.

The postcolonial press was shaped by law and politics. The Press Council of India, established under legislation of 1978 after the Emergency, was designed as a body to preserve press freedom and maintain standards, but it possessed limited enforcement powers. In Pakistan, press freedom fluctuated sharply across periods of military and civilian rule, with newspapers such as Dawn and the Jang group building large readerships while periodically facing censorship, advertising pressure, and arrests. In Bangladesh, a vibrant Bengali- and English-language press emerged after 1971 and expanded after the restoration of parliamentary politics in 1991 (Chapter 102). In Sri Lanka, newspapers in Sinhala, Tamil, and English reflected and sometimes inflamed the island’s linguistic divisions. In Nepal, the end of Panchayat rule in 1990 allowed private papers to flourish, and Kantipur Publications, founded in 1993, became a major commercial and journalistic institution.

After 1991, India’s press expanded in a way that surprised observers who expected television and the internet to displace it. The historian Robin Jeffrey, writing about the earlier period, described a “newspaper revolution” driven by Indian-language papers, and that trajectory continued: rising literacy, improved printing and distribution technology, regional advertising, and the expansion of towns and districts allowed Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali, Marathi, and other vernacular dailies to open new editions and reach new readers, often at very low cover prices subsidized by advertising. Large chains, including the Times of India group, Dainik Jagran, Dainik Bhaskar, Eenadu, Malayala Manorama, and Anandabazar Patrika, grew by establishing multiple city and district editions. The structure of their business, in which the reader paid little and the advertiser paid more, made newspapers dependent on commercial and political patronage in ways that shaped their coverage.

Newspapers retained influence for reasons that were not merely technological. They were a local institution: district editions reported on panchayat decisions, school results, municipal services, and local crime in ways that national television rarely did. They were an agenda-setting institution, as television and later social media frequently took stories from newspaper reporting. They were a source of social status and political legitimacy, so that political parties and candidates courted editors and publishers. And in much of the region print circulation grew or held steady while Western newspapers declined, because literacy and local-language readership were still expanding. Circulation figures, however, require care, since publishers’ claims and audited circulation figures differed, and readership surveys disagreed with one another.

The commercial transformation also generated serious problems. In India, the practice of “paid news,” in which political candidates paid for what appeared as editorial coverage, became a public controversy around the 2009 elections and was examined in a report prepared for the Press Council of India in 2010. “Private treaties,” in which companies gave equity stakes to media groups in exchange for favorable coverage, raised related concerns. Defamation law, in India and across the region, remained a tool that powerful actors could use to burden publishers with costly litigation. These issues, together with ownership concentration and political alignment, are examined in §108.4.

Print culture after 1991 also included books. Publishing houses expanded as English-language publishers such as Penguin India and HarperCollins India established local lists, as Hindi publishers adapted to new markets, and as literary festivals, beginning with the Jaipur Literature Festival in 2006 and followed by gatherings in Galle, Karachi, Dhaka, and Kathmandu, created new public occasions for writers and readers. The relationship between print and electoral politics, meanwhile, deepened: newspapers published opinion polls, endorsed or opposed parties, and covered campaigns, while parties launched their own publications and later digital outlets.

The sequence shows that media change was neither a single revolution nor a simple replacement of old media by new. Each phase added a layer to the earlier one: television did not end print, streaming did not end cinema, and social media did not end television. Acceleration occurred when technology, law, and economics converged, as in the early 1990s and again after 2016. The delay between technological availability and legal regulation, visible in the gap between the arrival of social platforms and the statutes of the 2020s, is one of the sequence’s recurring features.

Magazines and weeklies formed a second layer of the print public. News weeklies such as India Today (founded in 1975), Outlook (1995), and Frontline, women’s magazines, film magazines, children’s periodicals, and an enormous number of small-circulation “little magazines” in Bengali, Malayalam, Marathi, and other languages carried criticism, fiction, and debate that daily papers did not. Economic structure mattered here too. Newsprint costs, government advertising, and the audit of circulation figures by industry bodies shaped what publishers could afford to print, and the dependence of many titles on state advertising gave governments an informal lever over editorial lines. In Pakistan, the divide between a prestigious English-language press and a much larger Urdu press, and in Sri Lanka and Bangladesh the parallel existence of English and vernacular papers, meant that national public debate was in practice several debates conducted at different social levels.

Section 108.2

Languages, Publishing, Translation, and Cultural Representation

In South Asia, language is not a neutral medium of culture but a structure of cultural authority. India’s constitution recognizes a large number of scheduled languages; Pakistan’s national language, Urdu, is the first language of a minority of its people while Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki, and others have large speaking communities; Bangladesh was founded partly on the principle of Bengali linguistic identity; Sri Lanka’s history includes the Sinhala-only policies and Tamil responses that contributed to civil war (Chapter 103); and Nepal and Bhutan have their own multilingual landscapes. Publishing and media systems amplified some languages and constrained others.

Hindi, with its enormous number of speakers, supported a substantial publishing and newspaper sector and a film and television industry, but Hindi publishing also had characteristic limits, including a market dominated by textbooks, religious literature, and popular fiction, and relatively weak retail and distribution networks in many areas. Urdu, once the dominant literary language of north Indian Muslim and many other elite cultures, declined in India’s publishing market as state education shifted away from it, while it remained central to Pakistani literature, journalism, and television. Bengali sustained a strong and widely read literary tradition across India and Bangladesh, with fiction, poetry, and magazines in both Kolkata and Dhaka. Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, and Marathi had vigorous literary and newspaper cultures, with Malayalam in particular associated with very high literacy and newspaper readership. Sinhala and Tamil publishing in Sri Lanka, Nepali publishing in Nepal and the Nepali-speaking regions of India, and Dzongkha and English publishing in Bhutan were smaller markets with their own constraints.

English occupied a distinctive position. As a language of higher education, administration, law, business, and elite status, English supported a publishing industry whose books could reach both national and international readers. English-language fiction by South Asian writers gained international prominence from the 1980s and especially after 1991, often through London and New York publishers, literary agents, and prize systems. This created advantages and tensions. English-language writers could reach international audiences and sometimes substantial advances, while writers in other languages frequently depended on small domestic markets, on state or academic patronage, or on translation to cross linguistic boundaries. Critics debated whether a literature written in English by a small, often urban and educated, group could claim to represent the languages and experiences of hundreds of millions of others, and writers themselves differed on the question.

Translation became the means by which these hierarchies were partly bridged. Institutions such as the Sahitya Akademi and National Book Trust in India supported translation between Indian languages, and commercial and independent presses expanded English translation lists. International recognition of translated work in the 2020s was notable: Geetanjali Shree’s Hindi novel Ret Samadhi, translated by Daisy Rockwell as Tomb of Sand, won the International Booker Prize in 2022, and in 2025 Banu Mushtaq’s Kannada short-story collection Heart Lamp, translated by Deepa Bhasthi, won the same prize. These awards were widely read as recognition of languages that had rarely reached global readers. They also illustrated the continuing role of translators and international publishers as gatekeepers, since works reached prize lists through English.

Publishing institutions shaped what could circulate in other ways. Booksellers, distributors, literary editors, festivals, and prize committees all selected and promoted work. Legal and political pressures constrained circulation: books could be banned, withdrawn, or made the subject of litigation or public campaigns. The Tamil novelist Perumal Murugan’s announcement in 2015 that he was withdrawing from writing after protests against his novel Madhorubhagan (published in English as One Part Woman), followed by a 2016 Madras High Court ruling in favor of his freedom to write, became a widely discussed case of the pressures that local groups could exert on authors. Taslima Nasrin’s novel Lajja (1993) was banned in Bangladesh and she went into exile after threats in 1994, while Salman Rushdie’s The Satanic Verses (1988) had been banned in India shortly after publication. These episodes show that the public sphere was shaped not only by commerce but by social pressure and state authority.

Educational and market structures reinforced these patterns. School and examination textbooks formed the largest part of the publishing market in many languages, so the fortunes of a language’s publishers were closely tied to state education policy. The growth of English-medium schooling, which many families regarded as a route to employment, was widely discussed as reducing the number of young readers in some mother tongues, although the evidence varies by language and region and should not be assumed. Official recognition mattered as well: Nepali, for example, was added to the schedule of recognized languages in India’s constitution in 1992, which brought it within the scope of academy awards and translation programs. Bengali illustrates the cross-border dimension, since the same language supported separate but overlapping literary worlds in Kolkata and Dhaka, with different political histories, readerships, and publishing systems. Punjabi, divided between India and Pakistan and written in different scripts, shows how a single language could be split by script and border, so that readers on each side often could not read one another’s books without transliteration.

Section 108.3

South Asian Literature, Historical Memory, and Social Criticism, 1991–2026

Literature is among the most important sources for understanding how South Asians interpreted the history through which they were living, but it requires careful use. A novel is a work of imagination shaped by its author’s position, audience, language, and market. It reveals how some writers represented caste, gender, migration, violence, or memory, and how readers and critics responded. It cannot be treated as a statistically representative account of society, nor as a substitute for documentary evidence. Historians use literary works as evidence of social imagination and historical interpretation.

Partition remained one of the most persistent literary subjects, as an inheritance from earlier decades and as an interpretive lens for later conflicts. Bapsi Sidhwa, a Pakistani Parsi writer, depicted Lahore during Partition in her novel known in the United States as Cracking India, offering a perspective shaped by a small religious minority and by a child narrator. Amitav Ghosh’s The Shadow Lines (1988) had linked Partition, borders, and memory, and his later novels, including The Glass Palace (2000), the Ibis trilogy beginning with Sea of Poppies (2008), and The Hungry Tide (2004), connected South Asian history to the Indian Ocean, the opium trade, migration, and environmental change. His later non-fiction and fiction on climate, such as Gun Island (2019), anticipates themes of Chapter 109.

Arundhati Roy’s The God of Small Things (1997) combined a Kerala family story with a critique of caste and gender hierarchy and won the Booker Prize, and her 2017 novel The Ministry of Utmost Happiness extended her concerns to Kashmir, communal violence, and marginalized lives, while her political essays made her a prominent and polarizing public critic. Taslima Nasrin’s writing on women’s status and religious orthodoxy brought both international attention and severe domestic hostility. Pakistani writers such as Mohsin Hamid, Mohammed Hanif, and Kamila Shamsie used English-language fiction to examine political violence, military power, and migration, including Hamid’s The Reluctant Fundamentalist (2007) and Shamsie’s Home Fire (2017), while Urdu literature continued in its own register and with its own readers.

Sri Lanka’s civil war and its aftermath produced a significant literature. Michael Ondaatje’s Anil’s Ghost (2000) addressed forensic investigation of political killings. Shehan Karunatilaka’s The Seven Moons of Maali Almeida, which won the Booker Prize in 2022, used a satirical afterlife setting to portray the island’s wartime disappearances and killings, and Anuk Arudpragasam’s A Passage North (2021) explored the war’s psychological aftermath for Tamil characters. Nepali literature, including the work of writers such as Samrat Upadhyay and Manjushree Thapa in English and many writers in Nepali, engaged with the Maoist insurgency, migration, and political change (Chapter 104).

Marginalized literary traditions challenged dominant representations directly. Dalit literature, which emerged in Marathi in the 1960s and 1970s alongside the Dalit Panthers and spread to Hindi, Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Gujarati, and other languages, produced autobiographies and fiction that documented caste exclusion from the standpoint of those subjected to it. Omprakash Valmiki’s Joothan (1997), Baby Kamble’s The Prisons We Broke, written in Marathi, and Bama’s Karukku (1992) in Tamil are important examples, and Bama’s writing in particular brought together Dalit and Christian experience and gender. These works contested sanitized or upper-caste portrayals of village life, and their reception illustrates both the democratization of literary production and the continuing difficulty of access to mainstream publishers and critics. The social history of the movements that produced them belongs to Chapter 107.

Literary works also provoked controversy, and their reception can be as historically informative as their content. Attacks on authors, litigation over books, and campaigns of protest, including the 2022 attack on Salman Rushdie in New York, which was not a South Asian event but resonated across the region, forced debate about who may speak about religion, nation, and history. Students should therefore ask not only what a work says but who published it, in what language, for what readers, and who sought to restrict it.

Poetry and political culture were also intertwined. Verses by the Pakistani poet Faiz Ahmed Faiz were sung at protests in India against the citizenship law in 2019–20, and a complaint that one such performance was anti-Hindu prompted an inquiry at an Indian institute of technology, an example of how a poem composed decades earlier in a different political setting could be reinterpreted and contested. Literary writing could also bring mortal danger. In Bangladesh, the secular writer and blogger Avijit Roy was murdered in February 2015, and the publisher Faisal Arefin Dipon was killed in October 2015, in attacks associated with militant opposition to secular and freethinking writers; the killings drove several writers and publishers into exile or silence. Literary festivals, bookshops, and online communities served as semi-public spaces in which such issues were debated, though their audiences were socially narrow. These episodes underline that literature in the period was not only a record of historical memory but an arena in which memory and belief were actively and sometimes violently contested.

Section 108.4

Journalism, Press Freedom, and the Politics of Public Knowledge

Journalism is the institution through which societies are supposed to learn what their governments, corporations, and fellow citizens are doing. Its operation depends on editorial institutions with the resources to investigate, on legal protection against arbitrary punishment, and on an economic base that does not make truth a threat to survival. In South Asia after 1991, all three conditions were contested.

Historical Vocabulary
Public sphere, Vernacular Press, and Editorial Independence

The public sphere, a concept associated with the philosopher Jürgen Habermas, refers to the realm of communication in which people discuss matters of common concern and form opinions independent of state or market control. Historians use the concept critically, since access to any public sphere is unequal and many South Asian publics were excluded by language, caste, gender, and income. The vernacular press refers to newspapers and periodicals in languages other than English, which in South Asia often reach far larger audiences than English papers but have historically received less international attention. Editorial independence refers to the ability of editors and journalists to make decisions about coverage free from improper interference by owners, advertisers, or governments; it is a matter of degree and can be compromised by ownership concentration as well as by legal pressure.

These terms matter because the chapter asks whether the expansion of media produced a wider public sphere, and answering requires attention to who spoke, in what language, and under what constraints.

Investigative journalism in the period produced important disclosures but also exposed its limits. The Tehelka magazine’s 2001 sting operation on defense procurement, the publication of recorded conversations of a lobbyist in 2010, and reporting on corruption in sports, telecommunications, and public contracts demonstrated the capacity of both print and television to reveal wrongdoing. In Pakistan, journalists investigated military and intelligence conduct at considerable personal risk, and the killing of the journalist Saleem Shahzad in 2011 became a symbol of that danger. In Bangladesh, reporters covering corruption and political violence faced prosecution under the Digital Security Act of 2018, and the photographer and activist Shahidul Alam was detained in 2018 after commenting on protests. In Sri Lanka, the murder of the editor Lasantha Wickrematunge in January 2009, which remained a symbol of impunity for years, shaped a generation’s understanding of the risks of reporting. In India, the murder of the journalist Gauri Lankesh in 2017 and the arrests of reporters and media organizations under security and anti-terror laws, including the case of the news site NewsClick, which was investigated in 2023, generated persistent debate over press freedom.

Ownership and economics shaped the independent press. In India, large corporate groups acquired stakes in news channels and papers, and business interests often overlapped with political ones. Advertising from governments and large companies was an important source of revenue, and withdrawal of advertising was a recognized form of pressure. Many digital news outlets struggled to monetize, and the 2026 FICCI–EY report notes that news had only about four million paid subscribers in a market where free alternatives were abundant (see §108.17). These economic facts help explain why independent outlets could be fragile even where law did not forbid criticism.

Press freedom is commonly measured by comparative indices, and the best known is the annual World Press Freedom Index published by Reporters Without Borders (RSF). In the 2026 index, released on 30 April, Nepal was ranked 87th of 180 countries, Maldives 108th, Sri Lanka 134th, Bhutan 150th, Bangladesh 152nd, Pakistan 153rd, and India 157th, a fall from 151st in 2025; RSF classifies Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Maldives in the “difficult” category (scores from 40 to below 55) and India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Bhutan in the “very serious” category. These rankings should be treated as a documented assessment rather than an unquestionable measure. They combine expert surveys with quantitative counts of abuses, depend on methodological choices about indicators and weights, and can produce rank changes that exaggerate or understate real shifts. Rankings can also sit uneasily with local experience: Nepal’s relatively high rank coexists with reports, in the Kathmandu press, of attacks on journalists during unrest and of legal and economic pressure on media organizations. The index is most useful as a prompt for examining the evidence on which it rests, including the legal indicator, which RSF reported had worsened across much of the world in 2026.

Press freedom also carries responsibilities. Journalism that makes unverified allegations, publishes sensational or defamatory claims, or serves as advocacy while presenting itself as reporting can damage public trust and harm individuals. Self-regulatory bodies, press councils, and professional codes exist for this reason, though their effectiveness varied. The historian’s task is to distinguish journalism that was constrained by power from journalism that failed its own standards, and to recognize that both could occur in the same media system.

Journalists’ safety and legal exposure differed across the region but followed recognizable patterns. In Maldives, the disappearance in August 2014 of the journalist Ahmed Rilwan, who had reported for an independent online outlet, became an emblem of the dangers of reporting on political and religious extremism. In Pakistan, the killing of the journalist Arshad Sharif in Kenya in October 2022 deepened concern about the risks faced by reporters critical of powerful institutions, and in India the arrest in 2020 of the reporter Siddique Kappan on his way to cover a case of alleged assault in Uttar Pradesh, and his release from custody on 2 February 2023 after bail decisions in 2022, illustrated the use of security laws against working journalists. In Bangladesh, the death in custody in 2021 of the writer Mushtaq Ahmed, who had been held under the Digital Security Act, drew national and international criticism. These cases are not equivalent in circumstances or evidence, and the facts of several remain disputed or subject to legal proceedings; they are cited here as documented episodes that shaped the climate in which journalists worked, and which press-freedom assessments attempt to capture. Self-censorship, which leaves no record, is by its nature harder to document than arrests or killings, and historians should therefore treat visible cases as a minimum rather than a measure of the whole.

Cinema, Popular Narrative, and the Transformation of Screen Culture

Section 108.5

Hindi Cinema, Bollywood, and the Liberalization-Era Film Industry

Hindi cinema, produced chiefly in Mumbai, was already one of the world’s largest film industries in 1991, but its organization changed profoundly in the following decades. Before the late 1990s, the industry was financed largely through informal and sometimes unaccountable sources, since films were not recognized as an industry for purposes of formal credit. The Government of India granted the film business industry status in 1998, which opened the way to bank financing and more transparent corporate structures. Corporate studios, including firms that later merged into larger media groups, entered production and distribution, and established family-run studios such as Yash Raj Films adopted professional management and global marketing. Satellite rights, sold to television channels in advance, became a major source of film finance, and the music industry, anchored by labels such as T-Series, was another financial pillar.

Exhibition changed as well. The first multiplex in India opened in Delhi in 1997, and multiplexes spread through urban India in the 2000s, allowing higher ticket prices, different scheduling, and a market for films aimed at urban middle-class audiences who had stopped attending older single-screen theaters. Single-screen cinemas, which had served mass audiences and smaller towns, declined in number in many areas. At the same time, overseas markets became commercially important. Films aimed at diasporic audiences in Britain, North America, and the Gulf could earn significant revenue, and producers made decisions about settings, language, and themes partly with those audiences in mind. The term “Bollywood,” which came into wide use in the 1990s and 2000s, described this commercial Hindi cinema, though it was and remains contested, since some filmmakers regard it as a pejorative and since it is often misused to describe all Indian cinema.

Commercially influential films can be read as evidence of changing aspirations, with the caution that popularity does not show that audiences accepted the values on screen. Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge (1995), directed by Aditya Chopra, followed a romance between two young Non-Resident Indians in Europe and negotiated parental authority, tradition, and individual choice; its record-setting run in a Mumbai theater made it a cultural reference point and illustrated the importance of diasporic characters. Hum Aapke Hain Koun..! (1994) presented an opulent joint-family spectacle. Lagaan (2001), set in colonial India and centered on a cricket match, was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film and showed the appeal of nationalist historical narrative for both domestic and international audiences. Films of the 2000s, such as Dil Chahta Hai (2001) and Rang De Basanti (2006), addressed urban youth, friendship, and political disillusionment, while later films examined sport, small-town life, gender, and social issues with varying commercial success.

Films also became sites of political conflict. Controversies over historical films and religious sensibilities, such as the opposition to Padmaavat in 2017–18, campaigns to boycott films and actors, and the commercial success of politically charged films in the 2020s, including The Kashmir Files (2022) and The Kerala Story (2023), showed that cinema could be both a vehicle for and a target of mobilization around identity. The Cinematograph (Amendment) Act of 2023 introduced additional age-based certification categories and anti-piracy provisions, but the Central Board of Film Certification continued to be criticized for cuts and delays. The film industry also faced social questions within its own ranks, including debates about nepotism, access, and the treatment of women.

The industry’s position changed during the 2010s and 2020s. Hindi films such as Dangal (2016) found a very large audience in China, while in 2022 a series of Hindi box-office disappointments, set against the success of dubbed South Indian releases, produced a widely discussed narrative of Bollywood’s decline, which was complicated by the major successes of films such as Pathaan and Jawan in 2023. A careful historian should avoid treating either story as settled. Box-office performance fluctuates; the more durable development was that Hindi cinema no longer enjoyed uncontested cultural primacy, a theme developed in §108.6. It is important not to use “Bollywood” as a synonym for the entire Indian film industry, which comprised production in many languages.

The content of Hindi cinema diversified as the industry changed. Filmmakers working in the 2000s and 2010s, such as Anurag Kashyap, whose Dev.D (2009) and Gangs of Wasseypur (2012) drew on crime, regional speech, and the history of the coal belt, produced films aimed at multiplex and festival audiences that differed from the family spectacle of the 1990s. Portrayals of women, Muslims, and lower-caste characters were repeatedly criticized and revised: Muslim characters had often appeared in stereotyped roles, and scholars and critics debated how far newer films complicated or reinforced them. The relationship with organized crime, evident in the 1990s in extortion and in the 1997 murder of the music-industry head Gulshan Kumar, receded after the industry’s formalization, while piracy, first on cassette and video and later online, remained a persistent threat to revenue. Songs, once the central selling point of a film, became promotional tools, and star-driven marketing, social-media campaigns, and international premieres became standard practice. These changes illustrate that the industry’s economic restructuring was inseparable from changes in what kinds of stories were told and to whom.

Section 108.6

Regional Indian Cinemas and the Reshaping of Cultural Influence

India’s regional-language film industries have long histories and distinct characters, and by 2026 they were among the most important sources of cultural energy in the country. Tamil cinema was closely connected to the Dravidian political movement, whose leaders, such as C. N. Annadurai and M. Karunanidhi, wrote for the screen, and to the careers of star-politicians such as M. G. Ramachandran, who became chief minister in 1977; in Andhra Pradesh, N. T. Rama Rao founded the Telugu Desam Party in 1982 after a film career. Telugu cinema, centered in Hyderabad, developed large-scale commercial production with a strong audience in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Malayalam cinema became known for relatively modest budgets, literary connections, and, in recent years, widely praised realist and genre films. Kannada, Bengali, Marathi, Punjabi, and other cinemas had their own traditions, including Bengali art cinema associated with Satyajit Ray and Ritwik Ghatak and the Marathi stage and film tradition.

The ability of regional films to circulate beyond their language communities grew sharply after 2010. Dubbing and subtitling, which had once been peripheral, became central strategies. Baahubali: The Beginning (2015), a Telugu-Tamil production directed by S. S. Rajamouli, and its sequel in 2017, achieved very large national audiences in dubbed versions and demonstrated that a South Indian production could be marketed as a pan-Indian event. Sairat (2016), a Marathi film directed by Nagraj Manjule about a love affair across caste lines, became a major commercial success and generated discussion of caste in popular culture, and was later remade in Hindi. RRR (2022), also directed by Rajamouli, reached international audiences, and its song “Naatu Naatu” won the Academy Award for Best Original Song in 2023. Kannada, Malayalam, and Tamil films such as KGF: Chapter 2, Kantara, and Manjummel Boys also gained wide audiences, and the Malayalam-Hindi film All We Imagine as Light, directed by Payal Kapadia, won the Grand Prix at Cannes in 2024.

Regional industries also faced their own internal reckonings. In Kerala, the Women in Cinema Collective, formed in 2017, pressed for investigation of harassment in the film industry, and the report of the Hema Committee, released in August 2024, documented conditions affecting women in Malayalam cinema and led to legal and public debate. Tamil and Telugu cinema grappled with star-centered economics and the political uses of film celebrity. Filmmakers such as Pa. Ranjith and Mari Selvaraj in Tamil cinema, and Nagraj Manjule in Marathi, brought Dalit perspectives to commercial cinema, a development related to the social history in Chapter 107.

The economics and politics of these cinemas varied. Hyderabad’s large studio complexes, including Ramoji Film City, established in 1996, supported a high-budget production culture, while Chennai’s Kodambakkam studios and Kerala’s smaller production houses reflected different industrial scales. Star-centered fan associations in the South served as organizations with social and political functions, and the entry of actors into politics continued: the Tamil actor Vijay founded Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam in 2024 and was sworn in as chief minister of Tamil Nadu on 10 May 2026. His rise demonstrates that film celebrity can be converted into electoral organization and political authority, although celebrity alone cannot explain voter choices, party strategy, or legislative support. Regional cinemas also entered a language politics of their own. The label “pan-Indian,” widely used in the 2010s and 2020s for films released simultaneously in several languages, was criticized by some filmmakers who saw it as a way of presenting regional films through a Hindi-centered lens. A public exchange in April 2022 between a Kannada actor and a Hindi actor over whether Hindi was India’s “national language” showed how film publicity could ignite long-standing disputes about linguistic hierarchy, in a country whose constitution designates no single national language. Such episodes demonstrate that the rise of regional cinema was not only an industrial development but a contest over cultural recognition.

Historical Vocabulary
Film Distribution, Dubbing, Multiplex, and Regional Cinema

Film distribution is the process by which finished films reach audiences, through territorial rights sold to distributors, theatrical exhibition, television and satellite rights, and, later, digital licensing. In South Asia, language and territory long divided distribution into regional markets. Dubbing replaces a film’s original spoken dialogue with a new soundtrack in another language, enabling a film to reach audiences who do not speak the original language, while subtitling adds written translation. A multiplex is a theater complex with several screens, which allowed exhibitors to program different films and charge higher prices; the first in India opened in 1997. Regional cinema is the common Indian term for film industries working in languages other than Hindi, though the term can imply a hierarchy with Hindi at the center, and many scholars prefer to speak of language-based cinemas.

These terms matter because the reshaping of cultural influence described in this section depended on infrastructures of distribution and translation, not merely on the quality or popularity of films.

Industry reporting in 2026 documents how important regional-language production had become to India’s media economy. The FICCI–EY report published in March 2026 states that regional cinema accounted for more than 65 percent of films produced in India, and that regional languages accounted for 56 percent of the hours of locally produced content for digital platforms in 2025, excluding imported content (see §108.17 for a critical reading of the report). Such figures show scale and language share, but they do not by themselves measure cultural prestige or revenue, which depend on pricing, theatrical reach, and ancillary rights. Even so, the evidence supports the conclusion that linguistic industries had challenged established cultural hierarchies, and that Hindi cinema’s older claim to represent Indian popular culture as a whole was weaker than it had been.

Section 108.7

Pakistani and Bangladeshi Cinema and Audiovisual Culture

Pakistan’s commercial cinema, often called Lollywood after its center in Lahore, had a prosperous period in the mid-twentieth century in Urdu and Punjabi, but it declined from the 1980s as a result of censorship and Islamization under Zia-ul-Haq (Chapter 95), the spread of video and television, repetitive formulas in violent Punjabi films, the closure of cinemas, and a restricted market for imported films. By the early 2000s, production was very limited. A revival began in the later 2000s with films such as Khuda Kay Liye (2007), directed by Shoaib Mansoor, which was released in India in 2008, and Bol (2011), which treated family, gender, and religion. New multiplex cinemas in Karachi and elsewhere supported a small but growing urban market. The Legend of Maula Jatt (2022), a reworking of a famous Punjabi film of 1979, became the highest-grossing Pakistani film. Joyland (2022), directed by Saim Sadiq, which won a jury prize at Cannes and became Pakistan’s submission for the Academy Awards, was briefly blocked from domestic release by the federal government before the ban was reversed, illustrating the continuing role of censorship boards and political pressure.

Pakistani screen culture was long driven by television as much as cinema. Television drama, produced by private networks and presenting family, marriage, class, and gender issues, became a defining Pakistani cultural form, and dramas such as Humsafar (2011) and Zindagi Gulzar Hai (2012) gained audiences abroad, including in India through a Hindi-language channel that broadcast Pakistani serials for some years before political tensions ended the experiment. Music, discussed in §108.12, and film-linked soundtracks were closely connected to these industries. Political tension between India and Pakistan repeatedly interrupted cultural exchange: after the crisis of 2019, Pakistan suspended the release of Indian films, and in India, after the Pahalgam attack of April 2025, the government blocked a number of Pakistani YouTube channels and social-media accounts, a measure whose broader context belongs to Chapter 105.

In Bangladesh, the film industry, often called Dhallywood, had a long history that included major figures of Bengali cinema, but it faced decline in the 1990s and 2000s from piracy, poor theaters, and a perceived shift toward formulaic low-budget production. Independent and art cinema flourished alongside it. Tareque Masud’s Matir Moina (The Clay Bird, 2002), set before the Liberation War of 1971, was among the most internationally acclaimed Bangladeshi films, and Masud’s death in a road accident in 2011 was widely mourned. Mostofa Sarwar Farooki and other filmmakers developed independent features, and film festivals, including international festivals in Dhaka, promoted new work. Bangladeshi television dramas and the growth of Bengali-language streaming platforms, such as Chorki and Bongo and the India-based Hoichoi, which also served Bengal and diasporic audiences, linked cinema, television, and digital distribution.

In both countries, censorship, financing, state institutions, language politics, and audience preferences shaped screen industries. State censorship boards required approval of films, and cuts or bans could follow political, religious, or moral objections. Financing depended on small domestic markets, limited bank credit, and sometimes state or institutional support, with diasporic audiences and overseas festivals providing additional revenue and prestige. Language was both a unifier and a limit: Urdu-language films addressed a national audience in Pakistan while Punjabi and Pashto films addressed regional ones, and in Bangladesh the Bengali language was a founding symbol, which gave cinema a special role in the retelling of 1971. Detailed broadcast journalism and television politics are reserved for Division III.

Pakistani television drama merits closer attention because it was the country’s most influential popular medium. Private channels such as Hum and ARY produced serials on marriage, inheritance, class, and women’s agency, often written by women and aimed at female audiences, and the genre’s popularity gave it considerable influence in public conversation. It was also criticized, both for conventional portrayals of women and for treating sensitive social problems superficially, and regulators periodically warned or sanctioned channels over content deemed immoral. In film, censorship became more complex after constitutional changes in 2010 shifted some cultural responsibilities to the provinces, creating overlapping arrangements for certification. In Bangladesh, the growth of satellite channels created a large demand for dramas and serials, and many actors and directors moved between television and film. The decline of single-screen theaters in both countries, the arrival of multiplexes in a few large cities, and the shift of audiences to television and later to streaming meant that cinema’s role in national culture changed more than its artistic ambition.

Section 108.8

Sri Lankan, Nepalese, Bhutanese, and Maldivian Screen Cultures

The smaller industries of South Asia are often omitted from surveys, but they offer clear examples of how limited budgets and small audiences can coexist with significant cultural work. Sri Lankan cinema has a distinguished history that includes Lester James Peries, whose Rekava (1956) is widely regarded as a founding work of Sinhala art cinema. After 1991, the industry faced competition from television and imports, constrained theaters, and the effects of civil war. Filmmakers such as Prasanna Vithanage, whose Purahanda Kaluwara (Death on a Full Moon Day, 1997) treated the war’s effects on a rural family and was withheld from public screening for a period by authorities, and Asoka Handagama, used cinema to examine war, gender, and politics. Tamil-language cinema in Sri Lanka, closely linked to the experience of the Tamil communities and the war, developed through a smaller set of producers and diasporic networks, and filmmakers in the diaspora made films about the conflict.

Nepali cinema, centered in Kathmandu, developed both a commercial industry, sometimes called Kollywood, and independent and festival-oriented filmmaking. Commercial films drew on song-and-dance conventions influenced by Hindi cinema, but Nepali-language comedies and dramas had their own audiences in Nepal and among Nepali communities in India, the Gulf, and the West, and films such as the Chhakka Panja comedy series achieved large domestic audiences. Smaller productions explored the Maoist conflict, migration, and political change, as in Deepak Rauniyar’s work. Investment was limited, multiplexes were few, and distribution beyond Kathmandu was uneven, while dependence on Indian and international markets and festivals was significant.

Bhutan’s filmmaking developed late and in unusual conditions. The pioneering Bhutanese feature Gasa Lamai Singye dates to 1989, a decade before television and the internet were introduced in 1999. Commercial production in Dzongkha grew around the turn of the century, alongside local productions such as Rewaa (1999) and the international work of Khyentse Norbu, a Bhutanese lama and director, whose The Cup (1999) and Travellers and Magicians (2003) reached international audiences. Lunana: A Yak in the Classroom (2019), directed by Pawo Choyning Dorji, was nominated for the Academy Award for Best International Feature in 2022 and drew attention to a small national cinema. Bhutan’s cultural policy of managed modernization and its tiny population limited commercial scale but gave film a role in representing national identity and preserving cultural forms.

Maldivian audiovisual production grew from a small base. Television Maldives began in 1978 as a state broadcaster, and Dhivehi-language films and television dramas were produced by small companies and shown on local channels, in cinemas, and increasingly online. Training, investment, and an audience of fewer than a million people limited the industry, and religious and cultural norms shaped what could be depicted. The political liberalization after 2008 allowed more private broadcasting and film production, although politics remained polarized, as examined in Chapter 104. Scholarship on Maldivian media remains limited, and this section should be read with that limitation in mind.

These cases show how smaller film industries can become vehicles for language, social criticism, historical memory, and national representation. Their constraints, including small domestic markets, limited training institutions, restricted distribution, and dependence on festivals and streaming platforms, are real; but so are their achievements, which cannot be measured by box-office comparison with larger industries. A small film in Dzongkha or Sinhala can preserve a language on screen, document a conflict that official narratives avoided, and give a small country a place in international cultural exchange.

State institutions also shaped these small industries. Sri Lanka’s National Film Corporation, established in 1972, handled production support and distribution of foreign films, and its role in the control of imports shaped the market. Nepal’s film development board and Bhutan’s communications authority, created under a 2006 information, communications, and media law, regulated licensing and content. In the Maldives, the state broadcaster and later regulatory bodies controlled much of the airwaves. In all four countries, the shortage of trained technicians, the cost of equipment, the dependence on a few producers, and the difficulty of financing beyond a modest scale made international coproduction, festival funding, and diaspora investment attractive. Streaming platforms and video-sharing sites increasingly offered a way for such industries to reach audiences abroad, an issue discussed in §108.17.

Television, Broadcasting, Music, and the Politics of Mass Culture

Section 108.9

Satellite Television, Private Broadcasting, and New Mass Audiences

The transformation of Indian television began, as is often noted, with events outside the country’s control. During the Gulf War of 1991, households with access to cable and satellite dishes could watch international coverage on CNN, and the launch of Star TV from Hong Kong in 1991 introduced foreign satellite programming to Indian viewers. Domestic entrepreneurs moved quickly: Zee TV began broadcasting Hindi entertainment in 1992, and Sun TV began in Tamil in 1993. The state broadcaster Doordarshan, which in the 1980s had built a national audience with serials such as Hum Log, Ramayan, and Mahabharat, now competed with private channels whose programs were financed by advertising. A 1995 Supreme Court decision that airwaves are public property implied that broadcasting must be regulated by a public authority rather than left to the state or to unregulated private operators, and the same year Parliament passed the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act.

Most viewers did not receive satellite channels directly. Local cable operators, often small businesses, purchased satellite signals and distributed them to neighborhoods by wire, and this intermediary structure shaped the market: cable operators controlled access, negotiated payments, and were later the focus of regulatory efforts to digitize distribution and improve transparency. Satellite television changed household life. Programming in Hindi and regional languages expanded to include family serials, film-based programs, quiz shows such as Kaun Banega Crorepati (2000), reality and talent competitions, and news. Regional channels in Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Bengali, Marathi, and other languages multiplied, and regional groups such as Sun and Eenadu’s ETV became major players. Advertisers, seeking to reach increasingly prosperous consumers, shaped programming through ratings and sponsorship.

Across the region, broadcasting expanded on different schedules. In Pakistan, Pakistan Television had been a state monopoly, and satellite and cable television spread in the 1990s. The legal framework changed in 2002 when the military government of Pervez Musharraf (Chapter 101) established the Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority (PEMRA) and licensed private broadcasters, among them Geo, which began transmitting from Dubai in 2002 and which, with ARY, Hum, and other networks, built large audiences. In Bangladesh, ATN Bangla began in 1997 as the first private satellite television channel, and Channel i and Ekushey Television followed, with licensing disputes and political pressure over ownership. In Sri Lanka, private channels such as Sirasa and Derana competed with the state-owned Rupavahini. Nepal saw the growth of private television and, more importantly, community radio; Radio Sagarmatha, which began broadcasting in 1997, is often cited as the first independent community FM station in South Asia. Bhutan introduced television only in 1999, as a deliberate policy decision, while in Maldives broadcasting moved from state control toward private channels after 2008.

Distribution technology continued to change. Direct-to-home satellite services, which began to compete with cable in the early 2000s, offered households a direct subscription, and the state broadcaster launched a free-to-air satellite service in 2004 to reach homes without cable. The government’s mandatory digitization of cable, begun around 2012, and the telecom regulator’s later tariff orders, which required channels to be sold individually or in bundles with specified pricing, aimed to improve transparency in subscriber numbers and charges, though their effects on household bills and channel choice were disputed. These developments show that the “new mass audience” was created not only by programming but also by the infrastructure and regulation of distribution.

They tell historians what lawmakers said they intended and what powers they created. Cause and Effect: Liberalization, Media Ownership, Changing Audiences, and Cultural Production

Long-term causes

Rising literacy and incomes, the spread of television sets, a large linguistic diversity, and the state-monopoly broadcasting legacy that created pent-up demand for alternatives.

Short-term causes

Economic liberalization after 1991, satellite technology, court rulings on airwaves, and entrepreneurs who saw a market in advertising.

Transformation and immediate effects

Private channels in many languages, cable distribution, advertising-based programming, and the entry of corporate and political owners into media.

Long-term consequences

Larger regional-language audiences, commercially driven news and entertainment, new questions of ownership concentration, and later the preconditions for digital platforms.

Historical complexity: Liberalization did not by itself determine media outcomes. State regulation, political alliances, ownership choices, and audience preferences all shaped what private broadcasting became, and the results differed across countries that experienced similar technological change.

Section 108.10

Television News, Political Communication, and Mass Politics

Dedicated television news developed quickly after private broadcasting began. In India, Hindi and English news programs and then twenty-four-hour channels emerged around the turn of the millennium, among them Aaj Tak and NDTV 24x7 and later numerous regional-language news channels, supplementing and then rivaling newspapers as a primary source of political information. The Kargil conflict of 1999 was among the first conflicts widely covered live in India, the attacks on Mumbai in November 2008 were broadcast continuously and later criticized for operational and ethical problems in coverage, and election campaigns increasingly took place on television through debates, interviews, and advertisement. Similar developments occurred in Pakistan, where private news channels created a competitive political talk-show culture after 2002, and in Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.

Television shaped political visibility. Leaders were seen and heard by audiences who had never attended a rally, and politicians adapted to the medium by cultivating personalities, slogans, and staged events. Anchors became public figures in their own right, and prime-time debates became arenas in which spokespeople of parties argued before national audiences. Broadcast coverage of disasters, scandals, and protests created a sense of shared national attention and could force official response, as in coverage of the anti-corruption movement of 2011 and the protests after the Delhi gang rape of 2012. In Pakistan, the lawyers’ movement of 2007, during which the government banned private news channels after declaring emergency rule in November 2007, showed both the political importance of broadcasters and the willingness of authorities to silence them. Bangladeshi and Sri Lankan broadcasters were likewise subject to licensing, ownership, and political pressure.

The relationship between reporting, commentary, advocacy, and commercial sensationalism deserves analytic attention. Competition for ratings favored speed, drama, and confrontation, and ratings systems, including the Broadcast Audience Research Council in India, became subjects of controversy, including allegations in 2020 of ratings manipulation. Some channels identified openly with parties or ideologies, while others claimed neutrality but displayed bias in framing and guest selection. Regulatory and self-regulatory bodies could sanction or criticize particular programs, but their authority and independence were frequently questioned. Viewers could and did turn to other channels, to newspapers, and later to online sources, and audience surveys show that trust in television news was uneven.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Commercial Broadcasting Democratize Public Discussion or Concentrate Cultural Power?

The question: Did the end of state broadcasting monopolies and the rise of private, advertising-financed television widen public discussion and accountability, or did it concentrate cultural and political power in commercial and political owners?

Interpretation A — Democratization: Private broadcasting ended official control over what could be seen, introduced competing viewpoints, expanded programming in regional languages, brought politicians under greater public scrutiny, and gave audiences a wider choice. Studies of cable television, such as the economists Robert Jensen and Emily Oster’s 2009 analysis of Indian villages, found associations between cable access and changes in attitudes toward women’s status, which some read as evidence that television exposure can affect social norms.
Interpretation B — Concentration: Advertising-driven competition favored sensationalism and entertainment over investigative reporting, ownership was often concentrated in corporate and political hands, paid news and political alignment compromised independence, and regional channels were frequently owned by political families. On this view, formal pluralism of channels concealed a narrower range of voices and a dependence on advertisers and governments.

Evidence: Evidence for A includes the multiplication of channels, regional-language growth, and specific studies of attitude change; evidence for B includes media-ownership studies, the 2010 Press Council examination of paid news in the press, and allegations about ratings practices. The studies are not directly comparable, measure different outcomes, and often describe association rather than demonstrated cause.

Why it matters: The question bears on whether commercial liberalization should be seen as widening or narrowing the public sphere. Many historians conclude that both occurred, with outcomes differing by country, language, and period, and that viewing audiences as passive recipients of broadcast influence overstates what the evidence shows.

Coverage of crises exposed the strengths and weaknesses of the new broadcasting. The live coverage of the 2008 Mumbai attacks, in which channels broadcast the movements of security forces, was criticized by judges and by journalists themselves, and in a 2012 judgment the Supreme Court of India expressed concern about its operational consequences, leading to debate over self-regulatory codes for crisis reporting. Opinion polls, exit polls, and election-night broadcasts became part of political culture, and election commissions in India and elsewhere sought to regulate their timing and content. The format of the prime-time panel discussion, with multiple participants arguing before an anchor, was widely criticized as favoring volume over information, while defenders argued that it exposed viewers to unscripted political contest. Such arguments rest on interpretation as much as on evidence, which is why this chapter distinguishes between a medium’s visibility and its demonstrated effects.

Section 108.11

Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and Smaller-State Broadcasting Institutions

National media systems are shaped by institutions, and this section concentrates on those institutions rather than on political narratives told in Chapters 101–104. In Pakistan, state-run Pakistan Television and Radio Pakistan coexisted after 2002 with private networks regulated by PEMRA. The regulator’s powers to license, suspend, and fine channels were used at various times against broadcasters, and channels such as Geo experienced suspension and pressure, including in 2014 after disputes with security institutions. Private networks also built a highly profitable drama industry, a competitive news environment, and sports and entertainment coverage. Ownership by large business groups and pressure from governments and military institutions were persistent features, and journalists described self-censorship on sensitive subjects, particularly those involving the armed forces.

In Bangladesh, state-owned Bangladesh Television and Bangladesh Betar and the state news agency BSS stood alongside a large number of private television channels licensed after the late 1990s. The 2026 RSF assessment, as reported in the Bangladeshi press, states that the state-run broadcasters lacked editorial independence and functioned as government mouthpieces. Private broadcasters were often owned by business groups with political ties, and licensing and ownership questions mattered to editorial independence. After the political transition of 2024, debates about reform of the media and broadcasting institutions took place, but the outcome belongs to the political narrative in Chapter 102 and remained unfinished within the chapter’s research period.

Sri Lankan broadcasting included state institutions, such as Rupavahini, alongside private channels, and political ownership and pressure shaped coverage during the civil war and after. The 2009 murder of Lasantha Wickrematunge and other attacks on journalists affected media freedom, and later governments pledged reform while concerns persisted, as discussed in §108.16. In Nepal, the end of the monarchy’s direct rule and the constitution of 2015 supported a relatively open media environment, in which community radio played a distinctive role in reaching rural and linguistic-minority populations; the sector also experienced economic fragility, political pressure, and concerns about regulatory proposals. In Bhutan, the Bhutan Broadcasting Service and the government-supported newspaper Kuensel were joined after 2006 by private papers, and television and the internet arrived with explicit policy attention to cultural protection. In Maldives, state broadcasting predominated until the constitutional transition of 2008, after which private broadcasters and online outlets expanded; the criminal-defamation law adopted in 2016 was repealed in 2018, illustrating the volatility of the legal framework. The Media and Broadcasting Regulation Act (No. 16/2025), ratified on 18 September 2025, then dissolved the Maldives Media Council and reconstituted the broadcasting regulator as the Maldives Media and Broadcasting Commission, bringing print and broadcast regulation together.

Taken together, these systems show the range of arrangements by which South Asian states have sought to combine public service, commercial competition, and political control. Public-service obligations, such as providing education and programming in minority languages, were often weakly enforced, and commercial competition did not necessarily protect independence where regulation was politicized. Language programming varied widely: Urdu and English dominated national channels in Pakistan, regional-language channels grew more slowly, and Nepal’s community radio broadcast in dozens of languages.

Radio, though less visible than television, was in some places the more consequential medium. India’s private FM radio expanded from the early 2000s under restrictions that prohibited private stations from broadcasting their own news, while a policy permitting community radio, adopted in 2002 and broadened in 2006, enabled small local stations. Pakistan’s FM radio boom followed the 2002 liberalization, and Bangladesh adopted a community-radio policy in 2008. In Nepal, community and independent FM stations, numbering in the hundreds, broadcast news and local information in many languages and played an important role during the political upheavals of the 2000s, when authorities attempted to restrict their news content. Radio’s low cost, oral character, and suitability for audiences with limited literacy made it a medium of the rural and marginalized, and its history shows that media systems should not be measured only by the prominence of television and the internet.

Section 108.12

Music, Performance, and Popular Cultural Identity

Music was the most widely shared popular art in South Asia and the one that most easily crossed borders. Film music dominated popular listening in India and was widely heard in Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and the diaspora, and the cassette-based industry of the 1980s, led by T-Series, was followed by satellite music television and then streaming. The playback singer Lata Mangeshkar, whose career began in the 1940s and who died in 2022, was among the most recorded voices in the world and a symbol of the Hindi film song’s national reach. A. R. Rahman, who entered film music with Roja in 1992 in Tamil and whose work was then dubbed into Hindi, brought electronic production and new sounds into South Asian film music and became internationally known after his work for Slumdog Millionaire (2008). Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, a Pakistani qawwali singer who died in 1997, brought Sufi devotional music to world audiences through recordings, collaborations, and tours, and his career illustrates the role of diasporic audiences and international labels in extending the reach of a devotional tradition.

Other traditions flourished alongside film music. Ghazal, associated with Urdu poetry and performers such as Mehdi Hassan, Jagjit Singh, and Ghulam Ali, was popular in India and Pakistan. Qawwali continued at shrines and in concerts. The Baul tradition of Bengal, recognized by UNESCO on its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2008, and other folk and devotional forms continued across the region, often under economic and social pressure. Indian popular music in the 1990s included independent pop albums and music television channels such as MTV India and Channel [V], and Punjabi pop and bhangra developed into a large industry linked to the diaspora in Britain and Canada, which in turn influenced Hindi film music. Hip-hop developed in Indian and other South Asian cities, with the film Gully Boy (2019) popularizing Mumbai street rap, and independent musicians used online platforms to bypass film-music gatekeepers.

Pakistan’s Coke Studio, begun in 2008, blended folk, classical, and pop traditions and demonstrated how a corporate-sponsored television and online series could reshape popular taste; a Bangladeshi edition and an Indian edition appeared later. The song “Pasoori” (2022), performed by Ali Sethi and Shae Gill, became widely popular across the region and online despite the political separation between India and Pakistan. The Punjabi singer Sidhu Moose Wala, whose murder in 2022 caused widespread mourning, illustrated both the regional and diasporic reach of Punjabi popular music and its entanglement with violence and politics. Smaller countries had their own musical traditions, including Nepali popular and folk music, Bhutanese rigsar pop, Sri Lankan baila, and Maldivian music, which circulated on recordings and increasingly online.

The economics of music changed fundamentally. Cassettes and compact discs gave way to digital downloads, streaming, and short video platforms, with services such as Spotify entering India in 2019 and domestic services such as Gaana and JioSaavn competing for users. The 2026 FICCI–EY report records about 178 million music-streaming users in India, and notes that discovery was shifting toward short-video platforms. Labels and film studios retained large catalogues, while independent artists sought revenue from performance, advertising, and brand partnerships. Concerts became a major source of income. Throughout, musical genres should be treated as evolving traditions rather than fixed expressions of national or religious identity: qawwali was Sufi devotional music but was performed to varied audiences; ghazal was Urdu but loved across languages; and film music drew on classical, folk, Western, and electronic sources.

Music was also exposed to violence and restriction. In Pakistan, the attack on the Data Darbar shrine in Lahore in July 2010 and the assassination of the qawwali singer Amjad Sabri in Karachi in June 2016 showed that devotional music could be targeted by militant groups hostile to shrine traditions. Under Zia-ul-Haq, state broadcasting had imposed moral restrictions on women’s performance and on some musical forms, and their legacy lingered. In India and elsewhere, song lyrics, film songs, and concert performances periodically drew protests and police complaints. Women performers and composers faced both barriers to entry and, when successful, intense scrutiny. These conflicts show that music, though often celebrated as a unifier, was an object of political and religious regulation like other forms of cultural expression.

Social Media, Digital Journalism, and the Contested Online Public Sphere

Section 108.13

The Internet, Social Media, and the Democratization of Publishing

Internet access in South Asia began in the mid-1990s in a few cities and universities, expanded through cybercafes and desktop computers in the 2000s, and became a mass phenomenon only with cheap smartphones and mobile data in the late 2010s, a development whose technical and economic history belongs to Chapter 106. Early online newspapers, blogs, and discussion forums gave educated urban users new ways to publish. Orkut, a social network that was briefly very popular in India, was followed by Facebook, YouTube, Twitter (later X), WhatsApp, Instagram, and short-video platforms. WhatsApp became the dominant messaging service in India and widely used elsewhere in the region, and Facebook and YouTube were central in Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Pakistan.

The change was not simply that more information circulated. It was that publication ceased to depend on owning a press, a channel, or a studio. A student could film an event, a village could circulate a complaint, a small publisher could start a channel, and a political party could communicate directly with supporters. Independent news creators, comedians, religious preachers, and local entertainers reached audiences that rivaled those of established institutions, and the economics of the new platforms, in which creators earned from advertising or subscriptions, produced a vast array of online entrepreneurs. Established media responded by building websites, apps, and social-media operations of their own.

Participation remained deeply uneven. Language shaped the online experience, since early internet content was dominated by English and the growth of Indian-language and other regional-language content, supported by voice input and translation, narrowed but did not remove the gap. Gender differences in phone ownership and use meant that women were less likely than men to have independent access in many regions; income determined who could afford devices and data; and rural users faced weaker connectivity. Literacy limited text-based participation, which helped explain the importance of video and audio. These inequalities mean that the “democratization of publishing” should be understood as an expansion rather than a universal inclusion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Family Media Consumption: From the Newspaper and the Shared Television to the Smartphone and the Streaming Account

In the 1980s, a typical urban or small-town household might have read one newspaper, listened to the radio, and gathered around a single television set for a weekly serial or film, with neighbors sometimes joining. Viewing was shared and scheduled, determined by the state broadcaster, and the household’s authority structure, in which an elder often controlled the set, shaped what was watched. In the cable era, a household might subscribe to a bundle of channels through a neighborhood operator, and evenings were organized around family serials, news, and cricket, still largely as a shared experience.

By the 2020s, the pattern could be very different. Individual family members might each carry a phone, watching videos, listening to music, messaging relatives in other cities or in the Gulf, and following creators in their own language, often alone and at times of their own choosing. A shared television in the living room might now be a connected device displaying streaming applications, and a grandparent might receive political messages and forwarded videos from the family group chat. The shift altered authority, privacy, and age relationships, and gave young people, and especially young women with access to a phone, new spaces for information and expression, while also exposing households to commercial, political, and fraudulent messages. Evidence for such changes comes from audience surveys, ethnographic studies, and memoirs, and it is uneven: poor, rural, and elderly households experienced the transition very differently from affluent urban ones.

Digital news organizations were an important new category. Independent online outlets in India, such as The Wire, Scroll, The Print, The Quint, and Newslaundry, depended on donations, subscriptions, and advertising and sometimes faced legal and regulatory pressure. In Bangladesh, bdnews24, founded in 2006, was an early online news provider; in Nepal, outlets such as Online Khabar and Setopati reached readers on mobile devices; in Sri Lanka and Maldives, small online outlets offered alternatives to mainstream and state media. Their independence was real but fragile, since the economics of digital advertising favored the largest platforms rather than the publishers who produced the reporting, and many news organizations found that they were dependent on platform algorithms for audience. Language-specific video channels on YouTube, particularly in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, and Urdu, built large audiences and combined news, commentary, and entertainment, blurring professional and amateur boundaries.

The change in publishing was institutional as well as technological. In India, independent organizations such as Scroll.in, founded in 2014, and The Wire, founded in 2015, established digital-first newsrooms whose distribution did not require the ownership of a printing press or a television frequency. But reporting itself still cost money: salaries, travel, legal defense, editing, and the verification of documents could not be eliminated simply by putting a story online. Some publishers relied on advertising, others on reader subscriptions, donations, grants, or combinations of those resources. A publication could therefore gain independence from one set of gatekeepers while becoming dependent on another. The distinction between a newsroom and an individual account also remained important, since professional editorial practices could provide safeguards that were not inherent in the platform.

Language networks changed the scale of online participation. A reader no longer needed a bookseller or newsstand to find a publication from another district or country, and regional writers could use mobile keyboards, video, audio, or transliteration to reach readers and listeners. The difficulty of entering some scripts on earlier computers diminished as mobile operating systems and messaging applications made multilingual communication easier. Yet searchable archives, editorial verification, and translation remained uneven. An oral report circulating in a private group might reach a locality more efficiently than a published article but be exceptionally difficult for a historian to retrieve later. The transition to digital publication thus enlarged the range of usable historical sources while making their preservation and provenance more uncertain.

Section 108.14

Digital Activism, Citizen Journalism, and Political Mobilization

Social media became a tool of mobilization well before the events of 2024 and 2025. India’s anti-corruption campaign of 2011 and the protests that followed the Delhi gang rape of 2012 used television together with early social platforms, and later movements, including the protests against the citizenship law of 2019–20 and the farmers’ protests of 2020–21, used messaging and video to organize and communicate. Political parties, notably in India and Pakistan, built large digital operations of their own, including organized teams that produced and circulated messages. In Pakistan, the political party Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf and its jailed leader Imran Khan used online media extensively after 2022, including, in 2023, an artificially generated voice for a virtual address. In Sri Lanka, the protests of 2022 that led to the fall of the Rajapaksa government (Chapter 103) used social media for organizing and documentation.

Bangladesh’s uprising of July and August 2024 (Chapter 102) offers a clear comparative case of how information channels worked in mobilization. Student protests over civil-service quotas began in the summer, and authorities responded with force and then, from mid-July, with a communications shutdown that restricted internet access. Videos and images of violence against protesters, shared by those who could access networks and by Bangladeshis abroad, circulated widely and appear to have intensified public anger, while the shutdown itself impeded both organizing and the documentation of events. Mainstream media, under pressure, covered events unevenly. After the fall of the government of Sheikh Hasina on 5 August, social media was also the arena for rumor and for attacks on perceived opponents. The role of digital media should be assessed alongside the movement’s roots in grievances over jobs, repression, and political exclusion, which a media history cannot supply.

Nepal’s youth mobilization of September 2025 (Chapter 104) shows a related dynamic. On 4 September, the government blocked 26 social-media platforms, including Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, X, and YouTube, after the companies failed to meet registration requirements set by the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology. Protests by young people, many describing themselves as “Gen Z,” took place on 8 September, when police used lethal force and at least 19 people were killed, according to reports by human rights organizations and news agencies. The government lifted the ban on 9 September, and the prime minister, K. P. Sharma Oli, resigned. Researchers and journalists noted that the protests expressed broader anger at corruption and nepotism, with the ban as the immediate spark, and that protesters used virtual private networks and online campaigns to organize. A cautious conclusion is that the platform ban was a trigger but not the whole cause, a distinction that the media record, which captures the visible moment better than the underlying grievances, can obscure.

Citizen journalism offers opportunities and limits. Ordinary people with phones documented police violence, floods, lynchings, and elections, and their recordings sometimes became decisive evidence. At the same time, such material could be incomplete, miscaptioned, or deceptive; verification required time and skill; participants and bystanders faced risks of arrest, retaliation, or harassment; and platforms’ moderation decisions determined what remained visible. Fact-checking organizations, such as Alt News and BOOM in India and similar groups elsewhere, developed to examine viral claims, and their founders in some cases faced legal pressure. The chapter concentrates on media practices rather than the political histories of these movements, which are examined in the country chapters.

Online mobilization could also give voice to groups historically excluded from public speech. The #MeToo movement in India in October 2018, in which women publicly named harassers in media, film, and politics, led to the resignation of a Union minister and a series of defamation suits, and showed how social media could bypass institutions that had failed to address complaints, while also exposing complainants to legal risk. In Pakistan, the annual Aurat March, which began in 2018, used social media to publicize demands about women’s rights and to answer hostile responses. Kashmir illustrates the opposite dynamic: communications restrictions imposed in August 2019, including a prolonged suspension of mobile and internet services, limited residents’ ability to publicize events and journalists’ ability to report them, and India has recorded some of the world’s most frequent regional internet shutdowns, by the accounts of digital-rights organizations. The same technologies that enabled mobilization were thus also a target of state control, a pattern consistent with the comparative cases above.

Section 108.15

Misinformation, Algorithms, Online Harassment, and Information Integrity

The rumor-driven attacks of 2018 in India illustrate the difference between transmission and causation. Fabricated or miscaptioned messages alleging child abduction circulated through messaging services, and attacks on strangers occurred in several states. Authorities and reporters traced some of the messages to video material whose original purpose or location differed from the story attached to it. But the fact that a rumor circulated does not explain why a particular crowd responded violently: local fears, existing mistrust, the availability of police protection, and the actions of people on the ground all require investigation. A historically responsible assessment separates the original publication, its transmission, the claims made about it, and the subsequent physical event. Platforms introduced restrictions on forwarding and other responses, whose existence is evidence of institutional reaction rather than proof that the measures solved the problem.

During the 2024 Indian general election, AI-manipulated videos purporting to show film actors endorsing or criticizing political figures became examples of the increasing difficulty of attributing recorded speech. The potential injury was not confined to voters receiving an inaccurate impression: impersonation also deprived performers of control over the political uses of their likenesses. Verification therefore involved comparing the footage with an original recording, tracing the earliest accessible publication, seeking confirmation from the depicted person or a reliable representative, and distinguishing a synthetic fabrication from an authentic recording supplied with a misleading caption. Such procedures do not depend on knowing which political party stands to benefit; they are standards that can be applied to every claim.

These difficulties also affected historical research. A screenshot may preserve a claim without its original context, a widely shared post may subsequently be deleted, and a platform's published count of views may combine repeat encounters by the same person. Historians need to record dates, original links where available, source chains, and uncertainties, and to compare online material with independent records. Private messaging creates an additional evidentiary problem: a researcher rarely has a representative archive of the messages exchanged within thousands of separate groups. Conclusions about the prevalence of an allegation, its geographic spread, or its effect on elections must therefore be proportionate to the evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Algorithm, Misinformation, Disinformation, and Digital Rights

An algorithm, in this context, is a set of computational rules a platform uses to rank, recommend, or filter content, typically to maximize engagement. Misinformation is false or misleading information spread without necessarily intending to deceive, while disinformation is false information spread deliberately to mislead. The distinction depends on intent, which is often hard to establish. Digital rights refers to rights, such as freedom of expression, privacy, and access to information, as exercised online, and to the principle that they deserve legal protection.

These terms matter because the same viral post can be misinformation when a user shares it in good faith and disinformation when it is first fabricated for political purposes, and public debate often fails to distinguish the two.

Platform design shaped public communication in ways the original builders did not fully anticipate. Recommendation systems that promoted content likely to attract attention favored emotionally charged, sensational, and sometimes false material, and advertising incentives rewarded engagement. Political parties and interest groups coordinated messages, sometimes through paid networks or anonymous accounts. Fabricated images and manipulated videos, including AI-generated deepfakes, grew more common, and online harassment, including targeted abuse of women journalists, politicians, and activists, became a serious problem. Women who spoke publicly often received sexualized threats and doxxing, which discouraged participation.

Rumor-driven violence illustrated the dangers. In 2018, false warnings about child abductors circulated on WhatsApp in India and were linked to a number of mob killings, and the company responded by limiting message forwarding. Earlier, in 2012, rumors circulated by text message and online led to a panic among people from the northeast living in southern cities. In Sri Lanka, the government blocked social-media platforms during anti-Muslim violence in Kandy in 2018 and after the Easter bombings of 2019, citing the need to stop incitement, though critics argued that blocking restricted legitimate information. In India, communal incidents, including the Delhi violence of February 2020, were accompanied by competing online narratives. These cases show that misinformation could aggravate tensions, but they do not show that platforms were the sole cause of those conflicts, which had social and political roots.

Verification was hard for journalists, institutions, and ordinary users alike. Content in many languages, encrypted messaging, closed groups, and rapid circulation made monitoring difficult. Attribution of deliberate disinformation to particular actors was often disputed, and governments sometimes labeled critical or inconvenient reporting as “fake news.” Fact-checking organizations, news literacy programs, and platform labels offered partial remedies, but studies of their effectiveness were mixed.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Has Social Media Expanded Democratic Participation or Weakened Public Information?

The question: Did social media widen participation and accountability in South Asian politics, or did it weaken the reliability of public information and public trust?

Interpretation A — Expanded participation: Social media allowed marginalized groups, regional-language speakers, young people, and critics to speak without depending on gatekeepers, helped document abuses, and enabled mobilization, as in Bangladesh in 2024 and Nepal in 2025.
Interpretation B — Weakened information: Algorithmic amplification, organized messaging, deepfakes, and harassment degraded public information, intensified polarization, and drove some people, especially women, out of public discussion. Governments and parties also learned to use the platforms for propaganda.

Evidence: Evidence for A includes documented cases of mobilization and citizen reporting; evidence for B includes documented rumor-driven violence, studies of harassment, and cases of manipulated media. The two do not contradict one another, since the same platform can serve both purposes, and platform data needed to resolve questions about reach and causation are largely controlled by the companies.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether policy should emphasize protecting speech, regulating platforms, or strengthening verification and media literacy, and whether governments’ stated aims in regulating online speech are plausible.

Section 108.16

Media Regulation, Censorship, and Digital Rights through 2026

By the 2020s, South Asian governments had moved from asking whether to regulate online communication to asking how. Their stated rationales included national security, public order, protection from harmful content such as fraud, child exploitation, and incitement, protection of personal data, and combating misinformation, and many of these concerns were real. Journalists and civil-liberties organizations responded that the laws used vague terms, granted broad discretionary powers to executive bodies, and could be used against reporting and criticism. A careful analysis distinguishes three things: a government’s stated purpose, the legal powers the statute actually creates, and the evidence of how those powers are implemented.

In India, the Information Technology Act of 2000 and the Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code Rules of 2021 imposed due-diligence obligations on platforms and a code of ethics on digital news publishers and streaming services, with a grievance structure and government oversight. Critics argued that the rules extended executive control over speech, while the government cited accountability and user protection. A 2023 amendment providing for a government fact-checking unit was challenged, and the Bombay High Court struck down the relevant provision in September 2024. In February 2026 the Supreme Court restored the Union government’s appeal after its earlier procedural dismissal; restoration did not itself decide the constitutional merits. On 10 February 2026 the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology notified further amendments, in force from 20 February 2026, defining “synthetically generated information,” requiring relevant intermediaries to label defined synthetically generated audiovisual information, and shortening takedown timelines for flagged unlawful content. The stated rationale was to address deepfakes, fraud, and impersonation; debates concerned how quickly platforms could assess content, the risk of over-removal, and the wide executive role. The government also used blocking powers, including during the Kashmir communication restrictions after 2019 and against particular accounts and programs, and India recorded very frequent regional internet shutdowns. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023 added a privacy framework whose implementation was still developing.

In Pakistan, the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act of 2016 had been criticized for its use against journalists and critics. The Prevention of Electronic Crimes (Amendment) Act, 2025, enacted after the National Assembly and Senate passed it in a matter of days and the president assented on 29 January 2025, went further, as the primary source below shows. It drew sustained protest from journalists’ organizations, challenges in court, and criticism from international human-rights groups. The government’s information minister argued that the law was needed to regulate social media rather than the broadcast media already regulated by PEMRA. In Sri Lanka, the Online Safety Act, No. 9 of 2024, which came into force in early 2024, established an Online Safety Commission with powers over “prohibited statements” and online accounts. It was criticized by journalists, technology companies, and legal organizations for vague offenses and broad powers. Proposed amendments were gazetted in July 2024, including a proposal to remove the commission’s power to order disabling of access to online locations; publication of a bill did not itself enact those changes, and the government that took office later pledged review and consulted widely in 2025; reports in early 2026 indicated that the Act remained in force and that replacement or amending legislation was still under discussion. Nepal’s attempt in 2025 to enforce platform registration produced the reversal described in §108.14, while a separate proposed social media bill was withdrawn. The government decided to seek withdrawal on 3 February 2026, and the National Assembly approved withdrawal on 9 February; the proposal did not become law. Bangladesh’s Digital Security Act of 2018, widely criticized for criminal defamation-style provisions, was replaced in 2023 by the Cyber Security Act, and the interim authorities introduced a cyber protection ordinance in 2025. Parliament subsequently enacted the Cyber Protection Act, 2026 (Act No. 81, published on 10 April), replacing the ordinance and confirming the repeal of the 2023 framework. This sequence distinguishes revision of a law from abolition of all restrictions on online expression.

The Maldivian legislation provides another example of the importance of separating official purpose from implementation. The president's office described the September 2025 Media and Broadcasting Regulation Act as a measure to protect independent media, streamline registration, and address false information. RSF's 2026 country assessment, in contrast, questioned the independence of the reorganized commission and the proportionality of its sanctions. These accounts are not equivalent kinds of evidence: the first identifies the executive's rationale and the institutional changes, while the second assesses risks to journalists from the perspective of a press-freedom organization. To determine whether those risks materialized in specific cases, the historian would need the statute, commission decisions, records of sanctions and appeals, and testimony from affected journalists.

The Indian synthetic-media rules likewise illustrate a difference between the name of a technology and the legal definition of a regulated object. The February 2026 amendment defines its subject in terms of artificially generated or altered audiovisual information liable to be perceived as depicting an actual individual or event. Good-faith routine editing, certain educational or illustrative materials, and accessibility or translation work that does not materially distort underlying content are distinguished in the definition. Consequently, it would be inaccurate to say that every use of artificial intelligence in writing, translation, or editing automatically triggers the same labelling obligations. The relevant questions are what content was made, how it falls within the rule, which intermediary is responsible, and what obligations follow. Those distinctions matter for both protection against malicious impersonation and the freedom of legitimate cultural production.

Primary Source Window: Pakistan’s Prevention of Electronic Crimes (Amendment) Act, 2025

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Regulating the Online Public Sphere

Note: the perspectives below are analytical composites based on public positions taken by such groups, not quotations from named individuals.

A journalist: Reporters worried that vague offenses and broad executive powers could be used to punish critical reporting, that legal costs and the threat of arrest encouraged self-censorship, and that regulation aimed at online abuse could also catch legitimate investigation, while also recognizing that online harassment and falsehood harmed their own profession.
A filmmaker: Filmmakers and streaming producers pointed to certification, content codes, and takedown threats as constraints on storytelling, particularly on politically or religiously sensitive subjects, while some also supported protections against piracy and unauthorized AI imitation of performers.
A regional-language writer: Writers working in smaller languages depended on social media to reach readers without prestigious publishers, and feared that restrictions or takedowns would fall hardest on those with the fewest resources to challenge them, while also being targets of harassment when writing on contested subjects.
A digital creator: Independent creators earned incomes from platforms and were vulnerable to account blocking, content takedowns, and ambiguous rules, but some also benefited from clearer rules against impersonation, fraud, and deepfakes.
A media regulator or government official: Officials emphasized the real harms of fraud, child exploitation, incitement, and deepfakes, the need to hold foreign platforms accountable to national law, and the argument that unregulated online space was not a neutral or safe public sphere.

These groups did not hold equal power. Regulators could create and enforce rules; creators and writers frequently lacked the resources to challenge them. The purpose of the exercise is historical complexity, not equivalence.

The media-freedom environment documented through November 2026 was therefore one of expanded regulatory capacity and unresolved legal contest. The RSF index of 30 April 2026, discussed in §108.4, placed most South Asian countries in its lowest categories. Several legal and legislative questions, including any court ruling on challenges to Pakistan’s 2025 law and the fate of Sri Lanka’s reform of the Online Safety Act, were unresolved, and this chapter does not predict how they will be settled.

Streaming, Diaspora, Globalization, and South Asian Culture through 2026

Section 108.17

Streaming Platforms and the Transformation of Cultural Distribution

Subscription video streaming reached South Asia in the mid-2010s. Hotstar launched in India in 2015, followed in 2016 by Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and Jio’s own services, supported by the sharp fall in mobile data prices described in Chapter 106. Original series such as Sacred Games, Mirzapur, The Family Man, and Paatal Lok attracted large audiences, the pandemic of 2020–21 pushed film releases and viewing online, and platforms invested in regional-language series. Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Marathi, and other languages gained dedicated services or dubbed catalogues, while Pakistani and Bangladeshi viewers used a mix of international and local platforms and, in Pakistan, YouTube-based drama distribution. In 2024–25 India’s Reliance and Disney businesses merged into a single large media group, which launched a combined streaming service, an example of consolidation in the new landscape.

Streaming altered theatrical exhibition by creating a window after theatrical release and by sometimes bypassing theaters entirely. It altered television schedules, since viewers could watch on demand, and it altered advertising and audience measurement, since platforms held their own data and did not always share it. Platforms often purchased films before or after release, and the financing of films increasingly counted on digital rights, as satellite rights once had. Independent producers gained new outlets, and smaller and regional films, such as Malayalam features, could reach national and international audiences, as scholars of South Asian cinema have noted when describing streaming as a potentially significant change to the historically unequal film-distribution system. Concerns remained: concentration among a few large platforms, restrictive licensing terms, opaque algorithms that determined discoverability, subscription costs, platform decisions to commission or cancel content, and exposure to regulatory and political pressure, as in controversy over the series Tandav in 2021, which led to police complaints and was followed by regulation under the 2021 rules. Theaters retained importance, particularly for large-scale spectacles and shared events.

The difference between broadcasting and streaming was not simply that one used a television set and the other a phone. A broadcaster assembled a schedule, sold advertising against particular programs, and distributed the schedule through cable, terrestrial, or satellite infrastructure. A streaming service licensed or commissioned individual works and offered them through a catalogue. Subscription services derived revenue from periodic payments, advertising-supported services from commercial messages, and hybrid services from both. Exclusive rights could determine which service a household purchased, so the ownership of sports, films, or serials became a central part of competition. A household with inexpensive data but little disposable income might have access to a technically sophisticated device without access to several separately priced catalogues.

In India the combination of JioCinema and Disney+ Hotstar produced JioHotstar, announced on 14 February 2025 by the recently formed JioStar joint venture. This case shows the connections between broadcasting, sports rights, entertainment libraries, and mobile distribution. It also shows why audience statistics supplied by the companies need to be interpreted carefully: registered users, monthly viewers, paid subscribers, and people watching a live event are not interchangeable measures. In Bangladesh, Chorki launched on 12 July 2021, offering Bengali films, series, and documentaries as well as commissioned work. It illustrated another possibility for streaming: a domestic producer could assemble a catalogue oriented toward a linguistic community with both domestic and overseas audiences, rather than depend exclusively on the programming priorities of a global service. Pakistani television's extensive distribution of dramas through online video channels presented yet another model, built around the circulation of individual episodes and advertising-supported access.

For producers the change involved a new allocation of risk. A theatrical film could earn income from ticket sales but depend on the availability of screens and on the terms of distribution. A commission from a platform could supply financing before audiences saw the work, yet the producer might have little control over how long it remained available, which languages received promotion, or whether it was renewed as a series. Licensing a finished film preserved a different relationship between the independent producer and the buyer. Subtitles and dubbed versions could widen a film's potential market, but a catalogue containing hundreds of works did not guarantee visibility for any particular one. The movement from physical distribution to digital catalogues should therefore be studied as a reorganization of economic and editorial power, not as the disappearance of gatekeepers.

Section 108.18

Diasporic Communities and Transnational South Asian Culture

South Asian diasporas, whose history began in the labor migrations of the colonial period (Chapter 75) and expanded through postwar migration to Britain and North America and the Gulf labor migration after the 1970s (Chapter 97), became major audiences, producers, and critics of South Asian culture. In Britain, communities of Indian, Pakistani, and Bangladeshi origin supported Asian radio, television, and print, and British-Asian writers and filmmakers addressed racism, class, and identity. In North America, professional and student migration created large audiences for Hindi and regional films and for literature in English. In the Gulf, millions of migrant workers, from Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, consumed film, music, and online media in their own languages, making the Gulf an important market, particularly for Malayalam and Tamil cinema. Southeast Asian, Caribbean, East African, and other diasporas preserved older cultural connections.

Diasporic audiences affected production. Hindi filmmakers of the 1990s and 2000s set stories abroad, scheduled releases to coincide with overseas holidays, and measured success partly in overseas earnings. Overseas publishers, festivals, and prizes shaped the careers of writers, and diasporic investors and producers financed projects. Writers such as Jhumpa Lahiri, Kiran Desai, Mohsin Hamid, Kamila Shamsie, and Michael Ondaatje, among many others, represented migration, mixed identities, and relations with countries of origin, and filmmakers such as Gurinder Chadha explored British-Asian life. Diasporic communities also supported political and religious organizations, and their online media could carry political messages across borders, a subject linked to the geopolitical themes of Chapter 110.

International commercial success must be distinguished from cultural representation. A globally popular film or book does not necessarily express the experiences of all the communities associated with its national or diasporic background; it may reflect the tastes of a particular audience, the requirements of international publishers and distributors, or the choices of a relatively privileged group. Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan, and Nepali diasporas, and working-class migrants in the Gulf, generated their own cultural forms, which attracted less international attention than the work of globally marketed authors and studios. Questions of authenticity, such as who has the right to represent a community, accompanied these debates.

The story of Slumdog Millionaire (2008), a British-directed film set in Mumbai, shows the complexity of international reception. Its eight Academy Awards, including two for A. R. Rahman, brought attention to Indian cinema and music, but critics in India and elsewhere argued that it presented poverty for foreign consumption, and the film’s popularity in the West did not coincide with equal enthusiasm at home. Gurinder Chadha’s Bend It Like Beckham (2002) and Jhumpa Lahiri’s fiction, in contrast, were read as explorations of British-Asian and Indian-American experience, though each reflected the perspective of a particular community and class. Diasporic institutions, including British Asian radio, the BBC Asian Network, which began as a regional service and later became national, television channels aimed at overseas audiences, and Indian entertainment networks that launched international feeds, supported a continuing cultural market. Migrant workers in the Gulf, by contrast, usually consumed rather than produced such media for global audiences, and their cultural lives, documented in a smaller body of research, show how class shaped who counted as a diasporic public.

Section 108.19

Cultural Industries, Global Influence, and the Changing Public Sphere, 1991–2026

Bringing the industrial and cultural histories together shows an ecosystem in which ownership, advertising, state policy, distribution technology, and audience taste interacted. Corporate media groups consolidated across film, television, streaming, and news; advertising shifted from print and television to digital platforms, which by 2025 took a majority of advertising spending in India according to industry reporting; and states shaped the sector through licensing, censorship, tax policy, and sometimes direct ownership. Large industries, notably Hindi film and Indian broadcasting, retained economic dominance, but smaller linguistic and national sectors gained opportunities through streaming, festivals, and diasporic and online audiences, and cultural influence sometimes ran in directions that the older center did not anticipate: Pakistani drama in India, Malayalam film in the Gulf and online, Bhutanese film at the Academy Awards, Sri Lankan fiction at the Booker.

Generative artificial intelligence became a topic in cultural production by 2025–26. Demonstrable uses included AI-assisted translation, subtitling, and dubbing, visual effects, music and voice synthesis, content moderation, and the production of low-cost synthetic video and audio, including deepfakes. Courts, notably the Delhi High Court, issued orders in 2023–25 protecting the personality rights of prominent performers against AI imitation, and India’s 2026 rules, discussed in §108.16, imposed labelling duties on specified intermediaries for synthetic audiovisual information within the rules’ definition. The 2026 industry report treated AI as a growth driver. Claims about the future of artistic labor, however, were predictions, and the evidence available through 2026 does not establish how far AI would displace or merely supplement writers, translators, dubbing artists, musicians, and animators; labor groups and creators raised concerns, and the question was unresolved.

Cultural globalization also created disputes over representation, authenticity, censorship, artistic freedom, and commercial power. Disputes over who may tell which stories, over film censorship and online takedowns, over the dominance of a few platforms, and over the unequal pay and working conditions of creative workers were features of the period, not aberrations.

Ownership consolidation was part of the story. Large conglomerates acquired or merged media assets: the combination of Reliance’s and Disney’s Indian businesses in 2024–25 created one of the country’s largest entertainment groups, and in 2022 the Adani Group acquired control of a major news broadcaster, NDTV, a transaction whose significance for editorial independence was debated by commentators and which was followed by departures of the founders from the company’s board. Such cases do not by themselves prove changes in coverage, but they illustrate why scholars and press-freedom groups treat ownership concentration as relevant to editorial independence. Beneath the corporate level lay a large and often precarious creative workforce, including dubbing artists, translators, technicians, freelance journalists, and online creators, whose pay and security varied widely and who were among the first to feel the effects of platform decisions and, potentially, of AI tools. A history of the period that focuses only on stars, owners, and ministers would therefore omit the people who produced much of the content.

The ownership history also needs to be understood from the standpoint of creative labor and rights. A recording, film, or television series can yield income in several markets and over many years. The person who owns a copyright or contractual exploitation right may receive revenue when a work is licensed to a broadcaster, translated, remixed, or made available in another territory. The person who wrote, performed, translated, or edited the work need not share proportionately in those revenues. In earlier cinema the contest over rights often centered on theatrical distribution and music recordings; in the digital period, rights to catalogues, clips, dubbed versions, and international distribution became additional sources of value. The growing role of synthetic voices and images raised further questions about consent and compensation. Whether a technological development increased the value of a creative work and whether it improved the position of the people who worked on it were separate historical questions.

International visibility also varied by route. A blockbuster could reach overseas cinemas through a commercial distributor, a literary work through the labor of a translator and the prestige of a prize, and a small-country film through a festival programmer or an international coproduction. The resulting publics overlapped but were not identical. For example, Bhutanese cinema's success at international awards demonstrated a capacity to enter global circuits of recognition without the industrial scale of the largest Indian-language cinemas. Regional-language streaming created a different route to visibility, by making some works available to audiences that would rarely have encountered them in a local theater. Neither route eliminated differences in budgets, advertising, or control over distribution.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Cultural Authority and Freedom of Expression, 1991–2026

What Changed

  • State broadcasting monopolies gave way to private and digital media in much of the region.
  • Regional-language and smaller-country work gained visibility and economic weight.
  • Individuals and small publishers could reach audiences without institutional gatekeepers.
  • Regulation extended from print and broadcast to platforms, streaming, and synthetic media.

What Remained Continuous

  • Governments retained legal means of controlling speech, including defamation, sedition-style and security laws, licensing, and censorship boards.
  • Language and English-language prestige continued to structure cultural hierarchy.
  • Advertising and ownership continued to shape independence.
  • Cultural works remained objects of political and religious contest.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban, younger, and connected audiences, creators in regional languages, and journalists exposed to new legal and online pressures experienced the greatest change. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural, elderly, poor, and women audiences with limited access, and those who had long faced social exclusion from literary and media institutions. Why the pattern matters: It shows that new media altered the channels of authority more than the underlying contest between expression and power.

Section 108.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

Between 1991 and 2026, print, literature, cinema, television, popular music, digital platforms, and diasporic networks transformed political participation, language, social identity, cultural memory, and public debate in South Asia. Newspapers expanded in vernacular languages and remained locally influential. Writers in English and, increasingly through translation, in other languages interpreted Partition, caste, war, migration, and environmental change. Hindi cinema globalized and then lost its uncontested primacy as Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi, Bengali, and other industries reached national and international audiences. Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan, Nepali, Bhutanese, and Maldivian screen cultures developed within their own constraints. Satellite television ended state monopolies in practice, though not state influence, and twenty-four-hour news reshaped political communication. Digital platforms allowed mass publication, mobilization, and harassment, and governments responded with new regulation.

The relationship between cultural expression and political authority showed both continuity and change. Authorities continued to use licensing, censorship, defamation law, and security legislation, now extended to digital platforms, and societies continued to contest the boundaries of acceptable speech through protest and litigation. At the same time, more people than ever could publish, in more languages, and cultural influence flowed in more directions. Expanded communication did not automatically produce greater equality, reliable information, or unrestricted expression. It created new opportunities for participation alongside new concentrations of institutional and technological power, held by corporate media groups, political parties, platforms, and states.

The chapter connects to the broader transformations of Volume VI. Chapter 106 explained the economic and technological infrastructure, including connectivity, that made new media possible, and Chapter 107 examined the social struggles over caste, gender, and religion that media represented and amplified. This chapter has examined how those transformations were communicated, represented, and contested. Chapter 109 turns to climate, water, and the environment, where journalism, storytelling, and public knowledge again shape how South Asians understand, and respond to, a shared and changing landscape.

One way to interpret the period is to distinguish three forms of access. Access to reception expanded when newspapers became affordable, television sets became more common, and mobile connectivity made audiovisual content portable. Access to production expanded when more linguistic communities, small publishers, and individual creators could make material available. Access to authority, however, depended on the ability to finance sustained work, secure distribution, be found by audiences, and withstand legal or commercial pressure. The first two forms of access could increase without the third becoming equal. This is why a region crowded with channels, newspapers, and online accounts could still contain serious inequalities in the ability to be heard.

The regional comparison reinforces that conclusion. India's enormous advertising and subscriber markets supported industrial production on a scale that other countries could not replicate, but its media history cannot explain Pakistan's television drama, Nepal's community radio, Bangladesh's literary and streaming networks, Sri Lanka's artistic responses to conflict, or the managed expansion of Bhutanese and Maldivian broadcasting. Conversely, the circulation of works across national borders often undermined the idea that distinct media systems must produce isolated national cultures. In the history of South Asia, language, migration, commercial distribution, and political authority continually interacted across borders. The chapters that follow will ask how similarly interconnected societies respond to shared environmental dangers and negotiate their place in the wider world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that South Asia’s public sphere expanded dramatically between 1991 and 2026, but that the expansion was shaped by competition over language, representation, commercial influence, technological access, and political authority. It began with the postcolonial foundations of print culture and showed how newspapers, especially vernacular papers, grew with literacy, advertising, and new printing technology, and why they remained influential even as television and the internet spread.

It then examined language and publishing. English-language writing gained international access, while writers in other languages depended on translation, small markets, and institutions such as academies and festivals; recent international prizes for translated Hindi and Kannada work showed both the possibilities and the gatekeeping involved. Literature was treated as evidence of social imagination, including on Partition, caste, war, migration, and memory, rather than as documentary evidence of society.

Journalism and press freedom were analyzed through investigative work, ownership, economics, legal pressure, and violence against journalists. The 2026 RSF index, which ranked Nepal 87th, Maldives 108th, Sri Lanka 134th, Bhutan 150th, Bangladesh 152nd, Pakistan 153rd, and India 157th, was treated as a documented but contestable assessment.

The chapter then turned to cinema. Hindi cinema was reshaped by industry status, corporate finance, multiplexes, and diasporic audiences, and its cultural primacy was challenged by Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi, Bengali, and other cinemas, whose films circulated through dubbing, subtitling, and streaming. Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan, Nepali, Bhutanese, and Maldivian screen cultures were examined as distinct industries with their own constraints and achievements.

Television was shown to have changed from a state monopoly to a competitive private and regional medium, with satellite and cable distribution, new programming, and twenty-four-hour news. The debate over whether commercial broadcasting democratized public discussion or concentrated power was presented as unresolved, with evidence on both sides and a caution against equating audience reach with political effect. National broadcasting institutions in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and the smaller states were compared, and music was treated as an evolving set of traditions whose economics were transformed by recording, television, and streaming.

Digital media extended publishing to individuals and small organizations while remaining unequal by language, gender, income, and place. Case studies of Bangladesh in 2024 and Nepal in 2025 showed digital communication as part of broader political mobilization, not its sole cause. Misinformation, algorithmic amplification, and harassment were examined with attention to the difference between misinformation and disinformation and to the difficulty of attributing causation.

Regulation was analyzed by distinguishing stated purpose, legal power, and documented implementation. India’s IT Rules, including the February 2026 amendments on synthetic media, Pakistan’s 2025 amendment to its electronic-crimes law, Sri Lanka’s Online Safety Act, and Nepal’s platform ban were compared, and several outcomes were identified as unresolved at the research cutoff.

Streaming, diasporic audiences, and generative AI completed the account. Streaming changed distribution and financing and opened opportunities for regional and independent work while creating new gatekeepers. Diasporic communities shaped production and consumption, though commercial success abroad did not guarantee representative portrayal. AI was treated as a documented tool and a regulatory issue, with its long-term effects on creative labor left as open questions.

Taken together, the chapter shows that media and cultural institutions are not mirrors of society but arenas in which South Asians contested identity, politics, and memory. Its place in Volume VI is to explain how the transformations described in Chapters 106 and 107 were represented and argued over, preparing the turn to the environmental history of Chapter 109.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the great transformations of liberalization, connectivity, and social struggle in contemporary South Asia were never experienced as abstractions. They were read in newspapers, watched in cinemas and living rooms, heard in songs, argued over in novels, and, increasingly, carried in pockets. Culture was the medium in which South Asians recognized themselves and one another and disagreed about what they owed each other.

The chapter also reveals a recurrent pattern: each expansion of communication prompted new forms of control, and each form of control prompted new forms of improvisation, from translation and dubbing to virtual private networks and independent channels. Authority did not disappear when a monopoly ended; it moved into ownership, advertising, regulation, and platform design. That pattern links the region’s colonial and postcolonial press history to its digital present.

Finally, the chapter widens the history of South Asia. A history told only through Delhi, Mumbai, and English would miss Dhaka’s uprising, Karachi’s dramas, Colombo’s novels, Kathmandu’s community radio, and Thimphu’s late arrival of television. The six-volume sequence closes with the environmental and global questions of Chapters 109–111, in which how societies communicate what they know will shape how they respond.
