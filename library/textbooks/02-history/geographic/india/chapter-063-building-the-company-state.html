Building the Company State

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1770 a Company official could list sums spent on famine charity beside the costs of keeping the administration functioning. The same government claimed tax revenues and struggled to feed its subjects. How did a trading corporation become a territorial state—and who gained authority as it did?

In 1770 Bengal experienced a devastating famine while the East India Company collected the revenues granted to it by the Mughal emperor only five years earlier. Company officers could demand payments from an agricultural economy in crisis, yet the institutions through which they assessed distress, coordinated relief and answered for public welfare were fragmented. This mismatch between fiscal command and governmental responsibility is the opening problem of Chapter 63. The famine also changed the evidence available to historians: officials produced accounts of harvests, prices and charity, and later commentators used them to argue over why so many died.

Chapter 62 traced the transformation of the Company from a powerful trader into the principal military and fiscal power in Bengal. Chapter 63 asks how that corporation constructed a lasting administration. From the 1770s to the early 1790s, successive experiments in tax collection, courts, district government and metropolitan oversight produced a state capable of exercising authority through offices and records rather than through a few exceptional military and diplomatic victories. The resulting institutions were not designed all at once or implemented uniformly.

The first challenge was information. Officials needed to know which estates owed revenue, what an agricultural district could produce, who had authority to collect money, which court should decide a dispute, and how far a decision from Calcutta could be enforced. Much of this knowledge resided with Indian zamindars, qanungos, scribes, bankers, religious-law experts and village officials. British officers increasingly occupied the highest positions, but Company government remained dependent on the skills and social connections of Indians whom its regulations often treated as subordinate.

The second challenge was constitutional. In Britain, Parliament confronted the spectacle of a commercial corporation that employed soldiers, negotiated with rulers and collected the taxes of millions of people. Financial troubles and allegations of private enrichment prompted legislation in 1773 and 1784, but Parliament did not simply abolish Company administration. Instead it assembled a system of overlapping corporate and ministerial supervision. Warren Hastings’s impeachment revealed how disputed the standards of responsibility remained.

The third challenge concerned landed property. Warren Hastings tried temporary settlements and revenue farming in pursuit of accurate and reliable collections. Cornwallis and John Shore later debated how to interpret existing land rights and whether public assessments should be permanently fixed. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 was a landmark for the Company’s fiscal system, but its promised security for estate-holders was not the same as secure rents or protection for cultivators. Written law established categories whose effects were negotiated and contested in rural society.

Throughout the chapter, formal changes must be distinguished from practical outcomes. A new court might hear a petitioner yet remain inaccessible to someone unable to travel or pay fees. A higher official salary might reduce one incentive for personal trade yet confine prestigious appointments to Europeans. A fixed land-revenue demand might make treasury income more predictable while leaving disputes over rents unresolved. These tensions explain why the history of administrative institutions also belongs to social and economic history.

The five divisions proceed from famine and fiscal crisis to Hastings’s reforms, parliamentary supervision, Cornwallis and land settlement, and the emergence of courts, civil administration and military-financial organization. The aim is neither to celebrate a march toward efficient government nor to assume that every reform served one identical purpose. It is to show how specific choices altered the power of the Company and the people it governed. Chapter 64 will turn to the military and diplomatic contests in which that expanding state operated.

Historical Context

After the Mughal emperor granted the East India Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha in August 1765, Company revenue rights coexisted with the nominal political and criminal authority of the Bengal nawab. The arrangement known as dual government relied extensively on Indian deputies and local landholding interests. The Company received money while its responsibility for broader governance remained ambiguously distributed. Chapter 62 explained how this settlement followed the victories at Plassey and Buxar; the present chapter examines its weaknesses and the attempts to replace it with a more direct apparatus.

Bengal was a populous agrarian and manufacturing region whose rice harvests depended on monsoon and local environmental conditions. River traffic, merchants and banking networks connected its districts with port cities and inland markets. European Company officials could draw upon substantial financial resources without possessing complete knowledge of the agriculture, land rights and social obligations on which those resources depended. Older Mughal and nawabi institutions remained important, particularly for keeping revenue accounts and resolving local disputes.

The period also witnessed growing British political scrutiny of the Company. Its Court of Directors answered to shareholders and operated under parliamentary and royal authority while governing territories acquired through diplomacy and force. These overlapping responsibilities led to competing arguments about profit, public duty and the appropriate basis of colonial law. The administrative reforms associated with Hastings and Cornwallis emerged within this unsettled environment, not outside it.

Historical evidence includes Company consultations, revenue letters, published regulations, petitions, parliamentary debates, and legal reports. Such sources are especially rich for official intentions and disputes; they are less complete for the experience of landless laborers and cultivators. A methodologically responsible chapter must examine what a document actually establishes and resist treating every official assessment as a verified measure of social conditions.

Section 63.1

The Bengal Famine of 1770: Environmental Crisis, Agricultural Failure, and Food Scarcity

The famine conventionally dated 1770 began as an agricultural emergency during 1769, before the worst mortality became visible. Rainfall was deficient in parts of Bengal and Bihar, jeopardizing the autumn and winter rice crops upon which many households depended. In a rice-based economy a missed harvest meant more than one season of discomfort: cultivators needed seed for the next crop, laborers depended on employment from landholders, and artisans relied on the purchasing power of agricultural customers. Reports of rising grain prices therefore announced simultaneous threats to food, wages, credit, and future production. The disaster unfolded after the East India Company had acquired the diwani in 1765 but before it possessed an integrated apparatus for assessing distress or organizing relief. That administrative setting shaped the crisis without explaining the failure of rainfall itself.

The geography of the catastrophe was uneven. The comparatively dry western districts around Birbhum, Bankura, Burdwan, and parts of Bihar differed from eastern delta districts with heavier rainfall, extensive watercourses, and a different agricultural calendar. This does not imply that everyone in the wetter east escaped, that every settlement in the west failed, or that rivers mechanically guaranteed food. Rainfall, soil, access to canals or tanks, cultivated crops, transport costs, and the position of a household in local markets all influenced exposure. Historical sources rarely provide enough reliable district-level observations to map severity with the precision of a modern disaster assessment. An atlas can illuminate possible vulnerabilities; it cannot supply missing mortality data.

The rhythm of the agricultural year matters. When prices rose after a deficient harvest, some households purchased grain by selling cattle, tools, ornaments, seed, or claims on future labor. These transactions could keep families alive briefly while undermining their capacity to recover. Others depended on patrons, village elites, religious institutions, or merchants willing to extend credit. Grain might remain available somewhere yet become inaccessible to those whose wages or assets no longer purchased it. Farmers cultivating cash crops could suffer indirectly when the food they needed became expensive; weavers might lose employment as consumers ceased buying cloth. Distress thus traveled from damaged fields through market prices and debt into occupations far removed from harvesting rice.

Contemporary officials attempted to explain scarcity with incomplete evidence. They collected statements about crop conditions, prices, grain movement, rainfall, and revenue, but many observations reflected the districts and interests of particular Company residents. Later administrators and writers assembled disparate accounts into general explanations. John Lidwell-Durnin's study of the famine correspondence shows that officials debated whether the crisis was natural, produced by monopolists, or aggravated by government actions, and that administrative information itself became a political resource. Their categories reflected a search for responsibility as much as detached scientific measurement. Students must therefore distinguish a contemporary report, an administrator's diagnosis, and a modern historian's inference.

A failed monsoon was a major proximate cause, but the proposition that drought caused famine is incomplete until one explains why deprivation became fatal. Reserves and redistribution could have reduced exposure in some places; bad roads, uncertain information, transport bottlenecks, conflict, and inadequate purchasing power could make supplies inaccessible in others. Rice specialization increased reliance on one agricultural system, although trade and diverse local livelihoods could provide partial protection. The Company had fiscal power but limited coordinated administrative capacity, while Indian landholders and intermediaries retained substantial local influence. Neither the existence of markets nor the existence of government ensured an effective response. The same environmental shock could produce different outcomes among neighboring social groups.

For this chapter, the famine must be studied as an encounter between ecology and newly acquired Company power. Its significance lies not only in how many died, which cannot be determined exactly, but in what the emergency revealed about the state's incentives and information. A corporation that depended on remittances to finance armies, commercial purchases, and obligations to London approached disaster with strong reasons to protect its revenue. Its servants, meanwhile, lacked a settled legal and administrative framework for compelling coordinated relief. The famine tested the distinction established in Chapter 62 between possessing rights to provincial revenue and accepting comprehensive responsibility for provincial society.

Historical Vocabulary — Diwani and Nizamat
Diwani / Nizamat

The diwani comprised revenue and related civil-administrative authority; the nizamat encompassed political, military, policing and criminal functions associated with the nawab or nazim. After 1765 Company fiscal power and the nominal nawabi administration were formally separated. The 1771–1772 decision to “stand forth as diwan” meant exercising existing rights through more direct Company supervision—not a new Diwani grant.

Section 63.2

Mortality, Grain Markets, Relief, and the Social Consequences of Famine

The human consequences of the famine reached well beyond those who died directly from hunger. Those least able to purchase grain—landless laborers, poorer cultivators, dependent craft workers, itinerant workers, and people without secure patrons—faced the severest danger. Malnutrition increased vulnerability to disease, while migration exposed families to further losses of health, shelter, and familiar support. Household members did not necessarily receive equal portions of scarce food: status, age, gender, and earning capacity could affect survival, even when the evidence cannot recover individual experiences. Families might sell livestock, pledge ornaments, transfer rights over land, borrow at high cost, or leave their villages. A famine was therefore simultaneously a demographic shock, a transfer of assets, and a disruption of social relationships.

Grain prices became the visible signal of distress but were not a complete measure of food security. A price recorded in a market town tells us little about whether grain was available in a distant hamlet or whether a laborer's wage could buy enough. Traders faced danger, credit costs, transport uncertainty, and the risk of losing goods in transit. Officials accused some merchants of withholding supplies or combining to control prices; those accusations require examination rather than automatic acceptance. Hoarding for speculation, storage for seasonal consumption, precautionary reserves, and ordinary commercial distribution were not identical activities. Baijayanti Chatterjee's study emphasizes merchants, rice specialization, and the limits of public-granary schemes, but the precise contribution of each factor differed across districts and years.

Relief was fragmented. Some Company officials sanctioned rice purchases or charitable expenditure, while local notables, religious institutions, relatives, and merchants supplied assistance of their own. The survival of such actions in the archive depends heavily on what Company officials considered worth recording. A particularly revealing report by Richard Becher dated 20 November 1770 discussed spending in his district on rice charity and the removal of dead bodies alongside other official expenditures. These figures do not measure the province's total response, yet they show how officials translated distress into items of account. The comparison of emergency relief with competing administrative expenditures invites questions about priorities without by itself proving what was possible everywhere.

Historians frequently encounter the assertion that about one-third of Bengal's population died. This was an influential contemporary and subsequent estimate, not the result of a reliable census before and after the crisis. Different studies present substantial losses but disagree about the appropriate geographical denominator, the reliability of official statements, and the relation between migration and death. John R. McLane characterizes the calamity as extraordinarily severe while presenting a broad range; Tirthankar Roy warns that the meager systematic records make confident allocation of causes and deaths impossible. It is sound historical practice to acknowledge mass mortality and exceptional suffering without converting an estimate into an exact demographic fact.

The agricultural aftermath was prolonged. Death and flight could leave fields uncultivated and undermine the availability of plough animals, seed, and agricultural labor. Zamindars and revenue farmers still faced their own payments, which could encourage pressure on surviving cultivators or efforts to attract new settlers with concessions. Artisans lost customers and workers; rural creditors might acquire claims over land or crops but also lose debtors unable to pay. Some commercial centers recovered more rapidly than damaged interior districts, and improvements in rainfall did not instantly restore households that had sold their means of livelihood. An apparent recovery in recorded revenue might conceal uneven recovery below the level of district accounts.

The famine also acquired a powerful afterlife in British political debate. Critics described the disaster as evidence that Company servants subordinated human welfare to profit, while defenders emphasized natural calamity and the limitations of eighteenth-century communication and transport. Both arguments could simplify a catastrophe whose social mechanisms were varied and poorly measured. Historians should ask whose suffering is visible in official letters and whose is absent: a merchant's complaint, a collector's estimate, or a metropolitan speech offers a different vantage point from that of an agricultural family. The geography exercise accompanying this section invites students to connect environmental differences with archival silences rather than to invent a map of deaths.

Section 63.3

The East India Company, Revenue Demands, and Responsibility for the Famine

The East India Company's position during the famine was shaped by the constitutional anomaly established after 1765. It received Bengal's principal fiscal rights as diwan while much day-to-day government formally remained with the nawab's administration and Indian deputies. Company officers could direct policy or demand receipts without always controlling the local officials responsible for policing, grain movements, or customary relief. The separation of fiscal power from visibly acknowledged responsibility made it easier to disown an outcome and harder to coordinate a response. Yet this should not imply that the Company was an innocent bystander. It benefited from the revenue system and possessed growing military and political leverage over the institutions that administered it.

Land revenue was assessed through elaborate relationships among the Company, provincial revenue officers, zamindars, farmers of revenue, and village-level intermediaries. Officials in Calcutta wanted predictable receipts despite uncertainty in the harvest. Some remissions, accommodations, or revisions occurred in particular circumstances, but revenue demands continued during the famine and its aftermath. Historians must distinguish sums assessed, sums demanded, sums remitted to Calcutta, and sums extracted from cultivating households; the four were not identical. A surviving ledger could show a payment meeting its target while concealing a landlord's borrowing, coercion, or failure to provide customary support to tenants. Conversely, one district's revenue reduction does not prove that its poorest inhabitants received food.

Accusations of speculation and monopoly became politically explosive. Some Company servants had private commercial interests, and traders might obtain advantages through access to officials, warehouse capacity, or contracts. Contemporary observers suspected that merchants and servants restricted grain supplies in order to profit from inflated prices. Such claims must be assessed against actual transactions and supply conditions: a denunciation of monopoly is evidence of an allegation, not proof that a single coordinated group controlled Bengal's entire grain market. Lidwell-Durnin demonstrates that the language of artificial scarcity circulated within Company correspondence and British public criticism. Chatterjee likewise investigates mercantile strategies and the difficulty of implementing grain interventions. Neither approach eliminates the documented agricultural shortfall.

The scale of possible relief is itself contested. Some writers emphasize that earlier remissions or public grain purchase could have saved lives; others point to the quantity of food needed, the limits of eighteenth-century transport, deficient information, and the uncertain reach of government. Tirthankar Roy's work underlines the difficulty of reconstructing which of drought, conflict, and dysfunctional government contributed most, while a separate recent historical-economic analysis questions counterfactual claims that fiscal remissions alone could have averted the catastrophe. These arguments should not be reduced to an artificial choice between condemning the Company and excusing its behavior. Revenue policy could damage resilience even if no available measure would have prevented all mortality.

Responsibility must be considered at several levels. A local revenue farmer could demand payment when land was failing; a zamindar might provide relief to tenants or protect his own household first; a Company resident might authorize spending or prioritize remittances; directors in London might pursue dividends while being far removed from events. Not all decisions were equivalent, and not all information reached the same decision-maker. The distribution of authority made accountability difficult to locate. At the same time, the Company had acquired precisely the resources that could have supported a stronger provincial response, and its inability to deploy them became part of the case for transforming its administration.

The famine should therefore be assessed as both a catastrophic environmental episode and a test of colonial political economy. The important historical result is not a confidently assigned percentage of blame. It is the emergence of a regime that could mobilize revenue and soldiers effectively while struggling to protect people exposed to an acute failure of subsistence. This tension persisted as the Company announced new administrative reforms. The decision to reorganize government after 1771 reflected a fiscal and political crisis, as well as the humanitarian inadequacy made visible by the famine. The debate feature asks students to weigh different causal mechanisms and the unequal quality of the evidence supporting them.

Section 63.4

The Company's Financial Crisis and the Demand for Administrative Reform, 1770–1773

The famine formed part of a wider fiscal crisis facing the East India Company in the early 1770s. Territorial revenue had increased its apparent resources, but possession of rich provinces did not eliminate the costs of administration, military deployment, patronage, commercial investment, and obligations to shareholders. The Company could report large receipts while its cash balances remained strained: revenue was collected in places and at times that did not necessarily match expenditures, and political demands could interrupt expected remittances. Distance between Calcutta and London further complicated financial planning. Directors evaluated India through dispatches, shipments, and accounts that might arrive long after decisions were taken.

The Company still operated as a trading corporation purchasing Asian goods for resale. Its Bengal revenue could finance commercial investment, but acquiring territory did not transform every fiscal receipt into a liquid surplus. Soldiers required regular pay and supplies; fortifications and transport cost money; civil administration needed salaries; creditors expected repayment. Shareholders, encouraged by the reputation of Bengal's wealth after Plassey and the Diwani, pressed for dividends. Parliamentary critics considered it troubling that private investors might distribute profits while the population paying the revenue suffered. The tension was institutional, not merely evidence of a few dishonest officials: corporate incentives had not been designed for the burdens of government.

Political controversy intensified as wealthy Company servants returned to Britain. The popular figure of the 'nabob' represented fortunes made through presents, private trade, and political advantage, although individual careers differed. Observers questioned whether men possessing an interest in personal enrichment could supervise the collection of taxes and dispense justice. Such criticism became entangled with British domestic political rivalries and with unease over the influence that returning fortunes might buy in Parliament. Not every accusation was proved, and moral denunciation could obscure more general problems of uncertain legal authority and inconsistent administrative practice. Nevertheless, the Company's power now presented questions that could no longer be treated as internal matters of merchant management.

By 1772 the Company faced liquidity problems and sought financial assistance. The British government considered the relationship between public support and governmental supervision, ultimately providing loans under legislation passed in 1773 alongside the Regulating Act. The need for assistance weakened the argument that the Company's directors should enjoy unexamined authority over territories acquired by Company arms. Parliament had long granted and revised the corporation's monopoly privileges; now it confronted the political consequences of a corporation exercising forms of sovereignty. The question was not simply whether the Company should trade efficiently, but who should be responsible for the administration and protection of its subjects.

In Bengal, another response was already underway. Directors instructed their servants in August 1771 to 'stand forth as diwan', a phrase indicating that the Company should exercise the financial office it had held legally since 1765 rather than continue to govern chiefly through deputies. Warren Hastings implemented major elements of that program after taking office in 1772. This did not place every village under a British officer or eliminate Indian participation. It did make the Company's responsibility more visible and created new administrative instruments through which headquarters could monitor collectors, courts, and district reports. The reforms were inseparable from the pursuit of more reliable revenue.

The crisis therefore produced two overlapping reform processes: administrative reorganization in India and legislative supervision in Britain. Both were directed partly at corruption and accountability, but both also sought to safeguard the Company as a functioning imperial-financial institution. Famine, fiscal disorder, investor expectations, and debate over sovereignty converged without producing one agreed solution. For students of state formation, this is the crucial transition. The Company had first acquired fiscal rights through diplomatic and military victory; it now tried to create the offices, records, laws, and decision-making structures through which that power could be exercised repeatedly. The cause-and-effect feature below separates pressures for change from the different institutions ultimately established.

Section 63.5

Warren Hastings and the Assumption of Direct Administration, 1772

Warren Hastings came to Bengal with long experience of Company commercial and political affairs. He had served in India before the conquest of Bengal, developed familiarity with Persianate government and Company administration, and later held senior office in Madras. When he became governor of Bengal in 1772, the principal immediate question was how to implement the Court of Directors' instruction of August 1771 that the Company should 'stand forth as diwan'. The Company had possessed the legal right to Bengal's revenues since 1765; it had not yet administered them consistently through a network accountable to its own provincial government. The contrast between legal entitlement and administrative capacity was the problem Hastings had to address.

One part of his program was to reduce the authority of senior Indian deputy revenue administrators who had acted for the Company during dual government. Muhammad Reza Khan in Bengal and Raja Shitab Rai in Bihar came under scrutiny and investigation, in circumstances marked by Company factionalism and allegations of mismanagement. Their displacement should not be read as proof that Indian officials alone caused the preceding crisis. They had performed functions the Company required, and British officers continued to depend on their knowledge and colleagues. The investigations reflected an effort to identify responsibility and secure institutional control, but also demonstrated how accusation, patronage, and political change could shape individual careers.

The Company moved its effective revenue headquarters toward Calcutta, strengthening the connection between financial supervision and the presidency government. District collectors, initially drawn from Company service, were given authority to supervise assessments, accounts, and collection. A Committee of Circuit was sent to examine districts and organize settlements. The reforms promised more accurate information: a government able to compare expected and received income, track intermediaries, and intervene where officials were judged ineffective. Yet a man trained to purchase textiles or manage accounts was not necessarily familiar with land rights, crop cycles, village politics, or the distinctions among competing revenue claims. The Company expanded its supervision faster than it could acquire secure local knowledge.

Hastings described parts of his reform as a recovery of established institutions rather than an unprecedented replacement of Indian government. The Company's use of the title diwan, existing revenue offices, Persian documentation, and Indian advisers reinforced a claim of continuity. But the decisive authority to appoint, supervise, and dismiss increasingly rested with the corporation and its council. That combination of Mughal categories and new corporate command was typical of the Company's early territorial state. Its officers did not govern through a clean substitution of English law for Indian custom; they appropriated and modified administrative forms they had inherited.

The political purpose of the reform was also fiscal. Directors in London wanted revenues that could meet military charges, supply investment goods, support debts, and produce dividends. Calcutta wanted officers whose behavior it could review. District collectors wanted manageable assessments and reliable partners. Zamindars and peasants needed rules they could survive, although they had little influence over the terms of the directors' instructions. These goals overlapped imperfectly. Reforms pursued in the name of order could increase pressure if an ambitious assessment became easier to enforce; the same reforms could also clarify procedures or reduce the unchecked authority of a particular intermediary.

The date 1772 marks a major administrative change, not the completion of a modern civil service. Provincial councils and arrangements for revenue collection were revised in subsequent years, while the Company's legal jurisdiction remained contested. Hastings's early system relied on Indian accountants, record-keepers, local powerholders, and judges; enforcement varied from district to district. The contrast between the Company's stated intent and actual institutional reach is essential to the next three sections. Colonial government was assembled through decisions whose consequences were uncertain and whose original justifications were often altered by later experience.

Section 63.6

Revenue Experiments: Collectors, Revenue Farming, and the Five-Year Settlement

The five-year revenue settlement launched in 1772 was one of Hastings's most consequential experiments. The Committee of Circuit sought estimates of the revenues that districts might yield and invited bids from prospective collectors or contractors. Revenue farming transferred the obligation to pay a fixed amount to government to a farmer who expected to recover that sum, along with profit, from local collections. The Company hoped competitive bidding would reveal a district's productive potential and prevent long-established revenue elites from concealing resources. That expectation underestimated the difference between a bid made to win a contract and the amount cultivators could pay after an uncertain harvest.

The system confronted an information problem. Historical jama figures represented assessments or expectations, not necessarily net collections in a particular agricultural year. Officials had to distinguish cultivated from abandoned land, irrigated from rain-fed plots, commercial crops from food crops, customary deductions, and liabilities owed through intermediate rights. Much of that information was held in the records and memories of qanungos, village accountants, zamindari agents, and experienced merchants. English officers could commission reports, but identifying which accounts were accurate required the cooperation of people who might have incentives to minimize or exaggerate their figures. A printed regulation could not remove these incentives.

High bids created dangers. Revenue farmers who promised more than a district could sustainably yield might intensify pressure on tenants, borrow against future collections, or default. Zamindars displaced from familiar revenue functions could resist or seek new partnerships with successful bidders. Some established landed families participated in the system and others suffered losses; the categories of farmer and zamindar did not describe permanently separate social groups. The Company's problem was not simply how to punish corrupt officers. It had to decide whether land rights, hereditary claims, short-term contracts, or presumed market competition provided the most reliable foundation for the treasury.

The five-year experiment was revised before its intended simplicity could be realized. Collectors were withdrawn from some responsibilities, provincial revenue councils assumed functions, and headquarters altered its supervisory arrangements. The fluctuations reflected dissatisfaction with results, disputes over costs, and a persistent shortage of trustworthy local information. They also reflected political changes in the Calcutta council after 1774. Administrative records consequently describe an evolving apparatus rather than a single fixed scheme attributable to Hastings alone. Students should compare decisions at the beginning of the decade with revisions later in the 1770s and early 1780s before drawing conclusions about success or failure.

The social burden was unequal. A farmer guaranteeing a large payment faced risk of default; a cultivator confronted with higher rents or arrears could lose livestock, seed, or access to land. A substantial zamindar might negotiate terms or mobilize political connections that a small tenant lacked. Merchants and bankers supplied advances to farmers and landed households, potentially gaining influence over future crops and revenues. Some arrangements reduced uncertainty for particular landlords or improved local records. Others redistributed power and costs without increasing agricultural productivity. Actual collection figures cannot be used as a direct measure of rural well-being.

These experiments prepared the intellectual and institutional groundwork for later settlements. They forced the Company to debate what constituted property in land, which intermediaries were legally entitled to collect revenue, and whether assessments should be revised to reflect changing cultivation. Cornwallis and John Shore would return to these questions under different assumptions. The 1772 five-year settlement must therefore be treated as part of a sequence, not merely as a failed precursor to an inevitable Permanent Settlement. The vocabulary feature explains the difference between assessed revenue, contract, actual collection, and the rights of different participants in the fiscal chain.

Section 63.7

Hastings's Judicial Plan of 1772 and the Organization of the Adalat Courts

Hastings's plan for administering justice, adopted in August 1772, formed another central element of the Company's effort to govern more directly. Revenue claims and property disputes could not be separated neatly from administrative authority: a government that assessed land, enforced contracts, and paid officials needed mechanisms for deciding contested rights. The plan established civil courts in the districts, often called mofussil diwani adalats, presided over initially by collectors or Company officers, as well as criminal courts, or faujdari adalats, employing Indian judicial specialists under Company supervision. Higher appellate institutions provided oversight, although their location and composition were adjusted in later years. The scheme combined Company management with inherited legal practices.

The civil courts dealt with disputes involving property, inheritance, debts, and other claims falling under the evolving categories of civil jurisdiction. The criminal courts addressed offenses under rules and procedures associated with Indo-Islamic legal traditions. Qazis, muftis, maulavis, pandits, scribes, and interpreters remained indispensable. An English Company officer could not adjudicate a complex inheritance dispute in Bengali or Persian simply by invoking the corporate charter. He relied on experts who translated, explained, and sometimes contested the meaning of law. Those relationships could create opportunities for mediation but also dependency on particular intermediaries and their networks.

The Company's governing language increasingly described personal law according to religious affiliation, prescribing Hindu legal learning for Hindus and Muslim law for Muslims in specified matters such as inheritance and marriage. The neatness of this classification could obscure the diversity of local customary practices and the social position of the people who supplied legal opinions. A pandit's interpretation of a Sanskrit text was not necessarily identical to how a village resolved a dispute, just as a court's reading of Islamic jurisprudence did not reproduce every local practice. The Company's desire for consistency gradually encouraged a more textual and categorical account of Indian law, whose long-term consequences would become increasingly visible in the nineteenth century.

The district courts also posed a conflict of interest. A revenue collector who helped fix assessments and then adjudicated civil disputes affecting those assessments could hardly be treated as institutionally detached from the treasury. Officials sought efficiency by combining responsibilities, but litigants could question whether a claim against powerful revenue interests would receive independent scrutiny. The eventual Cornwallis reforms would separate some executive and judicial functions, in part as a response to this problem. It would be anachronistic, however, to project the later system back into 1772 or to assume that administrative separation automatically guaranteed equal access to justice.

Court procedure was as consequential as official doctrine. Filing a petition could require travel to a district headquarters, payment of fees, translation, witnesses, or assistance from a legal intermediary. Records could preserve a litigant's argument in a formal language unfamiliar to the person who had brought the case. A wealthy zamindar and an indebted tenant did not enter this world with comparable resources. At the same time, a written record and the possibility of appeal offered some litigants instruments unavailable in a purely private negotiation. Whether a court expanded protection or reinforced hierarchy often depended on the case, cost, and local enforcement.

The 1772 plan must be kept distinct from the royal Supreme Court established at Calcutta in 1774 and from the judicial code associated with Cornwallis in 1793. These institutions differed in their legal authority, personnel, and jurisdictions, and they could clash rather than combine smoothly. The source window in this section identifies Hastings's justice plan as preserved in published documentary histories, with its editorial paraphrase clearly distinguished from a verified transcription of every original clause. Students should read the plan as an administrative program whose impact depended on its execution by both British and Indian officials.

Section 63.8

Hastings's Government, Provincial Administration, and the Limits of Reform

Hastings's Bengal government developed through frequent alteration rather than unbroken implementation of an initial blueprint. Calcutta increasingly acted as the center of correspondence, revenue direction, and appeal, while districts and provincial councils remained the points where administrators encountered landlords, merchants, military officials, and litigants. Officers wrote reports on arrears, claims, harvests, troop movements, and disputes, but the information arriving at headquarters was selective and sometimes already outdated. A central council could issue instructions and replace an official more easily than it could verify what happened in a remote village. This gap between documentary authority and local practice was not incidental; it shaped the very form of Company rule.

The governor's powers also changed under the Regulating Act of 1773, implemented in 1774. Hastings became governor-general of Bengal and worked with a council established by the new legislation. Early in its operation a majority that included Philip Francis, John Clavering, and George Monson challenged many of his decisions. Important questions of patronage, diplomacy, revenue, and the conduct of Indian officeholders became matters of contested votes and hostile correspondence. The council was not merely an advisory body subordinate to Hastings. Political disagreement could block or redirect policy, which makes it misleading to write the decade as the execution of one administrator's settled program.

Those disagreements were carried into questions of territorial legitimacy. Company officials claimed the right to investigate Indian deputies, direct revenue systems, and negotiate with neighboring rulers, yet they had to decide how far such actions were consistent with imperial grants, treaties, and the Company's own commercial responsibilities. The accusations surrounding Muhammad Reza Khan and others illustrate how a shift in the Company's political coalitions could transform the career of a senior Indian administrator. These episodes also reveal that Company government needed an expanding network of trusted informants, accountants, translators, and creditors to manage its new responsibilities.

Relations with Indian officials were neither purely cooperative nor purely antagonistic. Experienced revenue specialists could offer the information needed for an assessment; a local banker could move funds across the countryside; a zamindar could supply order or armed resistance. Their continued influence limited the effectiveness of an administrative reform imagined as replacing Indian authority with European officers. At the same time, the Company increasingly reserved final power of appointment, discipline, and appeal to its own governing councils. Local elites adapted by acquiring Company patronage or learning new procedural rules. Dependence on existing society and the aspiration to command it developed together.

Legal and fiscal reform also generated new papers, offices, and opportunities for disagreement. A collector might claim that remittances were delayed because of harvest failure; a superior might suspect concealment; a landholder might contest the obligation altogether. Official records can reveal these conflicts, but they rarely provide an uncontested account of village life. The historian must consider how the bureaucratic record was produced: the language of an official report, the fact that a complaint reached Calcutta, and the absence of a peasant's testimony are all historically significant. Administrative centralization often increased the quantity of evidence while narrowing the range of voices preserved.

By the middle of the 1780s, the Company's state had more elaborate instruments of supervision than in 1765 but still lacked a stable settlement of authority. The relationship between district courts and the royal Supreme Court was disputed; revenue systems had been repeatedly adjusted; and British parliamentary intervention introduced a further line of oversight. Hastings's achievement lay in making direct corporate administration practicable enough to continue, not in finishing a fully standardized colonial state. The transition to the next division is therefore institutional: a company already exercising extensive powers in India increasingly had to answer to a British government uncertain how to supervise them.

Section 63.9

The Regulating Act of 1773 and the Creation of a Governor-Generalship

The Regulating Act of 1773 marked a constitutional turning point because the British Parliament undertook to regulate the government of territories administered by the East India Company. The Company was not a department of the British state. It had a royal charter, shareholders, directors, commercial interests, an army, and extensive rights acquired in South Asia through treaties and grants. Yet its fiscal crisis and the public controversy over its servants made it difficult for ministers to leave Indian territorial power entirely to corporate management. The legislation followed parliamentary inquiry and accompanied financial assistance. In effect, the British government asserted that the political consequences of the Company's activities could no longer be treated as exclusively private business.

The Act created the office of governor-general of Bengal and a council of four. Warren Hastings became the first holder of the new office when the arrangements took effect in 1774, but the composition and voting powers of the council constrained his freedom of action. The legislation also sought to improve supervision of the other Company presidencies, particularly in matters involving war and diplomacy. It did not erase the presidency establishments at Madras and Bombay or turn their officers into mere agents of a single all-powerful chief in Calcutta. Practical communications, competing provincial interests, and the limits of statutory drafting meant that the extent of Bengal's superiority remained contested.

Another central measure was the authorization of a Supreme Court at Fort William. Parliament and the Crown wished to subject aspects of Company and British behavior in India to a more recognizable form of judicial oversight. The resulting court derived its authority from the Crown, unlike the Company's district and appellate adalats. The Act also contained provisions concerning company officials' behavior and reporting. Its authors did not, however, supply a completely coherent division of jurisdiction across populations governed by different legal traditions. The formal imposition of new institutions did not instantly settle how they would fit together, and clashes emerged soon after the court began operating.

For people in Bengal the immediate significance of the Act varied. Merchants and Company servants might anticipate stronger supervision or more predictable legal rights; Indian officials might confront an additional source of orders; litigants might discover new opportunities for suit alongside confusion about which forum could hear their case. Colonial sovereignty was layered rather than neatly unified. The Company claimed powers through Mughal and regional arrangements, its directors issued instructions under a corporate charter, Parliament enacted rules, and a royal court interpreted its jurisdiction. These authorities could cooperate, but their coexistence also generated disputes that consumed time and money.

The Regulating Act did not convert the Company's territories into a directly Crown-administered colony. Directors still managed much of its business and appointed personnel, and Company government remained responsible for the administrative system in India. Nor did parliamentary regulation alone end conflicts of interest. A statute could establish an office or forbid behavior but could not create an honest workforce, an adequate system of information, or independent courts across large territories without further institutions. Revisions in the 1780s demonstrated that the 1773 settlement was regarded as incomplete even by its supporters.

The central historical importance of the Act is therefore neither complete reform nor negligible intervention. It made the Company's exercise of sovereignty a British constitutional issue and created institutional mechanisms through which political oversight could develop. It also disclosed the difficulties of governing one population through institutions that claimed authority from different sources. In the primary-source window, students should distinguish what the statute authorized from what a history of its actual operation must prove from letters, court records, and disputes. The chronology from 1773 through 1784 and beyond shows how repeated legislation built a new framework around an already existing territorial power.

Section 63.10

The Supreme Court at Calcutta, 1774, and the Conflicts of Colonial Jurisdiction

The Supreme Court of Judicature at Fort William began operating in 1774 under a royal charter authorized by the Regulating Act. Its first chief justice, Elijah Impey, and his colleagues brought English judicial forms into a legal environment already served by Company adalat courts and numerous local institutions. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction over British subjects was one thing; how far it could reach Indians, Company revenue officials, and conduct occurring outside Calcutta was far more contentious. Formal legal arguments could conceal a practical struggle over who could summon whom, enforce judgments, and subject government employees to a competing source of authority.

The Company's adalats derived from the exercise of diwani and the administrative plans adopted in 1772. They heard civil or criminal disputes through combinations of Company direction and Indian judicial personnel. The Supreme Court, by contrast, asserted powers under a royal charter and English legal procedures. Where the two systems touched, litigants and officials could become caught between contradictory rules. Revenue officers feared that summonses or damages issued by the royal court would obstruct tax collection. The court's judges argued that legal protections should not disappear simply because officials described their actions as revenue administration. Neither view automatically resolved the question of which inhabitants or territories fell within the charter.

The trial and execution of Maharaja Nandakumar, also known as Nuncomar, in 1775 became a lasting example of how judicial process and political conflict intersected. Nandakumar had made accusations of corruption against Hastings. He was then tried before the Supreme Court on a charge of forgery under the criminal law applied by the court, convicted, and executed. Later critics alleged that Hastings and Impey exploited the proceeding to eliminate a political accuser. These allegations demand careful handling: the chronology, charge, trial, and execution are identifiable events, but the existence and extent of a deliberate conspiracy cannot be treated as proved simply because opponents asserted it. The case also raises a broader question about applying imported criminal penalties in a society with differing legal practices.

Jurisdictional conflict became sufficiently serious that Parliament intervened again. The 1781 Act of Settlement clarified and restricted the royal court's reach in revenue matters and in certain proceedings against officers acting officially. It also recognized distinctions involving the personal law of Indian residents. The reform did not make law simple or eliminate the political character of court administration, but it sought to reduce clashes that threatened both revenue and judicial authority. Students should read the 1781 adjustment alongside the 1773 statute and the 1772 Hastings plan, rather than collapsing all three into a generic 'Company court system'.

The practical effects of legal pluralism were unequal. A wealthy merchant might employ counsel to exploit overlapping jurisdictions; a poor rural litigant could struggle with the cost of travel, translation, and unfamiliar procedure. Indian legal specialists preserved and interpreted established traditions but could be displaced or subordinated when courts imposed new rules for documentary proof. Different languages, administrative chains, and professional expectations produced confusion even when no official intended injustice. The long-term outcome was an increasingly formalized, expensive, and centralized legal order whose procedural promises existed beside substantial social barriers.

The Supreme Court's early history illustrates the paradox of colonial legal reform. British critics demanded law as a restraint on arbitrary Company power, while Company administrators insisted that judges who disregarded local political conditions could undermine effective government. Indian residents experienced those arguments through concrete decisions about property, debt, inheritance, taxation, and punishment. Whether the rule of law improved fairness depended partly on whose conduct the law could regulate and whose interests the courts prioritized. This distinction between institutional creation and practical experience also matters for evaluating the Cornwallis Code two decades later.

Section 63.11

Pitt's India Act of 1784 and the Restructuring of Company Oversight

The Regulating Act did not end parliamentary controversy over the Company's administration. By the early 1780s, disputes about war, revenue, gifts, patronage, and the conduct of the governor-general persuaded many British politicians that further intervention was necessary. Charles James Fox's India Bill of 1783 proposed to shift significant Company powers to a group of parliamentary commissioners. Its defeat emerged from a struggle over political patronage and constitutional authority as much as disagreement about India itself. Critics feared that whichever British faction controlled appointments to Company government would acquire enormous influence through a distant empire. India had become a contested arena of domestic political power.

William Pitt the Younger's India Act of 1784 established a different compromise. A Board of Control, staffed through the British government, obtained supervisory authority over important civil, military, and political affairs, while the Court of Directors retained substantial responsibility for commercial management and appointments. The arrangement neither nationalized the Company nor left it politically autonomous. Instead it created what historians often call a system of dual control, in which the directors and ministers possessed related but distinct roles. Lines of authority remained complex, particularly when questions of diplomatic secrecy, military expenditure, and patronage overlapped.

The Board's establishment changed the political position of the Company's officials abroad. A decision made in Calcutta might be judged not only by directors looking at profits and accounts but also by ministers concerned with wars, foreign relations, and Britain's international reputation. Instructions could still be delayed for months by distance. Ministers also depended upon corporate channels and reports to know what was happening. Greater metropolitan oversight therefore did not eliminate the discretion exercised by officers on the spot. It did create more occasions for correspondence, review, and political disagreement about whether an action was lawful, prudent, or financially sustainable.

Legislation passed in 1786 strengthened the governor-general's power in particular circumstances and enabled Cornwallis to combine the governor-generalship with the command of the Company's forces. These changes responded to difficulties experienced under an executive council whose majority could constrain its head. They also show that the British state did not adopt a single fixed constitutional formula in 1773 or 1784. Institutional reform continued as officials interpreted experience, administrative disputes, and changing strategic interests. The powers of the government in India were redefined within a framework that remained corporate even as it became more closely integrated with British imperial politics.

There was an enduring contradiction between the demand to prevent private enrichment and the system of patronage that sustained Company service. Directors and influential British politicians could still play roles in appointments, while service in India offered opportunities for advancement and wealth. Salary reform and restrictions on commercial activity sought to reduce incentives for abuse, but they also concentrated desirable positions within a smaller group of European officials. Regulatory change was thus accompanied by debate over who was entitled to rule and who could be trusted to interpret laws and manage revenue. Indian employees remained central to administration while being denied growing portions of senior formal authority.

Pitt's Act is best understood as a constitutional response to the Company's territorial transformation. A corporation whose profits relied on military and fiscal power could not easily be supervised as a merchant body alone. The British state sought control without assuming all administrative and commercial risks directly. Students should examine the arrangement as one stage in a longer process leading to direct Crown rule in 1858, not as its inevitable first draft. At the moment of enactment, alternative structures remained conceivable, and the question of political accountability continued to animate the impeachment of Hastings.

Section 63.12

Company Rule and British Political Debate: Corruption, Responsibility, and Warren Hastings's Impeachment

The Company's territorial rise unsettled British political expectations about commerce, government, and corruption. Rich returned servants, commonly caricatured as 'nabobs', appeared to critics as men whose fortunes had been accumulated through coercive power abroad and could then distort political life at home. The accusation could be exaggerated or deployed against personal enemies, yet it raised a constitutional problem: how could Parliament supervise a chartered corporation that collected taxes, made treaties, commanded soldiers, and exercised justice? The Company's directors, metropolitan creditors, shareholders, ministers, and servants did not have identical interests. Debates about its conduct therefore combined concerns about Indian subjects with struggles over British fiscal and political authority.

Edmund Burke became the most prominent parliamentary critic of Warren Hastings. The charges against Hastings involved, among other matters, the treatment of regional rulers, financial dealings, and the exercise of coercive authority. Burke argued that political power over distant peoples was a trust, not a license for arbitrary enrichment. His speeches insisted that Indian customs and legal obligations mattered and that an official could be judged by principles of justice even when serving an expanding empire. But Burke was a prosecutor making a case: his allegations must be evaluated alongside defense evidence, the documents he cited, and the broader politics of parliamentary rivalry.

The impeachment trial began in Westminster Hall in 1788, years after Hastings had left India, and ended with his acquittal in 1795. Its duration reflected the extraordinary complexity of investigating events separated by distance, language, and contested political circumstances. The trial was not simply a referendum on whether every aspect of Company administration was corrupt. Different charges concerned different actions; procedural decisions and the weight accorded to evidence mattered. Defenders argued that Hastings had governed amid war, financial pressure, and uncertainty, and that metropolitan critics underestimated the difficulties of administration. An acquittal did not erase the significance of the public debate, just as the accusations alone did not establish legal guilt.

The proceedings also exposed the limits of the voices considered authoritative. Senior Company officials, British politicians, and elite Indian witnesses appeared in speeches and documents; peasants, women, laborers, and many subordinate workers seldom spoke directly to Parliament. The British public could learn that the Company governed an enormous and complex society while receiving most of its evidence through institutions associated with conquest and administration. Translation, selection of documents, and the reputations of individual witnesses affected what became credible. The rhetoric of protecting India could itself become a means of justifying the continued authority of British institutions over Indians.

Historians have consequently offered competing readings of the impeachment. Some emphasize Burke's development of an argument that empire must remain answerable to law and moral responsibility. Others study the proceedings as a conflict within British elite politics over control of Indian patronage and policy, or as an episode in the formation of a new imperial political culture. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive. An argument motivated partly by domestic factionalism can still reveal serious abuses, and an appeal to universal principles can coexist with assumptions that exclude colonized people from political equality.

The 1788 opening speech is especially useful as a historical source because it states Burke's theory of accusation before the evidence on individual charges was formally presented. Students must distinguish its claims from findings by the House of Lords. The surviving published text can be checked in editions of Burke's works, including the first day's speech dated 15 February 1788. In combination with the Regulating Act and Pitt's India Act, the impeachment shows that the transformation of Company rule created sustained debate in Britain over who might use imperial power, what restraints should apply, and whose interests the resulting institutions served.

Section 63.13

Lord Cornwallis and the Reorganization of Company Government, 1786–1793

Charles Cornwallis arrived in India in 1786 as governor-general and commander-in-chief at a moment when British leaders increasingly associated stable Company government with disciplined administration. The authority of the governor-general had been strengthened through recent legislation, and London expected improved control over servants whose private interests had sometimes conflicted with corporate obligations. Cornwallis did not invent the project of reform; Hastings and other officials had experimented with many of the same institutions. What distinguished Cornwallis's administration was the effort to consolidate procedures, define responsibilities, raise standards of official conduct, and create more systematic limits on how senior Company servants could make money.

Higher salaries for senior civil officers formed part of this strategy. Company servants had long been poorly paid relative to some of the commercial opportunities available through personal trade, commissions, and political gifts. Cornwallis believed that adequate official remuneration, coupled with strict prohibitions and disciplinary rules, could make public service less dependent on private gain. The reform addressed a genuine conflict of interest, but the underlying assumption was selective: Europeans in trusted senior positions were expected to govern, while Indians were increasingly restricted to subordinate grades regardless of their administrative expertise. Professionalization and racial exclusion developed together, not as unrelated features of the system.

Cornwallis's measures also attempted to separate roles that earlier officers had combined. A man who assessed or collected revenue should not necessarily adjudicate disputes over those same assessments; a judge required protections from pressures arising out of district finance. Central supervision depended on accurate accounting and reports, while judicial procedure required distinct records. These principles would be elaborated through the 1793 regulations. Their application was complicated by the fact that Company offices remained expensive to staff, and trained Europeans were few. Indian record-keepers, court officers, and language specialists therefore continued to perform essential work even when excluded from prestigious senior appointments.

The changing status of Indian employees deserves particular attention. Under Mughal and regional governments, revenue offices had relied on hereditary and trained specialists in Persian accounting, local custom, and agrarian rights. The Company inherited those skills and could not govern without them. Cornwallis's policies nevertheless increased the prominence of European officials in final decisions, reflecting a combination of distrust, institutional self-interest, and developing racial ideology. A clerk might draft a document whose interpretation affected a district's revenue while having little prospect of becoming the officer who signed the final order. This was an important shift in the distribution of public authority.

Not all reforms could be judged from the text of regulations alone. High salaries required budgetary resources; prohibitions on private commerce required enforcement; appellate review required records and communication. Political patronage survived in Britain, and local dependence on powerful landed interests remained entrenched. An officer might comply formally with instructions while relying on agents who had their own financial interests. The historian should compare regulations with complaints, disciplinary proceedings, and district correspondence instead of accepting administrative promises as evidence of results. The gap between a rule and its execution is part of the history of colonial bureaucracy.

Cornwallis's years in office also included major military and diplomatic affairs, but the wars against Mysore belong to Chapter 64. Here the focus is the development of a state that could make recurring demands on land, adjudicate disputes, and support a professionalized upper administration. The reforms helped standardize Company government, but they also located formal authority more decisively in British hands while preserving dependence on Indian labor and knowledge. That pattern is central to evaluating whether the new institutions created accountable government, strengthened extraction, or did both for different groups.

Section 63.14

Land-Revenue Policy before 1793: The Debate over Assessment, Property, and Improvement

The Permanent Settlement emerged from a decade of debate over what the Company should know about land and whom it should recognize as possessing rights over it. Earlier temporary settlements had exposed unreliable assessments, contested records, defaults, and repeated demands for administrative revision. Company officers asked whether government revenue should be calculated afresh at regular intervals or fixed for a longer term. They also disputed whether zamindars were proprietors of the soil, officials collecting revenue, hereditary chiefs with multiple customary privileges, or a mixture varying by region. None of these terms mapped perfectly onto every existing relationship. Decisions about definitions therefore redistributed authority as well as recorded it.

Cornwallis placed considerable faith in secure property rights as an incentive to agricultural improvement. Some British political economists argued that landholders who could keep the benefit of increased cultivation would invest in irrigation, drainage, reclamation, and settlement. John Shore, a revenue specialist with substantial experience in Bengal, emphasized the need for better knowledge of districts and questioned aspects of making a permanent assessment before reliable information was available. The disagreement was not simply between men favoring prosperity and men favoring extraction. They differed in their estimates of what existing records proved, how strong zamindari rights were, and how a predictable government demand might alter incentives.

The shift to a decennial settlement was incremental and legally precise. Revenue regulations and decisions issued in 1789 and 1790 provided for assessments intended to last ten years, with the possibility of perpetuity subject to the Court of Directors' approval. Officials negotiated with landholders and attempted to establish what each estate would owe. The duration mattered because it reduced short-term uncertainty, but the conditional promise of future permanence remained different from a binding declaration that the government would never increase the assessed sum. Students should not compress these stages into a single reform dated 1793. The sequence demonstrates that legislation followed contested administrative judgment and metropolitan authorization.

One argument for permanence was fiscal rather than humanitarian. A predictable fixed payment could simplify the treasury's calculations, reduce the costs of constant reassessment, and place responsibility for collecting rents and expanding cultivation on landholders. By transferring some risks of agricultural variation to zamindars, the government hoped to secure reliable receipts. But a fixed public assessment did not fix the private rent charged to every tenant. Nor did it guarantee that a proprietor would invest profits in productive improvements. A landlord could increase income by raising demands on cultivators, acquiring adjoining rights, tightening collection, or restricting access to common resources as readily as by constructing irrigation works.

The debate depended on existing Indian actors as much as European theories. Zamindars pressed claims grounded in inheritance, office, grants, custom, and military-political power. Talukdars and subordinate right-holders might dispute a zamindar's assertion that he alone represented an estate. Village communities and raiyats possessed varying claims to occupancy and use, which a simple title issued to a large proprietor could obscure. Indian accountants and qanungos supplied information needed for settlement, yet their testimony passed through the Company's categories and administrative priorities. Colonial law often converted ambiguous or overlapping practices into written hierarchies whose later consequences could not be fully anticipated.

The eventual permanence decision must therefore be placed within both an intellectual argument about property and a practical struggle for administrative convenience. The Company sought an enduring settlement with the intermediaries it could identify and hold financially responsible. Cornwallis and Shore disagreed in part over timing and evidence, but neither proposed that every cultivator should participate on equal terms in determining assessments. The question for the next section is what the 1793 proclamation legally fixed—and what it left open to conflict in Bengal's countryside. The distinction between permanent government demand and variable agrarian relations is fundamental to assessing the policy's social consequences.

Section 63.15

The Permanent Settlement of 1793: Land Revenue, Zamindars, and Colonial Property

On 22 March 1793 the governor-general in council announced that assessments under the earlier decennial settlement would be fixed in perpetuity, pursuant to the necessary authorization from the Court of Directors. The principles were embodied in Regulation I of 1793. This was the landmark of the Permanent Settlement, which applied to specified territories of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha under Company revenue government, not to every territory in India. The proclamation should be read as a legal intervention in an existing fiscal system. It did not create land cultivation or zamindari authority from nothing, but it altered the legal terms on which government recognized estate-holders and expected payments.

The policy fixed the government's demand, or jama, on estates according to their assessment. Landholders who entered settlements were promised that, subject to the obligations imposed by law, the government would not later raise that demand simply because agricultural output or rental income increased. Zamindars, independent talukdars, and other recognized proprietors became the principal persons responsible for remittance. The distinction between government revenue and private rent is essential: the Company fixed what an estate owed the treasury, not a permanent ceiling on what its proprietor might charge cultivating tenants. The difference created incentives and conflict that later generations would confront through a succession of tenancy laws and lawsuits.

Supporters expected multiple benefits. They hoped a secure claim to the future fruits of improvement would encourage investments in land, attract capital, and make landed families more predictable partners of government. A fixed assessment could reduce the temptation for revenue officers to demand new payments whenever cultivation expanded. From the treasury's perspective, simplifying assessments promised more reliable accounts and lower administrative expense. But those anticipated gains were propositions about behavior, not results established by the act of proclamation. Investment required access to credit, practical knowledge, security against other claimants, and confidence that improvements would pay; legal title alone could not ensure them.

The settlement also strengthened the government's capacity to enforce payment. Default could expose an estate to sale for arrears, potentially displacing established families and opening opportunities for buyers with liquid capital. It is important not to project every later enforcement rule backward into the original proclamation. Procedures for sale and the particularly stringent 'sunset' rules associated with subsequent regulations developed over time, notably in 1799. The general risk of sale and loss of property was nevertheless integral to a system in which government expected fixed payments regardless of fluctuations in harvest income. Security for the proprietor and stringent public collection were two sides of the same settlement.

The legal status of raiyats remained complex. The proclamation's formal concern with recognized estate proprietors did not extinguish every cultivator's customary claim or automatically establish uniform rent arrangements. In practice, bargaining power varied sharply between substantial tenants and land-poor families. The regulations contained assurances about protecting subordinate rights, but translating such assurances into affordable legal remedies was difficult. Local officers, landlords, village elites, and courts interpreted rights through inherited custom and new paperwork, and the outcome could vary across regions. The language of a coherent property system was therefore simpler than the social relationships it sought to regulate.

Modern reproductions of Regulation I require careful source criticism. Some accessible online texts incorporate later amendments, bracketed geographic terminology, or confusing metadata about the year of enactment. The chapter's source window identifies the proclamation dated 22 March 1793 and treats later consolidated texts as witnesses requiring comparison with the contemporary form. Students should examine the stated conditions of permanence and ask which promises bound the government, which obligations fell on proprietors, and whose interests were largely outside the central agreement. The Permanent Settlement was a durable constitutional arrangement for revenue; its historical effects depended on land markets, finance, tenancy, and enforcement.

The documentary history of the settlement also shows how authority was assembled across institutions. The governor-general and council proclaimed the final policy, but the directors’ earlier consent made an unconditional promise possible. Existing district revenue officials and Indian accountants then had to identify estates, record assessments, and address challenges to claims. The legal declaration thus depended on administrative labor that had developed under Hastings and on local knowledge inherited from Mughal government. It also had fiscal implications for the future: whenever prices and production rose, the Company could not automatically raise the fixed assessment on settled estates, even as it remained responsible for financing armies and courts. New forms of revenue, territorial expansion, borrowing, and administrative economy would therefore matter to the continuing development of colonial finance. Permanence was a governing choice with consequences for what the state could and could not demand in later decades.

Section 63.16

The Social and Economic Consequences of Permanent Settlement

The Permanent Settlement affected different groups through unequal relationships to land, money, and legal authority. Some zamindars benefited from the security of a fixed government assessment when rents and cultivation expanded. Others faced difficulty making scheduled payments during poor harvests, disputes, or temporary declines in revenue. Estates could pass to new holders through sale for arrears, mortgage, inheritance, or market transaction. New purchasers included men whose resources came from urban commerce, finance, or official service; they did not necessarily possess the same local obligations or relationships as older landed families. The resulting changes were neither immediate everywhere nor socially uniform.

For cultivators, the most direct question was not whether the state had fixed the jama, but what rent and labor demands they faced from the person controlling their holdings. A proprietor whose liability to government remained unchanged could attempt to enlarge income by increasing rents, collecting additional dues, enforcing arrears, or clearing land for new cultivation. At the same time, local practices and tenant rights could restrict what a landlord could achieve. The category raiyat covered varied circumstances: some families possessed relatively durable occupancy claims, while others cultivated less secure plots or depended on seasonal arrangements. Historians should not assume that permanence made all farmers landless at once.

The settlement also transformed relationships among layers of intermediaries. Independent talukdars, under-tenure holders, village leaders, moneylenders, and revenue agents negotiated rights and responsibilities within the new framework. A large zamindar recognized by government might assert greater authority over subordinate claimants than they accepted. Disputes that had once been negotiated through administrative relationships or local patronage increasingly appeared in written deeds, estate accounts, and court proceedings. These documents are valuable to historians but do not by themselves establish that litigants had equal opportunity to defend a claim. Financial resources and access to clerks, lawyers, and courts shaped whose interpretation prevailed.

Long-term agrarian improvement was less automatic than proponents supposed. Some landholders invested in embankments, clearing, irrigation, or settlement expansion; others directed surpluses toward consumption, debt repayment, urban purchases, or family obligations. A fixed government demand could encourage improvement where property rights and credit were secure, but it could also produce speculative transfers and intensify rent collection. The Company's own priorities continued to privilege predictable fiscal receipts over systematic measurement of household welfare. A favorable trend in government revenue security was not evidence of rising wages or improved nutritional conditions among cultivating families.

The social geography mattered. Landholding systems and customary practices differed among Bengal's deltaic regions, Bihar's interior districts, and the Odisha territories included in Company claims. Agricultural environments varied from wet rice cultivation to more rainfall-sensitive production, and the place of zamindars differed accordingly. Later systems associated with ryotwari and mahalwari settlements in other parts of India should not be read back into the 1793 policy. The emergence of alternative arrangements makes clear that the Permanent Settlement was a historically situated choice rather than the only available model for colonial land revenue.

A balanced evaluation must separate intentions, instruments, and outcomes. The law aimed to make revenue predictable, define rights, and encourage investment; the instruments included fixed estate assessments and the threat of enforced sale; the outcomes depended on social hierarchy, land markets, credit, and litigation. Some landholders gained durable security; others lost estates; tenants experienced both continued customary protections and new pressures. Contemporary official praise and later nationalist criticism illuminate different aspects but should not substitute for district-level evidence. The accompanying multiple-perspectives feature asks students to consider the same settlement from the positions of a proprietor, a tenant, a creditor, and a Company collector.

Section 63.17

The Cornwallis Code, Judicial Reform, and the Reorganization of Company Courts, 1793

The Cornwallis Code of 1793 consolidated a broad set of regulations concerned with revenue, civil justice, and the behavior of Company officials. Its judicial provisions built on two decades of Hastings-era experiment and jurisdictional conflict. Regulation II articulated an intention to separate the authority of officials who collected public revenue from that of judges who decided civil disputes. Earlier district collectors had exercised substantial judicial responsibilities, creating the possibility that the treasury's interests would influence decisions about property and debt. Separation was meant to make adjudication more regular and less dependent on the immediate demands of revenue administration. It also expressed a British political ideal of government through specified offices and published rules.

District and appellate civil courts were organized in a hierarchy, with European officers occupying important judicial posts and Indian legal experts continuing to supply advice, interpretation, and procedural labor. The Company's criminal justice system underwent related changes, including a growing role for Company-controlled courts and supervision of existing Indo-Islamic legal personnel. The precise authority and location of institutions changed across the period; historians must avoid representing every court created between 1772 and 1793 as part of a single timeless system. The crucial shift was the Company's increasing power to appoint, inspect, and overrule the people who administered justice.

Written rules provided some possible safeguards. An aggrieved party could theoretically challenge a decision by identifying the appropriate jurisdiction, filing a claim, and seeking review. Officials were expected to document reasons, maintain registers, and follow recognized procedures. These devices could constrain arbitrary orders and produce evidence of abuses. Yet their practical value depended on cost, access to records, interpreters, and the willingness of officials to enforce decisions against powerful litigants. A distant appellate court offered less protection to a poor cultivator who could not travel or sustain prolonged proceedings than to a well-resourced estate-holder who could hire legal agents.

The new judicial organization also embodied inequality. The Company's highest offices were increasingly monopolized by Europeans, while Indian qazis, muftis, pandits, clerks, and petition writers performed much of the specialized work beneath them. British judges relied on translated explanations of law, but the power to decide and the prestige of office were distributed unequally. The codification of procedure could make local customary claims more dependent on written proof than they had been in earlier negotiations. Official claims of impartiality therefore existed alongside institutional distinctions of race, office, and wealth.

Cornwallis's reforms should not be confused with the complete introduction of English law throughout Bengal. The Company continued to draw on Indian legal traditions, differentiated legal categories, and evolving territorial regulations. The royal Supreme Court at Calcutta remained a distinct institution with a different basis of authority. Each court system had its own history of jurisdictional controversy and efforts at clarification. Students examining a surviving record must first identify the kind of court involved and the law it was expected to apply before drawing conclusions about 'colonial justice' in general.

The long-term significance was the emergence of a larger written administrative framework through which the Company sought predictable decisions and clearer supervision. Its success cannot be measured simply by the number of regulations issued. The continuity-and-change feature compares inherited Indian legal personnel and procedures with the growing concentration of decision-making authority in British hands. The historian's responsibility is to assess whether a reform described as separating powers actually improved the position of particular litigants, officials, and communities.

Section 63.18

Civil Administration, Recordkeeping, and the Expansion of Bureaucratic Authority

A territorial state requires more than laws announcing offices. It must know whom to tax, where a boundary lies, what property has been pledged, how soldiers will be paid, and which disputes are still unresolved. Between the 1770s and 1790s the Company expanded the administrative machinery that produced and circulated this information. District offices kept ledgers, forwarded reports, examined petitions, and compiled correspondence. Provincial and central boards reviewed arrears, settlements, and disciplinary questions. The expansion of paperwork was not ornamental: it allowed officials separated by long distances to compare claims and supervise subordinates, and it enabled Calcutta to present itself as the authoritative center of government.

Indian clerks, munshis, qanungos, accountants, interpreters, petition writers, and legal experts sustained much of this system. Their technical knowledge included Persian administrative vocabulary, local landholding history, established rates of assessment, and the practices by which merchants and landed families transferred money. A British collector without such assistance could not reliably identify a boundary, examine a dispute over hereditary rights, or understand an old revenue register. The growing number of European supervisory posts did not imply the disappearance of Indian expertise. It often meant that a more stratified bureaucracy placed the expertise of Indian employees under the signatures and orders of British officers.

Records both revealed and transformed the countryside. An estate register could clarify an assessment but also turn a complex claim of customary authority into a category of ownership recognized by the Company. A survey might render fields comparable for purposes of taxation yet obscure seasonal rights in pasture, water, or uncultivated land. Translating local practice into a standard form made disputes easier for headquarters to review, but could favor parties whose relationships fitted the official categories. The apparent neutrality of forms deserves scrutiny. Documents were created for political and fiscal purposes, and their omissions were often as consequential as the figures they recorded.

The administrative network linked Calcutta to district headquarters and market towns through messengers, rivers, roads, and official correspondence. Time delays made local discretion unavoidable. Some collectors were praised for accuracy or efficiency; others were accused of excessive demands, favoritism, or failing to understand conditions. The Company tried to manage these risks through instructions and reporting requirements, but every new report introduced opportunities for error or manipulation. A summary prepared for London could compress conflicting district experiences into a single numerical statement, while the original petitions behind it might be lost or remain unread.

These bureaucratic changes also reconfigured employment and authority. A family with traditions of Mughal revenue service might adapt to Company recordkeeping; another might lose influence when its customary office ceased to carry formal recognition. English literacy and familiarity with new procedures gradually created advantages for certain groups, although Persian and regional languages remained central during much of the period. Administrative institutions were therefore sites of cultural and social negotiation. European officials claimed command, Indian employees translated those claims into local practice, and subjects learned to petition or resist through newly formalized channels.

The geography study accompanying this section asks students to trace the offices and routes that linked Calcutta with Murshidabad, Patna, and eastern Bengal. Its purpose is not to draw a modern boundary around a settled colonial state but to show that governmental reach varied with transport, local intermediaries, and military security. The Company's expanding documentary capacity made it a more formidable ruler without overcoming every practical limit on its knowledge. That contrast between written comprehensiveness and uneven control is a recurring feature of the colonial state whose later history occupies much of Volume IV.

Section 63.19

Military-Fiscal Government: Revenue, the Sepoy Army, and the Costs of Colonial Power

The Company's government in Bengal was inseparable from the army it maintained. The sepoy establishment, composed predominantly of Indian soldiers serving under European officers, required regular pay, equipment, food, animals, transport, barracks, and medical provision. Fortifications, artillery stores, military administration, and supplies cost money whether troops were fighting or waiting. Revenue from Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha offered the Company a financial foundation that purely commercial investment could not reliably provide. The Company's ability to claim revenue therefore increased its capacity to recruit and deploy forces. This relationship is central to understanding how a corporation acquired attributes of a territorial power.

The connection worked in both directions. An army could enforce political arrangements and protect district administrators; military success could secure additional revenues or tribute; expanding commitments could require larger garrisons and new borrowing. High costs sometimes swallowed gains that looked impressive in accounts of territorial income. One scholarly study of Company finance notes that Bengal's military expenditure rose sharply between the mid-1760s and 1770–1771 while other governmental expenses also climbed. The Company was not simply taking a fixed sum from a passive province. Its fiscal and military institutions created mutually reinforcing demands and risks, particularly when officials faced the prospect of wars beyond Bengal.

Indian participation in this military system was substantial. Sepoys brought regional recruitment networks, skills, religious and social affiliations, and expectations about pay and treatment. Their officers, suppliers, transport contractors, bankers, and market traders sustained the army's daily operations. A military unit could not march without grain, animals, credit, and roads, and these resources were often organized through relationships with Indian elites and commercial specialists. The hierarchy of command nevertheless placed strategic decisions in Company hands, and differences between British and Indian ranks became more pronounced. The force was Indian in much of its composition but colonial in the political objectives it served.

The Company's fiscal administration supported war by assessing land, compelling remittances, negotiating loans, and channeling funds to treasuries. The same network might be asked to finance commercial purchases for export and make payments to creditors in London. The distinction between corporate commercial profit and territorial revenue was important in principle, yet the movement of funds could connect them. Private remittances by Company servants added another layer of financial activity. Parliamentary oversight after 1773 and 1784 therefore became entangled with questions about whether directors and ministers could control military commitments whose financial effects reached the Company's shareholders and the British state.

The military-fiscal state was not unique in world history. Contemporary South Asian regional governments also relied on the interaction between taxation, credit, and armies, as Chapters 58–60 showed. The Company differed in its corporate organization, growing metropolitan supervision, and ability to connect regional revenues with global credit and shipping networks. Its officers did not establish an entirely new principle that power required money; they developed a particular institutional mechanism for gathering and directing it. Comparison avoids the false idea that military and fiscal organization were European inventions introduced to an otherwise politically unstructured subcontinent.

This section must stop short of narrating the campaigns assigned to Chapter 64. Its question is how the early Company state financed and managed coercive capacity. The resulting authority helped make larger interventions possible, but those interventions depended on political alliances, resistance, logistical constraints, and strategic choices. The second historical debate asks students to consider whether administrative regularity and military-financial capacity constituted improvement in government, an intensified mechanism of extraction, or a combination whose beneficiaries and costs varied by social position.

Section 63.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

Between the late 1760s and the early 1790s, the East India Company's political position in Bengal changed through a series of crises and decisions rather than one complete administrative design. The famine of 1770 exposed weaknesses in food security, information, relief, and the responsibility of a revenue-seeking corporation. Financial pressure and controversy encouraged direct administration from 1772, when Hastings began implementing the instruction to 'stand forth as diwan'. The Company no longer depended chiefly on a small group of deputies to exercise its revenue rights. It increasingly sought to know, regulate, and supervise the countryside through appointed officers, settlements, and written accounts.

The ensuing reforms were experimental. Five-year revenue arrangements promised greater returns but exposed uncertainties in official knowledge and pressures on cultivators. Hastings's judicial plan organized new adalat institutions that relied on Indian legal specialists while extending Company oversight. The Regulating Act created the governor-generalship and a royal court; later jurisdictional disputes demonstrated that statutory reform was not equivalent to a coherent legal system. Pitt's India Act created additional oversight from Britain without abolishing the Company. The early territorial state was therefore layered, contested, and deeply dependent on administrators whose authority came from different institutions.

Cornwallis's program sought more systematic rules for officials, courts, and landed property. The Permanent Settlement fixed government demands on recognized estates rather than fixing every tenant's rent, and the Cornwallis Code reorganized judicial functions. These changes could reduce certain kinds of uncertainty while strengthening the power of major landholders and senior European officials. Indian intermediaries remained essential even as racial hierarchy became a more formal feature of the upper administration. The question was not whether colonial rule introduced a modern administrative apparatus from nowhere, but how it altered existing institutions and redistributed the ability to make binding decisions.

The Company's claims of good government must be distinguished from the experiences of those subject to it. Fiscal predictability for directors was not the same as security for tenants; a new avenue of appeal could coexist with fees and delays that effectively excluded poor litigants. Army finance made territorial government more formidable but also gave it recurring incentives to raise revenue. The famine showed the dangers of a system capable of collecting taxes without coordinating sufficient relief; later reforms sought to address some administrative failings while preserving the underlying importance of revenue and military power.

Historians continue to debate the balance between reform and extraction. Scholarship by P. J. Marshall, C. A. Bayly, Robert Travers, Radhika Singha, John R. McLane, and more recent famine historians examines different institutions, periods, and evidence; it should not be reduced to the claim that one group celebrates British modernization while another simply condemns it. Careful analysis asks whether a measure improved a specific procedure, who obtained access to that improvement, and whose interests the resulting system served. It also asks what contemporary sources permit us to know about people who left few formal records.

The chapter's governing conclusion is that the Company state was built by converting territorial revenue into administrative and military capacity through cooperation, coercion, experimentation, and legislation. Its foundations were neither purely imported from Britain nor merely inherited from Mughal government; they were assembled in the unequal circumstances of early colonial rule. Chapter 64 follows the military and diplomatic consequences beyond Bengal as Company armies confronted Mysore, Maratha powers, and other South Asian states. The transition is thus from the creation of governmental instruments to the larger struggles in which those instruments were used.

Chapter Summary

The early Company state emerged from the confrontation between broad fiscal power and uncertain administrative responsibility. Bengal’s famine of 1769–1770 revealed a rice economy exposed to environmental stress, uneven access to grain, inadequate relief and continuing revenue demands. Mortality cannot be established precisely from existing records. The disagreement among historians concerns the contribution of different causal mechanisms, not whether the disaster caused exceptional human suffering.

Hastings implemented the decision to exercise Company fiscal authority more directly after 1771–1772. Revenue farmers, collectors, Indian accountants and district officials participated in experiments whose goals included accurate information and dependable receipts. These arrangements were revised repeatedly as practical and political problems emerged.

Judicial innovations likewise developed in stages. Company adalats relied on Indian legal specialists within a new supervisory system; the royal Supreme Court created in 1774 exercised distinct powers that came into conflict with revenue administration. Jurisdictional changes in 1781 sought to resolve some disputes. The different institutions should never be combined into one undifferentiated colonial judiciary.

Parliament increasingly intervened in the Company’s affairs through the Regulating Act of 1773, the 1784 India Act and subsequent adjustments. Directors and ministers shared overlapping responsibilities while corporate administration continued. Burke’s prosecution of Hastings made political responsibility and the treatment of Indian people subjects of sustained public argument, without producing a simple judgment on every aspect of Company conduct.

Cornwallis sought to improve official discipline, reduce personal trading conflicts, and strengthen a higher service dominated by Europeans. The decennial settlements preceded the proclamation of permanence in 1793. The Permanent Settlement fixed the public assessment owed by recognized estates but did not fix every tenant’s rent; its consequences depended upon land markets, customary rights, local power and enforcement.

The Company used increasingly elaborate records, courts, district offices and Indian employees to convert claims of authority into daily government. Bengal revenues also sustained the sepoy army, while military costs encouraged continued fiscal demands. The resulting state combined institutional innovation with dependence on inherited Indian knowledge and unequal relations of colonial power. Chapter 64 follows its expansion through major regional wars.

Chapter Reflection

The Company’s early territorial government was fashioned from a contradiction: fiscal resources acquired through conquest had outgrown institutions designed to manage commerce. Famine, fiscal crisis, and debate over corporate responsibility stimulated reform, while the Company’s need for revenue and coercive capacity shaped the reforms themselves.

Hastings’s experiments, parliamentary supervision, Cornwallis’s regulations and the Permanent Settlement made authority more legible and enforceable, not necessarily more equitable. Indian personnel, producers, landlords and litigants continued to determine how policy worked in practice. Chapter 64 examines the regional military and diplomatic struggles that this growing state was able to pursue.
