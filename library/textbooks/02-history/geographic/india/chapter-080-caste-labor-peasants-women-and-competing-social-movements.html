Part XIV — Nationalism and Mass Politics

Caste, Labor, Peasants, Women, and Competing Social Movements

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In March 1927, in the small Konkan town of Mahad, a conference of several thousand delegates from the Kolaba district and beyond, presided over by the Bombay lawyer and scholar B. R. Ambedkar, ended with a short walk to a public water tank. The tank, known as the Chavdar tank, was municipal property that the local council had resolved in 1924 to make accessible to residents subjected to untouchability. In practice, people classed as untouchable had never been allowed to draw water from it. On 20 March the delegates walked to the tank and drank. What looked like a simple act of drinking water was, in the political language of the time, a claim to equal membership in the public.

The Mahad Satyagraha is a useful place to begin Chapter 80 because it shows how crowded and contested the political landscape of the 1920s had become. Chapter 79 traced the transformation of Indian nationalism under Gandhi, from the Rowlatt agitation and the Khilafat campaign through Non-Cooperation and its aftermath. That story is central to the history of the period. It is not the whole story. In the same years, tenants in Awadh formed associations to resist landlords, mill workers in Bombay struck for wages and dignity, a Brahmin-dominated public sphere was challenged by non-Brahmin organizations in Madras, and women formed associations to demand education, a vote, and changes in the law of marriage. Some of these movements overlapped with Congress campaigns. Many did not, and some openly criticized the party.

The chapter's central question is how these movements redefined the meaning of freedom in colonial South Asia. For many Congress leaders, freedom meant, first and above all, the end of British political control. For the peasant who paid unauthorized dues to a landlord, the worker who lived in a single room near the mill, the woman excluded from the franchise by property qualifications, or the Mahar who was refused a drink of water, foreign rule was one form of domination among several. Freedom, for them, necessarily included land, wages, schooling, access to public space, representation, and legal standing. These were demands for transformation within Indian society as well as against the colonial state.

This chapter treats these movements as independent historical developments rather than as appendices to Gandhi's career. They had their own leaders, such as Baba Ramchandra, Madari Pasi, Anasuya Sarabhai, N. M. Joshi, S. A. Dange, Periyar (E. V. Ramasamy), Ambedkar, Muthulakshmi Reddi, and Margaret Cousins. They had their own institutions, including kisan sabhas, trade unions, newspapers, schools, women's associations, and political parties. And they had their own intellectual foundations, which drew on earlier reformers such as Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule, on Marxist and socialist literature, on liberal and rationalist thought, and on the experience of daily life under hierarchies that colonial rule had often hardened rather than dissolved.

The chapter is divided into five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines peasant mobilization, from the Kisan Sabhas and the Eka movement in the United Provinces to the Bardoli satyagraha of 1928. Division II turns to industrial labor, the formation of the All India Trade Union Congress in 1920, the Bombay textile strikes, and the beginnings of communist organization. Division III follows non-Brahmin politics from the Justice Party to the Vaikom Satyagraha and Periyar's Self-Respect Movement. Division IV centers on Ambedkar and on other Dalit movements in different regions. Division V examines women's organizations, and then draws the strands together in a synthesis of the competing meanings of freedom.

The sources for these movements are uneven, and the chapter says so as it proceeds. Peasant voices usually reach historians through police reports, official inquiries, and the writings of sympathetic organizers; they are rarely recorded in their own words. Labor history relies on strike reports, company records, trade union publications, and commission evidence. Non-Brahmin and Dalit movements produced newspapers, pamphlets, conference resolutions, and autobiographical writing that are indispensable but often partisan. Women's organizations left journals, conference proceedings, and legislative debates. Each type of source preserves some voices and silences others, and a recurring task for the reader is to ask who is speaking, to whom, and with what purpose.

The principal period of the chapter is 1916 to 1929, with background reaching back to the late nineteenth century. The chapter stops short of the Civil Disobedience campaign, the Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award, and the Poona Pact, which belong to Chapter 81. Those developments grew directly from the movements examined here: once Ambedkar, the Justice Party, women's organizations, and others pressed for separate or special representation, the constitutional future of India became a contest among many claimants. By the end of Chapter 80, students should understand why national independence, social equality, economic justice, and democratic representation could reinforce one another without ever being the same political project.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how colonial land-revenue systems, tenancy relations, and indebtedness produced agrarian grievances that differed from the constitutional objectives of many Congress leaders.
  2. Distinguish among landlords, substantial cultivators, tenants, sharecroppers, and landless laborers, and explain why each group had different interests in agrarian politics.
  3. Compare the Awadh Kisan Sabha, the Eka movement, Kheda, and Bardoli in terms of class composition, organization, tactics, and outcomes.
  4. Describe the working and living conditions of industrial laborers and explain why workers developed demands that self-government alone could not meet.
  5. Trace the development of trade unionism from the strike wave of 1918–1920 through the AITUC, the Trade Unions Act of 1926, and the Bombay textile strike of 1928.
  6. Distinguish communism from the broader range of socialist and labor politics, and explain why the founding date of the Communist Party of India is itself a matter of historical disagreement.
  7. Analyze the origins of non-Brahmin politics in Madras and Maharashtra and distinguish elite non-Brahmin leadership from the aspirations of communities subjected to untouchability.
  8. Evaluate the Vaikom Satyagraha and the Self-Respect Movement as challenges to caste hierarchy, including their relationships with Gandhi, Congress, and the Justice Party.
  9. Interpret B. R. Ambedkar's early intellectual development and organizational work, and explain the significance of the Mahad Satyagraha and the burning of the Manusmriti.
  10. Assess regional Dalit movements, including Adi-Hindu, Ad Dharm, and Adi-Dravida organizations, without treating Ambedkar as the sole representative of oppressed-caste politics.
  11. Examine the growth of women's organizations and distinguish women's participation in nationalist campaigns from campaigns for women's rights under women's own leadership, including the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929.
  12. Evaluate whether a movement could oppose colonial rule while preserving inequalities of caste, gender, or economic power, using evidence from several movements.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did peasants, workers, women, and oppressed-caste communities so often define their grievances differently from the leaders of the national movement?
  2. How did the structure of agrarian society shape which peasants organized, which leaders emerged, and which demands were pressed?
  3. In what ways did industrial labor politics differ from peasant politics, and why did workers' organizations raise issues beyond the question of foreign rule?
  4. How should historians understand the relationship between social movements and Congress: as alliance, rivalry, absorption, or something more varied?
  5. What distinguished non-Brahmin politics, which often sought a share of institutional power, from movements of communities subjected to untouchability, which challenged exclusion from basic public goods?
  6. Why did access to water, roads, schools, and employment become such powerful political issues in the 1920s?
  7. Why did Ambedkar and other Dalit leaders conclude that independent organization was necessary?
  8. How did women's organizations navigate the boundary between nationalist politics and the pursuit of women's own legal and social rights?
  9. What does the history of these movements reveal about the limits of legal reform in changing everyday social practice?
  10. Can a political movement against colonialism be called emancipatory if it leaves other hierarchies intact?

Historical Context

Several developments from earlier chapters form the background to the movements of this chapter. Chapters 66 and 72 explained how colonial land systems (the Permanent Settlement, ryotwari, and mahalwari arrangements) redefined rights in land, tied cultivators to cash revenue demands, and expanded commercial crops and moneylending. Chapter 73 showed how census operations, ethnography, and administrative practice classified caste and shaped political identities. Chapter 74 examined nineteenth-century reform movements, including the Satyashodhak Samaj of the Phules, the Arya Samaj, and the Sikh reform movements. Chapter 75 described the growth of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, and of industrial labor in mills, mines, and plantations.

The First World War (Chapter 78) intensified these pressures. Wartime inflation raised the cost of food and cloth, while wages and agricultural incomes did not always keep pace. Recruitment, taxation, and the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 all fell heavily on rural and urban poor. At the same time, the war expanded Indian industry, especially cotton and jute, and created new concentrations of workers. The constitutional reforms associated with the Government of India Act of 1919 introduced "dyarchy" in the provinces, with limited elected legislatures and a franchise defined by property and education. This franchise determined who could vote, who could stand for office, and therefore which groups had formal political leverage. Most peasants, workers, and women were excluded or only partly included, which made extra-parliamentary organization attractive.

Chapter 79 showed how Gandhi and Congress drew masses of people into politics from 1919 to 1922. That mobilization had an important unintended effect. By inviting peasants, workers, and previously excluded groups into public life, it encouraged them to articulate their own demands. Sometimes those demands were compatible with Congress strategy; sometimes they were not. Gandhi's insistence on non-violence, his reluctance to endorse class conflict, and his preference for social reform within Hindu society, which he understood as consistent with national unity, drew both admiration and criticism from the movements discussed below. Students should keep in mind that the interactions were not simple. Many Kisan Sabha leaders were also Congress members. Many trade unionists wanted nationalist support. Ambedkar, Periyar, and women reformers each had complex and evolving relationships with Gandhi.

The evidence base for the chapter includes government reports (such as district settlement reports, the Royal Commission on Labour of 1929–1931, and the proceedings of provincial councils), the proceedings of organizations, newspapers in English and regional languages, pamphlets, autobiographies, and later oral testimony. Historians must read official sources against the grain because officials often described peasant and labor protest as the work of "agitators," while organizers' own accounts sometimes overstated unity and success. Scholars working on subaltern and social history over the last half century have emphasized recovering the agency of groups whom earlier nationalist and colonial narratives described as passive or merely manipulated.

Peasant Mobilization and Agrarian Politics

How landholding, rent, debt, and revenue assessment produced rural movements with aims of their own.

Section 80.1

Agrarian Society, Land Rights, and the Causes of Peasant Mobilization

Most people in early twentieth-century South Asia lived by agriculture, but the phrase "the peasantry" conceals deep differences. Colonial land systems, examined in Chapters 63 and 66, had created different bundles of rights in different regions. In permanently settled Bengal and in much of Bihar and Awadh, revenue was collected through intermediaries, zamindars and taluqdars, who held rights over estates and extracted rent from tenants. In ryotwari regions such as Bombay and Madras, the state in principle dealt directly with the individual cultivator, who paid revenue assessed on his holding. In the mahalwari tracts of the north-west, revenue was assessed on villages or estates. These distinctions shaped the form of grievance.

Within any region, rural society was stratified. At the top were large landlords, such as the taluqdars of Awadh, whose power rested on landholding, local authority, and often close relations with district officials. Below them were substantial cultivators, who held land with relatively secure rights, hired labor, and sometimes lent money. Occupancy tenants had some legal protection against eviction and enhancement of rent, while tenants-at-will, sharecroppers, and sub-tenants had little. At the bottom were landless agricultural laborers, many of them from groups subject to caste disabilities, who were paid in grain, cash, or the promise of subsistence and often remained bound by debt. Women worked in fields, in harvesting and processing, and in households, but rarely held formal rights in land. Students should avoid treating "the peasant" as a single actor, because a prosperous cultivator and a landless laborer could experience the same village as very different places.

Several pressures made the decades around the First World War a time of agrarian tension. Population growth and the subdivision of holdings pushed rents upward and made tenancies more competitive. Commercial agriculture exposed cultivators to price fluctuations; cash revenue and rent demands meant that a poor harvest or a fall in prices could push a family into the hands of the moneylender. Debt could lead to the loss of land or the transformation of an owner-cultivator into a tenant. Famine and epidemic disease, including the influenza pandemic of 1918, added to insecurity. Wartime inflation raised the price of what peasants bought without necessarily raising what they sold.

In Awadh and other zamindari tracts, the specific mechanisms of exploitation were well known to tenants. Landlords demanded rent above the recorded rate, as well as additional levies on occasions such as marriages, festivals, and the purchase of a landlord's horse or elephant. The payment known as nazrana was demanded when a tenancy was granted or renewed. Tenants could be evicted without effective notice, a practice called bedakhli, and the threat of eviction was an instrument of control. Unpaid or forced labor, begar, was demanded for the landlord's household and fields. In many areas the landlord's agents also exercised informal judicial authority, collecting fines and imposing punishments. The tenant's remedy lay in courts that were costly and distant.

In ryotwari regions, the main grievance was the revenue assessment itself. Settlement officers periodically revised the assessments, and an upward revision could threaten the viability of small holdings. Cultivators could petition, refuse to pay, or organize collectively, but the government held the power to forfeit land for non-payment. Peasant politics there therefore centered on the legitimacy and fairness of assessment, and on the state's willingness to negotiate.

Why did agrarian grievances differ from the constitutional objectives of many Congress leaders? Congress politicians in the early 1920s were largely lawyers, journalists, and professionals, often with links to landed families. Their main goals concerned representation, self-government, and economic nationalism. Some were landlords themselves, or relied on landlords for financial and political support. Mobilizing tenants against landlords posed a dilemma, because Congress sought national unity and wished to avoid an open class struggle, while peasants wanted relief from rent and eviction. Gandhi's own approach favored mutual obligation between landlord and tenant and resisted violent confrontation. Kisan organizers thus often found themselves allies of Congress in a campaign against the government while also being in tension with Congress over the landlord question.

The result was a distinctive pattern. Peasant movements drew on Congress's language, its organizers, and its prestige, and also developed local institutions, forms of leadership, and goals of their own. The sections that follow examine four cases, in Awadh, in the Eka districts, and in Bardoli, together with the earlier Kheda campaign of 1918 discussed in Chapter 79 for comparison.

Historical Vocabulary
Kisan, Tenant, and Landless Laborer

Kisan is a Hindi word, commonly translated "peasant" or "cultivator," used by organizers in the 1920s to unite a wide range of rural people. It could include tenants and small owners and, in some usage, laborers. Occupancy tenants held legally recognized rights to continue cultivating; tenants-at-will did not. Landless laborers held no tenancy at all. The shared word kisan sometimes concealed these differences, and the movements that used it were often led by better-off cultivators whose interests did not coincide with those of laborers.

Students should therefore ask, whenever a source refers to "the peasants," which category it has in mind and who is actually speaking.

Section 80.2

Kisan Sabhas and Peasant Organization in Awadh, 1918–1921

Awadh, the central region of the United Provinces, was dominated by large taluqdari estates. After 1857 the British had confirmed the taluqdars' position as a landed aristocracy that supported colonial rule (see Chapter 70). By the early twentieth century tenants in the region faced rent enhancements, additional dues, and arbitrary eviction. The first organized response came in 1918, when the United Provinces Kisan Sabha was formed, with leaders including Gauri Shankar Mishra and Indra Narain Dwivedi and with support from some Home Rule activists, among them the Congress politician Madan Mohan Malaviya.

A different style of organization appeared with Baba Ramchandra, a Maharashtrian who had earlier worked abroad as an indentured laborer, according to later accounts, and who from 1919 or 1920 worked among tenants in Pratapgarh and neighboring districts. He moved from village to village, reciting passages from the Ramcharitmanas of Tulsidas to audiences of tenants and using its language of justice and righteous authority to frame their grievances. Historians have noted how effectively such religious idiom allowed organizers to speak to peasant concerns. Baba Ramchandra encouraged villagers to refuse begar and to resist demands for illegal dues, and he led groups of peasants to present petitions to officials and to Congress leaders.

In June 1920 a group of Pratapgarh peasants marched to Allahabad to present their grievances, and the young Congress politician Jawaharlal Nehru was among those who met them. Nehru later recorded that this encounter, and his subsequent visits to villages in Pratapgarh, were a revelation to him of the conditions of the rural poor. In October 1920 the Awadh Kisan Sabha was formally established at Allahabad, with Baba Ramchandra, Gauri Shankar Mishra, and Nehru among those associated with it. Its organization reached across many villages, with village-level committees and a program emphasizing refusal to pay illegal dues, an end to eviction without cause, and boycott of those who broke collective decisions. The Sabha claimed very large numbers of members in the months that followed; such figures should be read with caution because they were reported by organizers and not independently verified.

The movement's relations with Congress during Non-Cooperation were close but uneasy. Congress's Non-Cooperation program from late 1920 encouraged mass participation, and many kisan leaders joined it. Some Congress leaders, however, worried that an agrarian movement might frighten landlords and divert energy from the national struggle. The Kisan Sabha leaders in turn felt that Congress had not given sufficient attention to rent and eviction. In December 1920 a huge gathering of peasants assembled at Ayodhya, reflecting the scale of the mobilization, and by early 1921 tenants across Rae Bareli, Pratapgarh, and Faizabad were holding meetings, boycotting landlords' servants and village functionaries, and in some cases attacking landlords' property.

The government responded with a mixture of repression and concession. In January 1921, at Munshiganj near Rae Bareli, police fired on a crowd that had gathered in connection with the arrest of local leaders, and a number of people were killed; accounts of the casualties vary and the event was long remembered by organizers as a massacre. Officials arrested leaders and prosecuted cases, but they also recognized that tenant grievances were real. The Oudh Rent (Amendment) Act of 1921 offered some protection against arbitrary eviction, though its provisions were limited and often proved ineffective in practice.

The Awadh movement shows several features that recur in later peasant politics. It was led by men of varied background, from educated Congress workers to wandering religious reciters, and was organized through village groups that enforced collective decisions with social pressure. It used the idiom of religion and kingship without being a purely religious movement. And it posed a challenge to Congress strategy, because the primary enemy of many tenants was the landlord rather than the British. After 1921 the organization fragmented. Some leaders remained in Congress, and others pursued separate paths. Later leaders in the United Provinces, including socialists and communists, would draw on this heritage, and the Awadh experience entered the memory of rural politics as a sign that tenants could organize collectively.

Primary Source Window

Awadh Peasant Petitions and Kisan Sabha Documents, 1918–1921

Attribution: Tenants' petitions, memorials, and resolutions of the United Provinces and Awadh Kisan Sabhas, with accompanying government reports · Date: 1918–1921 · Document type: Petitions, organizational resolutions, and official correspondence

Context: Petitions were the standard instrument through which Indian subjects addressed colonial authorities, and the Kisan Sabhas produced many, often drafted by literate organizers on behalf of villagers. Officials in turn compiled reports that described and assessed the movement. These two bodies of material, one produced by tenants' representatives and one by the state, offer contrasting accounts of the same events.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The typical petition complained that landlords demanded rent in excess of recorded rates, that additional payments were levied on occasions of marriage and festival, that tenants were evicted without legal process or compelled to perform unpaid labor, and it asked the government to restrain such practices and to give tenants secure rights of occupancy.

Analysis: The petitions show that tenants framed their demands largely in terms of legal and customary rights rather than in terms of national independence; they appealed to the government as a source of justice against landlords. This is important evidence that the agrarian movement had its own agenda. Official reports, by contrast, tended to emphasize disorder and to attribute the movement to outside agitators, though some officers acknowledged that grievances were genuine.

Source limitations: Petitions were usually written by literate intermediaries, not by tenants themselves, and may reflect the organizers' vocabulary. Official reports served administrative purposes and reflect official fears. The voices of landless laborers are almost entirely absent. Students should work from published scholarly collections and verify any quotation against the original before citing.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the petition say tenants wanted, and from whom did they seek relief?
  2. Who wrote these documents, and how might that affect their content?
  3. What can historians learn about agrarian relations from the list of grievances?
  4. What can they not establish from petitions and official reports alone?
Section 80.3

The Eka Movement and Regional Agrarian Protest, 1921–1922

When the first phase of the Awadh Kisan Sabha movement subsided under repression and the partial concessions of 1921, a second wave of protest emerged in the districts of northern and eastern Awadh, especially Hardoi, Sitapur, and Bahraich, and later in parts of Barabanki and Lucknow divisions. This was the Eka movement, from the Hindi word for "unity." It began in late 1921, drew on the experience of the earlier agitation, and was in some ways more radical, though shorter-lived.

The grievances were familiar. In these districts tenants complained that landlords and their agents demanded rents higher than the recorded rate, sometimes by as much as half again, and that rent-collection was sometimes farmed out to contractors who extracted still more. Tenants were also required to perform unpaid labor and to carry out other burdensome obligations. In addition, many cultivators objected to the practice of payment in kind at rates fixed by the landlord. The Eka movement set out to resist these demands collectively.

Eka meetings followed a pattern reported in official and later scholarly accounts. Gatherings were held at night, often in a secluded place, and participants took a collective pledge. They swore to pay only the recorded rent and to pay it on time, not to leave the land when evicted, not to perform forced labor, not to help those who broke the pledge, and to abide by the decisions of the panchayat. The ritual element, which sometimes involved a hole dug in the ground as a symbolic site for the oath, gave solemnity to the commitment and tied participants into a mutual obligation. As with Awadh, religious and customary forms provided the shape of political organization.

The social composition of the movement was notable. Its leadership drew from the middle ranks of the rural hierarchy and included small zamindars and substantial cultivators of the Thakur and other castes, who resented the intermediary contractors and the larger landlords' demands. Its mass base, however, included many cultivators from lower castes, such as Pasis and Chamars. One prominent leader was Madari Pasi, a Pasi cultivator, who became known for his readiness to defy landlords and who continued to evade capture after the government moved against the movement. His prominence suggests that low-caste leaders could take a central role in rural protest, though the sources about him are fragmentary and partly derived from official files that were hostile to him.

The relationship with Gandhian politics was ambiguous. Participants invoked Gandhi's name, and the movement's discipline and rhetoric of unity echoed Non-Cooperation. But Eka had its own demands about rent and landlords and was not committed to strict non-violence. Reports indicate that in some places Eka followers resisted police and rent collectors forcibly. Congress leaders in the region distanced themselves from such activity, and after the suspension of Non-Cooperation in February 1922 (see Chapter 79) Congress offered no systematic support.

Government repression came in the early months of 1922. Officials deployed police and armed forces, arrested organizers, and prosecuted leaders. By spring, the movement had largely collapsed in most districts, although small pockets of resistance continued and Madari Pasi remained at large for some time.

Why does the Eka movement matter to historians? It demonstrates that peasant politics in the 1920s was not simply a derivative of nationalism. It shows that cultivators created their own rituals of solidarity, selected their own leaders, and defined their own objectives. It also reveals divisions within the "peasantry": substantial cultivators and lower-caste laborers cooperated against landlords but did not necessarily share the same interests. And it illustrates the tension between local peasant objectives and the discipline of Gandhian politics. For Congress, the lesson was ambiguous: later Congress leaders, including Nehru, drew on such movements to argue for a program that included agrarian demands, while others continued to avoid challenging landlords directly.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Tenant Household in Awadh (Illustrative Reconstruction)

This feature is explicitly illustrative. It combines patterns documented in settlement reports and later scholarship and does not describe any named family.

A tenant household in an Awadh village around 1920 would typically cultivate a few acres, growing wheat, barley, millets, and pulses according to the season, and relying on a pair of bullocks for plowing. Its members would include adult men, who worked the fields and negotiated with the landlord's agent; women, who weeded, harvested, processed grain, tended animals, and managed the household; and children, who herded animals and from a young age took on tasks. Food was simple. Debt was a constant companion, and credit from the village moneylender or the landlord's agent was often needed to buy seed or to pay for a wedding.

Beyond the household's own labor, obligations to the landlord shaped the calendar. A household might be called to provide begar at the landlord's house or fields, or to supply fodder or labor for a festival, and these obligations fell at times when its own crops needed attention. Evidence from settlement reports and petitions suggests that these demands were resented but difficult to refuse. When a Kisan Sabha organizer arrived, the household's decision to join was not a simple act of political preference. It meant risking eviction, the loss of access to credit, and sometimes physical reprisal, which is why collective oaths and social sanctions were so important in sustaining participation.

Section 80.4

Bardoli, 1928: Peasant Satyagraha and Rural Leadership

Bardoli is a taluka in the Surat district of the Bombay Presidency, in a ryotwari region where cultivators paid land revenue directly to the state. In 1927 a revision of the revenue assessment raised the demand by around twenty-two percent, according to the standard account. Cultivators, most of them Patidars (Patels) and other landholding castes, argued that the increase was unjustified given the condition of agriculture, falling cotton prices, and the burdens they already carried. Their petitions and representations produced no change. In early 1928, with the support of the local Congress organization, they turned to satyagraha.

Vallabhbhai Patel, a lawyer from Gujarat who had worked with Gandhi in Kheda and Ahmedabad, took charge of the campaign. The decision was made in February 1928, at a meeting of peasants at Bardoli, to refuse to pay the enhanced revenue. Patel arrived in the taluka and divided it into zones, each under the care of a leader, and organized volunteer groups. A network of village committees was established to carry messages and to keep peasants informed. A newspaper, the Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika, and a range of printed bulletins carried news of the campaign.

The government replied with the standard weapons of revenue enforcement. Officers seized and auctioned property, including cattle, and threatened to confiscate land. In some cases buffaloes and household goods were taken, and some peasants had their land forfeited, though many of the forfeited lands found no buyers because the community refused to purchase them. The peasants' solidarity was reinforced by social boycott of those who cooperated with the officials.

Women played an important part in the campaign. Gujarat women's leaders, including Mithuben Petit, Bhaktiba, Sharda Mehta, and Maniben Patel, traveled in the taluka, addressed gatherings, and helped organize the population. Women took part in processions and meetings, and their presence helped sustain morale and bring households into the movement. It was reportedly the women of Bardoli who gave Patel the title "Sardar" (leader) in the course of the campaign, though the origin of the title is reported variously in the literature and should be treated with care. The women's role shows that rural satyagraha could mobilize women, though the leadership of the campaign remained largely male.

The social basis of the movement requires careful attention. The campaign was primarily a movement of landholding peasants who paid revenue. Alongside them lived the Kaliparaj, a group of tribal and low-caste agricultural laborers, many of them in a condition of bonded dependence on landholders, who did not pay revenue and who stood to gain nothing directly from a reduction in assessment. Gandhi's earlier work in the area had included a program for their welfare, and Congress workers made some efforts on their behalf, but the satyagraha itself was directed at the revenue question that mattered to owners. Historians have drawn attention to this limit: the movement's gains accrued mainly to the landholding peasantry and did not address the position of laborers.

By mid-1928 the campaign had attracted national attention, and sympathetic movements and demonstrations occurred elsewhere, including a hartal in Bombay. Gandhi, though not a participant in the day-to-day direction, offered support and advice, and Congress leaders and the press echoed the cause. Negotiations led to an arrangement under which the government agreed to appoint an inquiry into the assessment. The investigators, Broomfield and Maxwell, reported that the enhancement had not been justified and recommended a much smaller increase of around six percent. The peasants' forfeited lands were restored, and the satyagraha ended in August 1928. The settlement was widely regarded as a victory.

Comparing Bardoli with the earlier movements illuminates the variety of rural politics. Kheda in 1918, as examined in Chapter 79, was also a ryotwari satyagraha led by Gandhi and Patel, again centered on landholding Patidars. Awadh and Eka, by contrast, were tenant movements in zamindari territory against landlords. Bardoli's class base was more homogeneous and relatively prosperous; its demand was narrow and winnable; its leadership was disciplined and closely aligned with Congress; and its opponent was the state, not an intermediate landlord class. Awadh and Eka were broader in their social base and more radical in their targets, but they lacked the institutional protection and negotiating leverage that Bardoli enjoyed.

Historical Debate

Were Peasant Movements Part of Nationalism or Autonomous Struggles?

The question: Should historians interpret the peasant movements of the 1920s as expressions of the national movement, or as independent struggles over land and rent that sometimes overlapped with nationalism?

Interpretation A — Peasant movements as part of nationalism: Scholars in this tradition emphasize that peasant movements used nationalist symbols and invoked Gandhi, that Congress organizers often led them, and that mobilization during Non-Cooperation drew peasants into a common political culture. Bardoli, in particular, is often presented as an instance of Congress's ability to link local grievances with the national struggle.
Interpretation B — Peasant movements as autonomous: Historians influenced by subaltern studies and agrarian history emphasize that peasants pursued their own objectives, that their organization drew on local solidarities, ritual, and customary law, and that their relations with Congress were often tense or instrumental. The Eka movement, with its own pledges and leaders and its willingness to resist by force, is the clearest case.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same materials: organizers' papers, police and administrative reports, and later testimony. The disagreement concerns how to weigh the use of nationalist idiom against the content of the demands, and how to treat the evidence of intermediate leaders who stood between villages and Congress.

Why it matters: If peasant movements are seen as part of nationalism, Congress appears as the leader of a broad coalition. If they are seen as autonomous, Congress appears as one actor among several, and the national movement becomes a field of negotiation among competing groups with different interests.

Student task: Using the Awadh, Eka, and Bardoli cases, identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation and explain which interpretation better fits each case.

Division II · Sections 80.5–80.8

Industrial Labor, Trade Unionism, and Communist Politics

How workers organized in mills, mines, and railways, and how class-based politics entered South Asia.

Section 80.5

Workers and Industrial Society: Factories, Housing, Wages, and Everyday Life

By the 1920s South Asia contained a substantial, though proportionally small, industrial working class. The great cotton mills of Bombay and Ahmedabad, the jute mills along the Hooghly near Calcutta, the textile mills of Madras and Kanpur, the coal mines of Bihar and Bengal, the railways, ports, and tea and rubber plantations employed several million people. Industry remained a minor part of an overwhelmingly agrarian economy, but its concentration of workers in particular urban neighborhoods gave it political weight out of proportion to its numbers.

Bombay's cotton mills offer a well-documented example. The industry employed well over a hundred thousand workers, most of them migrants from the Konkan coast and the Deccan districts of Maharashtra, with others from the United Provinces and elsewhere. Many migrants were men who left families in their villages and returned periodically; this pattern affected the rhythm of urban life and the networks on which workers depended. Recruitment was often through jobbers, intermediaries who hired workers, supervised them, and took payments for the privilege of a job. The jobber's power was considerable, and he could be a source of both patronage and exploitation. Wages were low, and workers' real earnings were squeezed by the wartime and postwar inflation. Hours were long, factory discipline was strict, and fines and deductions could reduce a worker's pay.

Industrial accidents were common. Machinery was often poorly guarded, ventilation in mills was inadequate, and exposure to cotton dust and heat damaged health. Compensation for injury was limited until the Workmen's Compensation Act of 1923, and its coverage remained narrow. The Factories Acts of 1881 and subsequent revisions, including one in 1922, set limits on hours and employment of children, but enforcement was uneven. Workers in mines faced hazards including roof falls and gas, and plantation workers in Assam and elsewhere often labored under contracts that bound them to employers and under conditions of close supervision, as discussed in Chapter 75.

Housing was a central problem. In Bombay, many mill workers lived in chawls, multi-story tenements with single-room units shared by families or by groups of men, often with common latrines and limited water. Rents were high relative to wages, overcrowding was severe, and infectious disease spread easily. Contemporary investigations in the 1920s documented high infant mortality and poor sanitation in working-class districts. In Calcutta, jute workers lived in company lines or in crowded bastis. Housing patterns often followed caste, region, and language, and neighborhood networks of kin and village origin supported workers in unemployment, illness, and strike.

Women formed a significant part of the early industrial workforce, especially in some sectors. In the Bombay mills their numbers declined over the early twentieth century as mechanization and changes in labor organization favored male workers, but in jute, coal mining, and plantations women continued to be employed in large numbers. Women were typically paid less than men, held less skilled jobs, and were vulnerable to harassment by supervisors and jobbers. They also bore the burden of domestic labor. Children worked in some industries as well, although the Factories Acts restricted their employment.

Caste and community shaped the industrial workplace. In Bombay, certain jobs, such as the weaving department, were dominated by particular communities, and in some mills workers classified as untouchable, including Mahars, Chamars, and others, were confined to lower-paid departments, such as the spinning side, and excluded from better-paid weaving work. This is an important reminder that industrial society did not simply dissolve caste; instead, caste hierarchy was reproduced in new settings. It also means that the experience of an untouchable mill hand differed from that of a caste-Hindu worker, which later shaped the relations between Ambedkar's movement and the trade unions.

Why did workers develop political demands that could not be addressed simply by securing Indian self-government? Their immediate concerns were wages, hours, safety, housing, job security, and the authority of employers and jobbers, which would not necessarily change if an Indian government replaced a British one. Many mill-owners were Indians, including Parsi, Gujarati, and Marwari industrialists, and some were prominent nationalist supporters. Workers therefore confronted Indian employers as well as colonial officials. Nationalist leaders who depended on industrial finance were often cautious about supporting strikes. This created a distinctive political space for labor organizations: they could work with nationalists on some issues, such as opposition to the Rowlatt Acts or to British policy on tariffs, while pursuing their own agenda against employers.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Mill Household in Bombay (Illustrative Reconstruction)

This feature is explicitly illustrative, drawn from investigations of working-class life in the 1920s, and does not depict a named family.

A mill hand from the Konkan might live in a single-room chawl tenement shared with relatives or fellow villagers, rising before dawn to reach the mill by the first shift. He would work among noisy machinery for ten hours or more, under the watch of a jobber, with little time to rest. Food, bought on credit from local shops, was often simple, and the family's budget was precarious: rent, food, and repayments to moneylenders consumed most of the wage. Women in the neighborhood managed households, fetched water from shared taps, and sometimes took in work or sold goods to supplement income.

Illness or the loss of a job could be catastrophic, and worker networks of kin and village provided mutual support. Festivals and community associations offered social life and, increasingly, a basis for organization, as neighborhood and religious gatherings became sites where grievances were shared. These everyday networks help explain how large strikes could be sustained for weeks or months, though the cost to families was high.

Section 80.6

The Rise of Trade Unionism and the All India Trade Union Congress, 1920

Organized labor in India had earlier roots. In the nineteenth century there were worker petitions, short-lived associations, and welfare societies led by philanthropists, such as the Bombay Mill Hands' Association associated with N. M. Lokhande in the 1890s, which pressed for factory reform. These bodies were generally small and depended on sympathetic outsiders. The decisive change came during and just after the First World War, when inflation, high profits for employers, and wartime disruption provoked a wave of strikes in 1918–1920 across Bombay, Ahmedabad, Madras, Calcutta, Kanpur, and other centers.

Two organizations of this period illustrate different approaches. In Madras, B. P. Wadia, a Theosophist associated with Annie Besant's Home Rule movement, helped form the Madras Labour Union in April 1918, primarily among workers at the Buckingham and Carnatic mills. It is often called the first modern trade union in India with a continuous existence and a regular membership. It organized strikes and sought improvement in wages and conditions, though it struggled against employer resistance and later suffered from internal divisions. In Ahmedabad, after the mill-workers' dispute of 1918 in which Gandhi intervened, as noted in Chapter 79, Anasuya Sarabhai and Gandhi developed the Textile Labour Association (formally organized in 1920), based on principles of arbitration, moral persuasion, and trusteeship rather than class conflict.

The strike wave of 1919–1920 persuaded many labor and nationalist leaders that a coordinating body was needed. The founding of the International Labour Organization in 1919 also mattered: the question of who would represent Indian workers at its conference led to a demand for a representative national organization. On 31 October 1920 the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) began its first conference in Bombay. Lala Lajpat Rai, the Punjab nationalist leader, was its first president. N. M. Joshi was a leading organizer; V. M. Pawar held the early general-secretary office, and Dewan Chaman Lall succeeded him in 1921, as recorded in the federation's published executive minutes. Reports claimed that scores of unions with a membership of well over a hundred thousand were affiliated, although such figures are approximate and the early AITUC was a loose federation.

The AITUC brought together a diverse group. Some leaders, like Joshi, favored moderate trade unionism, collective bargaining, and legislation. Nationalists such as Lajpat Rai saw labor organization as part of a broader struggle against imperialism and capitalism, and invoked socialist ideas without a developed program. Younger socialists and communists later gained influence. Lajpat Rai's presidential address was noted for linking the labor question to the critique of imperialism, according to contemporary reports.

The legal environment developed slowly. Employers and officials often responded to strikes by dismissals, prosecutions, and, in some cases, force. The courts had held that union activity might expose officials to civil suit for conspiracy, as in the Buckingham and Carnatic case brought against Wadia in 1920–1921, in which an injunction against the Madras Labour Union's leaders raised the issue of trade union immunities. This episode contributed to demands for legislation. The Trade Unions Act of 1926, which came into force in 1927, provided for the registration of unions, gave registered unions limited protection from civil and criminal liability for legitimate trade-union activity, and permitted a separate political fund subject to conditions. It did not guarantee recognition by employers or the right to bargain, and it did not provide a framework for resolving disputes. Still, it was an important step in the legal recognition of unions.

The relationship with nationalist politics was complex. Congress leaders often courted labor support during mass campaigns, and some, like Lajpat Rai and later Nehru, took part in AITUC affairs. Yet many Congress leaders were close to industrialists, and Gandhi's approach emphasized harmony between capital and labor. Unions, in turn, could use nationalist platforms without subordinating their goals. For workers, collective bargaining, negotiated wage settlements, and the right to strike were practical priorities whose meaning was not defined by the national question. In the late 1920s, as communist influence grew in some unions, divisions within the AITUC sharpened, and by the end of the decade the federation was increasingly torn between moderates and radicals.

Section 80.7

The Bombay Textile Strikes and Militant Labor Politics

Bombay's cotton textile industry was the heartland of Indian industrial labor and the site of its most dramatic confrontations. After the postwar boom the industry entered difficult years. Competition from Japanese and Lancashire cloth, shifts in demand, and the high price of raw cotton squeezed profits, and mill-owners responded by trying to reduce costs. In 1923 and 1925 they attempted wage cuts and the withdrawal of a bonus, provoking a major strike in 1925 that lasted about two months. The employers' trade body, the Bombay Millowners' Association, defended its position by pointing to the industry's competitive problems, while workers pointed to their already low earnings.

By 1926–1927 the employers were pursuing "rationalization," a set of measures to increase output per worker, including requiring weavers to operate more looms or spinners more spindles and standardizing wage rates across mills. Workers viewed these measures as intensification of work and as a threat to employment, because fewer workers could produce the same output. Disputes also concerned the authority of employers, including the power to dismiss, to fine, and to determine the terms of work. In this context a new and more militant leadership emerged.

Between 1927 and 1928 a group of young socialists and communists, among them S. A. Dange, S. V. Ghate, K. N. Joglekar, and S. S. Mirajkar, built influence among Bombay textile workers. In April 1928 a large strike began in several mills over wages and working conditions and spread rapidly. The strikers formed the Girni Kamgar Union (the Mill Workers' Union), which adopted the red flag, and the union's membership surged. Estimates suggest that over a hundred thousand workers were out at the peak of the strike, which continued for roughly six months, into the autumn of 1928.

The strike was shaped by rivalry among organizations. The Girni Kamgar Union, communist-influenced, was the most militant. Moderate labor leaders, including N. M. Joshi, and the Bombay Textile Labour Union, with a more conciliatory approach, took different positions on strategy and negotiation. Nationalist politicians offered sympathy but also kept some distance. Employers refused to recognize the new union and sought to break the strike by bringing in outside workers and by supporting rival bodies. The Bombay government, concerned about order, deployed police and placed restrictions on meetings. Eventually, a committee of inquiry was appointed under Sir Frederick Fawcett to examine the dispute, and the strike ended in the autumn with limited concessions. The rationalization schemes were withdrawn, at least for a time, and the issue of wage standardization was referred for study.

The human cost was considerable. Workers who did not receive wages for months relied on credit, on the support of family and community networks, on remittances from relatives, and on strike funds, which were generally inadequate. Many returned to their villages or sought other work. Shopkeepers extended credit but eventually refused it. Reports indicate rising indebtedness and hardship. The strike also demonstrated the risks of prolonged industrial action: the employers could absorb losses in ways that families could not, and a union could be bankrupted by the effort to sustain thousands of strikers.

Women workers and workers from marginalized communities took part in the strike, though the leadership was predominantly male. The strike also exposed internal divisions. Untouchable workers, who were concentrated in lower-paid spinning departments and denied access to the better-paid weaving jobs, had particular grievances, and Ambedkar and his associates were critical of unions that neglected caste discrimination within the workforce.

The consequences were significant for the state and for labor politics. The government concluded that organized communist influence in the unions was a threat. In 1929 the Trade Disputes Act provided for conciliation and for restrictions on strikes in public utilities, and the Public Safety Bill proposed measures against foreign communists. In March 1929, the Meerut arrests, examined in §80.8, targeted many of the militant organizers. The strike of 1928 and the further strikes of 1929 thus mark both the high point of the early communist-led labor movement and the beginning of its repression. For historians, the Bombay strikes show that industrial workers could mobilize on a mass scale around issues of work and wages, that class-based and community-based organization interacted, and that the state would respond to labor militancy as a political challenge.

Section 80.8

Communist Organization, Socialist Ideas, and Workers' and Peasants' Parties

The Russian Revolution of 1917 and the establishment of the Communist International (Comintern) in 1919 introduced a new framework for Indian political thought: a class analysis of imperialism, a conception of revolutionary organization, and a model of a state claiming to represent workers and peasants. Indian students, journalists, and nationalists encountered these ideas through newspapers, pamphlets, and contact with émigré networks in Europe, the United States, and Central Asia. Socialist thought had earlier Indian sources too, and not all radicals who admired the Soviet experiment became communists. It is important to distinguish communism, meaning adherence to the Comintern's model of party organization and revolutionary strategy, from socialism and from the broader range of labor politics, which included trade unionists, Fabian reformers, Gandhian labor organizers, and nationalists who borrowed socialist language.

M. N. Roy, a Bengali revolutionary who had traveled abroad during the war, became a leading Indian figure in the Comintern. At the Second Congress of the Communist International in 1920 he debated Lenin on the role of national liberation movements in colonial countries, arguing for a more independent communist role than Lenin's formulation allowed. In October 1920 at Tashkent, a group of Indian exiles, including Roy and other emigrants, announced the formation of a Communist Party of India. Its membership was small and its connection with conditions in India was tenuous. Roy's writings, including India in Transition (1922), presented an analysis of Indian economy and society from a Marxist standpoint and circulated among radicals.

Within India, communist groups emerged independently in several cities. In Bombay, S. A. Dange published a pamphlet in 1921 comparing Gandhi and Lenin and later edited the journal The Socialist. In Calcutta, Muzaffar Ahmad worked with the poet Kazi Nazrul Islam and others on the Bengali paper Navayug and later Langal. In Lahore the Inqilab group and in Madras the veteran labor leader M. Singaravelu Chettiar, who celebrated May Day in 1923 and promoted labor and socialist ideas, were active. In Kanpur, Shaukat Usmani and others had contact with Roy's network.

The government regarded communist organization as a threat and acted early. In 1924 it prosecuted several leaders, including Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, Shaukat Usmani, and Nalini Gupta, in the Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case, charging them with conspiracy to deprive the King-Emperor of sovereignty over British India. Several were convicted and imprisoned. The case publicized communism more widely, despite its intended effect. In December 1925 a communist conference was held at Kanpur, with Singaravelu Chettiar presiding, at which a party organization was formed. Many Indian communists date the founding of the Communist Party of India to this Kanpur conference, while others point to Tashkent in 1920. The question is a matter of continuing disagreement, with the Tashkent group's small and exiled character on one side and the Kanpur gathering's domestic basis on the other, and this textbook treats both as part of the longer formation of a communist movement.

Because open communist organization invited prosecution, communists in the later 1920s worked through broader bodies. In Bengal, the Labour Swaraj Party was formed in 1925 and became the Workers' and Peasants' Party of Bengal in 1926, and similar parties were formed in Bombay, Punjab, and the United Provinces, forming an All India Workers' and Peasants' Party by 1928. These parties campaigned for labor rights, peasant relief, and radical social reform, published journals, and worked within Congress and the trade unions. Their influence in the AITUC and in the Girni Kamgar Union was considerable in 1928. Their approach combined organization of workers with an analysis of colonialism that criticized Congress leadership for what they saw as timid or bourgeois politics. Congress's own younger left, including Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, sympathized with some of these ideas without joining communist organizations.

The authorities struck again in March 1929, arresting thirty-one labor and communist leaders from across India, including British communists who had come to assist the movement, and charging them with conspiracy in the Meerut Conspiracy Case. The proceedings dragged on for years, from 1929 into 1933, and the prosecution of the accused became a political cause, attracting support from Nehru and others, who saw in it an attack on labor and on freedom of association. The arrests removed many leaders from active organization at a critical time, contributing to the decline of communist influence in the unions, and the Comintern's sharp left turn in 1928 also weakened the alliance strategy of the Workers' and Peasants' Parties.

For the history of social movements, the significance of this period is that communist and socialist ideas gave labor and peasant movements a vocabulary of class, exploitation, and revolution that was distinct from both liberal constitutionalism and Gandhian trusteeship. Communists also tended to treat caste as a secondary problem subordinate to class, a position criticized by Ambedkar and others and returned to in §80.19.

Non-Brahmin Politics and the Self-Respect Movement

Challenges to caste hierarchy in education, office, public space, and ritual authority.

Section 80.9

Caste Hierarchy, Education, and the Origins of Non-Brahmin Politics

Colonial rule did not create caste, but it transformed its political significance. Chapter 73 showed how the census, ethnography, and administrative practice classified communities and made caste a category of public life. In the nineteenth century, English-language education, government employment, and the legal profession became the main avenues to status and income, and access to them was unequal. Communities with a long tradition of literacy, including Brahmins and in some regions other literate castes, entered the new institutions in disproportionate numbers. Their predominance in universities, the bureaucracy, the press, and the legal profession meant that the educated public sphere that spoke for "Indian opinion" was heavily weighted toward a few communities.

The numbers, as reported in contemporary sources, were striking. In the Madras Presidency Brahmins formed only a small percentage of the population but held a large majority of posts in the higher administrative services and of places in the colleges and the legal profession; similar patterns were noted in the Bombay Presidency. Non-Brahmin leaders argued that this concentration reflected an inherited advantage that effectively shut out others, while defenders of existing arrangements pointed to merit and to the accumulated educational tradition of the literate castes.

The earliest articulated non-Brahmin critique came from Maharashtra. Jyotirao Phule, born into a Mali family, and his wife Savitribai Phule, opened schools for girls and for children of lower castes in Pune in the late 1840s and later. In 1873 Jyotirao founded the Satyashodhak Samaj ("Truth-Seeking Society") to challenge the authority of Brahmin priests, to propose alternative rituals without Brahmin mediation, and to promote education for non-Brahmins and for women. His book Gulamgiri (1873) argued that Brahmin domination was a form of slavery imposed on the lower castes, an interpretation that drew on contemporary racial theories about Aryan conquest (see Chapter 5 for the historiography of Aryan migration). The Phules thus laid the foundations of a tradition that combined critique of caste, advocacy of education, and attention to the condition of peasants and women.

In the early twentieth century non-Brahmin politics in Maharashtra gained a princely patron in Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur, who in 1902 reserved a large share of positions in his state's administration for communities not represented in it, and supported education and hostels for backward communities. The Satyashodhak movement was carried forward by leaders such as Keshavrao Jedhe and Dinkarrao Javalkar and by activist-publishers, and in the 1920s a Maratha-led non-Brahmin political tendency developed that drew on peasant and caste interests. In Maharashtra, then, non-Brahmin politics often expressed the aspirations of dominant peasant castes, notably Marathas, as well as the concerns of others.

In the Madras Presidency, non-Brahmin politics took a different shape. Here, a combination of educated non-Brahmin professionals, landholders, and merchants, together with a Tamil and Telugu cultural revival, developed a political identity framed by emerging scholarship on Dravidian languages and cultures (see Chapter 73 for colonial linguistic classification). This identity reinterpreted the South Indian past, claiming for Tamil and other Dravidian languages a literary and cultural tradition independent of, and in some presentations older than, Sanskritic Brahminism. The political expression of this movement is examined in §80.10.

A crucial distinction must be maintained. "Non-Brahmin" was a broad category defined by exclusion of one community, not by a shared condition. It included wealthy landlords and merchants, educated professionals, substantial cultivators, artisans, and those at the bottom of the hierarchy. Many non-Brahmin leaders belonged to dominant landholding or commercial castes, such as Vellalars, Reddis, Kammas, Nairs, and Chettiars, and had their own interests, which might conflict with those of subordinate groups. Communities subjected to untouchability, such as Paraiyars, Pulayas, Mahars, and Chamars, faced disabilities that no non-Brahmin caste experienced in the same degree: exclusion from temples, wells, roads, and schools, and a condition of ritual pollution that governed daily life. Non-Brahmin politics could advance the claims of the first group without addressing those of the second, and one of the central tensions of the period lies in the relationship between them.

Education was the principal arena of conflict. Schools and colleges were scarce, expensive, and often controlled by missionary or upper-caste managements. Children from untouchable communities were frequently denied admission to government schools or forced to sit apart, despite official policies in some provinces that opened schools to all. Scholarships, hostels, and reserved places became demands of non-Brahmin movements. Employment in government service was also contested: officials were recruited largely from educated groups, and the question of "communal" representation in jobs, meaning reserved proportions for specific communities, became a main political demand in Madras, Bombay, and princely states like Mysore and Kolhapur.

Behind these demands was a larger conviction that political reform and constitutional change would not alter hierarchies unless institutions were opened to groups previously excluded.

Historical Vocabulary
Non-Brahmin

The term non-Brahmin came into wide political use in the early twentieth century to designate all Hindu communities outside the Brahmin caste. It was a political category, created in opposition to Brahmin predominance in education and office, and it grouped together communities of very different wealth and ritual rank. Its meaning varied by region: in Maharashtra it often implied the dominant peasant castes, while in the Madras Presidency it was associated with a broader Dravidian identity.

Students should not equate "non-Brahmin" with "oppressed castes." Some non-Brahmin movements were led by wealthy landholders and sought a share of power for their own communities. The experiences of communities subject to untouchability, discussed from §80.11 onward, were different in kind and often found little room in non-Brahmin agendas.

Section 80.10

The Justice Party and Non-Brahmin Representation in Madras

The South Indian Liberal Federation, known popularly as the Justice Party after its English-language newspaper Justice, was founded in Madras in November 1916. Its principal organizers were Dr. T. M. Nair, a physician and Tamil-speaking Nair who had been active in Madras public life, P. Theagaraya Chetty, a wealthy Madras merchant and municipal leader, and C. Natesa Mudaliar, a physician and organizer who also founded the Dravidian Association. Earlier organizations, such as the Madras Dravidian Association and the Madras Non-Brahmin Association, had paved the way. The Federation sought to organize non-Brahmin opinion and, in the context of the debate on constitutional reform, to ensure that non-Brahmins would receive adequate representation.

In December 1916 the group issued the Non-Brahmin Manifesto, a public statement that criticized the predominance of Brahmins in the public services and in the leadership of the Home Rule movement and Congress in the Presidency, and warned that a rapid transfer of power to the existing educated elite would entrench the dominance of that elite. The manifesto urged non-Brahmins to cooperate with the British government for the time being as a guarantee of fair treatment, rather than to support Home Rule agitation led by Annie Besant and others. The movement's political stance, therefore, combined a claim for social justice with a cautious attitude toward the national movement's demand for self-rule, which its critics saw as a tactical loyalism.

Organization through the press was crucial. Besides Justice in English, the party issued the Tamil Dravidan and the Telugu Andhra Prakasika, which reached different linguistic audiences. These newspapers shaped a public in which "non-Brahmin" became a recognizable political category, and the party built its support in the Presidency's districts through local associations and the patronage of landed and mercantile leaders. T. M. Nair, the ideological voice of the early movement, died in 1919, and Theagaraya Chetty and, later, the Raja of Panagal became central leaders.

Under the Government of India Act of 1919 provincial legislatures were elected on a limited franchise, with certain subjects transferred to Indian ministers under dyarchy. Congress boycotted the first elections of November 1920, in line with Non-Cooperation, and the Justice Party won the largest number of seats in the Madras Legislative Council and formed the provincial government. A. Subbarayalu Reddiar became the first Chief Minister, followed by the Raja of Panagal in 1921. The party held office until 1926, a notable experience of non-Brahmin government under colonial dyarchy, though ministers' powers were limited by the reserved subjects retained by the governor and by their dependence on official support.

The Justice governments pursued policies to broaden access to education and employment. In 1921 the government issued a "Communal Government Order" setting out a system of reserved shares for different communities in public appointments, an early example of a quota-style approach in colonial administration, though its implementation was uneven and the proportions were revised later. It supported expansion of schooling, including in some cases schools for depressed communities, and the introduction of free meals and other aids for poor pupils in the 1920s, though accounts of these measures differ in detail. The government also took steps relating to the management of Hindu temples and endowments, culminating in the Hindu Religious Endowments Act of 1926, which created a board to supervise endowments and was criticized by orthodox Hindus and defended by reformers. In 1921 the Madras Legislative Council became the first provincial council to vote to enfranchise women on the same terms as men, a notable moment connected with the women's movement discussed in §80.17.

Historians evaluate the Justice Party with mixed conclusions. On one side, it established the principle of communal representation in jobs and education as a legitimate object of public policy, challenged Brahmin predominance in administration, and fostered a political identity that later fed Dravidian politics. On the other, its leadership was drawn largely from wealthy and educated non-Brahmins, often landholders and merchants, and its social policy served those groups' interests more than those of the poor. Its attitude toward untouchable communities was ambivalent. Some measures benefited depressed-class pupils, and some leaders favored uplift, but others, from dominant non-Brahmin castes, were reluctant to challenge the exclusion of untouchables or to support their claims. Leaders of the depressed classes, such as M. C. Rajah, who was nominated to the Legislative Council in the early 1920s and who argued for the interests of the Adi-Dravidas, often found that their concerns were secondary in Justice politics.

The party's loyalism, its reliance on official favor, and its inability to establish deep roots in the countryside made it vulnerable. In the 1926 elections it lost its majority, and Congress's Swarajist wing emerged as the largest group, though it declined to form a government. By the end of the decade the party was in decline, divided and losing ground to Congress and to the more radical Self-Respect Movement discussed in §80.12.

Section 80.11

Vaikom, Narayana Guru, and the Struggle against Caste Exclusion

The princely state of Travancore, in what is now southern Kerala, was in the early twentieth century among the most rigidly caste-stratified societies of South Asia. Brahmins, especially Namboodiris, held ritual and landed pre-eminence, and Nairs occupied a position of social and military standing. Ezhavas, a large community of cultivators, toddy-tappers, and traders, and Pulayas and Parayas, who worked as agricultural laborers, were subject to rules of distance pollution: certain castes were required to keep prescribed distances from higher castes and were barred from temples, from many roads, and from public institutions. These prohibitions were enforced by custom and, in significant respects, by state authority.

Reform movements arose within these communities. Narayana Guru (c. 1856–1928), an Ezhava saint and teacher, in 1888 consecrated a Shiva image at Aruvippuram, an act that challenged Brahmin monopoly over temple consecration. His teachings emphasized the unity of humankind, education, and the rejection of caste distinction; he is associated with a well-known message of "one caste, one religion, one god for humankind" in later tradition. In 1903 the Sri Narayana Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Yogam was founded, with the physician Dr. Palpu and the poet Kumaran Asan among the leaders, to organize Ezhava interests, promote education, and seek access to public institutions and employment. Ayyankali (1863–1941), of the Pulaya community, led a different strand of activism that emphasized the rights of the agricultural laborer castes: he organized processions on public roads in defiance of restrictions, in 1893 reportedly rode through the streets in a bullock cart, and founded the Sadhu Jana Paripalana Sangham in 1907. He campaigned for admission of Pulaya children to schools and supported agricultural laborers' strikes for dignity and wages in the early twentieth century.

The Vaikom Satyagraha, which began on 30 March 1924, is the best-known episode of this struggle. The temple at Vaikom in Travancore was surrounded by public roads on which members of untouchable and some lower castes, including Ezhavas, were not permitted to walk. The campaign sought the right of all to use these roads. An important historical distinction must be stressed: Vaikom was principally about access to the roads around the temple, not the immediate achievement of unrestricted entry into the temple itself. The goal of temple entry was a larger, later objective that was realized in Travancore through the Temple Entry Proclamation of 1936.

Local leaders included T. K. Madhavan, an Ezhava activist and journalist who had been prominent in the SNDP, and K. P. Kesava Menon, a Nair Congress organizer; K. Kelappan and George Joseph also played prominent roles. The Kerala Provincial Congress Committee, which sponsored the satyagraha, took the unusual step of campaigning against the practice of a princely state. Volunteers from different communities, including caste-Hindu Nairs and Namboodiris who supported the cause, walked toward the restricted roads, were stopped by police, and were arrested. Orthodox leaders opposed the campaign, and some volunteers suffered violence.

E. V. Ramasamy of Madras, later known as Periyar, came to Vaikom in April 1924 at the invitation of local leaders, after the arrest of Kerala organizers, and led the campaign for a period, being arrested more than once. His role earned him the title "Vaikom Veerar" (hero of Vaikom) in later tradition. Gandhi's involvement was more cautious. He supported the cause but insisted that the satyagraha be confined to the issue of road access and conducted without outside pressure, and he advised against the participation of non-Hindus, which led to Akali Sikh volunteers from Punjab who had set up a communal kitchen being asked to withdraw. In March 1925 Gandhi visited Vaikom and held discussions with orthodox Namboodiri leaders and with the Regent of Travancore, Maharani Sethu Lakshmi Bai, to seek a resolution.

The satyagraha ended in November 1925 with a partial settlement. The government opened the roads on three sides of the temple complex to all, regardless of caste, while the eastern approach, near the temple's main entrance, remained restricted. Many reformers regarded this as an incomplete outcome that preserved a core of discrimination, and some orthodox Hindus regarded even this much as a defeat. The settlement left untouched the broader system of exclusions from temples, schools, and public offices.

Vaikom's significance lies in several areas. It was among the first organized, sustained campaigns against untouchability in a princely state, drawing support from across caste lines and from Congress workers. It showed both the potential and the limits of the Gandhian approach, which sought to convert opponents by moral suasion and which restricted aims to avoid confrontation, thereby limiting what could be achieved. It brought together actors who would later diverge: Periyar, who soon left Congress and challenged Brahminical authority more radically (§80.12), and Gandhi, who regarded untouchability as a reformable blemish on Hinduism rather than a structural feature of caste.

Section 80.12

Periyar and the Self-Respect Movement, 1925–1929

Erode Venkata Ramasamy (1879–1973), known as Periyar ("the elder"), was born into a prosperous merchant family of the Naicker community in Erode in the Madras Presidency. He entered public life as a municipal figure and, after meeting Gandhi's movement, joined Congress in 1919, resigning many of his positions to take part in Non-Cooperation. He was active in the boycott of toddy shops, in the promotion of khadi, and served as president of the Madras Provincial Congress Committee.

Within Congress, Periyar pressed for a resolution on communal representation, which would reserve a proportion of posts for non-Brahmins, and found it repeatedly rejected by the Brahmin-led leadership. His frustration culminated at the Congress meeting at Kancheepuram in November 1925, where such a resolution was again defeated. He left the party and in 1925 launched what he called the Self-Respect Movement (Suyamariyadai Iyakkam). Its Tamil weekly, Kudi Arasu ("Republic"), began publication in May 1925 and became its main organ.

The movement's central claim was that caste hierarchy, sustained by Brahminical religious authority, deprived the majority of human dignity, and that dignity had to be asserted by rejecting priestly mediation and the scriptures that sanctioned inequality. Self-respect advocates attacked ritual customs, priests' role in weddings and funerals, and the belief systems that, in their view, supported hierarchy. They also promoted rationalism, challenging superstition, and criticized orthodox religion, a stance that distinguished them from reform movements that sought to purify Hinduism from within. Periyar was an outspoken critic of the Hindu epics and Puranas as instruments of Brahmin power, and of religion more generally, which brought him into conflict with traditional authorities and with Gandhi.

One of its most characteristic practices was the self-respect marriage, a ceremony conducted without a Brahmin priest and without Sanskrit rituals, often with simple vows and with an emphasis on equality of the couple. These marriages, held in public, also encouraged intercaste unions and the remarriage of widows, and rejected child marriage and dowry. The movement advocated women's education, women's right to divorce and to property, and access to contraception, positions advanced for their time. Women activists, including Moovalur Ramamirtham Ammaiyar and others such as Nagammal, took part, and in later conferences women's resolutions were adopted. The movement also encouraged members to drop caste suffixes from their names.

The first Self-Respect Conference, held at Chengalpattu in February 1929, drew large audiences and passed resolutions opposing caste distinctions, endorsing equal rights for women, calling for changes in marriage law, and supporting the removal of barriers to public access for the depressed classes, with several resolutions demanding that caste titles be abandoned and that schools and public places be opened to all. Conferences such as this functioned as public forums at which participants of diverse backgrounds, including men and women, were addressed in simple Tamil, and in which the movement's network of local associations and its newspaper reinforced a shared identity.

The movement's relation to the Justice Party was complex. Both opposed Brahmin predominance and supported communal representation, and some individuals took part in both. Periyar cooperated with Justice leaders at times and later became closely associated with the party, whose leadership he took over in the 1930s. But the movements differed in orientation. The Justice Party was a political organization oriented toward legislatures, offices, and the colonial state; the Self-Respect Movement was a social and cultural movement that rejected the authority of religion and addressed everyday life, and it often criticized Justice leaders for their moderation and their reliance on the government. It also reached beyond elites, appealing to artisans, small traders, and some depressed-class members, though its leadership was dominated by non-Brahmins of intermediate castes.

The movement challenged both colonial and domestic forms of hierarchy. It criticized British rule, though not always in the nationalist idiom, and was equally critical of what it perceived as a nationalism that preserved Brahminical power. Its slogan of equality and its attack on religious authority were uncomfortable for many nationalists, who regarded social reform as subordinate to national liberation or sought to reform Hinduism without breaking from it. Historians disagree about whether its Dravidian politics later helped to bridge or to divide communities, and about the degree to which its sharp rhetoric against Brahmins as a group shaded from a critique of an institution into an attack on a community. Nonetheless the Self-Respect Movement permanently altered the cultural politics of Tamil-speaking regions, and its themes of rationalism, women's equality, and caste critique continued to shape political debate long after the 1920s.

Section 80.13

B. R. Ambedkar: Education, Caste Discrimination, and Political Thought

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar was born on 14 April 1891 at Mhow, a military cantonment in central India, into a Mahar family. The Mahars, a large community in Maharashtra, were classed as untouchable and were traditionally assigned menial village duties such as carrying messages, removing dead animals, and watching boundaries. Ambedkar's father, Ramji Sakpal, served in the army and, like others in the army's schools, valued education; the family later settled in Satara and then Bombay. Ambedkar's childhood was marked by experiences of discrimination that he later described in autobiographical writings: in school he was made to sit apart, was denied access to drinking water except when poured by an upper-caste attendant, and encountered refusals of service in ordinary settings.

His education was exceptional for a member of an untouchable community in this period, and it depended on both individual determination and patronage. He passed the matriculation examination in Bombay, then studied at Elphinstone College, graduating in 1912. A scholarship from the Maharaja of Baroda, Sayajirao Gaekwad III, a ruler known for his support of education for depressed communities, allowed him to study in the United States at Columbia University, where he received an MA in 1915 and studied economics, history, and philosophy, coming under the influence of teachers such as the economist E. R. A. Seligman and, as he later acknowledged, the philosopher John Dewey. In 1916 he went to London to study at the London School of Economics and to read for the bar at Gray's Inn. His studies were interrupted by the ending of his scholarship and his return to Baroda service, where he again met discrimination, and he completed his degrees later in London, eventually receiving doctorates from Columbia and the University of London in the 1920s.

In May 1916, while at Columbia, Ambedkar presented a paper on caste at an anthropology seminar led by Alexander Goldenweiser, later published in the Indian Antiquary in 1917 as Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development. This is his earliest major scholarly study. The paper argued that caste was not simply a division of labor but a division of laborers, and that its defining characteristic was endogamy, the rule of marriage within the group. He proposed that endogamy had been imposed on groups by the practice of enforcing it on women, through customs such as sati, enforced widowhood, and child marriage, which controlled the marital possibilities of women and thereby preserved caste boundaries. Whether or not one accepts every element of his reconstruction, the paper shows his early attention to the connection between caste and the control of women, a theme he would return to.

On returning to India he worked briefly in Baroda and then Bombay, where he taught at Sydenham College of Commerce and began his involvement in public life. In 1919 he gave evidence before the Southborough Committee on franchise, arguing that depressed classes should have their own representatives elected by their own communities, because nominated or caste-Hindu representatives could not be trusted to represent their interests. This marks the beginning of a demand he would press for years: independent political representation for communities subject to untouchability.

The key insight of Ambedkar's emerging thought, and the one that separated him from many nationalist reformers, was that political independence would not automatically eliminate caste oppression. He argued that the removal of British rule could transfer power to a dominant elite who would preserve existing hierarchies, and that the struggle against untouchability needed its own organization and program. He did not oppose independence as such; rather he insisted that independence without social equality would leave the majority of the depressed classes without freedom. His approach combined scholarly argument, legal training, and engagement with the institutions of the colonial state, including the courts and legislatures, as arenas in which rights could be claimed.

It is important to present Ambedkar as a scholar, legal thinker, organizer, and political leader in his own right, and not merely as the antagonist of Gandhi or Congress. He drew on liberal political thought, on American pragmatism, on economics, and on a close reading of Indian religious and legal texts. He saw constitutional rights, education, and organization as tools, and he was skeptical of approaches that relied on the goodwill of the dominant castes. At the same time, his positions evolved, and the Ambedkar of the late 1920s differs from the Ambedkar of the 1930s and later, a point students should bear in mind.

Ambedkar's experience also illustrates the barriers faced even by the most gifted individuals of his community. A highly educated barrister and economist could still be refused lodging, denied water at his workplace, and treated as polluting. Such humiliations were not marginal anecdotes; they illustrate the everyday character of untouchability, in which ritual hierarchy translated into daily exclusion from services, spaces, and relationships.

Section 80.14

Organizations, Newspapers, and the Demand for Independent Dalit Representation

By the early 1920s, Ambedkar was building an organizational base. Earlier efforts to organize Mahars and other communities had been made by figures such as Gopal Baba Walangkar and Shivram Janba Kamble, and by reformers associated with the Satyashodhak movement and with the princely patronage of Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur, who encouraged Ambedkar and supported a conference of depressed classes at Mangaon in 1920 at which Ambedkar spoke.

In January 1920 he launched the Marathi fortnightly Mooknayak ("Leader of the Voiceless"). It was financed partly with help from Shahu Maharaj, and it gave a platform to the concerns of untouchable communities that were neglected by the mainstream press, which was dominated by caste-Hindu owners and editors. After Mooknayak ceased, he started Bahishkrit Bharat in 1927 and later other papers. The press was central to his strategy: it created a public in which untouchable communities were addressed as political agents, reported on discrimination, and argued in Marathi for rights and organization.

In July 1924, having returned from further study abroad and established himself as a lawyer in Bombay, Ambedkar founded the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha ("Outcastes' Welfare Association"), with the motto "educate, agitate, organize" associated with its work in later tradition. Its aims included spreading education and culture among the depressed classes, improving their economic condition, and representing their grievances to government and public opinion. It established hostels, libraries, and night schools, and its activities included holding meetings, publishing, and petitioning. Through it Ambedkar brought together lawyers, teachers, clerks, and former soldiers from his community and tried to broaden support beyond the Mahars. His entry into legislative politics followed when he was nominated to the Bombay Legislative Council in 1926, where he spoke on questions affecting the depressed classes, including access to public facilities and education.

Why did independent organization become central? Ambedkar and his colleagues concluded that existing structures were inadequate. Congress, though it passed resolutions against untouchability and Gandhi campaigned for its removal, was led by caste Hindus and treated the issue as one of reform within Hinduism, often subordinating it to national unity. Philanthropic organizations such as the Depressed Classes Mission, which were run by caste-Hindu reformers, were paternalistic. Government nominations to legislatures often chose individuals acceptable to local elites. In this context, Ambedkar argued that only an organization led by the depressed classes themselves could be trusted to represent their interests and to hold others accountable.

The activities of this period combined education, legal advocacy, and agitation. Ambedkar and his associates pressed for the opening of schools, wells, and public places under existing laws and municipal resolutions, which on paper provided for equal access but were often disregarded in practice. A 1923 resolution moved in the Bombay Legislative Council by S. K. Bole, a social reformer, had declared that public institutions supported by government funds, including wells, tanks, and schools, should be open to all, and local bodies in the Konkan, including the Mahad municipality, had passed corresponding resolutions. The gap between the formal legal position and social practice would be tested at Mahad (§80.15), and it exemplifies a broader pattern: rights on paper required collective action to become rights in fact.

Language and terminology require care. In the colonial period, the official term for communities subject to untouchability was "Depressed Classes," and sometimes "Exterior" or "Scheduled" castes later. Reformers used terms such as Harijan, popularized by Gandhi in the 1930s, and communities themselves used names such as Adi-Dravida, Adi-Hindu, and Ad Dharm. The term "Dalit," meaning "broken" or "crushed" in Marathi and Hindi, gained wide currency much later, notably from the 1970s, as an assertive self-designation. Historians therefore use "Depressed Classes" when describing the colonial category and "Dalit" cautiously, usually for later periods or as a general modern term for the communities concerned, while noting that it was not the term mostly used in the 1920s.

Historical Vocabulary
Depressed Classes and Dalit

Depressed Classes was the administrative and political term used by the colonial government and by many Indian politicians in the early twentieth century for communities subject to untouchability and associated disabilities. It was an official classification and carried the paternalistic implication of people to be "uplifted." Dalit is a later term, widely adopted from the 1970s as a self-chosen designation emphasizing the experience of oppression and a claim to dignity and political agency.

The two terms refer to overlapping but not identical groups, and they carry different political meanings. Students should use the historical terminology of the period when quoting sources and explain modern usage when it is employed in analysis.

Section 80.15

Mahad, 1927: Public Water, Civil Equality, and the Challenge to Caste Authority

Mahad, a small town in Kolaba district on the Konkan coast, was a trading and administrative center with a population including a substantial number of Mahars and other depressed-class families, many of whom had served in the army or worked in transport and mills in Bombay. The town's Chavdar tank was a large public water source. Following S. K. Bole's resolution in the Bombay Legislative Council in August 1923 and the provincial government's subsequent direction, the Mahad municipality resolved on 5 January 1924 that it had no objection to people subjected to untouchability using the tank. Yet the resolution was not implemented, and untouchables continued to be barred from the tank by caste-Hindu custom and by the fear of reprisal.

On 19–20 March 1927 a Kolaba District Depressed Classes Conference met at Mahad, with Ambedkar present and playing a leading role in its direction, and with local leaders and organizers including A. V. Chitre, Sahasrabuddhe, and others. Several thousand delegates assembled; accounts of the numbers vary. The conference passed resolutions on education, on opposing customary services that the community was expected to perform, and on other issues. On the second day, after the formal proceedings, Ambedkar led the delegates in a procession to the Chavdar tank. At the tank he took water in his hands and drank, and the delegates followed. The act was peaceful and symbolic as well as practical: by drinking from the tank, the delegates exercised a right that the municipality had formally recognized and that custom denied.

The reaction of the local caste-Hindu community was hostile. Rumors spread that the delegates planned to enter the Veereshwar temple, a charge that organizers denied and that had no basis in the program. A group of caste Hindus attacked some of the delegates, beating a number of people and damaging property in the town, though the main body of delegates had begun to disperse. Afterward the caste-Hindu community performed a ritual of purification of the tank, which reasserted the idea that the touch of untouchables polluted the water. Under pressure, the municipality revoked its access resolution on 4 August 1927. In December, caste-Hindu plaintiffs sought an injunction and claimed exclusive rights to the tank, which was publicly held; the ownership claim was later abandoned on appeal. The legal dispute continued until the Bombay High Court rejected the exclusionary claim on 17 March 1937, showing how slowly formally recognized rights became enforceable.

To understand what the Mahad Satyagraha meant, one must consider both its practical demand and its symbolic dimension. Practically, access to drinking water was a basic need, and exclusion from public sources forced untouchable families to depend on the discretion of caste-Hindu neighbors, to walk long distances to inferior sources, or to use polluted supplies. The demand was modest in form: no new right was sought beyond what the law already seemed to grant. The symbolic dimension was larger. Drinking from the tank asserted that the Depressed Classes were equal members of the public, entitled to the same civic facilities as others, and that the legitimacy of customary exclusion could be tested before law and public opinion. Ambedkar presented the issue as a question of civil rights and equal citizenship, in contrast to a view of untouchability as a question of charity or of Hindu religious reform.

The second Mahad conference was held at the end of December 1927. Organizers had planned to resume the satyagraha at the tank, but because of the pending court case they decided not to proceed with it, and the gathering turned to other issues. On 25 December 1927, in a public ceremony, the participants burned a copy of the Manusmriti (the Laws of Manu), a Sanskrit legal and ritual text that in the dharmashastra tradition set out rules of caste hierarchy and the duties and disabilities associated with it. The burning, carried out in the presence of Ambedkar, with the assistance of reform-minded Brahmin associates such as G. N. Sahasrabuddhe according to accounts, was a deliberate political and symbolic act. The participants adopted a resolution condemning the text for its sanction of inequality. Ambedkar was aware that the text was not a legal code in force and that its historical authority was complex, but he argued that it stood as a symbol of the ideology of caste hierarchy and its justification, and that burning it expressed rejection of that ideology.

The burning provoked a storm of controversy. Orthodox Hindus condemned it as sacrilege; some reformers, including Gandhi's supporters, were uneasy; and some critics argued that it confused a historical text with contemporary practice. Supporters countered that texts that had legitimated oppression could not be treated as neutral. Historians view the event as a landmark in the development of Dalit political culture: the public rejection of a canonical text marked a move from petitioning for relief toward asserting equal citizenship on the community's own terms, and it set a precedent for later symbolic acts. In 1927 and 1928 further conferences and local campaigns followed, and the anniversary of the burning came to be remembered in later Dalit tradition.

Mahad also illustrates the limitations and tensions of the period. The Satyagraha drew on Gandhian methods but was independent of Congress, and it showed that satyagraha could be used against caste authority, not only against the colonial state. Caste-Hindu reformers who sympathized with the cause were divided over methods. The violence that followed demonstrated the strength of resistance, and the legal wrangling showed the limits of municipal resolutions without enforcement.

Section 80.16

Dalit Political Movements beyond Mahad: Adi-Hindu, Ad Dharm, and Regional Organizations

Ambedkar was the most prominent but not the only leader of oppressed-caste movements. In many regions, communities subject to untouchability developed their own organizations, ideologies, and leaders, often with histories that predated or were independent of Ambedkar's activity.

In the United Provinces, the Adi-Hindu movement was associated with Swami Achhutanand (born Harihar Das, 1879–1933), who had worked as a preacher and Arya Samaj sympathizer before developing his own approach. He and his associates argued that the untouchable communities were the original, indigenous inhabitants of India, the "Adi-Hindus" or original Hindus, who had been subjugated by later Aryan invaders. This reinterpretation of history, which drew on contemporary theories of Aryan invasion discussed in Chapter 5 and revisited in Chapter 111, gave a historical basis for political claims: the Dalits were entitled to respect and rights as the native inhabitants. Achhutanand established associations, held conferences in the 1920s in cities such as Kanpur, Delhi, and Allahabad, and published pamphlets. His movement sought recognition as a distinct community, representation in councils, and educational and economic advancement, and it was critical of both Congress and the Hindu reform organizations.

In Punjab, Mangoo Ram (Mugowalia) of Jalandhar district, who had worked abroad in the United States and had links with the Ghadar movement before returning, led the Ad Dharm movement. Ad Dharm organized Chamars and other depressed castes in the Doaba region and presented them as followers of an indigenous, ancient religion distinct from both Hinduism and Sikhism and Islam, with its own religious tradition drawing on saints such as Ravidas and Kabir. The Ad Dharm Mandal was organized in 1926, and its leaders sought recognition of Ad Dharmis as a separate religious community in the census, which would give them representation as a distinct group. In the 1931 census a large number of persons in Punjab returned themselves as Ad Dharmis, which was a significant demonstration of the movement's reach. The movement also set up schools and promoted a sense of community pride. Its political demands were pursued in the Punjab context, where the competing claims of Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs shaped the politics of representation.

In southern India, Adi-Dravida organizations had a long history. Iyothee Thass (1845–1914), a Tamil physician and scholar from the Nilgiris, argued that the Paraiyars and other depressed castes were the original Buddhists of the region, and in 1898 he organized the Sakya Buddhist Society and later published the journal Tamizhan (1907–), advocating a Buddhist identity as a way out of caste hierarchy. His work prefigured the later turn to Buddhism by Ambedkar and others. Rettamalai Srinivasan (1859–1945), a Paraiyar leader from the Madras Presidency, organized the Paraiyar Mahajana Sabha in the 1890s, edited a journal, and represented the depressed classes in the Madras legislative council. Later in the 1920s and early 1930s he worked at the national level, including attending the Round Table Conference, as Chapter 81 notes. M. C. Rajah, a leader of the Adi-Dravidas, held office as a nominated member of the Council and campaigned for education and civil rights, at times in cooperation with the Justice Party and at times in friction with it.

Comparing these movements shows several approaches. Some emphasized political representation, seeking reserved or separate seats and recognition in the census or the franchise. Others emphasized religious reinterpretation: Ad Dharm and Adi-Hindu, which presented untouchable communities as followers of ancient indigenous traditions, and Iyothee Thass, who looked to Buddhism. Others stressed educational and economic development, building schools, hostels, and cooperative societies. Many combined these strategies. The movements also differed in their relationship to Hinduism: some aimed at reform within it, others at distinct identity. They differed too in their relations with Congress, with the colonial government, and with Ambedkar, whose personal contacts with leaders such as Mangoo Ram developed later in the 1930s.

These regional movements often had to deal with the divisions within the Dalit population itself. Caste distinctions persisted among communities classed as untouchable, and leaders sometimes represented one jati more than others. The category "Depressed Classes" grouped together communities with different histories, occupations, and rank, and the demand for unity coexisted with rivalries. Historians caution against treating "Dalit politics" as a single movement, and prefer to see a plurality of movements that sometimes converged and sometimes competed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Reform Traditions before 1916 and the Movements of the 1920s

What Changed

  • Reform that was often led by individuals and small societies became mass organization through unions, kisan sabhas, parties, and conferences
  • Petitions to authority gave way, in many cases, to direct action: satyagrahas at Vaikom, Mahad, and Bardoli, strikes, and refusal of dues
  • Oppressed communities increasingly spoke for themselves rather than through sympathetic reformers
  • The press expanded in regional languages, from Kudi Arasu to Mooknayak, creating new publics
  • The constitutional reforms of 1919 gave representation a concrete institutional form, shifting debates toward franchise and reserved seats

What Stayed the Same

  • The central concerns of education, access to public goods, and dignity continued from the Phules and earlier reformers
  • Many leaders still depended on patrons, including princely rulers like Shahu Maharaj and Sayajirao Gaekwad, and on sympathetic officials
  • Women's labor and women's rights were still often subordinated to other agendas
  • Local elites continued to resist, and legal rights remained far from social practice

Who experienced change most directly? Urban workers, educated members of oppressed communities, and participants in organized campaigns experienced the shift most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Landless laborers, women in rural households, and communities far from organizing centers faced conditions that changed little in this period.

Why the pattern matters: The movements of the 1920s drew on earlier traditions but transformed their scale, methods, and claims, while exposing the persistence of structures that organization alone could not quickly alter.

Section 80.17

Women Organize: Suffrage, Education, and the Women's Indian Association

Women's public activity in South Asia had earlier roots. Chapter 74 described the nineteenth-century reform movements that debated widow remarriage, child marriage, and women's education, and the work of reformers such as Pandita Ramabai, Savitribai Phule, and Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain. Most nineteenth-century reform, however, was proposed by men on women's behalf. The distinctive feature of the 1910s and 1920s was the formation of associations led and run by women, who articulated demands in their own name and pursued them as a political constituency.

The Women's Indian Association (WIA) was founded in Adyar, Madras, in 1917. Its founders included the Irish-born Theosophist and educator Margaret Cousins, Dorothy Jinarajadasa, and Annie Besant, who as leader of the Home Rule League gave the organization visibility, together with Indian women such as Malathi Patwardhan and Ammu Swaminathan, and, in its early years, Sarojini Naidu and others. The WIA established branches in many towns, published a journal, Stri Dharma, from 1918, and set out aims that included promoting women's education, opposing child marriage and the exclusion of widows, and securing the franchise. Its membership consisted largely of educated, urban, middle-class women, a limitation that critics later emphasized, though it also had members in smaller towns.

The central political campaign of the early WIA was for the vote. In December 1917, when the Secretary of State for India, Edwin Montagu, visited India to consult on constitutional reform, a deputation of women led by Sarojini Naidu and including members of the WIA presented a memorandum asking that women be given the franchise on the same terms as men. The demand was supported by sympathetic British women's organizations and by some Indian men. Montagu received the deputation courteously but the Montagu-Chelmsford Report of 1918 declined to recommend women's franchise, arguing that Indian opinion was not yet ready and leaving the question of women's enfranchisement to be decided later.

The Government of India Act of 1919 did not enfranchise women directly. Instead, it left it to provincial legislatures to decide, by resolution, whether to remove the sex disqualification from the franchise. This produced a variable pattern. The Madras Legislative Council, under the Justice Party ministry, adopted such a resolution in 1921, and Bombay followed in the same year; other provinces acted in the following years, and by about 1930 all provinces had removed the sex bar, though not necessarily on equal terms with men. The rule of Travancore and some other princely states gave women the vote earlier. In 1926 the Madras Presidency took a further step, allowing women to stand for election to the council, and Muthulakshmi Reddi, a physician and WIA activist, was nominated to the Madras Legislative Council in 1927 and became its deputy president, the first woman to hold such a position in a legislative body in India.

Yet the enfranchisement achieved in the 1920s was narrow. Voting qualifications were based on property, tax payment, or educational attainment, and women qualified mainly as wives of propertied men or as owners of property in their own right, so the number of women voters was very small compared with the adult female population. Women in the great majority of rural households, and poor women generally, were excluded, as were most men, and the electorate under the 1919 Act was only a small fraction of the population. This property-based design meant that women's formal inclusion coexisted with the exclusion of most women, and it later led women's organizations to demand universal adult franchise, a demand that became prominent by the end of the 1920s.

Women's associations also worked on education, a central demand. They campaigned for the expansion of girls' schools, the improvement of teacher training, and access to higher education and professional courses such as medicine. The Women's University founded in Pune by D. K. Karve in 1916 and the Lady Hardinge Medical College in Delhi (1916) are among the institutions that expanded opportunities for women in this period.

It is important to distinguish women's participation in nationalist campaigns from women's campaigns for rights under their own leadership. Women took part in Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience, in picketing and in processions, and Gandhi's appeal drew many into public life, a subject treated in Chapters 79 and 81. This participation often gave women a new public presence. But women's organizations like the WIA made demands specific to women's status: the vote, education, marriage law, and legal rights. Sometimes the two were in tension, as when nationalist leaders discouraged women's separate demands as divisive, or when the colonial government invited women's organizations to testify about reforms.

Women leaders also debated whether to demand reserved seats or equal treatment. At the end of 1917 the WIA deputation sought the vote on the same terms as men and not reserved seats; later, as the franchise question was reconsidered in the late 1920s, women's organizations repeatedly stated opposition to separate electorates for women and demanded equal rights, a position that Chapter 81 will explain in the context of the constitutional negotiations of the 1930s.

Section 80.18

The All India Women's Conference, Marriage Reform, and Women's Rights, 1927–1929

The All India Women's Conference (AIWC) grew from a series of regional women's conferences on education. Margaret Cousins took the initiative in 1926 to invite women across the country to meetings that would discuss women's education and propose reforms. The first session of the AIWC met in Pune in January 1927, at Fergusson College, with the Maharani Chimnabai Gaekwad of Baroda, a ruling-house woman known for her support of women's education, presiding over the session. Women from many provinces and communities attended, including Hindu, Muslim, Parsi, and Christian delegates, and its leadership included Sarojini Naidu, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Hansa Mehta, Begum Hamid Ali and others who became prominent in the movement.

The conference's founding focus was education. It called for the expansion of girls' schooling, a curriculum suited to girls' needs, teacher training, and the provision of higher education. In its first years the AIWC deliberately emphasized education and social welfare, partly to attract a broad membership and avoid immediate confrontation with orthodox opinion or with party politics.

The social issues the AIWC took up were closely linked to marriage. Child marriage, in which girls were married at young ages and often began married life in adolescence, had high costs: early pregnancy, maternal mortality, curtailed education, and subordination within the marital household. A long history of reform campaigns had addressed these issues, from the Age of Consent Act of 1891, which raised the age of consent for sexual intercourse to twelve after long controversy (Chapter 74), to later efforts. In 1927 the Indian Legislative Assembly considered proposals to raise the age of consent and to address child marriage. The publication in 1927 of Katherine Mayo's polemical book Mother India, which used the practice of child marriage to condemn Indian society and to justify British rule, provoked a furious reaction among Indian nationalists and complicated the debate: reformers were forced to argue for change without appearing to endorse colonial criticism, and many Indian women's leaders wrote in response, rejecting Mayo's generalizations while pressing for reform.

The legislative outcome was the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929, commonly called the Sarda Act after Harbilas Sarda, a member of the Central Legislative Assembly who introduced the bill. After debate and a government committee of inquiry, the measure passed the legislature in September 1929 and received the Governor General's assent on 1 October 1929. In its original form, the Act defined a child as a girl under fourteen or a boy under eighteen. Its passage was a landmark and was supported by the AIWC, the WIA, and many other organizations, which lobbied and held meetings in its favor. It is necessary to distinguish its passage from its commencement: the Act came into force on 1 April 1930, and the period in between saw a rush of marriages as families sought to marry children before the law took effect. Some orthodox groups and some Muslim organizations objected to the law as interference with religious practice, and the colonial government was reluctant to enforce measures that might provoke protest.

Why did legal reform not necessarily produce immediate change in social practice? The Sarda Act was weakly framed: it made child marriage an offense but did not render such marriages invalid, penalties were light, enforcement depended on complaints and on local officials who were often reluctant to act, and the law relied on proof of age in a society where births were not systematically registered. Social custom, family pressure, and fear of dishonor sustained early marriage, particularly in rural and poorer households. Census data later showed that child marriage persisted widely.

Women's legal rights beyond marriage were also debated. Reformers raised the matter of inheritance and property: under the dominant personal laws, women's rights to property were limited and varied by community and region. Proposals to improve widows' and daughters' rights were introduced but made little headway in the 1920s. Women's employment, including their exclusion from some professions and their low pay in industry, also drew comment, and the question of women's political representation continued to be debated. The AIWC's evolution in the late 1920s toward a more explicit advocacy of women's legal and political rights, including equal treatment in law, anticipated its role in the constitutional debates covered in Chapter 81.

Women's reform also had its limits and exclusions. Leadership was dominated by elite, educated, mostly upper-caste or upper-class women, and it often spoke for women in general without fully representing the concerns of working-class women, rural women, or women from depressed communities. Dalit women, for example, faced the double burden of caste and gender discrimination, and their particular concerns did not always find space in either the women's organizations or caste-based movements, which were led largely by men.

Section 80.19

Nationalism and Social Revolution: Competing Meanings of Freedom

The movements examined in this chapter did not share a single definition of freedom. For the national leadership of Congress, freedom meant, first, an end to colonial rule and the establishment of self-government, though what form that government should take was itself debated. For peasants, it meant relief from rent, eviction, and unjust assessment, and in the more radical versions control over the land they worked. For industrial workers, freedom meant a living wage, safe conditions, collective bargaining, and protection against arbitrary employer authority. For communists and socialists, it meant the end of exploitation by landlords and capitalists and the rise of a classless society. For non-Brahmin movements, it meant an end to Brahmin predominance in education and public office and, for the more radical, the end of priestly authority itself. For Ambedkar and other Dalit leaders, it meant civil equality, access to public goods, and independent political voice. For women's organizations, it meant education, the vote, and legal equality.

These meanings sometimes overlapped. A peasant movement could use Congress's organization, a labor union could draw on nationalist sentiment, and anti-caste reformers could cooperate with Gandhians at Vaikom. They could also collide. Gandhi's approach to social change relied on moral persuasion and on reform within Hindu society, and he regarded untouchability as a stain that caste Hindus must remove through repentance and self-purification. Ambedkar saw that framing as inadequate, because it left the decision about whether and how to end untouchability in the hands of the dominant groups; he emphasized rights, law, and the independent organization of the oppressed. Periyar's critique of religious authority put him at odds with Gandhi's belief that Hinduism could be reformed from within. Trade unionists and communists criticized the Gandhian doctrine of trusteeship as a means of preserving employers' power, while Gandhi worried that class conflict would divide the nation. Women's leaders had to navigate between nationalists who wanted unity and reformers who wanted legal change. It is misleading to reduce these differences to personal rivalries.

A central question is whether a movement could oppose British colonialism while maintaining inequalities of caste, gender, or economic power. The evidence of the 1920s suggests that it could. Congress campaigns relied on the support of landlords, industrialists, and orthodox leaders alongside peasants and workers, and the movement's leaders often chose to avoid issues that might alienate those allies. Anti-colonial nationalism, in other words, was compatible with the persistence of hierarchy, and some historians argue that nationalist leaders consciously subordinated social questions to unity. Others stress that Congress also adopted reform programs, such as the campaign against untouchability and the promotion of khadi and of women's participation, and that nationalism opened spaces in which other movements developed.

Overlapping identities complicate any tidy division into movements. Dalit agricultural laborers were both members of an oppressed caste and members of the rural working poor, and a movement addressing only caste or only class could not capture their situation. Women workers in mills, mines, and plantations faced employers' power and gendered subordination, and were underrepresented in the unions led by men. Non-Brahmin women, such as those who took part in the Self-Respect Movement, experienced both caste and gender hierarchies and sometimes found the movement more responsive on gender than other nationalist organizations. Untouchable mill workers in Bombay, confined to the lower-paid spinning departments, found that the union movement's class language did not always address the caste discrimination they faced. Participants moved among several constituencies, and the movements often failed to recognize the compound disadvantages some members faced.

These tensions are also visible in how communists treated caste. Marxist analysis tended to regard caste as part of an ideological superstructure that would fade as class consciousness developed, and thus to subordinate anti-caste demands to class struggle. Ambedkar and others argued that caste was a distinct system of graded inequality that class politics alone could not dissolve, because workers themselves were divided by caste. This disagreement, which Ambedkar later developed, stands as one of the enduring debates in modern South Asian political thought and is a reminder that "social revolution" had competing definitions.

Historical Debate

Did Nationalism Advance or Constrain Social Reform?

The question: Did the national movement create conditions favorable to social change, or did its emphasis on unity limit the pursuit of caste, class, and gender equality?

Interpretation A — Nationalism as enabling: Nationalist mobilization drew millions into politics, created networks, and placed issues such as untouchability, women's participation, and economic inequality on the public agenda. Gandhi's campaigns made the removal of untouchability a national question, and many reformers worked within Congress.
Interpretation B — Nationalism as constraining: Critics argue that the movement's leadership, drawn largely from dominant castes and classes, subordinated radical demands to national unity, relied on propertied allies, and framed social issues as matters for internal reform. On this view, independent movements of peasants, workers, Dalits, and women had to organize apart precisely because the national movement was limited.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Congress resolutions, leaders' writings, the records of the independent movements, and the outcomes of specific campaigns such as Vaikom, Bardoli, and Mahad. Historians differ on how to weigh stated commitments against practice and on how to treat variations among Congress leaders and regions.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the origins of postcolonial social policy, including reservations and land reform, and whether the independent movements are seen as extensions of or challenges to the national movement. Many scholars now see both processes at work.

Cause and Effect

Social Inequality → Organization → Collective Political Demands

Long-term causes

Colonial land and revenue systems, industrial growth under unequal conditions, caste hierarchy and unequal access to education and office, and the legal subordination of women created deep structural inequalities.

Short-term causes

Wartime inflation, revenue enhancements, wage cuts and rationalization, the constitutional reforms of 1919, the mass mobilization of Non-Cooperation, and the spread of print and new organizations encouraged groups to articulate grievances.

Immediate effects

Kisan sabhas, trade unions, parties, non-Brahmin and Dalit associations, and women's organizations formed; satyagrahas and strikes took place; legislation such as the Trade Unions Act and the Sarda Act was passed.

Long-term consequences

The movements widened the vocabulary of rights and representation, shaped the constitutional debates of the 1930s, and left legacies in later politics of caste, class, and gender.

Historical complexity: Inequality did not automatically produce organization. Leaders, resources, communication, legal openings, and the response of authorities all mattered, and many unequal groups, such as landless laborers, remained poorly organized. The outcomes also varied: Bardoli won a negotiated reduction, Eka was suppressed, and the Bombay strike was largely lost, so the same underlying inequality led to different results.

Section 80.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined five clusters of movements that, between 1916 and 1929, widened the political arena of colonial South Asia. Taken together they show that the struggle for freedom was not a single campaign directed at a single opponent.

The agrarian movements challenged landholding power and the revenue system. In Awadh, tenants organized Kisan Sabhas against rack-renting, unlawful dues, and eviction; the Eka movement showed the capacity of cultivators to create their own institutions and leadership; and Bardoli demonstrated that a disciplined, relatively prosperous peasantry could use satyagraha to secure a negotiated reduction in revenue. The contrasts among these cases, in class composition, in relations with Congress, and in outcome, illustrate the variety of rural politics. Labor organizations expanded industrial political participation. The AITUC created a national framework, the Trade Unions Act provided legal recognition, and the Bombay strike of 1928 showed the scale and risks of militant action. Communists introduced class analysis and an international frame, and were met with prosecution at Kanpur and Meerut.

The non-Brahmin and anti-caste movements raised the question of who controlled education, office, and ritual authority. The Justice Party made communal representation a matter of public policy and governed Madras for six years, yet its leadership remained largely elite. Vaikom pressed the issue of access to public roads and revealed both the possibilities and the limits of cross-caste cooperation. The Self-Respect Movement went further in attacking priestly authority and advocating rationalism and women's equality. Ambedkar's early scholarship and organizations, the Mahad Satyagraha, and the burning of the Manusmriti asserted equal citizenship for communities subject to untouchability and the need for independent political voice, while regional movements such as Adi-Hindu, Ad Dharm, and Adi-Dravida organizations showed that Dalit politics was plural. Women's associations, from the WIA to the AIWC, secured partial enfranchisement, extended educational opportunity, and achieved the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929, while also revealing the gap between legislation and social change.

Across these movements several patterns recur. First, inequality at the base of society, whether in land, work, caste status, or gender, generated demands that did not coincide with the demand for self-government. Second, new tools of organization, including associations, newspapers, conferences, and legal petition, allowed groups to speak for themselves. Third, outcomes depended on the strength of organization, the response of authorities, and the leverage available: movements with concrete, winnable demands and strong allies, such as Bardoli, achieved more than those confronting entrenched local power. Fourth, formal reform often outpaced practice, as the Mahad tank and the Sarda Act show. Fifth, intersecting identities were poorly accommodated, with Dalit laborers, women workers, and non-Brahmin women often falling between movements.

The relationship between these movements and the national struggle is the chapter's most important interpretive issue. They reinforced one another in several ways: mass nationalism created political space and trained organizers; social movements expanded the base of anticolonial politics and gave it moral force; and the critique of colonial rule often supplied a language for challenging other forms of domination. But the projects were not identical. A victory over British rule did not by itself guarantee land for tenants, wages for workers, equal access to water for Dalits, or equal rights for women, and the leaders of different movements knew this.

These disagreements move to the center of Chapter 81. As constitutional reform returned to the agenda with the Simon Commission, the Nehru Report, and the Round Table Conferences, the question of who would be represented, and how, could no longer be postponed. Ambedkar's demand for independent Dalit representation, the claims of Muslim and other minority leaders, women's demands for equal political rights, and Congress's insistence on a single national claim all converged in the debates that followed. The developments of the 1920s examined in Chapter 80 are the background to the Communal Award and the Poona Pact, which Chapter 81 treats in full.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Indian nationalism in the 1910s and 1920s was not a single movement directed exclusively toward independence from British rule. Alongside and sometimes against the Congress-led campaigns examined in Chapter 79, peasants, workers, non-Brahmin groups, Dalit leaders, and women organized around grievances of their own and formulated their own meanings of freedom.

Division I examined agrarian politics. Colonial land systems produced varied relationships among landlords, tenants, cultivators, and laborers, and the grievances of rent, eviction, forced labor, and revenue assessment often differed from Congress's constitutional goals. The Awadh Kisan Sabhas and the Eka movement demonstrated peasant organization built on local ritual and leadership, and were met with repression and limited concessions. Bardoli in 1928, under Vallabhbhai Patel, was a more disciplined campaign by landholding peasants that secured a reduction through negotiation, though laborers gained little.

Division II turned to industrial labor. Workers in the mills, mines, railways, and plantations lived in crowded housing and worked in hazardous conditions, with caste and community shaping their position. The AITUC of 1920 and the Trade Unions Act of 1926 created institutional frameworks, while the Bombay textile strike of 1928 demonstrated both the scale of militant labor and its costs. Communist and socialist ideas introduced a class analysis; the founding of the Communist Party is dated differently by different traditions, and the state responded with the Kanpur and Meerut prosecutions.

Division III examined non-Brahmin politics. Rooted in the tradition of the Phules and in the unequal distribution of education and office, non-Brahmin movements developed differently in Maharashtra and Madras. The Justice Party governed Madras from 1920 to 1926 and introduced communal representation in employment, while remaining largely elite. Vaikom pressed access to roads near a temple in Travancore and ended in a partial settlement. Periyar's Self-Respect Movement attacked Brahminical authority and promoted rationalism, marriage reform, and women's equality.

Division IV centered on Ambedkar. His education and early study of caste led him to argue that independence would not by itself end caste oppression. Through Mooknayak and the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha, he built independent organization. The Mahad Satyagraha of March 1927 asserted the right of untouchables to use a public tank, and the burning of the Manusmriti in December 1927 rejected the textual sanction for caste hierarchy. Regional movements, including Adi-Hindu in the United Provinces, Ad Dharm in Punjab, and Adi-Dravida and Buddhist-oriented movements in the south, show that Dalit politics was plural and not confined to Ambedkar.

Division V examined women's organizations. The Women's Indian Association and the All India Women's Conference pursued the franchise, education, and legal reform under women's own leadership. Provincial enfranchisement came on property-based terms that excluded most women, and the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929, in force from April 1930, illustrated how legal reform could outrun social practice.

The synthesis in §80.19 compared the different meanings of freedom held by Congress nationalists, peasants, workers, communists, non-Brahmin leaders, Dalit activists, and women reformers. It argued that the movements could reinforce one another yet remained distinct, that anti-colonial nationalism was compatible with the persistence of other hierarchies, and that overlapping identities were poorly served by movements organized around a single axis of inequality.

Methodologically, the chapter has emphasized the varied nature of the evidence: petitions, union proceedings, party publications, newspapers, conference resolutions, and legislative records, each of which preserves some voices and obscures others. Peasants, laborers, Dalit women, and others are often heard only through intermediaries, and historians must read sources critically and acknowledge silences.

The movements of this chapter prepared the ground for the constitutional debates of the 1930s. As Chapter 81 shows, the questions of representation, separate electorates, and the relation of social reform to national unity moved to the center of Indian politics, and the independent movements examined here became principal actors in that contest.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the history of freedom in South Asia cannot be told only as the history of a nation freeing itself from a foreign power. It was also the history of people asking what freedom would mean in the village, the factory, the temple road, the household, and the legislature. Their answers differed, and the differences were not trivial.

The movements examined here shaped much that followed. The demand for communal representation and reservations in office and education, the independent political voice of oppressed castes, the legal recognition of trade unions, the language of class, and the claim of women to be citizens in their own right all re-emerge in the constitutional debates of the 1930s, in the making of the Constitution examined in Chapter 88, and in the politics of the postcolonial states. The tension between formal rights and social practice, visible at the Chavdar tank and in the Sarda Act, also persisted well beyond 1929.

Within the six-volume sequence, this chapter links the mass nationalism of Chapter 79 to the constitutional contest of Chapter 81 and widens the cast of historical actors. It reminds students that Gandhi, Congress, and the British state were not the only agents of change, and that a history of the period must account for peasants in Awadh, mill hands in Bombay, Mahars at Mahad, Self-Respect activists in Madras, and women organizing in Pune and Adyar. Chapter 81 now takes up the question these movements made unavoidable: who would speak for India, and on what terms.
