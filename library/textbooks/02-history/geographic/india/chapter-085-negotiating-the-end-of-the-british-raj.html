Negotiating the End of the British Raj

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 24 March 1946, three members of the British Cabinet landed in Delhi carrying an unusual mandate. They had not come to decide whether British rule in India would end; the Labour government had already accepted that it would. They had come to discover what, and who, would replace it. Over the following seventeen months, a Secretary of State, a Viceroy, and a succession of Indian leaders would argue over that question in drawing rooms, hill stations, legislative chambers, and finally the streets of Calcutta, Noakhali, Bihar, and Punjab. By August 1947 the answer would be two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, rather than a single self-governing India, the outcome preferred by Congress and many others but opposed by the Muslim League's increasingly organized Pakistan movement.

Chapter 84 followed South Asia through the wartime crises of Quit India, the Indian National Army, and the Bengal famine of 1943, crises that eroded the political legitimacy of the colonial state even as its armies and administrators won the war in the east. This chapter begins where that erosion had already done its work. By the summer of 1945 the central question was no longer whether Britain would transfer power but under what constitutional arrangement, to whom, and on what timetable. The chapter's central argument is that the end of British rule was agreed upon before the political form of independence had been settled. Britain's withdrawal was the product of structural imperial weakness. The division of the subcontinent was the product of a distinct set of negotiations, electoral contests, mutual suspicions, and decisions by British and Indian leaders that might, at several points, have gone differently.

That distinction governs how the chapter is organized. Division I examines the postwar crisis of British authority: the failed Simla Conference of 1945, the Indian National Army trials, the Royal Indian Navy uprising, and the elections of 1945–1946 that gave the Congress and the Muslim League rival but powerful claims to speak for large constituencies. Division II turns to the Cabinet Mission, the last comprehensive attempt to design a framework for a united subcontinent, and to the conflicting constitutional priorities that Congress and the League brought to it. Division III follows the collapse of the Cabinet Mission settlement, the communal violence that began in Calcutta in August 1946, and the uneasy Interim Government. Division IV traces the last efforts at a common settlement, Prime Minister Clement Attlee's announcement of February 1947, the territorial alternatives raised by Punjab, Bengal, Assam, and the Frontier, and Louis Mountbatten's rapid negotiations. Division V examines the 3 June Plan, the Indian Independence Act of 18 July 1947, and the preparations for the transfer of power.

The chapter has deliberately limited boundaries. It explains why partition was chosen; it does not narrate Partition itself. The Radcliffe boundary awards, the division of Punjab and Bengal on the ground, mass violence, refugee movement, gendered violence, dispossession, and the memory of these events belong to Chapter 86. The integration of the princely states and the first India–Pakistan war belong to Chapter 87, and the drafting of the 1950 Constitution belongs to Chapter 88. Chapter 85 reaches back to Chapter 84 for wartime context only when needed, and it ends at the transfer of power, where Chapter 86 begins.

The history of these negotiations has often been told as a drama of a few personalities: Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Jinnah, Wavell, and Mountbatten. Individuals mattered, and this chapter examines their decisions closely. But they acted within constraints that no individual created: a depleted British treasury, an army and civil service strained by war, a restricted electorate organized into religious constituencies, provinces whose political arithmetic did not fit any simple two-party division, and a landscape of rising insecurity. The chapter therefore pairs the high politics of negotiation with attention to the soldiers, sailors, voters, party workers, and provincial politicians through whom national decisions were experienced and sometimes shaped.

The evidence for this period is unusually abundant and unusually contested. British official records, especially the twelve-volume collection The Transfer of Power, 1942–47, preserve the correspondence of Viceroys, Secretaries of State, and Cabinet committees. Parliamentary debates preserve public explanations that sometimes differed from private reasoning. Party resolutions, speeches, newspapers, memoirs by participants such as Maulana Azad and V. P. Menon, and provincial and electoral records supplement the official archive. Each source has a perspective, and later scholarship has argued sharply over how to weigh them. The chapter's source windows invite students to read this evidence as historians do: asking what a document establishes, whose view it records, and what it leaves out.

Students who complete this chapter should be able to explain why Britain left in 1947, why the Cabinet Mission Plan failed to produce a united constitutional settlement, how the elections and communal violence reshaped political possibilities, and how the 3 June Plan came to be accepted. They should also be able to resist two tempting simplifications: that Partition was foreordained by deep and unchanging religious antagonism, and that it was merely the result of one conference, one speech, or one man's haste.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the end of the Second World War, Labour's election victory, and Britain's economic and military position changed the context of Indian constitutional negotiations.
  2. Analyze why the Simla Conference of 1945 failed, with particular attention to the dispute over who could nominate Muslim members of the Executive Council.
  3. Assess the political significance of the Indian National Army trials and the Royal Indian Navy uprising without overstating their effect on the loyalty of the armed forces as a whole.
  4. Interpret the results of the 1945–1946 elections, distinguishing seats, votes, separate electorates, restricted franchise, and provincial variation.
  5. Compare the constitutional priorities of Congress and the Muslim League on central authority, provincial autonomy, minority safeguards, and sovereignty.
  6. Describe the provisions of the Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946, distinguishing the Union, the three Sections, the groups, and the Constituent Assembly procedure.
  7. Distinguish the text of the Cabinet Mission Plan from the rival interpretations of grouping advanced by Congress, the League, and the British government.
  8. Evaluate the causes and political consequences of Direct Action Day and the Calcutta violence of August 1946, while attributing casualty estimates and avoiding collective blame.
  9. Explain why the Interim Government and the Constituent Assembly failed to create a common constitutional program.
  10. Evaluate the reasons for Attlee's announcement of 20 February 1947 and the role of Mountbatten in accelerating negotiations.
  11. Analyze how the interests of Punjab, Bengal, Assam, the Frontier, and the Sikhs introduced territorial alternatives into negotiations.
  12. Contextualize the 3 June Plan and the Indian Independence Act as the political and legal decisions that ended British rule, distinguishing them from the later Radcliffe awards and from the history of Partition violence.

Historical Context

The constitutional structure that the negotiators of 1945–1947 inherited was the Government of India Act of 1935, examined in Chapter 81. That Act had created autonomous provincial governments, responsible to elected legislatures, in eleven provinces of British India: Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces and Berar, Bihar, Orissa, Bengal, Assam, the Punjab, Sindh, and the North-West Frontier Province. It had also provided for a federation joining British India to the princely states, but the federal provisions never came into operation, and at the centre the Viceroy's Executive Council remained responsible to the Crown rather than to the Central Legislative Assembly. The franchise was limited by property, tax, and educational qualifications; only a minority of adults could vote. Electorates for legislatures were organized by community, with separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, and other groups, a system that dated from the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 and that the Communal Award of 1932 had extended.

The Second World War loosened this structure. The Congress ministries had resigned in 1939 in protest at India's declaration of war without consultation (Chapter 83), and the Quit India movement of 1942 had been suppressed with the imprisonment of the Congress leadership (Chapter 84). During these years the Muslim League expanded its organization, particularly in provinces where it had previously been weak, and adopted the Lahore Resolution of 1940 demanding that Muslim-majority areas in the northwest and east be grouped to constitute independent states. The resolution did not specify borders, constitutional machinery, or the relationship of such states to the rest of the subcontinent, ambiguities that remained politically consequential. The war also strained the Indian economy through inflation, requisition, and shortage, and it left India, remarkably, a creditor to Britain, with sterling balances in London amounting to over a billion pounds.

Britain itself had changed. The Labour government elected in July 1945 under Clement Attlee was more sympathetic to Indian self-government than its Conservative predecessor, and it faced domestic priorities of reconstruction, a balance-of-payments crisis, and demands for demobilization of its own forces. It also inherited an administrative machine in India that was thinly staffed. Recruitment to the Indian Civil Service had slowed during the war, many British officials were exhausted and anxious to leave, and the senior ranks of the Indian Army were still largely British. The Viceroy, Field Marshal Lord Wavell, who had taken office in 1943, was a soldier, not a politician. He was increasingly pessimistic about the prospects of holding India by consent.

The central problem of 1945–1947 was thus one of representation. Congress claimed to speak for all Indians, Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and others, and pointed to its large membership and its nationalist record. The Muslim League, under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, claimed to be the sole spokesman of India's Muslims, who numbered roughly a quarter of the population of British India, and insisted that Muslims were a nation and not merely a minority. Other actors, including Sikh parties, the Unionist Party in Punjab, Scheduled Caste leaders such as B. R. Ambedkar, Muslims who opposed the League, regional parties, communists, and the rulers of the princely states, complicated any bilateral formula. Britain, in turn, was committed both to leaving and to leaving behind a settlement that would not collapse into civil war. This chapter traces how those commitments were negotiated and why they ultimately diverged.

The Postwar Crisis of British Authority

Military discontent, nationalist mobilization, British political change, and electoral competition reshape the end of empire.

Section 85.1

The End of World War II, the Wavell Plan, and the Simla Conference of 1945

When the war in Europe ended in May 1945, Lord Wavell had already been pressing London for a political initiative. The Congress leadership had been imprisoned since August 1942, the Muslim League governed or influenced several provinces, and the Viceroy's Executive Council, though largely Indian in composition, still answered only to the Viceroy and the Secretary of State. Wavell feared that a postwar India without any political settlement would face unrest while a weakened Britain lacked the means to suppress it. In June 1945 he returned from consultations in London with a proposal, announced on 14 June, to reconstitute the Executive Council so that, apart from the Viceroy and the Commander-in-Chief, all its members would be Indian political leaders. The Council would operate within the existing constitution, but it would be a step toward Indian control of the central government, and the portfolio of external affairs would be transferred to an Indian member.

The most consequential feature of the Wavell Plan was its principle of parity: the Council would contain equal numbers of caste Hindus and Muslims, with additional places for Scheduled Castes and Sikhs. For the League, parity was an affirmation that Muslims, a minority of roughly a quarter of the population, were a distinct community entitled to an equal voice in central government. For Congress, the phrase "caste Hindus" implied that Congress represented only one community, an implication it rejected, since its membership and leadership included Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, and Scheduled Castes, and its president, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, was himself a Muslim. The plan thus raised, in the form of a seemingly technical proportion, the underlying question of who represented whom.

Historical Vocabulary
Parity

In the Wavell Plan, parity meant numerical equality between "caste Hindu" and Muslim members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, regardless of the two communities' unequal population shares. Earlier constitutional arrangements, such as the Communal Award of 1932, had weighted communal representation in different ways, usually offering Muslims more seats than their population share in some provinces and fewer in others. Parity was therefore not simply "fairness" in a modern arithmetical sense but a negotiated political principle, intended to reassure minorities that the majority could not dominate central decisions. It mattered because it linked the League's claim to speak for all Muslims with the question of political equality between communities, a link that persisted in later demands for parity in constitutional bodies. Congress objected less to the number than to the principle that a party representing all communities should be treated as a Hindu organization.

The conference opened at Simla on 25 June 1945 with twenty-one invitees, including Azad for Congress, Jinnah for the League, the Unionist Premier of Punjab Khizr Hayat Tiwana, representatives of the Scheduled Castes and the Sikhs, and the premiers of the provinces. The discussion of the Executive Council's composition quickly narrowed to a single issue. Jinnah insisted that the League alone had the right to nominate all Muslim members of the Council. Congress, for its part, wished to nominate at least one Muslim from its own ranks. The Unionist Party, which governed Punjab with a large Muslim membership and which the League did not control, also claimed that a Unionist Muslim should be included. Wavell, who had hoped that lists of names could be negotiated, was unwilling to proceed over the League's objection, and Jinnah was unwilling to modify his demand. On 14 July 1945 Wavell ended the conference, taking responsibility for its failure upon himself.

The collapse of Simla mattered for three reasons. First, it gave Jinnah a decisive demonstration of the League's ability to veto any constitutional arrangement that did not recognize its representative claim, thereby strengthening his position among Muslim politicians who had previously been uncertain. Second, it showed Congress that the League's insistence on sole representation was not a rhetorical position that could be negotiated away. Third, it left the British government without an interim arrangement at the very moment when the political calendar was turning. Within two weeks of the conference's end, the results of the British general election were declared. Labour had won an overwhelming majority, and Clement Attlee, who had served as Deputy Prime Minister in the wartime coalition and had earlier sat on the Simon Commission on Indian constitutional reform, became Prime Minister.

The war's end altered the strategic calculus further. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945, and the formal surrender followed on 2 September. India had been a base for the war against Japan, and the Indian Army, which had expanded to over two million men, was now to be demobilized. Britain's finances, strained by the war and by the ending of American Lend-Lease aid in August 1945, made a long and expensive imperial presence unattractive. Wavell, meeting the new ministers in London in August, secured agreement that elections to the central and provincial legislatures would be held in the coming winter, as the last elections under the 1935 constitution, with a view to forming new governments and convening a constitution-making body. The announcement of elections on 21 August 1945 set the political agenda for the next year: the leaders emerging from prison or wartime restrictions would now have to compete for votes, and the competition would define the claims they carried to any future negotiating table.

Section 85.2

The Indian National Army Trials and the Mobilization of Nationalist Opinion

Chapter 84 examined the military history of the Indian National Army (INA): the recruitment of Indian prisoners of war after the fall of Singapore in 1942, the leadership of Subhas Chandra Bose from 1943, and the campaigns in Burma and northeastern India. This section is concerned not with that military history but with what happened when its survivors returned. After the Japanese surrender, thousands of INA personnel were taken into British custody. The Indian Army's commander-in-chief, General Sir Claude Auchinleck, and the Government of India decided to try a number of them, reasoning that the Indian Army's discipline and the principle of loyalty to the Crown required some form of accountability. They announced a policy of distinguishing categories among the prisoners: most would be released or dismissed, while those charged with brutality or with murder would face courts-martial.

The first trial, opened at the Red Fort in Delhi on 5 November 1945, was a joint court-martial of three officers: Captain Shah Nawaz Khan, Captain Prem Kumar Sahgal, and Lieutenant Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon. They were charged with waging war against the King-Emperor, and in some cases with further charges of murder or abetment of murder. The choice of defendants proved politically unfortunate for the government. The three were a Muslim, a Hindu, and a Sikh, and the symbolic force of a joint trial of three officers from different communities was impossible to miss. Indian public opinion, which since the war had been preoccupied with communal divisions, found in the accused a remarkable image of unity.

The Indian National Congress, which had been ambivalent about Bose's alliance with Japan, now made the defense of the accused a public cause. It established a Defence Committee, and the legal team, led by the veteran lawyer Bhulabhai Desai, included Tej Bahadur Sapru, Kailash Nath Katju, Asaf Ali, and Jawaharlal Nehru, who put on a barrister's gown for the first time in decades. The defense argued, among other points, that the accused had acted under the authority of the Provisional Government of Free India, and that international law recognized the right of a belligerent authority to demand the service of those under its control. The prosecution replied that the Indian soldiers had sworn an oath to the King-Emperor and that no government in exile could release them from it. The legal debate was inconclusive, but its political effect was far greater than its jurisprudential one: the courtroom became an arena in which the legitimacy of colonial authority itself was argued.

Primary Source Window

The INA Trial: Charges, Defense, and Public Reaction

Source type: Court-martial proceedings, defense statements, and press and party commentary · Date: November 1945–January 1946 · Place: Red Fort, Delhi, and Indian cities · Edition note: Students should consult documentary collections such as The Transfer of Power, 1942–47, vol. VI, and published trial records; the passages below are editorial paraphrases, not quotations.

Context: The court-martial was convened by the Government of India under military law. The defense was organized by Congress sympathizers, and press coverage in English and Indian-language newspapers made the proceedings a national event.

Editorial paraphrase: The prosecution presented the accused as officers who had broken their oath of allegiance and had taken up arms against the Crown alongside an enemy power. The defense presented them as patriots who had joined an Indian provisional government to liberate their country from foreign rule and who were entitled to treatment as belligerents. Commentary across the press, regardless of party, expressed sympathy for the accused as Indians who had fought for national independence, while many commentators also made clear that this sympathy did not extend to every aspect of Bose's alliance with Japan.

Analysis: The proceedings establish the legal arguments and the political reception of the trials. They do not by themselves establish the reliability of the Indian Army as an institution, nor the personal motives of every INA recruit, who joined for varied reasons including conviction, coercion in prison camps, and a desire to escape captivity. The sources represent the views of the court, the lawyers, and urban public opinion; the views of ordinary soldiers within the Indian Army, many of whom had fought against the INA in Burma, are underrepresented.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the composition of the defendants suggest about the political meaning of the trial?
  2. Who created these records, and for what purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from the public reaction that they cannot learn from the court record alone?
  4. What can historians not establish from these sources about the opinions of serving Indian soldiers?

Public reaction was extensive. In Calcutta, on 21 November 1945, student demonstrations in support of the INA defendants clashed with police and military pickets, and several demonstrators were killed in firing, an episode that produced a tram and transport strike and drew crowds of many communities. Further protests in Calcutta in February 1946, following the sentencing of Abdul Rashid, an INA officer, to seven years' imprisonment in a separate trial, again involved large numbers of students and workers and were met with force. In many cities, processions flew Congress, League, and communist flags together. The phrase "Chalo Delhi," associated with Bose, appeared on banners and walls. The League leadership also expressed support for the accused, though its response was more guarded than Congress's, and its leaders were attentive to the fact that Muslim officers were among those tried.

The court-martial concluded at the end of December 1945. The three defendants were found guilty of waging war against the King-Emperor, with the murder charges not sustained against some, and sentenced to transportation for life with cashiering. The Commander-in-Chief confirmed the findings but, on 3 January 1946, remitted the sentences of transportation, a decision that preserved the principle of the verdict while avoiding a political confrontation that the government could not afford. Subsequent trials were scaled back, and most INA personnel were released after screening. Auchinleck, whose papers show his concern about the effect of the trials on the Indian Army, regarded the experience as a warning.

The political significance of the INA trials lay less in their legal outcome than in their effect on how Indian politicians and British officials understood the political terrain. For Congress, the trials were an opportunity to reclaim a nationalist narrative that had been dominated, in the war years, by the suppression of Quit India. The party's leaders, who had been unsympathetic to Bose's alliance with Japan, now identified themselves with the soldiers he had led. For the British, the trials revealed that a significant section of public opinion, including Indian officers and civilians who had served the Raj, saw the INA men as heroes and not as traitors. It would be an overstatement to say that the trials destroyed British control of the army. The Indian Army remained, in the main, disciplined and obedient. But the episode showed that the moral basis on which the army had been used for imperial purposes could no longer be taken for granted, and that the political class had learned how to mobilize around an issue that cut across communal lines. Both points would weigh on the Labour government's thinking over the following year.

Section 85.3

The Royal Indian Navy Uprising of February 1946 and Military Discontent

The Royal Indian Navy (RIN) had expanded rapidly during the war, from a small force of a few thousand to many times that number, recruiting skilled ratings, including signalmen and telegraphists, who tended to be literate, urban, and politically aware. By 1946 many of these men were waiting for demobilization amid shortages of food, poor living conditions in shore establishments, and perceived discrimination: Indian ratings were paid less than their British counterparts, were served inferior food, and in some cases were subject to racial insults from British officers. The ratings were also aware of the INA trials, of the political ferment in the cities where they were stationed, and of rumors that the Indian Navy would be slow to benefit from postwar reforms.

The immediate trigger was a protest at the signals training establishment HMIS Talwar in Bombay. On 18 February 1946, ratings refused to eat or to obey orders, following the arrest of a signalman, B. C. Dutt, who was accused of writing slogans such as "Quit India" on a ship. Within a day the protest had spread to ships in Bombay harbor, and a Naval Central Strike Committee was formed, chaired by M. S. Khan, with Madan Singh among its leaders. The ratings presented demands that combined service grievances, such as better food, equal pay, and demobilization, with political demands, including the release of INA prisoners and other political prisoners and the withdrawal of Indian troops from Indonesia. The uprising eventually involved, by commonly cited estimates, about seventy to eighty ships and some twenty shore establishments, with about twenty thousand ratings, and spread beyond Bombay to Karachi, Madras, Calcutta, and other ports.

The responses of the political parties revealed their differing positions. The Communist Party of India, which was influential among naval and urban workers in Bombay, supported the ratings and called a general strike in the city on 22 February. The Congress leadership, notably Vallabhbhai Patel, and the League leadership, including Jinnah, both withheld support for the uprising and urged the ratings to surrender. Congress leaders were concerned about indiscipline in the armed services that might one day belong to an independent India, and about the leadership of the movement passing to the left. Gandhi publicly criticized the ratings' action. The British authorities, under Rear Admiral J. H. Godfrey, the Flag Officer commanding in Bombay, brought in troops and the Royal Navy, and threatened to use force. After Patel assured the ratings that their grievances would be considered and that they would not be victimized, the ratings surrendered on 23 February.

The civilian dimension of the uprising was substantial and violent. In Bombay, the strike called by the communists drew workers into the streets, and clashes with police and troops resulted in many deaths, with commonly cited estimates of civilian fatalities running to over two hundred. The sources differ, and students should regard exact figures as uncertain. After the ratings' surrender, many were dismissed from service, and the promise of no victimization was not fully honored; the dismissed ratings were not reinstated in independent India or Pakistan, a grievance that persisted for decades.

British officials were alarmed. The uprising suggested that politicization had reached the technical branches of the armed forces, and officers who had earlier dismissed the INA as a collaborationist aberration now saw evidence of discontent in a loyal service. But the historical conclusion should be measured. The Indian Army, in the main, did not follow the ratings, and the uprising was confined to the navy and to a few units of the Royal Indian Air Force. The unreliability of the whole force was a possibility that British planners feared and discussed, rather than an established fact in February 1946. The uprising contributed to a sense of urgency and a belief among ministers that the Raj might not be sustained by force in a crisis, but it did not by itself end British rule. Its significance should be understood together with the INA trials, the strikes of 1945–1946, and the demobilization problem, as one of the postwar pressures that reduced the British government's confidence in its ability to govern India by coercion over the long term.

For such a man, the political outcome of the negotiations that followed would be experienced not as constitutional abstraction but as a question of whether his service would be recognized by the state that emerged.

Section 85.4

The Elections of 1945–1946: Congress, the Muslim League, and Competing Claims to Representation

The elections held between December 1945 and the spring of 1946 were the last under the 1935 Act and the first since 1937. They were held on a restricted franchise, which enfranchised roughly a tenth of the population on the basis of property, tax payment, and educational qualifications, and on the basis of separate communal electorates. Voters were enrolled on separate rolls for Muslim, Sikh, and "general" (that is, non-Muslim, non-Sikh) constituencies, and in each reserved constituency candidates competed only for the votes of members of that community. This meant that the elections could not be a direct comparison of Congress and the League among the whole electorate. Instead they tested, in parallel, Congress's strength among general voters and the League's among Muslim voters, with other parties and independents competing in both.

The first round, in December 1945, elected the Central Legislative Assembly, which had one hundred and two elected seats. Congress won the large majority of the general constituencies, with 57 of the 102 elected seats, while the Muslim League won all thirty Muslim seats and received around eighty-six percent of the Muslim vote, according to commonly cited figures. The remaining seats went to independents, the Akali Sikhs, and European members. In the provincial elections held in January to April 1946, Congress won absolute majorities in Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, Assam, and the North-West Frontier Province. The League won the great majority of Muslim-reserved seats in the provinces, representing the large majority of Muslim-reserved constituencies, though the number of reserved places and party classifications vary among compilations. In Bengal, the League won 113 or 114 Muslim seats depending on the compilation; in Sind and Punjab it emerged as the largest party, without an overall majority in Punjab.

The meaning of these results was, and remains, debated. For the League, the elections were a vindication. Jinnah could now claim, with more justification than at any earlier time, that the League represented the overwhelming majority of Muslim voters, and that no settlement could be reached that bypassed it. Congress, for its part, could claim to have won the general constituencies and to govern a majority of provinces, and argued that it spoke for the nationalist movement as a whole, including Muslims who supported it, notably in the Frontier. The elections thus strengthened the rival representative claims without resolving their conflict. Neither result was a plebiscite on a precisely defined constitutional outcome. A Muslim voter who supported the League in 1946 might have been voting for Pakistan as an independent state, for a loosely federal arrangement in which Muslim-majority provinces would have strong autonomy, for the protection of Muslim interests against perceived Hindu majority domination, or for the League as the strongest available defender of community interests. The campaign slogans were powerful because they were flexible.

Provincial variation, moreover, complicated the national picture. In the Frontier, a Muslim-majority province, the Congress and the Khudai Khidmatgar movement of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a Pashtun movement with a strong commitment to nonviolence and to cooperation with Congress, won a majority, and the League was in a minority. In Bengal, the League formed a ministry under Suhrawardy, but with a Hindu minority of about forty-five percent of the population represented chiefly by Congress and the Hindu Mahasabha. In Assam, a Congress ministry governed a province with a large Muslim population, including in the Sylhet district. The map of mandates thus did not divide cleanly into a Hindu India and a Muslim India; it was a patchwork in which the most politically important provinces, Punjab and Bengal, were divided internally.

Section 85.5

Congress and the Question of Federation: National Unity, Sovereignty, and Central Authority

By 1946, Congress leaders agreed on certain fundamentals. They wanted a united, independent India, governed by a constitution framed by a Constituent Assembly elected by Indians, and free of British control. They differed, however, on how much power the centre should hold, how to protect minorities, and how to respond to the League's demands. The positions of the leading figures reflected different political temperaments as well as different constitutional ideas.

Jawaharlal Nehru, who became Congress president in July 1946, favored a strong centre capable of planning economic development and ensuring national unity. His political vision included secularism, socialist-inspired planning, and an internationalist foreign policy; he regarded the League's demand as an obstacle to such a program and doubted that the Muslim masses were committed to its communal logic. Vallabhbhai Patel, a pragmatist and organizational leader, was concerned with order, administration, and the practical consequences of a weak centre; he was more skeptical than Nehru about the prospect of accommodating the League. Maulana Azad, who led the Congress delegation to the Cabinet Mission, was the Congress's most prominent Muslim leader, committed to a composite nationalism in which Hindus and Muslims were partners, and he believed that the Cabinet Mission scheme offered Muslims sufficient safeguards. Gandhi, though no longer formally a Congress leader, remained its moral authority, and he opposed any division of India, which he described as a vivisection. He also doubted constitutional arrangements that would perpetuate communal categories.

The Congress position distinguished among several matters. It accepted an elected Constituent Assembly and a united India. It was prepared to accept a federal system in which provinces would have substantial autonomy, and in practice it had governed under such a system between 1937 and 1939. It was reluctant, however, to accept a centre confined to only three subjects. Many Congress leaders argued that defense, foreign affairs, and communications could not be separated from finance, customs, and economic planning without making the centre unworkable. A government that had to ask provinces for revenue and could not regulate the economy would struggle to integrate a vast and diverse country or to defend it. There was also a more principled objection to the compulsory grouping of provinces into sections with the power to frame their own constitutions, which Congress feared would entrench communal blocs. Provinces such as Assam and the Frontier, which Congress governed or influenced, were assigned to sections in which Muslim-majority provinces would dominate. Congress leaders considered it unacceptable that these provinces could be compelled to join groups dominated by the League against the wishes of their own legislatures.

A further question concerned sovereignty. Congress insisted that a Constituent Assembly, once elected, would be a sovereign body, free to determine its own procedures and to frame a constitution without external dictation. The British, by contrast, had indicated that certain basic provisions would be required if the Assembly was to represent all communities and that a treaty would be needed to settle matters between Britain and India. The difference between a sovereign Assembly and an Assembly bound by procedures set by the Cabinet Mission would become a central point of conflict in July 1946, as §85.9 explains. Congress's concerns about central authority were not purely theoretical: they reflected fears that an India that could not defend its borders, regulate its economy, or ensure the integrity of its territory would be vulnerable to external pressure and to the princely states' separatism, and a preference for a strong state as the instrument of social and economic transformation.

Section 85.6

The Muslim League and Pakistan: Jinnah, Provincial Interests, and Constitutional Safeguards

After the elections, Jinnah's claim to speak for Indian Muslims had never been stronger. A barrister trained in London, a former Congress member, and a man of formidable negotiating skill and personal reserve, Jinnah had transformed the League from a party of landed elites and urban professionals into a mass organization during the war. At the League legislators' convention in Delhi in April 1946, he secured a resolution that referred to a sovereign independent state comprising the zones in the northwest and the northeast, a formulation that departed from the plural "states" of the Lahore Resolution of 1940 and which later became the subject of controversy among historians. The League's demand for Pakistan was thus a demand that remained consistent in its principle but flexible in its precise constitutional form.

It is important to distinguish three levels of the League's position. The first was the principle of Muslim nationhood: the claim that Muslims were a nation, not a minority, and were entitled to political equality with Hindus as a community. The second was a territorial demand: the claim that the Muslim-majority areas of the northwest and northeast should constitute sovereign state or states. The third was the possible constitutional arrangements through which these principles might be realized: a fully independent Pakistan, a confederation of autonomous Muslim and non-Muslim units with a minimal centre, or a system of grouped provinces within a loose Union. Historians have argued over which of these Jinnah regarded as the essential objective and which as bargaining positions. Ayesha Jalal has argued that Jinnah used the Pakistan demand as a bargaining counter to secure parity and effective safeguards within an all-India framework, an argument others have disputed by pointing to the commitments the League made to its supporters and to the depth of the mobilization it organized. Whatever Jinnah's private calculation, the Cabinet Mission scheme, with its grouped provinces and limited centre, offered a framework in which the League's essential claims about Muslim political autonomy appeared to be partly met.

The League's territorial demand was more problematic than its slogans suggested. The provinces claimed for Pakistan included Punjab and Bengal, which had large non-Muslim minorities, with Hindus and Sikhs forming roughly forty-five percent of Punjab's population and about forty-five percent of Bengal's, and with significant concentrations in particular districts. Assam, which the League also claimed, had a Muslim population of about a third, while the Frontier province, which was almost entirely Muslim, was governed by a Congress ministry. A Pakistan that included all of these provinces would contain very large non-Muslim minorities, while a Pakistan from which non-Muslim-majority districts were excluded would be smaller than Jinnah's maximum claim, a prospect Jinnah had earlier dismissed as a "moth-eaten" Pakistan. For this reason, some League politicians in Punjab and Bengal regarded the grouped-province arrangement, in which the whole of the northwest or northeast would form a section with its own constitution, as preferable to the partition of provinces.

Muslim opinion was not unanimous. The Unionist Party in Punjab, led by Muslim landlords, had long defended a regional politics that cut across communal lines. The Khudai Khidmatgar movement in the Frontier and the Congress Muslims, such as Azad, the Jamiat-ul-Ulama-i-Hind, and the Momin Conference, opposed the League's demand. Sikhs, who formed about thirteen percent of Punjab's population and who dominated particular districts, were alarmed by the prospect of living in a Muslim-majority state. In Bengal, politicians such as Suhrawardy and Abul Hashim advanced regional ideas that sometimes diverged from the League's all-India line. The League's electoral success, therefore, should not be mistaken for unanimous Muslim support for a single formula.

Section 85.7

The Cabinet Mission Arrives: British Negotiations and the Search for a United India

On 19 February 1946, as the naval uprising was starting in Bombay, Attlee announced in the House of Commons that a mission of Cabinet ministers would go to India to confer with Indian leaders on the means by which India might attain full self-government. The mission consisted of three men with distinct roles. Lord Pethick-Lawrence, the Secretary of State for India, was a veteran Labour politician sympathetic to Indian aspirations. Sir Stafford Cripps, President of the Board of Trade, had visited India in 1942 with an offer that had failed (Chapter 83) and was acquainted with Congress leaders, especially Nehru. A. V. Alexander, First Lord of the Admiralty, was a trade unionist and cooperative leader who played a less prominent role in negotiations. Wavell, the Viceroy, was associated with their discussions, though relations between him and the Mission were sometimes strained.

The Mission arrived in Delhi on 24 March 1946 and spent nearly three months in India. It interviewed a large number of political leaders, including Gandhi, Nehru, Azad, Patel, Jinnah, Liaquat Ali Khan, representatives of the Sikhs, Scheduled Castes, Indian Christians, and the princely states, and provincial premiers. The strategic objectives of the Mission were set out by the British government and can be summarized under four heads. The first was to transfer power to Indian hands quickly and honorably. The second was to avoid civil war, which British officials increasingly feared as communal tensions grew. The third was to maintain essential administrative continuity, particularly in the army and the civil service. The fourth, which Cripps and Pethick-Lawrence emphasized, was to preserve an all-India framework wherever possible, because the defense, communications, and economic integration of the subcontinent seemed best served by a single structure.

The Mission's early discussions revealed that the Congress and the League held positions that could not be reconciled by simple adjustment. A second conference at Simla, convened from 5 to 12 May 1946 and involving delegations from both parties, tested whether the two parties could agree on a scheme of their own. The League proposed a union of two groups of provinces, one Muslim-majority and one Hindu-majority, with a minimal centre limited to defense, foreign affairs, and communications, and with parity between the groups in a central body. Congress rejected the parity principle and the notion that the groups could form separate constitution-making bodies. The conference ended without agreement.

The Mission then decided to issue a statement of its own. In drafting it, they confronted the central dilemma. The League's demand for Pakistan, if conceded in full, would require the partition of Punjab and Bengal, a prospect the Mission regarded with grave reservations. Congress's demand for a strong centre, if conceded in full, would alienate the League and risk the very civil conflict the Mission hoped to avoid. The solution they devised sought to combine a Union with a limited centre, extensive provincial autonomy, and the optional formation of groups of provinces, so that the League could claim that its essential aims were partly met and Congress could claim that the unity of India was preserved. The Mission also had to consider the princely states, whose rulers, through the Chamber of Princes, were concerned about the end of British paramountcy, the doctrine under which the Crown had claimed supremacy over the states and responsibility for their external relations and defense.

Section 85.8

The Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946: Union, Provincial Groups, and Constituent Assembly

The statement issued by the Cabinet Mission and the Viceroy on 16 May 1946 is the most important constitutional document of the negotiations. It began by rejecting the League's demand for a fully sovereign Pakistan. The Mission argued that the Muslim-majority areas claimed for Pakistan contained very large non-Muslim minorities, that a division of Punjab and Bengal would violate the unity of those provinces, and that a separate state would complicate defense and communications. It also acknowledged that the League's concern for safeguarding Muslim interests was legitimate, and it sought to answer it within a different structure.

Historical Vocabulary
Sections and Groups

In the Cabinet Mission Plan, Sections were three clusters of provinces that would meet, after the Constituent Assembly's preliminary session, to decide on provincial constitutions and on whether to form a group with a common constitution and common subjects. Section A comprised the Hindu-majority provinces (Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Central Provinces, and Orissa). Section B comprised the Muslim-majority provinces of the northwest (Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Sindh, and Baluchistan). Section C comprised Bengal and Assam. The terms were not synonymous with "Hindu India" and "Pakistan": Section B had a non-Muslim minority, including Sikhs and Hindus, of significant size in Punjab, and Section C contained Assam, a province with a non-Muslim majority. The Sections' procedure, and especially whether provinces could decline to join their assigned Section at the outset, became the central dispute of the Mission settlement.

The Plan proposed a Union of India, comprising British India and the princely states, with powers limited to foreign affairs, defense, and communications, and with the power to raise the finances required for those subjects. All other subjects and all residuary powers would belong to the provinces. The Union would have an executive and a legislature composed of representatives of the provinces and the states. A major communal question in the Union legislature would require a majority of the representatives of each of the two main communities present and voting, as well as a majority of all members present and voting, a safeguard intended to protect the Muslim minority. The Union constitution could be reconsidered after ten years, and provinces would be free to propose amendments.

Provinces would be free to form groups with executives and legislatures, and each group could determine the provincial subjects to be taken in common. The Plan envisaged three Sections: A, B, and C. The Constituent Assembly would be constituted in a prescribed manner. It would have 389 members, of whom 292 would represent the provinces of British India, 93 would represent the princely states, and four would represent the Chief Commissioners' provinces. The provincial seats would be allotted to the three communities (General, Muslim, and Sikh) in proportion to population, with approximately one representative per million, and the members would be elected by the provincial legislative assemblies by the system of proportional representation with the single transferable vote. After a preliminary session in New Delhi to settle the order of business and to appoint advisory committees on minority rights, the representatives of the provinces would divide into the three Sections. Each Section would decide the provincial constitutions for its provinces and, if it wished, a group constitution. When these procedures were completed, the whole Assembly would reassemble to settle the Union constitution. A provision allowed any province, after the first general election under the new constitution, to call for a reconsideration of the terms of the group or to opt out of it.

The distinction between initial participation in the three Sections and the later right to leave a provincial group was the pivotal technical point of the Plan, and it was carefully drafted. The Plan stated that the provinces would, after the preliminary meeting, proceed to the Sections. The provision for opting out came afterward: once the group and provincial constitutions had been framed and the first general elections held under the new constitution, a province could call for reconsideration of its group's terms or leave the group. The League read these provisions as meaning that provinces were to be bound to their Sections at the beginning, so that the northwestern and northeastern Sections would settle their constitutions as groups, with Assam and the Frontier unable to leave until after the first elections. Congress argued that the Plan's reference to provinces' freedom meant that each province could decide for itself whether to join its Section from the outset. The Mission's later explanations, discussed in §85.9, supported the League's reading of the procedure.

The mechanics of section-making were not an abstract quarrel over punctuation. Section A comprised the largely Hindu-majority provinces; Section B grouped Punjab, Sind and the North-West Frontier Province; and Section C joined Bengal with Assam. These groupings crossed the boundaries of local political mandates. In Assam, a Congress ministry presided over a province in which some leaders feared that the larger population and political weight of Bengal would dominate any common institutions. In the Frontier, Congress and the Khudai Khidmatgar movement held office despite the province's Muslim majority. A requirement that their representatives first deliberate in Sections B or C could therefore affect the degree of autonomy their electorates had recently endorsed. The Plan did not simply create two Muslim states inside an Indian Union: its sections had to negotiate provincial and possible group constitutions, and a province's ability to depart belonged to a later stage. The difference mattered because the same constitutional machinery that promised safeguards to one community could seem to threaten self-government to another. For Congress, imposing grouping before provincial consent appeared to weaken the very idea of a sovereign Constituent Assembly; for the League, allowing provinces to reject grouping at once would remove the strongest constitutional assurance that Muslim-majority areas could act collectively. Neither objection can be understood without tracing the sequence of votes and constitution-making prescribed in the actual 16 May statement.

Section 85.9

Congress and League Responses: Acceptance, Grouping Disputes, and the Failure of the Cabinet Mission

The sequence of acceptance, interpretation, and rejection between May and July 1946 was complex, and its details matter for understanding why a plan that both parties had at one stage accepted nevertheless collapsed. The statement of 16 May dealt with the long-term constitutional framework. The Mission followed it on 25 May with a clarifying statement, and on 16 June it issued a separate set of proposals for an Interim Government of fourteen members: six from Congress, including one Scheduled Caste representative, five from the League, and one each for the Sikhs, Indian Christians, and Parsis. This second statement gave rise to a controversy about the composition of Congress's quota, since Congress wished to nominate a Muslim, which the League contested.

The Muslim League Council, meeting in Bombay, accepted the Plan on 6 June 1946. The resolution did not abandon Pakistan. It stated that the League accepted the scheme because the compulsory grouping of the Muslim-majority provinces in Sections B and C, in the League's view, laid the foundation for Pakistan and because it hoped that the scheme would lead to a sovereign Pakistan at its final stage. Jinnah and many of his colleagues saw the grouping as an effective alternative to partition: the northwest and the northeast would function as units with their own constitutions and substantial autonomy within a weak Union, and the League would retain the option of reconsideration after the first elections. The League's acceptance was therefore tied to a particular reading of the grouping provisions.

Congress's response was more fraught. The Working Committee debated the Plan at length. Gandhi, whom the leaders consulted, advised against acceptance of the grouping provisions, and he was reportedly suspicious of the Plan's communal framework. On 25 and 26 June, the Working Committee resolved to accept the long-term plan in the form of the 16 May statement, while rejecting the 16 June proposals for the Interim Government, partly because of the nationalist Muslim representation. The statement of acceptance also expressed Congress's own interpretation of the grouping provisions, according to which provinces were free to decide whether to join a group, a reading which contradicted the League's. The AICC ratified the Working Committee's decision on 7 July 1946, and Congress entered the elections to the Constituent Assembly, which took place in July and August, winning the great majority of general seats and a handful of Muslim seats, while the League won nearly all the Muslim seats allotted to the provinces.

The decisive moment came after Nehru succeeded Azad as Congress president on 6 July 1946. At a press conference in Bombay on 10 July, asked whether Congress had accepted the Plan unconditionally, Nehru said that Congress was entering the Constituent Assembly unfettered by any agreements and free to act as it saw fit; that the Assembly, once constituted, would be sovereign and could change the Plan; and that, in his judgment, the grouping scheme was unlikely to survive because the Frontier and Assam would not agree to be grouped. Nehru's statement reflected a genuine reading of Congress's position: that the Assembly's sovereignty could not be limited by a document drafted by the British. Jinnah and the League read it differently. To them, the statement showed that Congress had never accepted the Plan's essential bargain, and that the grouping on which the League's safeguards depended would be dismantled once Congress controlled the Assembly. Azad, in his later memoir, regarded Nehru's statement as a serious mistake, though historians differ on how far it was the cause of the breakdown or a pretext for decisions the League's leadership had reason to take.

The League Council met in Bombay from 27 to 29 July 1946. It resolved to withdraw its acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan, which it said had been made on the assumption of good faith, and to launch what it called Direct Action to achieve Pakistan. The resolution also called on Muslims to renounce titles conferred by the British. Jinnah, in his speech, said that the League had decided to abandon constitutional methods, a statement widely reported and then explained as a reference to the failure of negotiation rather than a call to violence. The resolution did not specify the form of Direct Action, a vagueness that left the term open to different interpretations by local organizers. The Cabinet Mission, which had left India in late June, was now no longer in the country, and the Plan's long-term procedure was left to the Constituent Assembly, from which the League had withdrawn its participation.

Historical Debate

Could the Cabinet Mission Plan Have Preserved a United India?

The question: Did the Cabinet Mission Plan offer a workable compromise that failed because of particular decisions, or did its structure contain contradictions that made failure highly likely?

Interpretation A — A viable compromise undone by contingent decisions: Some historians and participants, among them Maulana Azad in his memoir, argue that the Plan offered both sides what they most needed: a united India for Congress and effective autonomy and safeguards for the Muslim-majority regions for the League. Ayesha Jalal's work suggests that Jinnah accepted the Plan as a means of securing Muslim political power within an all-India framework, and that Pakistan as a sovereign state was not necessarily his preferred outcome. On this view, Nehru's statement of 10 July, Congress's insistence on the Assembly's sovereignty, and the Viceroy's and Mission's handling of the interim arrangements disrupted a process that could have succeeded.
Interpretation B — A compromise built on incompatible assumptions: Other historians emphasize that the Plan's ambiguities concealed incompatible expectations. The League saw grouping as the foundation of a near-sovereign Muslim bloc, and Congress saw the Union as a framework for a strong, unified state. A weak centre, with Congress governing most provinces and the League controlling Sections B and C, would have been tested by every economic, defense, and constitutional question. On this view, even a clearer procedure on grouping would not have removed the underlying disagreement about the nature of the Union, and the breakdown was a matter of when and not whether.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the text of the 16 May and 25 May statements, party resolutions, speeches, private correspondence in The Transfer of Power, and memoirs written by participants after the events. Memoirs written after Partition may be shaped by hindsight and by the writers' wish to assign or avoid responsibility.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether Partition is understood as the consequence of fundamental incompatibility or as the outcome of contingent failures that might have been avoided. It also affects how responsibility is distributed among Congress, the League, and the British government.

Section 85.10

Direct Action Day, 16 August 1946, and the Great Calcutta Killing

The League Council's resolution set 16 August 1946 as Direct Action Day. The political situation in Bengal gave the date a particular significance. Bengal was the only Muslim-majority province, besides Sindh, where the League formed the government, led by Premier H. S. Suhrawardy, a politician with a reputation for effective machine politics and strong ties to Calcutta's working-class Muslim communities. The Calcutta population was roughly three-fourths Hindu and one-fourth Muslim, and the city was beset by the effects of the 1943 famine, wartime inflation, black-market profiteering, and recent unrest linked to the INA trials and strikes. In this atmosphere, the League's declaration of a holiday and its planned mass meeting on the Maidan, which Suhrawardy as Premier announced as a public holiday, raised the possibility of confrontation.

The events of 16 August began with processions and meetings. Congress and Hindu Mahasabha leaders, who regarded the holiday proclamation as provocative, urged shopkeepers to remain open, and clashes broke out in the morning in several parts of the city. During the day, the violence spread rapidly through the northern and central quarters of Calcutta and continued for several days. It involved killing, arson, looting, and the destruction of homes and shops, with large numbers of the poor, including laborers and migrants, among the victims. Estimates of the death toll vary widely. An official figure often cited is about four thousand killed and some ten thousand injured, and unofficial estimates were higher; students should treat all figures as approximate and contested. The violence ended after several days, as troops took control and as the retaliation of Hindus and Sikhs, who in some areas were well organized, changed the balance of the fighting.

Historians have analyzed the violence in terms of political mobilization, local organization, rumor, and policing. The League's decision to hold a mass meeting and to declare a holiday contributed to the tension, and it is clear that political leaders and local organizers on all sides bore some responsibility for the atmosphere. Contemporary and later accounts also document the activity of organized groups, including groups of ex-servicemen and neighborhood bands, and the role of rumor in spreading fear. The performance of the police, who were partly Muslim in composition and partly under the authority of the Muslim ministry, and the late arrival of troops, became subjects of bitter dispute. It is essential to distinguish responsibility for calling a political demonstration from responsibility for specific acts of violence. A resolution calling for Direct Action and a government declaration of a holiday were political decisions; the killings were carried out by individuals and groups on all sides, and the evidence does not support the claim that a single organization initiated and directed all the violence. For the purposes of this chapter, the significance of the Great Calcutta Killing lies in its political and constitutional consequences. The violence destroyed what remained of the possibility of mutual trust between Congress and the League in the provinces where they confronted each other. It convinced many Hindus and Sikhs in Punjab and Bengal that a Muslim-majority administration could not be trusted to protect them, and many Muslims that they faced violent retaliation if their provinces were governed by Hindu-majority authorities. It also altered British perceptions: Wavell's concern about a possible breakdown of order increased, and the thought that Britain would have to withdraw before widespread civil conflict engulfed the country hardened. The wider human consequences of communal violence, including the movements of populations that followed, are examined in Chapter 86.

Section 85.11

The Spread of Communal Conflict and the Collapse of Political Confidence

The violence in Calcutta was followed by outbreaks elsewhere. In October 1946, in the Noakhali and Tippera districts of eastern Bengal, where Muslims formed a large majority and Hindus a minority of landowners and professionals, Muslim crowds attacked Hindu villages, and there were killings, forced conversions, abductions, and destruction of property. The attacks began around the time of a Hindu festival in mid-October, and the numbers of dead are uncertain, with estimates in the hundreds, though the scale of displacement and fear was larger than the numbers of fatalities suggest. In Bihar, in late October and November, Hindu crowds attacked Muslim communities in what was widely understood as retaliation for Noakhali, and many thousands, by some estimates, were killed. In the United Provinces, there were smaller outbreaks, as at Garhmukteshwar in November, and in Punjab, tensions rose in the winter and spring of 1947, culminating in the violence in Rawalpindi and neighboring districts in March, discussed further below. The sequence was not a single wave but a series of interrelated outbreaks, each of which was cited as justification or as warning in the next.

Gandhi responded by traveling to Noakhali, where from November 1946 until early 1947 he walked from village to village, sometimes barefoot, attempting to restore confidence between communities and to persuade Hindus to remain in their homes. He also supported efforts to rebuild trust in Bihar. His mission was a conspicuous attempt to apply his principles of nonviolence and reconciliation to a situation of extreme communal fear. It was not an attempt to negotiate a constitutional settlement, and he was increasingly distant from the Congress leadership's calculations. The presence of an eminent figure in a village in Noakhali could reduce tension locally, but could not alter the conduct of national politics. Nehru and other Congress leaders traveled to Bihar to restore order, and the Bihar ministry, a Congress one, was criticized by the League for its failure to prevent violence.

The mutual accusations of the parties constituted a political dimension of the conflict. The League pointed to Bihar as proof that Muslims were unsafe in Congress-governed provinces. Congress and Hindu leaders pointed to Calcutta and Noakhali as proof that Muslims were unsafe from the League. Both sets of claims contained truth and were used for political purposes. The provincial administrations faced a collapse of confidence in their ability to maintain order. The police and local officials in several districts were divided along communal lines or unwilling to act, and the British officers who had long served as the "steel frame" of the administration were fewer and, in many cases, anxious to leave. The result was an erosion of the state's capacity to guarantee security at the very moment when it was being asked to oversee a political transition.

For the negotiations, the consequences were profound. Politicians who had earlier discussed constitutional arrangements as problems of representation now confronted the possibility that the arrangements could not be implemented without force. The threat of civil war, which had been a rhetorical concern of the Cabinet Mission, became a practical concern of the Viceroy and of ministers in London. The pressures to settle quickly, and the willingness of some leaders to contemplate a partition they had earlier rejected, grew. This section deliberately treats the violence selectively. Its full human history, including the experiences of those displaced, killed, or assaulted, is the subject of Chapter 86.

Section 85.12

The Interim Government of 1946: Congress, the League, and the Struggle to Share Power

While the League had withdrawn its acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan, Wavell, who believed that the Congress had accepted the long-term plan and that an Interim Government was needed to restore confidence, invited Nehru on 12 August to form an interim administration. The Interim Government was sworn in on 2 September 1946, with Nehru as Vice-President of the Viceroy's Executive Council and as the member for external affairs. The Council included Patel as Home Member, Rajendra Prasad for food and agriculture, C. Rajagopalachari for education, Jagjivan Ram for labor, and Baldev Singh for defense, along with three Muslims nominated by Congress or associated with it, Asaf Ali, Shafaat Ahmad Khan, and Syed Ali Zaheer, and several non-Congress members. The League was not part of the Council at its formation, and its supporters observed the day with black flags.

The Interim Government remained part of the colonial constitutional structure. It was legally the Viceroy's Executive Council, appointed under the 1935 Act, and the Viceroy retained reserve powers, including the authority to overrule his ministers. Nehru, in practice, hoped to treat it as a cabinet, and he and his colleagues acted as ministers responsible for departments. But the legal position left uncertainty about how far the Council was a government and how far an advisory body. The British retained the army's command structure, and the Viceroy continued to preside over meetings. The ambiguity affected the way both Congress and the League approached the Council.

Wavell, concerned about the Calcutta violence and anxious to include the League, negotiated with Jinnah, and on 26 October 1946 the League nominated five members to the Council: Liaquat Ali Khan, I. I. Chundrigar, Abdur Rab Nishtar, Ghazanfar Ali Khan, and Jogendra Nath Mandal, a Scheduled Caste leader from Bengal. The League's decision to enter was ambiguous. Jinnah said the League joined to safeguard Muslim interests, not to accept the Cabinet Mission Plan. The portfolios were allotted after a dispute: Congress retained home affairs, while the League accepted finance, to which Liaquat Ali Khan was appointed, along with commerce, communications, health, and law. The League hoped to use the Council as a platform to establish its claim to parity, and Congress hoped that its participation would lead the League to cooperate in the Constituent Assembly.

The coexistence of ministers who rejected one another's legitimacy produced friction. Liaquat Ali Khan, as Finance Member, was in a position to review the spending proposals of other departments, and in his budget presented in February 1947 he proposed measures including a tax on business profits and a commission to investigate tax evasion, measures which Congress-aligned industrialists regarded as aimed at the Hindu business community. The League members did not attend the Constituent Assembly, and the Council failed to function as a cooperative body. The central lesson of the Interim Government is that the establishment of a common administration did not create a common constitutional program. Ministers could share offices without sharing a conception of what India would become, and their contest within the administration intensified, rather than resolved, the disputes outside it.

The Finance portfolio made the conflict concrete. Financial scrutiny was not a ceremonial function: expenditure estimates, departmental requests, and taxation affected what every other member could accomplish. Congress ministers could propose administrative programmes, but Liaquat Ali Khan could question their fiscal basis, while League ministers suspected that Congress was treating the Council as the cabinet of a future unitary India. Liaquat's February 1947 budget became especially contentious because business taxation and investigations of evasion were interpreted by some Congress supporters as politically selective. Yet those accusations do not establish that every proposed fiscal measure lacked an economic rationale; postwar inflation, uneven war profits and revenue shortages were genuine administrative concerns. The historical problem is to distinguish disputes over policy design from struggles for institutional leverage. A common cabinet normally relies on collective responsibility, agreement on priorities and some recognized mechanism for resolving disagreements. This Council possessed offices and procedures but not those shared foundations. It was also shaped by the Viceroy's continuing authority: British officials could mediate conflicts, but mediation could reinforce each party's belief that it was dealing through the colonial executive rather than directly with its Indian counterparts. The interim arrangement consequently offered important practical experience while exposing how little constitutional agreement had been achieved.

Section 85.13

The Constituent Assembly, the Muslim League Boycott, and the Problem of Sovereignty

In early December 1946, Attlee invited Nehru, Jinnah, Liaquat Ali Khan, and Baldev Singh to London in an effort to resolve the grouping dispute. The talks, held from 2 to 6 December, failed. On 6 December the British government issued a statement saying that the Cabinet Mission's intention had been that decisions in the Sections should be taken by simple majority vote. If the Assembly proceeded on any other basis, the statement said, the League would be justified in refusing to participate. The statement added that if a constitution were framed by an Assembly in which a large section of the population had not been represented, the British government could not contemplate forcing such a constitution upon unwilling parts of the country. The statement reaffirmed the League's reading of the procedure while acknowledging the possibility that the constitution might not apply to all of India.

The Constituent Assembly nevertheless met on 9 December 1946 in New Delhi, with the Muslim League members absent. The oldest member, Sachchidananda Sinha, presided as provisional chairman, and on 11 December the Assembly elected Rajendra Prasad as its president. The League's boycott meant that the Assembly lacked representation from the community that the League claimed to speak for, and that many Muslims who were not League members, along with representatives of the Congress, took part. The princely states had not yet generally sent their representatives. The legal and political legitimacy of an Assembly that excluded the League was immediately in question.

On 13 December Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution, which declared India to be an independent sovereign republic and affirmed that its constitution would be framed by the people, with guarantees of social, economic, and political justice and safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed classes. The resolution described the constituent territories as autonomous units, with residuary powers vested in them, language that reflected an effort to accommodate the federal demands of the Cabinet Mission Plan. The Assembly adopted the resolution on 22 January 1947. Nehru presented it as a statement of the aims of the Assembly. For the League it was a declaration by a body that did not represent it, and its adoption of the term "republic" and its claim to sovereign status confirmed the League's suspicions of Congress's intentions.

The conflict over sovereignty was thus a conflict about the source of constitutional authority. Congress held that the Constituent Assembly derived its authority from the Indian people and was therefore sovereign, even though it had been convened under a British statement. The League held that the Assembly derived its authority from the Cabinet Mission Plan and that, since the Plan required the parties' agreement on the grouping procedure, an Assembly proceeding without that agreement was not legitimate for the Muslim-majority areas. The British government, caught between these positions, had said that it would not impose the Assembly's constitution on the unwilling. Congress, after some deliberation, accepted the British statement of 6 December on 5–6 January 1947, with reservations concerning the position of Assam and the Sikhs in Punjab, while the League Council, in late January, rejected the statement as insufficient. The Assembly's initial dispute over legitimacy is distinct from its later drafting of independent India's Constitution, which belongs to Chapter 88.

The boycott posed an unusually difficult problem of democratic legitimacy. The Assembly had been indirectly elected by provincial legislatures chosen under a limited franchise, not by a universal vote of the people, and its founding procedure had been designed by a British mission rather than by an already sovereign Indian state. That did not make its resolutions meaningless; many delegates represented organized constituencies and could undertake detailed constitutional work. It did, however, complicate Congress's assertion that the Assembly spoke for the whole subcontinent. The Muslim League could point to its sweeping victories in Muslim constituencies and argue that constitutional decisions binding those provinces could not legitimately proceed without its representatives. Congress countered that a boycott could not permanently paralyze the institutions through which self-government would be achieved. These positions offered competing answers to a fundamental question: was sovereignty created by the constituent body's representative claim, or did sovereignty require prior agreement among communities whose territorial interests differed? The Objectives Resolution's promises of justice, safeguards and autonomous units were therefore simultaneously statements of aspiration and attempts to resolve a crisis of authority. Studying its adoption in January 1947 without examining who remained outside the Assembly risks making the constitutional process appear more consensual than it was at the time.

Section 85.14

The British Decision to Withdraw: Attlee's Announcement of 20 February 1947

On 20 February 1947, Attlee announced in the House of Commons that the British government intended to take the necessary steps to effect the transfer of power into responsible Indian hands by a date not later than June 1948. He also announced that Wavell would be succeeded as Viceroy by Rear-Admiral Viscount Mountbatten. The statement added that if it appeared that a fully representative Constituent Assembly would not have framed a constitution by that date, the government would have to consider to whom the powers of the central government in British India should be handed over: whether to some form of central government for British India, to the existing provincial governments, or in some other way that seemed most reasonable. The announcement thus set a deadline and, by naming alternatives to a single central authority, opened the possibility of more than one successor.

The reasons for the decision can be grouped under several heads. The first was fiscal. Britain emerged from the war with a large national debt, with a dependence on American credit, including the loan of 1945, and with a need to reduce overseas commitments. The costs of maintaining a large army in India, and of the administrative apparatus necessary to hold a country of four hundred million people, were considerable. The second was administrative. The Indian Civil Service and the police were under strain; recruitment had fallen, and Indian officers in the services were increasingly oriented to the prospective political masters. Wavell's reports warned that the loyalty of the Indian officials could not be taken for granted and that British officers could not remain indefinitely. The third was military. The INA trials and the naval uprising had shown that the services were not immune to political pressure, and the Indian Army was needed elsewhere.

Cause and Effect

Why Britain Decided to Leave India in 1947

Long-term causes

Decades of nationalist organization, constitutional concessions from 1909 to 1935, and the growing role of Indian ministers and officials had undermined the assumption of indefinite British rule. The war accelerated these processes.

Short-term causes

Britain's postwar financial strain, the INA trials, the naval uprising, strikes, the failure of the Simla Conference, the Cabinet Mission's collapse, communal violence, and Wavell's pessimistic reports about administrative breakdown.

Event

The announcement of 20 February 1947, setting a deadline of June 1948 and replacing Wavell with Mountbatten.

Immediate effects

Political leaders gained certainty that British rule would end but lost certainty about the successor authority; the League's incentive to obstruct a unitary outcome increased; Punjab's coalition collapsed within days.

Long-term consequences

The announcement ended the assumption of an extended transition, shifted the negotiation from constitutional design to the logistics of transfer, and contributed to the compressed timeline that shaped Partition.

Historical complexity: The decision was not caused by a single uprising or election. It resulted from the convergence of structural weakness and the specific judgments of ministers who concluded that continued rule was impracticable and that a deadline might concentrate Indian minds. Historians disagree on how far the deadline pressed the parties to agree and how far it created a race for position.

The fourth head was political. The Labour government's own commitments, its sympathy for self-government, and the pressure of American and international opinion all favored a transfer of power. The fifth was practical: Attlee and his advisers concluded that Wavell's approach of managing a gradual transition under existing arrangements was not producing agreement. Wavell had proposed a "breakdown plan" for withdrawal province by province, beginning with the evacuation of British civilians and troops from the northern provinces and a retreat to the southeast, and the Cabinet found this approach, which could have resulted in the abdication of responsibility in the north, unacceptable. They chose a political rather than a military withdrawal and sought a Viceroy with fresh authority.

The announcement created uncertainty even as it ended it. Independence was now promised, with a date. But the identity and number of successor authorities remained unresolved, and the phrase about alternatives to a single central government implied that the British would not necessarily wait for a united settlement. For the League, the announcement strengthened the argument that Muslims should hold firm: if the Constituent Assembly did not include them, power might pass to provinces or to separate authorities. For Congress, it meant that a failure to secure the League's participation could lead to the dismemberment of the country. The next weeks, especially in Punjab, would show how rapidly the political atmosphere could change.

Section 85.15

Punjab, Bengal, the Frontier, and the Politics of Territorial Alternatives

The political interests of particular provinces complicated any all-India agreement, and in the weeks after 20 February, the question of territorial division, which the Cabinet Mission had hoped to avoid, entered the negotiations. The most volatile province was Punjab. The coalition government of Khizr Hayat Tiwana, supported by Unionists, Congress, and the Akali Dal, had faced a League campaign of agitation in early 1947, including civil disobedience, and on 2 March Khizr resigned. The League, as the largest party, was invited to form a ministry but could not command a majority, and the Governor assumed direct control under Section 93 of the 1935 Act. On 3 March, in Lahore, Master Tara Singh, the Akali Dal leader, drew his kirpan on the steps of the Assembly in a public rejection of a Muslim League government. In the first half of March, violence erupted in Lahore, Amritsar, Multan, and especially in the Rawalpindi region, where Sikh and Hindu villages suffered heavy attacks. The Congress Working Committee, on 8 March, passed a resolution calling for the partition of Punjab in order to separate Muslim-majority and non-Muslim-majority areas.

The Sikh position was particularly difficult. Sikhs were about thirteen percent of the population of Punjab, scattered across the province but concentrated in the central districts, where they held land, and the Sikh holy places and many farms were in the western districts that a Pakistan would likely contain. The Akali Dal opposed Pakistan and sought safeguards that the Cabinet Mission Plan did not provide, for the Sikhs would have been a minority in Section B. By March 1947, Sikh leaders increasingly supported the division of Punjab, as a means of retaining a Sikh presence in an Indian province, even though a division would put many Sikhs and their property on the other side of the line.

In Bengal, the alternatives were wider. The demand for the partition of Bengal came from Hindu leaders, including members of the Hindu Mahasabha such as Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, and from Bengal Congress politicians, who argued that if India was to be divided, Bengal should be divided as well. Against this, a group including Suhrawardy, Abul Hashim of the League, and Congress dissenter Sarat Chandra Bose, brother of Subhas, put forward a proposal in April and May 1947 for a United Sovereign Bengal, a state that would be independent of both India and Pakistan, with a joint electorate and equal Hindu and Muslim representation in its cabinet. Jinnah, when asked, indicated that he would not oppose it, apparently on the assumption that a united Bengal would remain on friendly terms with Pakistan. The Congress high command, including Patel and Nehru, rejected the scheme, and Gandhi did not support it. Bengal Hindu opinion, in the main, was hostile, and the proposal was not adopted. Historians continue to debate whether it had a real chance. Joya Chatterji has argued that Hindu bhadralok support for partition was a deliberate political choice shaped by concern for their status, and not simply an effect of communal violence.

The North-West Frontier Province presented a different case. Its ministry was Congress, led by Dr. Khan Sahib, brother of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, and its population was overwhelmingly Muslim and largely Pashtun. The Khudai Khidmatgar, a nonviolent movement, had opposed the League and Pakistan, while the League, in the winter and spring of 1947, organized civil disobedience against the ministry. The Frontier's choice between India and Pakistan, with no geographical continuity to India, was complicated by the Khudai Khidmatgar's interest in a Pashtun identity that did not fit either state. Assam, where Congress governed, feared inclusion in Section C and the possible loss of its autonomy to a Bengal-dominated group, and its premier, Gopinath Bardoloi, opposed grouping. The Sylhet district, a Muslim-majority district in Assam adjoining Bengal, became a matter of dispute.

Multiple Perspectives

The Prospect of Dividing Punjab and Bengal, Spring 1947

Sikh political leaders in Punjab: Feared a Muslim-majority Punjab or Pakistan in which Sikhs would be a minority, yet recognized that a division would split the community and its lands. Many concluded that partition of the province was the least damaging option, while some sought a Sikh-majority arrangement that no plan provided.
Bengali Hindu professionals and landholders: Many believed that a Muslim-majority Bengal would reduce their political position and sought a Hindu-majority province within India. Their support for partition reflected concerns about power, security, and status, and was not uniform across the community.
Bengali Muslim League politicians: Many wished for a Pakistan including the whole of Bengal, while Suhrawardy and some colleagues pursued a sovereign Bengal. Rural Muslim cultivators had their own concerns about land, debt, and credit that did not always map onto the League's all-India demands.
Khudai Khidmatgar and Pashtun leaders: Opposed the League, faced a choice that did not match their regional aspirations, and saw their province treated as a counter in a contest among others.

These groups did not have equal power over the outcome. The decisions were made chiefly by the Viceroy, the Congress and League high commands, and provincial leaders with legislative votes; many of those most affected had no direct role.

This section examines how territorial choices entered the negotiations, not the final boundary lines or the subsequent population movements. The central point is that, by the spring of 1947, the politics of Punjab and Bengal had made the principle of dividing provinces a live option, advanced by Congress and Sikh leaders as a counter to the League's demand for Pakistan as a whole. The League's insistence on Pakistan as it was claimed, including whole provinces, and Congress's insistence that if the country was divided, the provinces must be divided as well, combined to create the framework that the 3 June Plan would later adopt.

Section 85.16

Mountbatten's Arrival and the Final Negotiations, March–June 1947

Louis Mountbatten, a naval officer, a cousin of King George VI, and until recently Supreme Allied Commander in South East Asia, arrived in Delhi on 22 March 1947 and was sworn in as Viceroy on 24 March. He had been given wide discretion by Attlee and instructions to secure a settlement before the June 1948 deadline, preferably on the basis of a united India. He was a man of considerable charm and self-confidence, with a talent for personal diplomacy and a capacity for decisive action, and he came to India with a small team of advisers, including Lord Ismay as Chief of Staff and, later, Sir George Abell and V. P. Menon, a senior Indian official whose knowledge of the political landscape and relations with Patel were of great importance.

Mountbatten held a series of interviews with Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Jinnah, and other leaders in the first weeks. His discussions with Jinnah, whom he found immovable on the demand for Pakistan, and with Nehru, with whom he developed a close working relationship, convinced him that the Cabinet Mission scheme could not be revived. Gandhi, in his meetings with Mountbatten, urged him to invite Jinnah to form a government of the whole of India as an alternative to partition, a proposal that Mountbatten considered but that Congress leaders did not support. Meanwhile, Mountbatten visited the Frontier and Punjab, where violence continued, and these visits reinforced his belief that the time available was short. He concluded that the Cabinet Mission scheme had failed and that if the British were to withdraw without leaving chaos, a rapid decision was required.

His initial plan, drafted by Ismay and Abell in April and early May, and sometimes called "Plan Balkan," envisaged transferring power to the provinces, or to groups of provinces if they wished, which could then decide on their own affiliations. Mountbatten took the draft to Simla and, as an informal courtesy, showed it to Nehru on the evening of 10 May. Nehru reacted strongly. He argued that the plan would encourage fragmentation of the country into many units, including the princely states, and would result in "Balkanization." Mountbatten, recognizing that the plan would be unacceptable to Congress, withdrew it. He turned instead to V. P. Menon, who had been working on an alternative that provided for the transfer of power to two successor governments, each with Dominion status, with the provinces of Punjab and Bengal divided if their legislatures so decided. Menon's plan answered Congress's wish for a strong centre in the successor India and the League's wish for a sovereign Pakistan, albeit a smaller one. Dominion status, with its immediate transfer of power, also made it possible to speed up the date of independence.

Mountbatten flew to London on 18 May to obtain the approval of the Cabinet, which he received. On his return, he held a meeting with the Indian leaders on 2 June, at which he presented the plan, and the leaders were asked to give their response. Nehru and Patel for Congress, Jinnah and Liaquat Ali Khan for the League, and Baldev Singh for the Sikhs agreed to the plan or to its acceptance in principle. Jinnah's acceptance, notably, was conveyed in a form that did not commit the League formally until its Council could approve, and Mountbatten conveyed his assurance to the Cabinet. The plan was broadcast on 3 June, and on 4 June, in a press conference, Mountbatten announced that the transfer of power would take place not in June 1948 but on 15 August 1947.

Assessing Mountbatten's role requires care. He was not the sole architect of Partition. The League's demand, Congress's acceptance of division, and the collapse of constitutional compromise were the result of long processes involving many actors. But his actions affected the outcome. He shortened the timetable by nearly ten months, abandoned the Cabinet Mission scheme without exhausting every possibility, and presented the plan as a fait accompli once the leaders had given their assent. The speed of the timetable had consequences for the arrangements for the division of the army, the civil service, and the assets, and for the demarcation of boundaries, which is examined in §85.19. Historians differ about whether the acceleration was necessary to prevent a wider breakdown or was an unwise gamble, and these differing views are explored in the second Historical Debate below.

Section 85.17

The 3 June Plan: Agreement to Establish India and Pakistan

The statement of 3 June 1947, issued by His Majesty's Government and broadcast by Mountbatten, Nehru, Jinnah, and Baldev Singh, set out the procedure by which British India would be divided. It began by noting that the Cabinet Mission Plan had not been accepted by the Muslim-majority areas, and that the Constituent Assembly, as constituted, could not frame a constitution for them. It stated that the British government had no intention of imposing a constitution on unwilling parts of the country, and that it would transfer power on the basis of Dominion status to one or two successor authorities, according to the decisions reached through the procedures described.

The provisions concerning Punjab and Bengal were central. The Legislative Assemblies of the two provinces would each meet in two parts: one comprising the members from the Muslim-majority districts and the other comprising the members from the remaining districts. The statement identified the districts in each part by reference to the census of 1941. If either part, voting separately, decided in favor of partition, the province would be divided, and a Boundary Commission would be set up to demarcate the boundaries on the basis of ascertaining the contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims, and, in doing so, to take into account other factors. The British government portrayed this procedure as allowing elected provincial representatives to choose; nevertheless, the rules, timetable, and limited alternatives were established by the departing imperial government in consultation with the main party leaders. A vote conducted under those rules cannot by itself settle the wider question of responsibility for partition. The first part of the Bengal and Punjab assemblies to vote would also decide, if the Muslim-majority part so chose, to join the existing Constituent Assembly or a new one.

For Sindh, the Legislative Assembly would decide which Constituent Assembly to join. For the North-West Frontier Province, whose ministry was Congress but whose population was overwhelmingly Muslim, a referendum would be held to decide whether to join the existing Constituent Assembly or the new one; the plan did not provide a third option of independence or union with Afghanistan, a point the Khudai Khidmatgar leaders protested. For Baluchistan, which lacked a representative legislature, a mechanism was to be devised to consult opinion. For the Sylhet district of Assam, a referendum would determine whether it should be amalgamated with the new province of East Bengal if Bengal was partitioned. The princely states were not directly affected by the plan, which stated that the British government's policy toward them was as laid down in the Cabinet Mission Plan, with paramountcy lapsing with the transfer of power, without promising that any princely state could maintain a viable, internationally recognized independent position after the British withdrawal. The statement also provided that if partition was decided, the Dominion status would be conferred on the successor authorities earlier than June 1948, and that the necessary legislation would be introduced in Parliament.

Historical Vocabulary
Dominion Status

Dominion status was the constitutional position of self-governing members of the British Commonwealth, such as Canada, Australia, and South Africa, which were equal in status to Britain and had control over their own domestic and foreign affairs while sharing allegiance to the Crown. For India in 1947, Dominion status meant immediate self-government without waiting for the framing of a full constitution; the existing Government of India Act of 1935, modified, served as an interim constitution, and the Governors-General were representatives of the Crown. It mattered because it made the speedy transfer of power legally possible and because the Congress leadership, which had demanded complete independence (purna swaraj) in 1929, accepted Dominion status as an interim arrangement, with the intention of leaving the Commonwealth if the Constituent Assembly so decided. Its acceptance was also facilitated by the practical advantages, including continued British support for the armed forces and administration.

The legislatures of Bengal and Punjab voted in the second half of June. In Bengal, on 20 June, the Legislative Assembly meeting as a whole voted by a majority of 126 to 90 against joining the existing Constituent Assembly, which was in effect a vote for Pakistan; but when the members from the Hindu-majority districts met separately, they voted 58 to 21 for partition, and the members from the Muslim-majority districts voted 106 to 35 against it. Since the plan provided that partition would take place if either part so decided, Bengal was to be divided. In Punjab, on 23 June, the members from the Muslim-majority districts voted 69 to 27 against partition, while those from the non-Muslim districts voted 50 to 22 in favor, so Punjab too was to be divided. These figures, and the separate joint-sitting votes on which Constituent Assembly to join (Bengal 126–90 and Punjab 91–77 for a new Assembly), are documented in the editorial notes to Nehru’s letter to Mountbatten of 24 June 1947. Votes on partition are different from votes on Constituent Assembly affiliation. On 26 June, the Sindh Legislative Assembly voted to join the new Constituent Assembly. In July, the Sylhet referendum, held on 6 and 7 July, resulted in a majority for joining East Bengal, and the North-West Frontier referendum, held between 6 and 17 July, resulted in a majority for Pakistan, although Khan Sahib's party boycotted it and the turnout was a matter of dispute. Baluchistan's Shahi Jirga and the Quetta municipality opted for Pakistan. Nehru Archive documentary reference.

Historical Debate

Was Partition the Only Practicable Settlement by June 1947?

The question: By June 1947, had political conditions narrowed the options so far that a division into two states was the only workable outcome, or did alternatives remain?

Interpretation A — Political necessity: Some historians stress that by spring 1947 the Interim Government was paralyzed, communal violence was spreading, the League had won overwhelming support in Muslim constituencies, and Congress leaders had concluded that a strong centre could not be built with the League's participation. On this reading, a united India could have been preserved only by coercion or at the price of a weak centre that Congress would not accept. Partition, however painful, appeared to leaders as the least bad option, and the British, committed to withdrawal, could not impose a different solution.
Interpretation B — Alternatives existed but were foreclosed: Others argue that alternatives remained available, among them a looser federation, a united Bengal, a longer timetable under the existing statement of February 1947, or a transfer to provinces in groups. They point to the speed of Mountbatten's timetable, Congress's willingness to accept division once Punjab and Bengal could be divided, the League's acceptance of a smaller Pakistan, and elite decision-making that left little room for the provinces most affected. On this reading, the settlement reflected choices by a small group of leaders and officials working under pressure that they themselves intensified.
Interpretation C — Structural and contingent factors combined: A third view treats the two as overlapping: the deep structural weakness of the colonial state and the logic of separate electorates narrowed the options, while specific decisions, such as the timing, the choice of the plan, and the failure to agree on grouping, determined which of the remaining paths was taken.

Evidence: The debate draws on the records of Mountbatten's meetings in The Transfer of Power, party resolutions, memoirs by Menon, Azad, and others, and later scholarship including that of Jalal, Chatterji, and Khan. Each source is shaped by the author's position and by hindsight.

Why it matters: Whether Partition is seen as inevitable or contingent affects the attribution of responsibility and the lessons drawn about decolonization, minority protection, and timetable.

Section 85.18

The Indian Independence Act of 18 July 1947: The Legal End of British Rule

The legislation to give effect to the 3 June Plan was introduced in the House of Commons in early July and passed with little opposition. The Indian Independence Act received the Royal Assent on 18 July 1947. It provided that, from 15 August 1947, two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, would be set up in place of British India. It described the territories of Pakistan as those of East Bengal, West Punjab, Sindh, Baluchistan, and the North-West Frontier Province, together with Sylhet if the referendum so decided, subject to the decisions on the partition of Bengal and Punjab and to the awards of the Boundary Commissions. India would comprise the remaining territories of British India.

The Act provided that each Dominion would have a Governor-General appointed by the Crown on the advice of the Dominion government, and that the Constituent Assembly of each Dominion would serve as its legislature until a new constitution was framed. The Government of India Act of 1935, with adaptations, would serve as the interim constitution of each Dominion. The Act ended the responsibility of the British Parliament and of the Secretary of State for the government of the Dominions, and gave each Dominion legislature full power to make laws, including laws with extraterritorial effect and laws repealing or amending British statutes in their application to the Dominion. It also provided that the suzerainty of the Crown over the princely states would lapse, along with all treaties and agreements with the rulers, but it did not transfer paramountcy to either Dominion. Accession to India or Pakistan required further political and legal arrangements; the lapse of British obligations did not guarantee practical autonomy, diplomatic recognition, or secure independence to each ruler. Chapter 87 examines what the states then did.

The Act transformed the legal structure of imperial government while leaving numerous practical problems unresolved. The statutory "appointed day" was 15 August 1947 for both Dominions. Pakistan's first Governor-General, Jinnah, was sworn in at Karachi on 15 August, and the Pakistan Constituent Assembly met in Karachi on 10 and 11 August, while Mountbatten attended ceremonies in Karachi on 13 and 14 August, and Pakistan marked its independence on 14 August, a date later observed as its national day; India observed 15 August, with the Constituent Assembly's midnight session on 14–15 August. The difference between the legal appointed day and the national observance should be kept in mind. Mountbatten, at the request of the Congress leaders, became the first Governor-General of the Dominion of India, while Jinnah, who had declined Mountbatten's suggestion that he serve as common Governor-General of both Dominions, became Governor-General of Pakistan. The Act did not settle the Boundary Commissions' awards, the status of the princely states, or the division of assets.

The legal transformation also had important limits. Dominion status ended the ordinary authority of the British Parliament over the two new governments, but it did not instantly replace the elaborate administrative rules by which the Raj had operated. Both Dominions initially adapted the Government of India Act of 1935, preserving familiar offices and procedures while shifting responsibility to new political authorities. The Constituent Assemblies could legislate during the transition; they were not required to complete permanent constitutions before independence. This created a workable legal bridge, but it also meant that institutional continuity coexisted with a profound change in sovereignty. Nor could a statute by itself allocate trained personnel, divide records or settle a disputed boundary. Those problems required commissions, inter-Dominion negotiations and decisions made at exceptional speed. The provision ending paramountcy over princely states illustrates the difference between legal release and political settlement: extinguishing Crown treaties did not produce an internationally guaranteed third path for every ruler, nor did it determine accession to one Dominion or the other. That required separate choices and often intense negotiation, as Chapter 87 examines. The Act should thus be read both as a decisive constitutional instrument and as a framework whose implementation remained uncertain. Its clearly specified appointed day of 15 August 1947 did not imply that all institutions, territorial borders or administrative assets had already been settled on that date.

Section 85.19

Preparing the Transfer of Power: Institutions, Personnel, Finances, and Unresolved Questions

The ten weeks between the 3 June announcement and 15 August were occupied by a task of extraordinary administrative complexity: dividing a state that had been constructed over two centuries as a single machine. A Partition Council, chaired by Mountbatten and including Patel and Rajendra Prasad for Congress and Jinnah and Liaquat Ali Khan for the League, was established to oversee the division, supported by a Steering Committee and a number of expert committees. The Council's decisions covered the army, the civil services, the financial assets and liabilities, the railways and other communications, the postal and telegraph systems, the archives, and the physical assets of government, down to furniture and stationery in government offices.

The division of the armed forces was among the most sensitive tasks. The Indian Army was to be divided on the basis of the communal composition of its units, with Muslim personnel going to Pakistan and non-Muslim personnel to India, and mixed units to be separated. Individual soldiers could choose, within limits. Field Marshal Auchinleck, as Supreme Commander of the undivided forces during the transition, was responsible for the process, which was to be completed in stages and, in practice, was not completed by 15 August. The division of the civil services followed a system of options: officers of the Indian Civil Service and other services could choose between India and Pakistan, subject to the needs of the successor governments, and many British officers decided to retire. Pakistan, with a smaller share of experienced administrators, had to establish a capital at Karachi and to organize ministries virtually from scratch.

Financial and material assets were divided according to formulas agreed in committee. The central government's cash balances and public debt were apportioned, with Pakistan's share of assets set at approximately seventeen and a half percent, a proportion that reflected an estimate of its share of population, revenue, and other factors. The division of other assets, including military stores, railways and rolling stock, and records, produced disputes that continued after independence, and the disagreement over the transfer of cash balances arose again in 1948 (Chapter 87). The two Constituent Assemblies, for their part, prepared to function as legislatures, and the Pakistan Constituent Assembly elected Jinnah as its president on 11 August.

On 30 June 1947, the government announced the establishment of two Boundary Commissions, one for Punjab and one for Bengal, each consisting of a chairman and four members, two nominated by Congress and two by the League. Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a British barrister who had never visited India, was appointed chairman of both commissions. He arrived in India on 8 July and worked on the awards over the following five weeks. This chapter does not reproduce the awards, which were announced after independence and are examined in Chapter 86. What is relevant here is that the political decision to divide the provinces had been taken in June and that the lines were to be drawn after the transfer of power had been effectively scheduled. A force of troops, the Punjab Boundary Force, was established to maintain order in the districts around the expected line, but its commanders were later criticized, and its jurisdiction was dissolved in the aftermath of the violence.

The consequences of the accelerated timetable were severe, and they should be distinguished from questions of political responsibility. The decision to partition was taken by the political leaders and the British government; the speed was Mountbatten's and the government's decision, supported by the leaders; the execution of the division fell to administrators who had little time, few precedents, and limited information about the situation on the ground. It is a mistake to attribute the subsequent violence only to the administrative failures or only to the decision to divide. The relationship between the political decision, the timetable, and the violence is a subject of continuing scholarly argument, to which Chapter 86 returns. The remaining uncertainties as of 14 August included the line of the boundary, the status of the largest princely states, the control of the Boundary Force, the security of minorities in the provinces about to be divided, and the unresolved division of the army and assets.

Section 85.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Why the British Raj Ended in Two States

This chapter has argued that the end of British rule was agreed upon before the political form of independence had been settled. Two historical questions have been intertwined throughout: why Britain left, and why the departure produced two states. The answers are related but distinct.

Britain left because the conditions that had sustained the Raj no longer held. The war exhausted British finances and manpower and made India a creditor. The administrative services were thinned. The Indian Army, while still largely obedient, had been shaken by the INA trials and the naval uprising, and ministers could not be sure that it would respond to a political crisis as it had in earlier decades. The nationalist movement, with its mass base and its capacity for mobilization, had not been broken by the repression of 1942, and the elections of 1945–1946 demonstrated the strength of both Congress and the League. The Labour government was committed in principle to Indian self-government. None of these developments alone was decisive. The INA trials did not destroy the army's loyalty; the naval uprising was confined to one service; the elections did not provide a single mandate. Together they convinced Attlee's government and its advisers that continued rule by consent was impossible and that rule by force was too costly.

The division into two Dominions arose from a different sequence. The first element was the question of representation: Congress claimed to speak for all Indians, the League for all Muslims, and the elections of 1945–1946 strengthened both claims without reconciling them. The second element was the contest over constitutional design. Congress favored a strong centre and a sovereign Assembly; the League sought parity, grouped provinces, and guarantees. The Cabinet Mission Plan attempted to bridge these positions through a limited Union and grouping, but its ambiguity about the procedure for joining Sections allowed each side to read it as it wished, and the dispute that followed, together with Nehru's statement of 10 July and the League's response, ended the settlement. The third element was the breakdown of public order: Direct Action Day, the Calcutta killings, and the violence in Noakhali, Bihar, and Punjab destroyed trust and made negotiation a matter of security as well as of constitutional principle. The fourth element was the provincial dimension: the interests of the Sikhs, of Bengal's Hindus and Muslims, of Assam, and of the Frontier meant that any partition would raise the further question of partitioning the provinces. The fifth was British policy: the announcement of 20 February 1947 set a deadline, and Mountbatten's arrival led to a rapid reassessment that discarded the Cabinet Mission framework and produced the 3 June Plan.

Whether the failure of constitutional federation reflected incompatible priorities, mistrust, disputed representation, breakdown of order, or decisions by individuals cannot be settled by choosing one explanation. The evidence suggests that all were involved. The priorities of Congress and the League were in tension, but both had at times shown flexibility. Mistrust deepened with each episode of violence. The claims to representation were genuine but incomplete. The breakdown of order narrowed the space for compromise. And specific decisions, including Nehru's statement, the League's withdrawal, Wavell's handling of the Interim Government, the timing of the British announcement, and Mountbatten's acceleration, shaped the path taken. A responsible historical account will resist both the view that Partition was an inevitable result of religious difference and the view that it was merely a product of one actor's miscalculation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Colonial State in Its Final Years

What changed?

  • British policy moved from managing constitutional reform within empire to negotiating withdrawal.
  • Indian politicians moved into the Executive Council and eventually into effective control of the central government.
  • The political map shifted from a single colonial territory toward two successor states and numerous princely states with an uncertain future.
  • The capacity of the colonial state to maintain order declined.

What remained continuous?

  • The legal framework of the Government of India Act of 1935, which served as the interim constitution of both Dominions.
  • The structure of the administrative services, army, and provincial boundaries inherited from the Raj.
  • Separate electorates and communal representation, which shaped the claims of the parties.
  • The princely states' position until paramountcy lapsed.

Who experienced change most directly, and who continuity?

Political leaders and officials in Delhi experienced rapid institutional change. Residents of Punjab and Bengal experienced the most acute and disruptive change, as their provinces were about to be divided. Villagers in provinces not divided, and many in the princely states, experienced greater continuity in daily life during these months.

Why does the pattern matter?

It shows that independence was a transformation of sovereignty built on inherited institutions. The strengths and weaknesses of those institutions shaped the new states, as the following chapters show.

The chapter ends at the transfer of power. On 14 and 15 August 1947, two Dominions came into being, each with a Governor-General, a Constituent Assembly, and a government drawn from the leadership that had negotiated the transfer. The boundary between them had not yet been announced, the largest princely states had not decided their futures, and in Punjab and Bengal, communities whose provinces had been divided by votes in June awaited the lines that would determine where they would live. Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the negotiations between Britain, the Indian National Congress, the Muslim League, and other political actors from the summer of 1945 to the transfer of power in August 1947. Its central argument is that the end of British rule was agreed upon before the political form of independence had been settled. Britain's withdrawal resulted from structural imperial weakness, while the creation of two Dominions resulted from a distinct sequence of contested negotiations and political choices.

Division I examined the postwar crisis of British authority. The Simla Conference of 1945 failed over the question of who could nominate Muslim members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, exposing the dispute between Congress and the League over representation. Britain emerged from the war financially strained and under a Labour government sympathetic to self-government. The INA trials of 1945–1946 mobilized opinion across communities and forced the government to remit the sentences, while the Royal Indian Navy uprising of February 1946 alarmed officials without proving the unreliability of the armed forces as a whole.

The elections of 1945–1946, held on a restricted franchise and under separate electorates, gave Congress a dominant position among general voters and the League an overwhelming one in Muslim-reserved seats. The results strengthened rival claims to represent the population but did not constitute a referendum on a precisely defined Pakistan, and provincial variation, particularly in Punjab, Bengal, Assam, and the Frontier, complicated any national interpretation.

Division II examined the Cabinet Mission. Congress favored a united India with a strong centre and a sovereign Constituent Assembly; the League sought Muslim parity, grouped provinces, and safeguards, with Pakistan as its stated goal. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946 rejected a sovereign Pakistan and proposed a Union with limited powers, three Sections of provinces, and a Constituent Assembly elected by provincial legislatures. Its ambiguity about whether provinces had to join their Sections at the outset, as distinct from a later right to leave a group, became the central point of dispute.

Division III followed the breakdown of compromise. The League accepted the Plan on 6 June, reading the grouping as the foundation of Muslim autonomy; Congress accepted the long-term plan while stating a different reading of grouping. Nehru's statement of 10 July led the League to withdraw its acceptance on 29 July and to call for Direct Action. The violence of 16 August in Calcutta, followed by outbreaks in Noakhali, Bihar, and elsewhere, destroyed trust and narrowed the space for negotiation. The Interim Government of September–October 1946 brought Congress and the League into a shared administration without a common constitutional program.

Division IV traced the last attempts at a common settlement. The League boycotted the Constituent Assembly that met on 9 December 1946, and the dispute over sovereignty and grouping remained unresolved despite the British statement of 6 December. Attlee's announcement of 20 February 1947 set a deadline of June 1948 and replaced Wavell with Mountbatten. In the following weeks the collapse of the Punjab coalition, the demand to partition Punjab and Bengal, the unsuccessful United Bengal proposal, and the position of the Frontier and Assam introduced territorial alternatives. Mountbatten, after discarding a plan to transfer power to provinces, adopted a plan that transferred it to two successor governments.

Division V examined the decision and transfer. The 3 June Plan provided for divided votes in the Punjab and Bengal assemblies, decisions in Sindh, referendums in the Frontier and Sylhet, and early Dominion status. The legislatures of Bengal and Punjab voted for partition through their divided sections. The Indian Independence Act of 18 July 1947 established the two Dominions from 15 August and ended British paramountcy over the princely states. Preparations for the transfer, through a Partition Council and two Boundary Commissions, took place under severe time pressure, with the boundary awards and many questions of assets and personnel unresolved at independence.

The chapter has emphasized the roles of regional and minority actors: Sikhs, Bengali Hindus and Muslims, the Frontier's Khudai Khidmatgar, Assam's Congress ministry, and the princely rulers. It has also emphasized the experience of those outside elite negotiations, including ratings, voters, and the victims of communal violence, whose full story belongs to Chapter 86.

The chapter's lesson is that the partition settlement was neither inevitable nor merely accidental. Structural constraints and individual decisions interacted, and the historian's task is to explain how a range of possibilities narrowed to one. The transfer of power on 15 August 1947 was the end of one history and the beginning of another, to which the next chapter turns.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Viceroy's Executive Council
The body of ministers, appointed under the 1935 constitutional framework, that advised the Viceroy and ran departments of the central government; Indianized during the war and reconstituted as the Interim Government in 1946.
Parity
Equal numerical representation of caste Hindus and Muslims on the Executive Council proposed in the Wavell Plan, a principle central to the League's claim to equality of status.
Separate electorates
A system in which voters of designated communities elected representatives only from reserved constituencies, shaping the meaning of election results.
Restricted franchise
Voting rights limited by property, taxation, and education, enfranchising roughly a tenth of the population in 1945–1946.
General seats
Legislative constituencies for voters who were neither Muslim nor Sikh, in which Congress won most of its seats.
Muslim-reserved seats
Constituencies in which only Muslim voters could vote, where the League won overwhelmingly in 1945–1946.
Red Fort trials
The courts-martial of INA officers held in Delhi from November 1945, notable for the joint trial of a Hindu, a Muslim, and a Sikh officer.
Royal Indian Navy uprising
The strike of naval ratings that began on 18 February 1946 at Bombay and spread to many ships and shore establishments before surrendering on 23 February.
Cabinet Mission
The mission of Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, and Alexander that visited India from March to June 1946 to negotiate the transfer of power.
Union
The central authority proposed in the Cabinet Mission Plan, with powers limited to foreign affairs, defense, and communications.
Sections A, B, and C
The three clusters of provinces in the Cabinet Mission Plan: Hindu-majority provinces (A), the northwestern Muslim-majority provinces (B), and Bengal with Assam (C).
Grouping
The procedure by which provinces within a Section could frame a common group constitution, disputed between Congress and the League.
Residuary powers
All governmental powers not assigned to the Union, which the Plan left with the provinces.
Constituent Assembly
A body convened to draft a constitution; the Assembly that met on 9 December 1946 was boycotted by the League.
Sovereignty
Ultimate legal and political authority; the dispute over whether the Assembly was sovereign or bound by the Plan was central to the breakdown.
Paramountcy
The British claim to supremacy over the princely states, which lapsed under the Independence Act.
Interim Government
The Executive Council reconstituted under Nehru in September 1946, joined by League nominees in October.
Direct Action
The League's call, issued on 29 July 1946, for action to achieve Pakistan, observed on 16 August 1946 without a defined form.
Great Calcutta Killing
The communal violence that followed Direct Action Day in Calcutta, 16–19 August 1946, with contested casualty estimates.
Objectives Resolution
Nehru's resolution of December 1946, adopted in January 1947, declaring the aims of the Constituent Assembly.
Breakdown plan
Wavell's contingency scheme for phased British withdrawal if negotiations failed, rejected by the Cabinet.
Unionist Party
The cross-communal, landlord-led party that dominated Punjab politics until 1946 and led the coalition government of Khizr Hayat Tiwana.
Khudai Khidmatgar
The Pashtun nonviolent movement led by Abdul Ghaffar Khan, allied with Congress in the Frontier.
United Bengal scheme
The 1947 proposal by Suhrawardy, Abul Hashim, and Sarat Bose for a sovereign Bengal outside both India and Pakistan.
3 June Plan
The British statement of 3 June 1947 providing for the division of British India and the procedures for provincial decisions.
Dominion status
The constitutional position of self-governing members of the Commonwealth, adopted as the interim status of India and Pakistan.
Partition Council
The body chaired by Mountbatten that supervised the division of assets, services, and institutions between the successor governments.
Boundary Commission
Each of the two commissions, for Punjab and Bengal, chaired by Cyril Radcliffe, appointed to demarcate the new boundaries.
Appointed day
The statutory date, 15 August 1947, on which the two Dominions came into being.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 85 matters because it explains how the political form of independent South Asia was decided. The question of who should inherit power from the British, and as one state or two, was not answered in advance by history. It emerged from elections, negotiations, violence, and decisions made under pressure by leaders whose capacities and constraints this chapter has examined.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that shaped the twentieth century. The weakening of European empires after the Second World War, the politics of representation in plural societies, and the problem of designing constitutions that can reconcile unity and diversity were not unique to South Asia, but the subcontinent's experience was among the most consequential. The distinction between the decision to end an empire and the decision about its successors is relevant to many decolonizations.

The settlement of 1947 shaped what followed. Chapter 86 examines the boundary awards, violence, and displacement that accompanied it. Chapter 87 examines the princely states and Kashmir, where the lapse of paramountcy left unfinished business. Chapter 88 examines how independent India framed its constitution using, in part, the legal inheritance described here. In Pakistan and later Bangladesh, the same inheritance and the unresolved questions of the 1947 settlement shaped political life for decades.

In the six-volume sequence, this chapter closes the colonial period and opens the postcolonial. It shows that the transfer of power was both an ending and a beginning, a moment when the institutions of the Raj were inherited by the states that replaced it. Students who understand why Britain left, why unity failed, and how partition was chosen are better prepared to understand the history of the states that emerged.
