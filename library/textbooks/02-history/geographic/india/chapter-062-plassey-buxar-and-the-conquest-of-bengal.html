Part XII — Company Rule and Colonial Conquest

Plassey, Buxar, and the Conquest of Bengal

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 12 August 1765, in a camp near Allahabad, the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II issued a grant that, on its face, looked like an ordinary act of imperial administration. It conferred the office of diwan for Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha on a corporation chartered in London. The emperor lacked effective control over Delhi and depended on stronger regional powers for his security and income. The recipient was a trading company that had, nine years earlier, lost its principal Bengal settlement to the provincial government. Between those two moments, a European commercial corporation became the party that collected the revenue of one of the wealthiest regions of South Asia.

This chapter explains how that happened. It begins in April 1756, when a young nawab, Siraj ud-Daulah, inherited the government of a prosperous Mughal province, and it ends in 1772, when the East India Company announced that it would collect Bengal's revenue through its own servants. In sixteen years the Company moved from being a privileged trader in Bengal, tolerated and taxed by its rulers, to being the dominant political, military, and fiscal power in the eastern subcontinent. Chapter 61 traced how the European companies became participants in South Asian political and military competition, and Chapter 60 examined the Bengal nawabi that Murshid Quli Khan founded and Alivardi Khan consolidated. This chapter follows the point at which those two histories collided.

The central argument is that the conquest of Bengal was a staged transformation, not a single event. Three moments are often compressed into one. Plassey, in June 1757, produced a change of government and gave the Company a ruler dependent on its support. Buxar, in October 1764, destroyed the prospect that a rival coalition could reverse that dependence by force. The Diwani grant of 12 August 1765 supplied the legal and financial foundation that turned military predominance into a revenue base. Each changed the Company's position in a different way, and only the combination of the three explains why 1772, rather than 1757, is the year in which the Company began to administer Bengal's revenue directly.

A second argument concerns Bengal itself. The older colonial narrative described a small European force defeating an Asian state weakened by despotism. The evidence examined here does not support that account. Bengal's rulers governed a sophisticated fiscal-military state, financed by a large banking sector and a productive agrarian and textile economy. The Company's success depended on divisions within Bengal's political elite, on the willingness of powerful bankers and military officers to change allegiance, on the Company's ability to pay and discipline a sepoy army, and on the legal forms of Mughal sovereignty that survived the empire's political fragmentation. Bengali actors made choices, and those choices shaped the outcome.

The chapter also asks whose interests the conquest served. The Company's directors in London, its servants in Calcutta, Bengal's financiers and merchants, its zamindars and cultivators, its weavers, and its nawabs did not stand to gain or lose in the same way. The private fortunes accumulated by Company officials after 1757 differed from the Company's corporate revenue, and both differed from the condition of those who produced the wealth. The chapter therefore pays attention to commerce, customs, credit, and taxation as well as battles and treaties.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Company records preserved in the British Library, including Clive's correspondence and the Bengal Public Consultations, show how British officials explained their decisions to their employers. Persian chronicles, above all Ghulam Husain Khan Tabatabai's Siyar al-Muta'akhkhirin, written roughly two decades later, offer a Bengali and Mughal-administrative perspective that is also retrospective and has its own sympathies. Imperial grants, treaties, and battle plans survive in copies, and their dating and wording sometimes differ. The chapter treats these sources as arguments to be examined, not as neutral windows on events.

The sections that follow are organized in five divisions. The first examines Siraj ud-Daulah's government and the crisis of 1756. The second analyzes the conspiracy and battle of 1757. The third follows Mir Jafar, Mir Qasim, and the commercial dispute that led to war. The fourth turns to Buxar, the Treaty of Allahabad, and the Diwani. The fifth examines dual government, its social consequences, and the historiography of the conquest. Bengal's famine of 1770, Warren Hastings's reforms, parliamentary regulation, and the Permanent Settlement belong to Chapter 63, and appear here only as a forward reference.

Students should keep in mind that the ideas of "conquest" and "empire" in the chapter title are retrospective. No one in Calcutta or Murshidabad in 1757 was planning the British Raj. Treating later outcomes as the intentions of earlier actors is the main interpretive danger in this period, and the chapter's attention to contingency, choice, and the layered character of Company authority is meant to guard against it.

Historical Context

In the mid-eighteenth century, Bengal was a subah, or province, of the Mughal Empire that had become largely self-governing. Chapter 60 showed how Murshid Quli Khan, appointed diwan of Bengal in 1700 and later its governor, built a provincial regime that combined the offices of revenue collection and executive government. His successors, Shuja-ud-Din and, from 1740, Alivardi Khan, continued to acknowledge Mughal sovereignty in coinage, court ritual, and nominal tribute while controlling appointments, revenue, and the army. The province's capital, Murshidabad, was a major commercial and financial center. Its banking houses, above all that of the Jagat Seths, handled revenue remittances, minted coin, and extended credit to the government and to merchants.

Bengal's economy was large and commercialized. Its cultivators produced rice and, increasingly, commercial crops such as sugar, silk, and indigo. Its weavers produced cotton and silk textiles for Indian and overseas markets, and Bengal supplied a large share of the textiles that European companies exported to Europe and Southeast Asia. European and Asian merchants imported silver, which funded these purchases and supplied the province's currency. The Company, the Dutch, the French, and other companies had factories along the Hugli, the western distributary of the Ganges delta, and each negotiated the terms of its trade with the nawab's government.

The Company's legal position rested on the farman granted by the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar in 1717, which allowed the Company to trade duty-free in Bengal on presentation of a certificate called a dastak, in exchange for a fixed annual payment. The farman gave the Company a privileged place in Bengal's commerce, but successive nawabs disputed how far it extended, especially to the private trade of individual Company servants. These disputes were not trivial. Internal customs and tolls were a significant source of provincial revenue, and a Company servant's exemption was a Bengali merchant's disadvantage.

The 1740s and 1750s also brought pressures that made the political settlement fragile. Maratha raids in Bengal and Odisha between 1742 and 1751 damaged parts of the province and strained its finances, and Alivardi Khan's wars left a large army to be paid. The Seven Years' War, which began in Europe in 1756, extended Anglo-French rivalry to India, where the Carnatic Wars (Chapter 61) had already shown that European companies could fight a regional war using Indian soldiers trained in European drill and supported by artillery. In Bengal, these developments combined with a succession crisis. The nawab's death in April 1756 left the province to a young grandson with rivals within his own family and powerful officials whose loyalty could not be assumed.

Section 62.1

Bengal under Siraj ud-Daulah: Succession, Sovereignty, and Provincial Politics

The Bengal that Siraj ud-Daulah inherited was an established regional state with its own institutions, revenue system, army, and court. Murshid Quli Khan had united the offices of provincial governor and diwan, reorganized the revenue administration around a class of large zamindars and revenue-farmers, and moved the capital from Dhaka to Murshidabad. Shuja-ud-Din (r. 1727–1739) extended the arrangement, and Alivardi Khan, who seized power in 1740 after defeating Shuja-ud-Din's son Sarfaraz Khan, governed until his death on 9 April 1756. Each ruler acknowledged the Mughal emperor in form while securing imperial confirmation by payment and negotiation rather than by obedience to a functioning imperial administration. The Bengal nawabs thus held a layered authority: they were Mughal officeholders by title, but regional sovereigns in practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Nizamat and Diwani

In the Mughal provincial system, two principal offices governed a subah. The nazim (or subahdar) held the nizamat: command of the army, maintenance of order, and criminal justice. The diwan held the diwani: the collection of revenue and the civil fiscal administration, including civil justice in revenue matters. Mughal practice originally set the two offices as a check on each other, with the diwan answerable to the emperor. Murshid Quli Khan and his successors in Bengal combined the two in practice. The distinction matters to this chapter because the Company's grant of 1765 separated them again: the Company obtained the diwani, while the nizamat remained with a nominal nawab.

Why it matters: The terms show that Company power in Bengal was built through Mughal legal categories, not outside them.

Siraj ud-Daulah was Alivardi's grandson, the son of his youngest daughter's husband, Zain-ud-Din Ahmad Khan, who had died in 1748. Alivardi had no surviving son, and he designated Siraj as heir, a choice that other relatives did not accept. His eldest daughter, Ghasiti Begum, controlled substantial resources at Motijhil near Murshidabad. His cousin Shaukat Jang, the governor of Purnea, also pressed a claim, and was defeated and killed in October 1756. Siraj's accession in April 1756 thus began with a challenge from within the family, and the way in which he resolved it, by confiscation and force, alienated some of the court's powerful figures.

Siraj also faced a government whose senior officers had their own ambitions. Mir Jafar, who was married to Alivardi's half-sister, commanded a large part of the army and was a seasoned officer. Rai Durlabh held high revenue office and had followers of his own. Mir Madan and Mohan Lal were among those loyal to the new nawab, and Siraj promoted some of his own favourites, including Mohan Lal, over older officers. Contemporary and later sources, especially those written after Siraj's fall, describe him as headstrong and arbitrary. Some of that criticism is probably well founded, and some reflects the interests of those who profited from his removal. The principal point for a historian is that his early months show a young ruler seeking to assert authority over an elite that had grown accustomed to bargaining with his grandfather as a senior partner.

The banking house of the Jagat Seths occupied a distinct place in this structure. Based at Murshidabad, the family handled remittance of revenue to Delhi, supplied credit to the government, and dealt in bullion and exchange for local and foreign merchants, including the European companies. Their wealth depended on a stable government that paid its debts and protected trade, and some accounts record their displeasure at Siraj's treatment of them. They did not simply serve the nawab, nor were they his enemies by nature, and their calculation of risk would become important in 1757. Revenue officials, zamindars with their own armed retainers, and Hindu and Muslim military officers formed the rest of the political structure on which a nawab depended.

The nawab's relations with the European trading companies formed part of this setting. Murshidabad allowed the Dutch at Chinsura, the French at Chandernagore, and the English at Calcutta and Kasimbazar to trade, to maintain fortified warehouses, and to pay customs under agreed terms. The nawabs valued the revenue and the supply of silver, and regarded the companies as subjects of the provincial government, however privileged. Alivardi Khan, in later life, was reported to have warned his successor about the dangers of allowing the Europeans to fortify, and he used the Europeans' conflicts in the Carnatic as an example. Even if the specific reports reflect later embellishment, they show that the question of European armed strength was already in the court's mind. Siraj's task was to govern an established state, protect its revenue, and maintain control over the Europeans; it was not to obstruct Company trade as such.

Section 62.2

Company Privileges, Fortification, and the Breakdown of Relations

The immediate quarrel between Siraj and the Company arose from four linked issues: the extent of the Company's trading privileges, the private trade of its servants, the shelter given to a political fugitive, and the strengthening of Calcutta's defences. Each touched the question of who held authority in Bengal's principal commercial port.

Historical Vocabulary
Dastak

A dastak was a pass or certificate, issued under the Company's authority, that exempted goods from inland customs duties. Under the 1717 farman, goods moving for the Company's own trade could pass duty-free on presentation of a dastak. Company servants, however, also traded on their own account, and many sought to cover private cargoes with the same certificates. Bengal's rulers held that the farman protected corporate trade only; Company officials often claimed wider coverage or issued dastaks to Indian merchants for a fee.

Why it matters: A dastak was both a commercial document and a claim about sovereignty, since the right to tax commerce was a core attribute of provincial government.

The 1717 farman allowed the Company to import and export goods in Bengal without paying the usual customs, on the strength of dastaks signed by the president of Calcutta, in return for an annual payment. The Company took this as a generous and permanent right. Bengal's governments accepted the farman as a privilege to the Company's corporate trade but disputed how far it extended: to the Company's inland trade, to the private trade of its employees, and to goods belonging to Indian merchants who borrowed the Company's name. Private trade was a recognized part of the Company's employment system, since servants received low salaries and were expected to make their living by trade. As a result the volume of private trade by Company servants was very large. Each dastak used for a private cargo reduced the nawab's customs revenue and gave the Company servant an advantage over Bengal's merchants, who paid the duties.

Fortification was the second problem. As the Seven Years' War approached, the Company, the French, and the Dutch all considered how to protect their settlements. Calcutta's officials began to repair and extend the defences of Fort William and its batteries, reasoning that a French attack from Chandernagore was a real possibility. Siraj regarded this as a breach of the understanding under which the Europeans traded, and as a danger to his own control over the river. He ordered the work stopped and the new fortifications demolished. The French and the Dutch, by most accounts, gave replies that satisfied him in form, though the French did not, in fact, stop all preparations. The Company's replies were evasive in his view and in the view of some of his advisers.

The third issue involved Krishna Das (Krishnaballabh), the son of Raj Ballabh, an official of Dhaka whom Siraj had pursued. Krishna Das came to Calcutta with his family and, reportedly, a considerable portion of their wealth, and the Company's officials gave him shelter. Siraj demanded his surrender. The Company's refusal, and the manner in which it treated the nawab's messenger, are given prominence in the contemporary Company and Persian accounts, though they interpret the episode differently. For Siraj it was a case of an inferior power sheltering an offender against the provincial government and defying a direct demand. For the Company's officials, the refusal was a protection of a petitioner and of their right to run their own settlement.

It is important to see why these were not merely disputes about commercial regulation. Whoever decided which goods paid duty, which fortifications were permitted, and whom the settlement could protect was exercising the powers of a sovereign. The Company's statements of its position stressed its charter, its farman, and its need to defend itself in wartime. The nawab's position, expressed in surviving communications and in the accounts of his officials, was that the Company was a privileged guest in his territory, and that the privileges were subject to his government's continued approval. The surviving evidence on each side was written to justify actions already taken, and the chronology of what was said and when is not always certain. What is clear is that two inconsistent understandings of the Company's status met in the summer of 1756: one treating it as a subordinate trading community, the other as an autonomous corporation with its own defensible territory.

Section 62.3

The Capture of Calcutta and the Black Hole Controversy, June 1756

In late May 1756, Siraj marched on Kasimbazar, the Company's factory close to Murshidabad, which was held by only a small garrison under William Watts. The factory capitulated on 4 June, and Watts and others were taken to the nawab's camp. The army then moved down the river towards Calcutta, and by 16 June had begun its attack on the outlying defences. Calcutta's settlement included Fort William, the commercial quarter, and a sprawling area occupied by Indian merchants, artisans, and dependants. The Company's garrison was small and its defences, still under repair, were incomplete.

The siege lasted a matter of days. The nawab's army overran the town's perimeter and, after a series of losses, a number of the Company's leading officials, including the governor Roger Drake, left the fort by boat and moved downriver, joining the ships. Command of those who remained fell to John Zephaniah Holwell, a Company surgeon turned official. The fort surrendered on 20 June 1756. Siraj occupied Calcutta, which he renamed Alinagar, installed a garrison under Manik Chand, and returned to Murshidabad. Many of Calcutta's Indian merchants and residents had fled or been displaced, and the town's commerce stopped. The Company's survivors were stranded at Fulta, downriver, under the protection of the ships, awaiting reinforcements from Madras.

The Black Hole is the best-known story of this episode. In Holwell's account, published in 1758, the nawab's soldiers on the night of 20 June confined the British prisoners in a small guardroom in the fort. Holwell stated that 146 people were imprisoned and that all but 23 had died by morning. The story, repeated in Company writing and then in British histories of India, became a defining image of Bengal's rule as despotic and of the conquest as retribution. Siraj, in Holwell's telling, was the author of an atrocity.

Modern historians have approached the story with caution, for several reasons. Holwell's account is the principal source for the number of prisoners and for the death toll, and it was written for publication after the Company's recovery of Calcutta, when its author had reason to emphasize the injustice that the Company had suffered. Other contemporary evidence is thin. The size of the room, as reconstructed by later investigators, makes the claimed numbers hard to accept. The scholar Brijen K. Gupta, in a reassessment of the episode, argued that the group confined was much smaller than Holwell stated, and that the number of deaths was correspondingly lower, though still a real loss of life. Many historians accept that some prisoners were confined and died, but the size of the group, the number who died, the circumstances, and the reliability of Holwell’s reconstruction remain contested; no adequate evidence establishes that the nawab personally ordered their deaths. The episode then acquired a political life of its own. Lord Curzon, as viceroy, built a monument at Calcutta to its supposed victims in the early twentieth century, and Indian nationalists in the 1930s campaigned for its removal. This chapter therefore treats the Black Hole in two ways: as an event of uncertain scale, and as a story whose repeated use in British writing helped to present the conquest of Bengal as a moral rescue.

The fall of Calcutta also exposed the vulnerability of a trading settlement that had grown without a military or political strategy for defending itself. It further showed that Siraj could act decisively. But the outcome was not a strategic success for him. He had not destroyed the Company's capacity to retaliate, because its naval and military resources in southern India were intact, and he had not secured a settlement that would bind the Company. His quick withdrawal to Murshidabad left open the question of what would follow, and his court included members who regarded the war as unnecessary and dangerous.

Context: Roger Drake was the Company's governor at Calcutta when the settlement fell, and wrote his narrative afterward, addressed to his superiors, in circumstances where his own decision to leave the fort was open to criticism. The letters of Siraj ud-Daulah to Khwaja Wazid, an Armenian merchant active in the nawab's dealings with Europeans, belong to the nawab's own diplomatic communication.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim transcription): The Company narrative presents the fall of Calcutta as the result of the nawab's aggression and the overwhelming number of his forces, and it explains the governor's departure as a necessity. The surviving correspondence belongs to the nawab’s diplomatic dealings during the dispute. Read alongside the documented controversy over fortification and the sheltering of Krishna Das, it offers a possible counter-perspective to the Company’s official explanation; this edition does not attribute specific uncollated assertions to any one letter.

Analysis: The two sources serve opposing purposes. Drake needs to show that the loss was unavoidable and the Company the injured party; Siraj's letters assert the authority of the provincial government to regulate its European merchants. Neither is a neutral narrative. Placed side by side, they reveal that the dispute was understood by each side as a matter of sovereignty and obedience. The documents say little about the experience of Calcutta's Indian residents or of the soldiers on either side. The wording of these documents should be checked against a collated edition before it is quoted.

Section 62.4

Robert Clive, Charles Watson, and the Recovery of Calcutta, 1756–1757

News of Calcutta's fall reached Madras in mid-August 1756, and the Madras council resolved on an expedition. The Company had acquired military experience in the Carnatic, and it possessed a body of Indian sepoys trained and paid by the Company, some European infantry and artillery, and the support of a Royal Navy squadron under Rear-Admiral Charles Watson. Robert Clive, who had returned to Madras from England after earlier service, was chosen to lead the land force. Clive had entered the Company as a writer in 1744, shifted to military service, and gained wide reputation through the defence of Arcot in 1751. His appointment reflected a judgment that the Company needed a commander who had proved he could win with sepoys. His force and Watson's ships reached Bengal in December 1756.

The expedition moved quickly. After an action at Budge Budge on 29 December, Clive and Watson advanced on Calcutta, and the garrison there surrendered on 2 January 1757. The Company also attacked and sacked Hugli, a major trading town upriver, to put pressure on the nawab. Siraj marched south with his army and camped near Calcutta in early February. On 5 February Clive launched an attack on the nawab's camp. It was not a clear victory, but it demonstrated the Company's willingness to fight and the discipline of its troops, and it shook the nawab's confidence.

A reason for the nawab's willingness to settle was the situation on his western frontier. In January 1757, Ahmad Shah Durrani's Afghan army had sacked Delhi, and Siraj had reason to fear an invasion of Bihar and Bengal. This danger to the north, together with the Company's military pressure and the reluctance of some of his officers to continue the war, led him to open negotiations. The resulting Treaty of Alinagar, dated 9 February 1757, restored the Company's privileges under the 1717 farman, allowed the fortification of Calcutta, compensated the Company for its losses, and permitted it to mint coin. It was in effect a restoration of the Company's position on terms more favourable than before. The Company was now not only a privileged trader but a party with an acknowledged right to defend itself.

The Anglo-French war then widened the conflict. News arrived that Britain and France were at war, and Clive, with Watson's agreement, decided to attack the French settlement of Chandernagore, which was protected by the nawab's neutrality and which the French might use as a base against Calcutta. Siraj, caught between his wish to keep both European powers in check and his fear of the Company, wavered. The Company took Chandernagore on 23 March 1757 after a combined naval and military assault. The French lost their principal Bengal settlement. Some of their officers escaped to join the nawab's forces, and these included Jean Law, who would later be a prominent French agent in the region.

Clive's own attitude changed over these months. The Company's official policy remained mainly commercial, but Clive had already concluded from the repeated disputes that an arrangement dependent on the good will of a volatile nawab was unsafe. In the weeks after the French defeat he, Watts at Kasimbazar, and the Company's Calcutta council began to consider whether the nawab could be replaced by someone who would honour the Company's privileges. Siraj also grew suspicious of the Company's intentions and moved some troops to Plassey. The nawab's court included many who wished to avoid another war and some who wished to remove the nawab. That court, rather than the Company alone, would shape what followed.

Section 62.5

The Conspiracy against Siraj ud-Daulah: Mir Jafar, the Jagat Seths, and Bengal's Political Elites

The Battle of Plassey cannot be understood without the political plot that preceded it. By March and April 1757, a group of influential figures in Siraj ud-Daulah's court, army, and banking community had concluded that the nawab was a liability. Their reasons were not identical, and the Company was not the initiator of every move. It was, rather, a participant that could supply what Bengal's own factions lacked: a disciplined army outside the nawab's control, and a willingness to guarantee the position of the man who would replace him.

The conspirators' grievances combined personal, financial, and political concerns. Mir Jafar, who commanded a major part of the army, had been passed over, in his view, by the nawab's favourites and had reason to fear that Siraj would deprive him of power. Rai Durlabh, a senior revenue officer, shared a sense of insecurity. The Jagat Seths, according to Persian and British sources, had been slighted by the nawab, but their central concern was probably commercial: the war with the Company had interrupted trade, and the nawab's unpredictability, together with the threat from the Afghans in the north and the Marathas to the south, made a stable government seem unlikely under his rule. They also had financial stakes in the Company's trade and in the settlement of the Company's claims. Other officers and zamindars were drawn in at different stages.

On the Company side, William Watts at Kasimbazar was the principal channel. Watts, who had been released after the capture of the factory, used the Armenian merchant Khwaja Petrus and a network of intermediaries to open discussions. Clive, in Calcutta, took the lead in negotiating terms. The proposal was to replace Siraj with Mir Jafar, who in return would confirm the Company's privileges, pay substantial sums as compensation, and grant it additional rights. The conspirators wanted assurance that the Company's forces would actually support them, and the Company wanted assurance that Mir Jafar's soldiers would not fight.

Omichand (Amir Chand), a wealthy Calcutta merchant with connections to the nawab's court, entered the story as an intermediary who learned of the plan. According to the accounts, he threatened to reveal it unless he was promised a large payment, and Clive and Watts responded by drawing up two treaties: a genuine one that omitted the sum, on white paper, and a fraudulent one that included it, on red. Admiral Watson, it is reported, refused to sign the false version and Clive had his signature added. This story is documented in Company and later accounts, including testimony given in Britain, but those accounts reflect the interests of their authors. Clive later defended the deception as a necessary response to blackmail. For the historian, the episode is a revealing example of the Company's willingness to use deceit and of the role of merchants as political intermediaries, but its details should be treated with the caution suited to testimony given in self-defence or controversy.

The agreement was finalized in early June 1757. Under its terms, as reported, Mir Jafar was to confirm the privileges of the Company, to pay indemnities for losses at Calcutta, including large sums to the Company, to British inhabitants, and to Indian and Armenian inhabitants, and to cede the Company the zamindari of the area south of Calcutta later known as the 24 Parganas. The French would be excluded from Bengal. Clive would march on Murshidabad, and Mir Jafar would join him or at least withdraw.

The location of the bargaining mattered. Watts operated from the nawab’s own commercial and administrative world, where confidential messages could be passed through merchants who already knew both the European factories and the Murshidabad establishment. Khwaja Petrus Aratoon and other Armenian intermediaries were not incidental messengers: they offered linguistic skills, credit connections, and personal access. Nor were the Jagat Seths interchangeable with the military officers. A banking household sought security for loans, government deposits, minting interests, and the movement of money, whereas Mir Jafar sought the authority and household resources of the nawabi. Their temporary agreement concealed the different expectations that would surface once the regime had changed.

The agreement also raised a problem of credibility. A conspiracy depended on assurances offered privately by people who could publicly deny their obligations, and the Company's promise of future payments could not by itself secure compliance on the battlefield. Each party had an incentive to wait for another to act first. The allocation of compensation to European, Indian, and Armenian claimants revealed the coalition's commercial reach, but those promised sums were also claims on a provincial treasury already subject to competing military and fiscal demands. The settlement therefore planted a financial contradiction within the proposed government before the first shot was fired.

One feature of this conspiracy deserves emphasis. It involved men who were not strangers to power, and who understood the risk of changing a ruler. Their acceptance of the Company's role suggests that they regarded the Company as a manageable ally and likely expected it to remain a trading power that would protect commerce and leave governance to Bengalis. That calculation would prove mistaken, but it was an informed one. The rest of Bengal's court remained loyal to Siraj, as events would show, and he still held substantial forces.

Section 62.6

The Battle of Plassey, 23 June 1757

Clive left Calcutta in mid-June with a force of about 3,000 men, of whom roughly 800 were Europeans and the rest Indian sepoys, together with a small number of artillery pieces. The march went up the Bhagirathi, partly by river and partly on land. As he approached the nawab's army, Clive was seized by doubt about the wisdom of fighting if Mir Jafar failed to act, and he reportedly held a council of war that was divided on whether to continue. He decided to cross the river and advance, a decision whose full risk was only apparent after the crossing, since retreat would then be difficult. On the evening of 22 June his troops took position in a mango grove near the village of Palashi, close to the nawab's entrenched camp.

Siraj's army was far larger in number. Estimates, which vary widely, run to between 35,000 and 50,000 or more, including infantry, cavalry, and an artillery train, though the numbers and the proportion of reliable troops are uncertain. The nawab's artillery included large guns drawn by oxen, and a small contingent of French artillerymen, under an officer usually named Sinfray (St Frais), served some of the guns. The nawab's army was also divided in its command. Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, and Yar Latif Khan commanded significant parts of it. Mir Madan and Mohan Lal were among those on whom Siraj could rely most closely.

The fighting began on the morning of 23 June with an artillery duel. The nawab's guns opened early and with some effect, and the Company's troops, in the grove and behind a bank, suffered casualties. The Company's artillery replied, and by late morning heavy rain fell. The Company's gunners had covered their powder with tarpaulins, while the nawab's artillery, by Company accounts, did not protect theirs, and the nawab's fire slackened. Mir Madan, who was leading the nawab's more loyal troops, was killed by a shot, and this was a major blow to the nawab's side. Siraj, advised by Mir Jafar to withdraw to his camp, apparently concluded that the situation was serious and that he could not rely on all his commanders. When the Company advanced on the camp in the afternoon, most of the nawab's troops did not resist. Siraj fled with a small retinue towards Murshidabad.

Casualties were light for a battle of such consequence: the Company's losses are usually put at under 100 killed and wounded, while the nawab's side lost several hundred. These figures, drawn from British accounts, should be read with caution. The notable fact is that the large part of the nawab's army was not engaged. Mir Jafar's troops, and those of other conspirators, remained apart from the fight, and the principal fighting was done by the units under Mir Madan and Mohan Lal and by the French gunners.

The older narrative treats Plassey as a triumph of European tactical discipline and superior firepower, in which a small force defeated an army many times larger. That account is not sustainable. It overlooks the political arrangements that left much of the nawab's army out of the contest, the effect of the rain on the competing artillery, and the nawab's loss of his most dependable commander. It also overlooks that the Company's force, although small, was well drilled, well supplied, and led by an officer who had learned in the Carnatic the use of disciplined infantry and artillery. Both elements were needed: a competent military force, and a conspiracy that deprived the opponent of effective use of his numbers. The National Army Museum's catalogue of battle plans and its discussion of the engagement are helpful for reconstructing the positions, though the plans themselves were produced by Company officers and reflect their view of events.

The outcome also depended on decisions taken during the day. Clive's hesitation about his own position, Mir Jafar's calculation about how to avoid exposure, and Siraj's reaction to the news that Mir Madan had fallen all shaped the course of the day. Plassey was not predetermined. If the rain had not fallen on the nawab's powder, if Siraj had pressed the Company's position earlier, or if Mir Jafar had been less cautious about declaring himself, the result could have been different. The lesson of Plassey is that outcomes in this period emerged from the interaction of military action, political conspiracy, uncertain loyalties, and individual decisions.

Historical Debate 1

Was Plassey Primarily a Military Victory or a Political Revolution Engineered through Elite Alliances?

The question: How much of the outcome at Plassey, and of the change of government that followed, resulted from the Company's military capability, and how much from the conspiracy among Bengal's own elites?

Interpretation A — A military victory by a superior force: Older British narratives, and some later popular histories, emphasize the discipline of the Company's troops, the effectiveness of its artillery, and Clive's leadership. On this view the battle demonstrated that a European-trained force could defeat larger Indian armies, and it opened the way for further conquest.
Interpretation B — A political revolution engineered by Bengal's elites: Historians such as Sushil Chaudhury, and others who study Bengal's political and financial structure, emphasize that the change in 1757 was the result of a conspiracy among powerful bankers, officials, and commanders who sought to replace a ruler they distrusted. In this reading the Company was an instrument of an internal political realignment as much as a conqueror, and the battle itself was a minor engagement.

Evidence: Both rest on the same documents: the treaty material, Clive's journal and correspondence, Persian chronicles, and military plans. A military reading draws on the Company's discipline, the small size of the battle's casualties on the Company side, and the continuing decisive role of its forces after Plassey. A political reading draws on the evidence of the conspiracy, the non-participation of much of the nawab's army, and the role of Mir Jafar and the Jagat Seths.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians describe Bengali agency. If Plassey was a military conquest, Bengal's elite appears as a passive victim. If it was a political revolution, Bengali actors made choices with consequences they did not foresee, and responsibility is shared in more complex ways. The two readings are not mutually exclusive. The conspiracy created conditions in which a small army could win, and the army gave the conspiracy practical effect.

Section 62.7

Siraj's Fall, Mir Jafar's Accession, and the Political Settlement of 1757

Siraj ud-Daulah fled from the field and reached Murshidabad, but found little support there. His army had dispersed, and officers who had stood aside at Plassey had no reason to rally to him afterward. He left the city, apparently intending to reach Patna or the French forces under Jean Law, but he was recognized and seized near Rajmahal by the soldiers of a local official. He was brought back to Murshidabad and, on 2 July 1757 (the date most commonly accepted, although the precise sequence of his capture and execution differs among accounts), killed on the orders, or at least with the approval, of Miran, the son of Mir Jafar. The accounts of his last days, in both Persian and English sources, differ in detail, and the version of his death that circulated was shaped by the political interest of those who benefited from it. What is clear is that the new regime moved swiftly to eliminate the former nawab and that this removed any immediate prospect of his return.

Mir Jafar had entered Murshidabad on 25 June, and Clive arrived a few days later to install him publicly as nawab. The agreement between Mir Jafar and the Company was then confirmed and expanded. Its principal provisions, as recorded in Company documents, included the restoration of the Company's privileges and its right to trade without payment of duties, the confirmation of the Company's possession of the lands around Calcutta, the payment of indemnities for the Company's losses and those of the inhabitants of Calcutta, the exclusion of the French from Bengal, and a commitment that the nawab would maintain troops at the Company's request. The sums promised were very large, and the state treasury at Murshidabad was found to hold less than the totals promised. This would prove a recurrent problem. The Company's officers and the nawab agreed on a series of installments, and the Jagat Seths were involved in arranging the credit and settlement of payments.

The new regime had immediate effects on those who served the old one. Mohan Lal and others close to Siraj lost position, and some court officials were dismissed. Bankers, merchants, and military officers took positions that served their interests under the new nawab. In Calcutta, the Company moved to restore the settlement, rebuild its fortifications, and open a mint. A considerable quantity of treasure from the Murshidabad treasury was transported to Calcutta in boats, an event well known in the Company's own accounts.

A critical distinction concerns what the treaty could deliver immediately. A nawab could confirm Company privileges and authorize payments, but paying those sums meant mobilizing bankers, accountants, treasury officers, and provincial tax revenues over time. The grant of the Twenty-Four Parganas likewise conferred a revenue interest and a territorial foothold; it did not instantly supply the dense apparatus required to govern the entire province. The new political order still relied on offices, landholding rights, and chains of personal allegiance inherited from the earlier nawabi. Plassey's beneficiaries therefore faced the practical task of making extraordinary promises compatible with ordinary government, and this helps explain the instability of Mir Jafar's reign.

It is important not to overstate what changed in June and July 1757. The Company had determined who would be nawab, and it had gained commercial concessions and a large cash settlement. It had not taken over the government. Bengal's revenue administration, its courts, its army, and its officials continued under the nawab. The Company's territorial holding was limited to the area around Calcutta and the 24 Parganas, which it held as a zamindar, that is, as a landholder with revenue rights within the nawab's system. The distinction between the ability to install a ruler and the assumption of responsibility for governing his districts is central to the chapter. In 1757 the Company's political leverage was extraordinary, but its formal position was still that of a privileged subject of a Mughal province, and it relied on the nawab's government to collect revenue and keep order.

Section 62.8

Company Political Manipulation, Private Fortunes, and the Reconfiguration of Power

The financial consequences of Plassey extended far beyond the formal succession. The settlement of 1757 created a system of payments that enriched individuals as well as the Company and that placed heavy demands on Bengal's treasury. Company officers received substantial sums in the form of gifts and compensation, and Clive himself later acknowledged receiving a very large personal payment, a figure that is often given as roughly £234,000 though the sources and the exact composition of the amount vary. Members of the Calcutta council, military officers, and others also received sums. The money came from the treasury of Bengal and was settled through Indian financial intermediaries, who earned fees and strengthened their own claims on the new regime.

The private enrichment of the Company's servants was not a side effect. It reshaped the Company's internal politics. The Company's directors in London, who had wanted a stable commercial position, now found that their employees possessed fortunes and political leverage they themselves lacked. The distinction between the Company's corporate interest and the personal financial interests of its servants became an issue that would run through the next fifteen years. Servants had an incentive to promote nawabs who would grant favours and to resist reforms that restricted private trade, while the Company's interest lay in a reliable revenue and an orderly commerce. Clive's own position was ambiguous: he was both the Company's commander and a major beneficiary of the arrangements, and in 1758 the Company confirmed him as governor of Bengal. In 1759 Mir Jafar granted Clive a personal jagir of the rent due to the nawab from the Company's own lands near Calcutta, under which the Company paid its own most powerful servant the rent due on its lands near Calcutta, an arrangement that made Clive, in effect, the Company's landlord.

The effect on the nawab was constraining. Mir Jafar needed Company troops to maintain his position, and the Company expected payment, both for its military services and for the sums promised in the treaty. The payments competed with the demands of the nawab's own army, whose soldiers wanted their arrears, and with the ordinary costs of government. The Company's military establishment in Bengal was financed increasingly from the revenues of the provincial government, including the assignments of districts that the nawab made to cover his obligations. Bengal's government thus came to support a foreign military force that it did not control. In effect, the Company had begun to extract from Bengal the resources needed to maintain an army that could, in turn, guarantee its own predominance.

Indian financiers were part of this process. The Jagat Seths and others arranged credit, converted payments into transferable instruments, and advised both sides. Their role shows that the new relationship was not a simple matter of coercion. Bankers saw an opportunity in extending credit to a regime that needed money, and some Indian merchants gained from association with the Company. They also took risks, since a political settlement dependent on foreign troops was unstable and a failure to collect on loans could be ruinous.

The financial settlement also needs to be read through the distinction between different recipients. Reimbursement to the corporate Company, compensation awarded to those who had lost property in Calcutta, military prize money, and personal gifts to officers were not the same category of expenditure. Yet the political negotiations enabled all of them, allowing public purposes and private profit to become entangled. English Heritage reports that Clive's personal presents following Plassey were valued at approximately £234,000, a figure traceable to subsequent parliamentary scrutiny; it should be understood as a historically reported valuation, not a modern-currency comparison. Senior officials who received gifts had strong incentives to influence decisions about appointments and revenue, while directors in London confronted the difficulties of supervising servants thousands of miles away.

The changing terms of accountability produced a second problem. A Company officer could describe a payment as restitution for losses, an Indian court official could treat it as a customary political gift, and a later critic in Britain could interpret it as corruption. These categories did not operate under one universally accepted set of legal rules. Nevertheless, the scale of personal distributions mattered: they increased political intervention by creating immediate financial rewards for changes of government. The resulting conflict between a company's commercial mission and its servants' private enrichment would become a persistent subject of reform debates in Britain.

By 1758, therefore, the relationship between nawab and Company was one of unequal dependence. The nawab could not govern without the Company's support, and the Company could not realize its gains without a functioning provincial government. The structure placed pressure on both sides. The nawab sought to preserve his authority by balancing among the Company, his own officers, and external allies. The Company's officials looked for ways to ensure payment and to protect their private fortunes. The British Library's collection of Clive's correspondence and the documents of the 1757 settlement in the series IOR/H/192 give the best insight into how Company officials themselves understood this relationship, but they reflect that perspective and must be read alongside Persian and Bengali evidence.

Mir Jafar, Mir Qasim, and the Struggle for Bengal

Section 62.9

Mir Jafar's Government and the Contradictions of Indirect Control, 1757–1760

Mir Jafar inherited a government he had helped to destabilize. His authority rested on the Company's guarantee, but his legitimacy as nawab depended on his ability to satisfy the officers, zamindars, and financiers who made up the provincial political community. The Company expected payment of the sums promised in 1757. His army expected pay. Zamindars who had paid revenue to Siraj's government looked to see what the new regime would demand. Between 1757 and 1760 these competing pressures made his rule increasingly difficult.

The treasury was the first problem. The promised indemnities and gifts, together with the cost of maintaining Company troops at Bengal's expense, exceeded what the regime could pay from ordinary revenue. Mir Jafar met part of the obligation by assigning the revenues of districts, notably Burdwan, Nadia, and Hugli, to the Company, which gave its officials direct experience of revenue collection. These arrangements further reduced his own receipts. Meanwhile his own troops complained of arrears, and officers who had helped him to power demanded recognition.

His government also faced threats from outside. In 1759 Prince Ali Gauhar, the son of the Mughal emperor Alamgir II, who would shortly be recognized as Shah Alam II, led an expedition into Bihar with allies, seeking to establish himself in the eastern provinces. The invasion was repulsed, with Company troops and the forces of Ramnarain, the deputy governor of Bihar, playing important roles, but it demonstrated that the Mughal imperial house still retained claims on the eastern provinces and could mobilize a following. In November 1759 a Dutch force from Batavia, thought by some to have been invited by Mir Jafar in a bid to counterbalance the Company, was defeated by Colonel Francis Forde near Chinsura. Whatever Mir Jafar's role, the Company reacted to the Dutch expedition as a challenge, and its victory increased its military dominance.

The nawab's family contributed to the instability. His son Miran, who had been the agent of Siraj's killing, was an ambitious and violent figure, and his death in July 1760, reportedly in a thunderstorm during a campaign in Bihar, removed a possible heir and left the succession uncertain. Mir Jafar's remaining advisers were divided and he was reported by contemporaries to be ageing and reluctant to act. Some Company officials, notably Holwell, who briefly served as governor in 1760, and Henry Vansittart, who succeeded him that year, concluded that the nawab was an ineffective partner.

Mir Jafar could not resolve the conflict simply by imposing higher demands on the countryside. Provincial receipts arrived seasonally, many collections depended on intermediaries with armed followers, and the bankers who could advance cash had reasons to guard against default. Payments made at accession thus drew down the resources needed to maintain the nawab's own court and troops. As Company demands continued, a ruler installed to stabilize commerce found that his capacity to provide the expected political security was narrowing. Different Company servants disagreed about whether the answer was to support him more firmly or replace him with a more solvent candidate; in either case, Bengal's succession was becoming a matter of corporate bargaining.

The dispute had an international dimension but was not simply a European contest. Mir Jafar's contacts with Dutch merchants and the Dutch expedition on the Hugli in 1759 exposed how closely Bengal's political rivalries had become connected to competing foreign establishments. The defeat of that expedition by English forces strengthened the Company's military predominance, but neither the Dutch presence nor the nawab's efforts to maneuver among companies justified treating him as an actor without his own objectives. Such diplomacy was a familiar resource of regional courts trying to resist dependence on a single powerful patron.

The contradiction at the heart of indirect control was now clear. The Company wanted a ruler who could pay its demands, preserve order, and not obstruct its trade, but its own demands weakened the ruler's capacity to do any of these. A dependent nawab could not impose his will on powerful subjects or on the Company's servants without losing the Company's support, and the Company could not leave him to govern without his needing to make payments to its officers. The structure produced instability for both parties. Contemporary observers, including the author of the Siyar al-Muta'akhkhirin, described a province in which the nawab's authority had been hollowed out, though they wrote from a later perspective and with their own political leanings.

Section 62.10

Mir Qasim's Accession and the Attempt to Reconstruct Nawabi Authority, 1760–1763

In October 1760 Mir Jafar was set aside and replaced by his son-in-law, Mir Qasim. The change was negotiated with Company officials, above all Vansittart, and involved payments and territorial grants. Mir Qasim assigned to the Company the revenues of the districts of Burdwan, Midnapore, and Chittagong, a grant that significantly extended the Company's revenue base, and he undertook to meet outstanding obligations. Mir Jafar was permitted to retire to Calcutta. The episode confirms the central role of the Company in determining who held the nawabi, but Mir Qasim's subsequent policy suggests that he accepted the Company's help in order to pursue a program of his own.

That program had three parts. First, Mir Qasim sought to repair the provincial finances. He examined the accounts of revenue officials, pressed arrears, curtailed grants, and reorganized the fiscal administration. His methods were demanding, and some officials and zamindars who were pressed for payment resented them. He enforced these measures with rigour, and some of his actions, such as the confiscation of officials' property, were harsh. The effect was to improve his treasury, and he was able to meet the payments to the Company more regularly than Mir Jafar had done.

Second, he moved his capital and military center. He established himself at Munger (Monghyr), on the Ganges in Bihar, away from the centers of Company influence at Calcutta and Murshidabad. He built up the fortifications there, set up a manufactory for firearms and cannon, and organized his army on lines closer to the Company's own. He employed officers trained in European methods, among them the Armenian commander Gurgin Khan and the European mercenary Walter Reinhardt, known as Sumru or Sombre. Troops were drilled, paid regularly, and supplied with improved weapons. This was a deliberate imitation of the Company's military organization and an attempt to reduce the dependence of the nawab on Company troops.

Third, Mir Qasim sought to reconstitute the authority of the nawabi in its relations with Company servants. He challenged the Company's officials' practice of private trading, insisted that they pay customs on goods not belonging to the Company, and attempted to control the movement of Company-protected merchants. This policy, examined in Section 62.11, brought him into direct conflict with the Company's servants and ultimately with the Council in Calcutta.

The revenue transfer promised to the Company also shaped Mir Qasim's room for maneuver. Burdwan, Midnapore, and Chittagong were not merely a gift of nominal territory: they represented streams of assessed taxes, district officials, local landholding relationships, and obligations that had previously contributed to the nawab's own finances. As those resources passed under Company supervision, the nawab sought to compensate by increasing the regularity of collections elsewhere. This created tensions with zamindars and bureaucrats, some of whom viewed his reforms as an attack on customary protections. The apparent paradox was that Mir Qasim obtained office through concessions that made a more autonomous government harder to finance.

Munger was therefore both a practical and symbolic choice. Its location on the upper Ganges facilitated communication with Bihar and with potential allies farther west, while separating the court from the daily pressure of Company residents in Calcutta and Murshidabad. Fortifications, the arsenal, and infantry training required gunfounders, artisans, transport, food supply, and predictable pay. The program was an attempt to establish a sustained fiscal-military capacity, not just to imitate uniforms or parade drill. It also created new concentrations of authority around officers who could organize money, materials, and men without relying on Company protection.

It would be a mistake to see Mir Qasim only as another dependent ruler, or only as a figure of tragic resistance. He pursued a coherent program of state consolidation of a kind familiar from the history of eighteenth-century regional states: fiscal reform, military modernization, centralization of authority, and the search for external legitimation. He also, like other regional rulers, sought connections with other powers, including the Mughal emperor and the nawab of Awadh. His difficulties arose partly from the harshness of his methods and from the opposition of elites, and partly from the fact that his program required that the Company accept a diminished position. The Company's servants, many of whom had large personal interests in the existing arrangements, would not accept that, and the Calcutta Council was divided.

Section 62.11

Dastaks, Customs Duties, and the Conflict over Commercial Sovereignty

The central quarrel between Mir Qasim and the Company concerned inland trade. Company servants, including many of its senior officials, engaged in private trade in commodities such as salt, betel nut, tobacco, and textiles, and they used the Company's dastak to pass their goods through customs posts without paying duty. They also used agents, known as gumashtas, in the interior who enforced their commercial demands on local merchants and producers, sometimes by force. Contemporary complaints, including those of Mir Qasim's officials and of the Company's own Resident at Murshidabad, record cases of agents compelling villagers to sell at low prices or to buy at high ones, and of local officials who did not dare to challenge them.

For Bengal's government, the consequences were financial and political. Inland customs were a major source of revenue, and every cargo that passed under a dastak was a loss. Indian merchants, who paid duties, were undercut by Company servants who did not, and some sought protection by buying dastaks or by associating with Company agents. The nawab's own revenue officers found themselves unable to impose the government's authority on traders under Company protection. Mir Qasim objected that the dastaks were being applied to goods in which the Company had no right to exemption, that the arrangement damaged his revenue and his subjects, and that the sovereign authority to levy duties belonged to the nawab.

Vansittart, who tried to reach an arrangement, negotiated with Mir Qasim in 1762 a compromise under which Company servants would pay a reduced duty on certain goods. The Calcutta Council rejected the compromise, and many of its members held that Company servants had a right to trade freely. In response, Mir Qasim took a drastic step in March 1763: he abolished all inland duties for all merchants for two years. This removed the Company servants' competitive advantage, since Indian merchants would no longer pay duties either. It also showed that the nawab was prepared to forfeit revenue to protect what he saw as the rights of his government and of Bengal's traders. The Company's servants regarded the measure as an attack on their privileges.

The political meaning of a customs post was particularly tangible to those who operated it. A provincial officer confronting a Company-protected caravan had to decide whether to enforce the nawab's orders and risk intimidation, or let the goods pass and lose revenue. Indian competitors, meanwhile, faced the choice of accepting unequal duties or seeking patronage from the privileged trader. The practice thus encouraged the development of brokerage and protection networks that reached far beyond the formal limits of the Company's own commerce. A privilege originally granted to encourage foreign trade became, in disputed hands, a way to reorder transactions among merchants already established in Bengal.

Mir Qasim's abolition of inland duties should not be mistaken for an early declaration of free-trade ideology. He was defending the government's discretion to set the rules of trade in its own territory by removing the fiscal discrimination that Company servants exploited. The fiscal cost was real, and its consequences fell on a government already under pressure to pay troops and officials. A measure that created formal equality among merchants could therefore intensify conflict over the ultimate question of who possessed the right to regulate them.

The conflict illuminates the distinction between corporate privileges and personal trade. The farman of 1717 and the associated practice covered the Company's own commerce, and the Company's directors repeatedly asserted that servants' private trade was not entitled to exemption. The servants, whose incomes depended on private trade, ignored this where they could. The result was that commerce, in the language of this period, became a question of sovereignty. If a nawab could not control trade in his province, or tax those who carried it on, his sovereignty was nominal. If the Company's servants could not trade without constraint, their income and the Company's influence were threatened. The Company's directors in London, for their part, were concerned about the damage to the Company's standing and to Bengal's government, but were far from the scene and could not enforce discipline.

Section 62.12

War with Mir Qasim, 1763–1764, and the Formation of a Regional Coalition

War broke out in the summer of 1763. A Company envoy, William Ellis, the chief at Patna and an opponent of the nawab's policy, took the city by surprise on 25 June, but was soon driven out by the nawab's troops and captured with his men. The Calcutta Council, which had earlier insisted on its rights, now declared Mir Jafar nawab again and sent an army under Major Thomas Adams. In a series of actions in July, at Katwa on 19 July, at Giria on 2 August, and at Udhua Nala in early September, Company troops defeated Mir Qasim's forces, who fought with determination but could not prevent the reconquest of Murshidabad and Munger. Mir Qasim withdrew toward Patna.

As he retreated, Mir Qasim carried out an act that turned many against him. On 5 October 1763 at Patna, prisoners held there, including some 150 Company servants and soldiers, were killed, on his orders and executed under the command of Reinhardt (Sombre). The killings were a massacre of captives and are reported in Company accounts with great bitterness. They also deepened Company hostility to Mir Qasim and contributed to the British view of the conflict as a moral struggle. The violence was not one-sided: both sides committed severe acts in the war, though the Patna killings were especially notorious. Historians should treat the episode as a real atrocity and also recognize that its prominence in British narratives served the Company's justification of the war.

Mir Qasim, with his remaining army, then fled into Awadh. There he sought the help of Shuja-ud-Daula, the nawab of Awadh and the wazir of the Mughal Empire, and of the emperor Shah Alam II, who was at that time under Shuja's protection after his years of wandering. The three parties formed an alliance in 1764, but their interests differed. Mir Qasim wanted to regain Bengal. Shuja-ud-Daula, who controlled the strongest military force in the region, saw an opportunity to extend his influence into Bihar and to prevent a hostile Company from dominating the eastern Ganges. Shah Alam II sought recovery of authority over the eastern provinces, which would give him revenue and a base, and he had reason to hope that Shuja's army and Mir Qasim's treasure could secure it. The alliance was therefore an agreement of convenience among rulers with distinct aims, not a unified movement of resistance to foreign rule. It also had a weak financial foundation: Mir Qasim's remaining resources were limited, and the arrangement depended on promises of future revenue.

The military narrative should not obscure the experience of towns caught between the armies. The reoccupation of district centers altered which authority could demand provisions, transport animals, arrears, or cooperation from local officials. A banker who extended credit to one side might be punished by the other; merchants holding grain or cloth could face requisition and interrupted routes. This helps explain why the war did not consist only of movements by formal battalions. Each force required local intelligence and access to supplies, while civilians had to negotiate with the commanders who temporarily controlled a district.

The Patna killings present a particular problem of evidence and ethical judgment. British accounts established the event's prominence in later imperial narratives, but numerical totals vary with the categories of captives counted and with the reports subsequently published. It is not necessary to establish an exact death toll to describe the intentional killing of prisoners as an atrocity. At the same time, the Company's retaliation and military discipline must be evaluated independently rather than treated as justified by the earlier killings. Mir Qasim's defeat in Bengal and his later alliance with Awadh and the emperor mark the transition from a provincial rebellion against Company interference to a wider contest over the regional balance of power.

The Company, meanwhile, had to deal with its own military problems. Its troops, including sepoys, had grown restive over arrears of pay and the demands of campaigning, and a mutiny of sepoys at Patna in 1764 forced Major Hector Munro, who had succeeded to the command, to suppress it by harsh methods, including the execution of men by blowing them from guns. The Company's army returned to discipline, but the incident showed the strains of financing a war from Bengal's revenue. By October 1764, the Company's army had gathered at Buxar on the Ganges, facing the allied forces, in a confrontation that would determine whether the Company's victory in Bengal could be sustained against a larger regional coalition.

Section 62.13

The Battle of Buxar, October 1764

Buxar stands on the south bank of the Ganges, just inside Bihar, on the road between Patna and Varanasi. In October 1764 the Company army under Major Hector Munro moved there from Patna to meet the allied army that had crossed from Awadh. Munro's force numbered roughly 7,000 men, of whom some 850 were European infantry and artillerymen and the large majority were sepoys, with a small body of cavalry and a train of field guns. It was a smaller army than the allied force, whose numbers are variously estimated at 40,000 or more, including the troops of Shuja-ud-Daula, the contingent of Mir Qasim, and imperial troops associated with Shah Alam II. These totals, which derive largely from British sources, almost certainly include many irregular and non-combatant followers, and should not be read as a count of effective fighting men.

The composition of the two sides is important. The Company's army was an organized force with European officers, standardized drill, regular pay (however strained), and a coordinated use of infantry and artillery. Shuja-ud-Daula's army was among the best of the regional forces of northern India. It included disciplined infantry, large bodies of cavalry, and a substantial artillery, and Shuja had spent years developing it. Mir Qasim's army, trained on European lines at Munger, was a smaller but experienced component. The imperial contingent had prestige but little organizational strength. The coalition's difficulty was not a want of soldiers or guns but divided command, uncertain finance, and different political purposes.

The battle took place on 22–23 October. The date is conventionally given as 22 October 1764 in many general histories, but contemporary British military records place the principal fighting on 23 October, after the two armies had approached each other on the 22nd. Different accounts have used one date or the other, or conflated the approach and the engagement. This textbook therefore speaks of the engagement of 22–23 October and treats 23 October as the day of the main battle. The discrepancy is a reminder that the dating of well-known events in this period often depends on which documents a later writer used.

The engagement was hard fought. The allied army attacked in the morning, and its cavalry and infantry pressed the Company's lines. Munro's army held its ground, and its artillery and disciplined fire inflicted heavy casualties on the attackers. After several hours the allied army began to give way, and during its withdrawal a bridge of boats across a small river was destroyed, either by accident or design, and many troops were lost in the confusion. Company casualties, usually put at several hundred killed and wounded, were far fewer than those of the allies. Shuja withdrew with his remaining force toward Awadh, and Mir Qasim's army effectively dissolved. Mir Qasim himself fled and, after years of wandering, died in obscurity near Delhi in the late 1770s.

The outcome owed much to the Company army's organization and discipline, but it also reflected political divisions in the coalition. Shah Alam II, who was not committed to Shuja's or Mir Qasim's fortunes, took the opportunity after the battle to open negotiations with the Company, and his withdrawal from the coalition removed the imperial standing that had given the alliance its legitimacy. Reports, which should be handled with care, suggested that Shuja and Mir Qasim also quarrelled over money and the disposition of Mir Qasim's treasure. The factors contributing to the result therefore include battlefield performance, command structure, finance, and political calculation.

The date of Buxar also demands source criticism. Many standard narratives assign it to 22 October 1764, whereas the British Library catalogues Alexander Champion’s battle plan, account, and casualty returns under 23 October. The chapter therefore preserves the distinction rather than choosing a falsely unanimous date. Establishing exact sequence would require collating other contemporary military, Persian, and diplomatic accounts.

Buxar was more important to the Company than Plassey had been. At Plassey the Company's force had helped to execute a political conspiracy, and the fighting was limited. At Buxar the Company's army defeated, in open battle, a major regional army fighting on its own ground and led by the wazir of the empire, with the emperor himself present. The outcome demonstrated that the Company's military capability was sufficient to defeat the strongest forces in the eastern Gangetic plain and that the Company, rather than the nawab or any Indian coalition, was the leading military power in the region.

Section 62.14

The Political Consequences of Buxar: Bengal, Awadh, and the Mughal Emperor

After Buxar, three questions had to be settled: what to do about Mir Qasim, what to do about Awadh, and what to do about the emperor. They were answered over the following year, and not in a way that victory alone dictated.

Mir Qasim ceased to be a political force. He had lost his army and treasury, and no ruler had reason to support him against the Company. The nawabi of Bengal, which had been returned to Mir Jafar in 1763, passed on his death in February 1765 to his son Najm-ud-Daula. The Company's council negotiated the terms of the succession. In a treaty with the new nawab, the Company obtained the right to appoint the principal deputy administrator and to control the army, in return for the nawab's retention of his title and an allowance. Muhammad Reza Khan, a Bengali Muslim administrator from Dhaka and Murshidabad, was named deputy governor, an appointment that would shape the system of administration in the following years.

Shuja-ud-Daula remained a serious rival, though a defeated one. After Buxar he fell back toward Allahabad and Rohilkhand, tried to rebuild his forces, and was defeated again by the Company army under Major John Carnac at Kora in early May 1765. His position then became hopeless, and he soon sought terms. The Company could have annexed Awadh, then a rich province with a large army and revenue. In the event it did not.

Shah Alam II had separated from the coalition soon after Buxar and placed himself under Company protection, living in a camp near Banaras and then at Allahabad. His situation was unusual: he was the nominal sovereign of the Mughal Empire, but he had no capital, no army, and no reliable revenue. Delhi was in the hands of others and he could not safely return there. He had sought recognition and resources from a succession of patrons. For the Company, the emperor's presence in its camp offered a source of legitimacy, since the Mughal emperor remained the only authority whose grants carried imperial legal force.

Robert Clive returned to Bengal in May 1765, with the Company's directors having reappointed him governor and given him instructions to restore order and control the private trade and corruption of the Company's servants. The negotiations over the settlement were conducted with attention to the Company's financial and strategic interests. The Company's army was expensive, its costs having risen during the war, and the directors in London wanted a secure revenue. Clive and the Calcutta council were wary of overextending the Company by annexing large territories that would require garrisons and administrators. They also feared that the direct assumption of sovereignty would bring complaints from other European powers and from Parliament and the Crown, who might question a trading corporation's claim to rule. These considerations led to a settlement that preserved existing political forms.

The settlement was thus a political choice, shaped by financial and strategic calculation. Victory made several outcomes possible: annexation of Bengal, annexation of Awadh, installation of a new nawab, restoration of the old order with Company oversight, or acquisition of formal rights within the Mughal structure. The Company chose the last, combined with the preservation of the existing government and the creation of a buffer in Awadh. Its leaders believed this to be the least costly route to the revenue and security they sought.

Section 62.15

The Treaty of Allahabad, 1765: Imperial Authority and Regional Settlement

The negotiations at Allahabad in the summer of 1765 involved three parties, the Company under Clive, the emperor Shah Alam II, and Shuja-ud-Daula, and produced several distinct instruments in August. It is a mistake to treat the whole as a single treaty. The agreements covered different subjects and bound different parties.

The first set of arrangements concerned Awadh. By a treaty concluded on 16 August 1765, Shuja-ud-Daula was restored to most of his dominions. He agreed to pay the Company an indemnity, commonly given as 50 lakh rupees, to accept continued Company occupation of Chunar until the indemnity had been fully paid, and to enter an alliance in which each side would help the other in defence. The territories of Allahabad and Kora, which had been under Shuja, were assigned to the emperor to provide him with a base. The ruler of Banaras, Balwant Singh, was confirmed in his possessions as a tributary of the Company's ally. The effect was to leave Awadh as a substantial independent state, bound to the Company by treaty and by debt, and standing between the Company's possessions and the powers of northern India. Under Article VII, Chunar was to remain occupied only until fulfillment of the indemnity terms, not transferred in perpetuity.

The Company's reasons for preferring this arrangement to annexation were substantial. Awadh was larger and its army stronger than the Company could easily garrison. A friendly Awadh protected Bengal and Bihar from attack from the west, whether by Marathas, Afghans, or other powers. The indemnity and the alliance made Awadh a financial and political client. Later chapters will show how this arrangement developed into a pattern of subsidiary alliance with Indian states, but in 1765 it was an improvisation for a particular strategic situation.

The second set of arrangements concerned the emperor. Shah Alam II received Allahabad and Kora and an annual payment, and in return he issued the grants described in the next section. The emperor's position was weak, and the Company's willingness to recognize and protect him was a considerable benefit. He was also treated with ceremonial respect by the Company's officers, and the ritual forms of imperial authority were observed in the transactions. This continued importance of imperial recognition is a central feature of the settlement. The Company did not claim that it had won Bengal by conquest alone, but sought a grant that conformed to Mughal legal practice. For the emperor, the arrangement gave him a patron and income. For the Company, it supplied legitimacy that its military victory could not.

Article VI of the treaty fixed Shuja-ud-Daula’s indemnity at fifty lakh rupees, payable in instalments. Article VII made English occupation of Chunar conditional on satisfaction of that indemnity, not a permanent cession. Other articles protected Balwant Singh’s Banaras estates on existing revenue terms and stipulated reciprocal military assistance. These provisions show how Company commanders secured payments, a defensible position, and a regional alliance without annexing Awadh.

It is notable that the Company acquired its principal gain, the revenue of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha, not through the treaty with the defeated Shuja but through a separate grant from a ruler who had no military means to enforce it. The grant could function because the Company had the means to enforce what the emperor granted, and because the emperor's authority still had a recognized legal meaning in the eastern provinces. That relationship between formal legal title and effective power explains why imperial forms survived long after the empire had lost control of the provinces.

Section 62.16

The Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha, 12 August 1765

Historical Vocabulary
Diwani

In this chapter, the Diwani refers to the grant of the office of diwan (the chief revenue and civil financial officer) of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha to the East India Company in 1765. It is distinct from the nizamat, which covered military command and criminal justice, and which remained with the nawab. The grant gave the Company the right to collect and manage the revenues of the three provinces, subject to payments to the emperor and to the nawab for the support of the nizamat.

Why it matters: The Diwani turned the Company from a participant in Bengal's politics into the legal holder of its principal revenue, while leaving much of the machinery of administration in Indian hands.

On 12 August 1765, in a ceremony at Allahabad, Shah Alam II issued a farman granting the East India Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha. The grant made the Company the emperor's diwan in those provinces. In the Mughal system the diwan was the official responsible for collecting the revenue, accounting for it, and remitting the imperial share. The Company was now, in legal terms, the holder of that office, with the right to collect the revenue and to retain what remained after meeting stipulated obligations.

Those obligations were two. The Company agreed to pay the emperor an annual sum, usually stated as 26 lakh rupees, from the revenue. It also undertook to provide for the expenses of the nizamat, the nawab's government, which meant paying an allowance to the nawab, an allowance set out in the September 1765 settlement as approximately 53.86 lakh sicca rupees annually, divided between the nawab’s household and the costs of the nizamat and later revised, and to meet the costs of the administration of criminal justice and of the military functions that remained nominally with him. The balance of the revenue, which was very large relative to these payments, would be available to the Company to finance its army, its administration, and its commercial purchases.

It is important to be clear about what the Diwani did and did not do. It conferred a powerful legal and fiscal position. It gave the Company a Mughal imperial title to the principal revenues of the richest provinces of the empire, and it gave the Company's actions a legal form that Indian rulers and European competitors alike could recognize. It did not, however, replace the existing administrative personnel. The collection of revenue continued to be carried out by the existing network of zamindars, revenue officials, and accountants, under the supervision of Indian deputies such as Muhammad Reza Khan in Bengal and Shitab Rai in Bihar. The Company had few officials with knowledge of the language, accounts, and local conditions, and in 1765 it had little interest in learning them. It took the revenue without taking over the administration.

The effect on the Company's position was fundamental. Before 1765, the Company depended on the nawab for the funds that supported its army and on its commerce for the funds that supported its trade. After 1765, it had its own legal claim to a revenue that exceeded anything it had earned from trade. The revenue could finance the Company's military establishment in India, pay for the purchase of goods for export without the need to import silver, and support the dividends to its shareholders and the fortunes of its servants. The Company had acquired the means to fund the expansion that later chapters describe, using resources drawn from the territories it was expanding into.

Contemporaries understood part of this. Within the Company, Clive regarded the Diwani as the foundation of the Company's strength and as a means of making the Company's position legitimate. In Britain, opinion was divided, and the directors and shareholders hoped for large revenues while others worried about the implications of a trading company becoming a sovereign power. The question of the status of the Company's gains, and the right of the Crown and Parliament to control them, became a significant political issue, as Chapter 63 will show.

The separate 1765 nizamat arrangement specified 53,86,131 sicca rupees and nine annas annually for the nawab’s household and establishment. Neither was the fifty-lakh indemnity owed by Awadh under the treaty of 16 August. Confusing these payments obscures the different political obligations that Bengal revenue was expected to meet.

Institutional continuity after the grant was substantial. A revenue order could travel through established clerks, qanungos, zamindars, and district officers much as it had before, but the recipient of the surplus and the final center of authority had changed. The Company's lack of administrative expertise was therefore not a barrier to extracting fiscal benefit: its effective strategy was to govern through trained personnel while controlling appointments, military force, and remittances. The resulting structure was workable in the short term, but it could also leave uncertainty about who was responsible when revenue demands or abuses were challenged.

The central analytical conclusion is that, by August 1765, the Company held an imperial legal claim to the principal revenues that could sustain its military and commercial establishment. Plassey had given it a dependent nawab; Buxar had removed the military threat to that arrangement; the Diwani gave it the money and the legal form. The combination, and not any single step, produced the transformation in British power that this chapter describes.

Section 62.17

Dual Government, 1765–1772: Diwani, Nizamat, and Indirect Administration

The administrative arrangement that followed the Diwani is usually called the dual government of Bengal. The label, which was applied later and has been debated, describes a system in which the Company held the diwani, the revenue, while the nawab retained the nizamat, the formal responsibility for civil government, criminal justice, and order. In practice, the Company collected the revenues through Indian deputies and administered little directly, while the nawab's officials carried on the work of government without control over the money or the army.

The key figures were Indian administrators. Muhammad Reza Khan, whom the Company had named naib nazim (deputy governor) under Najm-ud-Daula, was also given the responsibility for revenue in Bengal, and Shitab Rai was the principal official in Bihar. These men were experienced administrators with networks among zamindars and revenue officers. They had the knowledge the Company lacked, and the Company used them. The Company also maintained a Resident at Murshidabad and a Council in Calcutta, who monitored their work and, increasingly, directed it. The appointments and dismissals of the Indian deputies depended on the Company's approval, and in practice they became accountable to the Company rather than the nawab.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Government in Bengal, 1756 to 1772

What Changed

  • The nawab ceased to control the army, the treasury, or his own succession
  • The Company moved from commercial privilege to military supremacy and then to a legal claim to the revenue
  • Revenue was diverted to support a Company army and the Company's purchase of goods for export
  • Company servants acquired large private fortunes and political leverage

What Stayed the Same

  • Mughal legal forms, titles, and the vocabulary of office remained in use
  • Indian officials, zamindars, accountants, and bankers continued to run revenue collection and local government
  • The agrarian economy and the village-level revenue relationships remained in place in the short term
  • Murshidabad's court and its titles survived, though with reduced power

Who experienced change most directly? The nawab's court and military, and those who paid revenue or traded under pressure from Company servants, experienced the most direct change. Who saw greater continuity? Many village cultivators dealt with the same zamindars and revenue officials, though under heavier demands. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Company's power was built on existing institutions, which obscured how much real authority had moved.

Describing this arrangement as a balanced division of powers between two governments misses the central fact. The Company held the money and the army. The nawab held the titles and the responsibility. The nawab's allowance depended on the Company's payment, and the nawab could not raise troops, since the Company's army was the principal force in the province. The Company's servants, meanwhile, could interfere in local administration, and often did, without being accountable for its consequences to the people affected. The result was an increasingly unequal distribution of financial power, military capacity, administrative responsibility, and political accountability. Power without responsibility lay with the Company, and responsibility without power with the nawab and his deputies.

The arrangement depended on a carefully maintained distinction between legal form and political leverage. Shah Alam's farman made the Company diwan, while the nawab continued to issue orders within the inherited framework of the nizamat. Yet a deputy's position was insecure if the Company withdrew support, and a local zamindar seeking relief could find his formal superior unable to change the fiscal demands backed by Company power. The system therefore retained much of the personnel and terminology of Mughal provincial government while placing the decisive financial and coercive resources outside the nawab's control.

There were practical reasons for this indirect method. The Company had too few trained European officials to supervise thousands of village-level transactions, and its servants had incentives to concentrate on military and commercial affairs rather than civil administration. Delegating day-to-day work to experienced Indian officers reduced immediate costs and allowed collections to continue. But the structure also multiplied opportunities for blame shifting: Company councillors could attribute unpopular decisions to deputies, and deputies could invoke orders from Calcutta. Later reformers used these failures to argue for the clearer administrative responsibility that emerged in 1772.

The arrangement proved increasingly unsatisfactory to the Company's directors. They received large revenues but were concerned about corruption, the private trade of their servants, and the lack of control over collection. In 1769 the Company appointed supervisors to oversee revenue collection in the districts, a step toward more direct involvement, but the supervisors themselves often engaged in trade and were poorly equipped for the work. The 1770 famine, discussed in Chapter 63, exposed the weaknesses of the system. In their instructions of 29 August 1771, the directors resolved that the Company should “stand forth as diwan,” and the policy was implemented during 1772 under Warren Hastings, who moved to administer the revenue more directly through Company servants. The Indian deputies were removed, the treasury was transferred to Calcutta, and Muhammad Reza Khan was put on trial, though he was acquitted. The detailed reforms belong to Chapter 63, and the 1772 change marks the end of dual government and the beginning of the Company's direct administration.

Section 62.18

Revenue Collection, Company Servants, Indian Intermediaries, and Social Consequences

The new governing arrangement altered relationships at every level of Bengal society. At the top, the Company's fiscal power shifted the balance among those who had stood between the cultivator and the state: zamindars, revenue officials, bankers, and merchants. Zamindars, who collected revenue from cultivators and paid it to the government, now answered to a body that demanded regular payment and whose servants were less embedded in local relationships than the nawab's officials had been. Those who failed to pay could be removed, and revenue was increasingly farmed out to the highest bidders. Revenue farming raised short-term collections but encouraged exactions on cultivators and discouraged investment.

Indian bankers and financiers, including the Jagat Seths, continued to play an essential role in remitting revenue, but their relative position changed. As the Company became the principal recipient of revenue, it could build its own financial arrangements, and the great Murshidabad houses lost the central role they had held under the nawabs. Many smaller Indian merchants and agents attached themselves to Company servants as brokers (banians and gumashtas), making fortunes of their own. The Company's Indian employees in the administration, including clerks and accountants, gained influence as the Company relied on their expertise.

Commerce was transformed. The Company now used part of the revenue it collected in Bengal to purchase the textiles and other goods it exported, an arrangement known as the "investment," so that it no longer needed to bring silver from Europe to pay for them. The import of bullion to Bengal, which had long supported its economy and currency, fell. Weavers and artisans who had earlier sold to a range of buyers found themselves dealing with Company agents who could use coercion and advances to secure deliveries, and the evidence of complaints from weavers about low prices and compulsion is substantial though it needs careful interpretation. Private trade by Company servants continued, and, after the end of Clive's "Society of Trade," which in 1765–1768 organized a monopoly in salt, betel, and tobacco for senior officials, other forms of trade under official protection persisted. The Company's directors repeatedly condemned these practices, but without effective enforcement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Weavers, Cultivators, and Tax-Collectors under Company Fiscal Power

For a weaver in the textile districts, the change meant dealing with Company commercial agents who advanced money and expected cloth of a set quality at a fixed time and price. Some weavers benefited from regular demand, but others complained that they could not sell to other buyers and that agents punished delay. For a cultivator, the central relationship remained with the local zamindar or revenue-farmer, but the demand passed down from the new authority was heavier and more rigid than in earlier years, and payment was required in cash. A farmer whose crop failed could borrow from a moneylender at high interest, and indebtedness could lead to loss of land.

For the families of Company servants and for Indian brokers who worked with them, the period offered new opportunities for gain. For women, evidence is thin, but records of disputes over inheritance, the effects of male migration to Calcutta, and the work of women in spinning and household production show that women were integral to the textile economy, though rarely visible in official documents. Surviving sources are overwhelmingly official and commercial, and the testimony of weavers and peasants is mostly mediated through officials or petitions. Historians must therefore read these records against the grain to glimpse the lives of those who produced the wealth.

The effect of fiscal transfer differed across social groups and districts. Some merchants and accountants profited by supplying capital, grain, information, or services to the new regime; others lost customers or political protection when the nawab's court contracted. The Company's commercial 'investment' depended on agents able to secure cloth of particular grades and deliver it on schedule, making bargaining power within weaving villages a central issue. Producers who accepted advances might gain cash before delivery but could also face penalties and coercion when prices shifted or supplies fell short. The historical question is not whether every contract was forced, but which buyers and sellers could refuse unfavorable terms.

Revenue assessments, likewise, should not be read as evidence of what cultivators actually paid in every locality. Collection depended on rainfall, local bargaining, enforcement, and intermediary claims, and the transition to more direct Company oversight was uneven. Military and commercial expenditure could draw resources out of the same districts that supplied grain and textiles, while households had little capacity to insure against harvest failure. The famine of 1769–1770 requires its own examination of weather, disease, relief, market responses, and policy in Chapter 63; Chapter 62 establishes only the institutional pressures in place before that crisis.

It is necessary to distinguish between the accumulation of resources by Company officials and investors and the conditions of the province's population. The Company's revenue and the personal fortunes of its servants grew, while the effects on Bengal's producers were mixed and, for many, adverse. Scholars disagree about how far Bengal's economy declined in the late 1760s, and about whether the pressure on revenue in these years was a main cause of later hardship. What is clear is that heavy revenue demands, tied to the Company's military and commercial needs, were being imposed on an agrarian economy exposed to monsoon variation. In 1769 and 1770, the failure of the rains and the resulting crop shortage coincided with these demands, and Bengal suffered one of the most devastating famines of the century. Its chronology, mortality, causes, and consequences belong to Chapter 63, but the structural pressures described here form part of the background.

Section 62.19

Was Plassey the Beginning of Empire? Historiography and the Making of Company Rule

Historians have long asked what Plassey and the events that followed meant. Older British imperial historiography, including much nineteenth-century writing, treated Plassey as the founding moment of British India and Clive as its founder, a heroic account that depicted British victory as natural and Indian defeat as the consequence of weakness or despotism. Indian nationalist writers reversed the moral judgment but sometimes kept the framework, describing Plassey as the result of treachery and the start of colonial subjugation. The Black Hole story and the Plassey conspiracy each served in different ways to support these narratives.

Modern scholarship has complicated both. P. J. Marshall, in his study of eighteenth-century Bengal, emphasized the extent to which the Company's power depended on cooperation with Indian elites and was built gradually, within the political and economic structures of the region, and he described British power as a "bridgehead" before it became an empire. C. A. Bayly argued that the Company's rise should be understood against the background of the "military fiscalism" of eighteenth-century Indian states, in which rulers competed for revenue and soldiers, and that the Company succeeded by drawing upon the commercial and financial networks of Indian society. Sushil Chaudhury has stressed Bengal's economic vitality before Plassey and the role of Bengali merchants and bankers in the political realignment of 1757, rejecting the notion of a decadent province. Sudipta Sen has examined how the Company used and adapted Mughal forms of sovereignty and ritual to build legitimacy, showing that the legal and symbolic dimensions of the transfer were important to its success. Other historians, including those who study the Company's private traders and their wealth, have emphasized how the interests of individuals shaped corporate policy.

These approaches differ in emphasis but share a rejection of simple explanations. A first set of questions concerns intention. Did the Company deliberately seek territorial empire? The evidence suggests that its directors were reluctant to take on the costs of government and that the decisions of 1756–1765 were made by men on the spot responding to immediate pressures: commercial disputes, the threat from the French, the need to pay for an army, and opportunities offered by Bengal's internal divisions. A second set concerns causation. Plassey was a political conspiracy; Buxar a military test; the Diwani a legal and fiscal arrangement. No single cause accounts for the whole. A third concerns Bengal's agency. The actors in Murshidabad, Patna, and Allahabad had their own aims and made choices, and some of those choices helped the Company.

Narratives that portray British victory as inevitable, or that read Indian defeat as evidence of civilizational weakness, are historically inadequate. Bengal's state was not simply decayed: it was a functioning fiscal-military regime, with a large economy, that was vulnerable to a particular combination of factional division, external pressure, and a foreign force that had access to different financial resources. The Company's success depended on contingent events, including the weather at Plassey, the actions of individuals, and the decisions of Indian rulers about whom to support. At the same time, narratives that make the Company only a passive beneficiary of Indian politics also fail, since the Company's military organization, its capacity to raise money through London credit and Indian bankers, and its readiness to use force and deception were real advantages.

Historical Debate 2

When Did the Company Become a Territorial Power: 1757, 1764, 1765, or 1772?

The question: At what point did the East India Company cease to be a trading corporation within a Mughal province and become a territorial power?

Interpretation A — 1757: Plassey gave the Company the ability to determine who ruled Bengal, and it acquired land near Calcutta. On this view, the power to install and remove rulers is the essence of sovereignty, and the Company possessed it from 1757.
Interpretation B — 1764 or 1765: Buxar established the Company as the leading military power of the region, and the Diwani of 1765 gave it a legal right to the revenue. On this view, military supremacy together with fiscal title marks the point at which the Company acquired territorial power in the sense that mattered, even if it did not administer directly.
Interpretation C — 1772: Only in 1772, when the Company took over revenue administration through its own servants, did it accept direct responsibility for government. On this view, sovereignty requires the assumption of administrative duty, and what came before was influence and control without responsibility.

Evidence: Each interpretation draws on the same record: the treaties of 1757, the military outcomes of 1764, the farman of 1765, and the administrative changes of 1769–1772. They differ in their definition of sovereignty.

Why it matters: The question clarifies that the Company's power was acquired in stages, and that its character changed at each. It also affects how historians assign responsibility for the effects of Company rule, since the period before 1772 featured power without formal responsibility.

Section 62.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the transformation of Bengal from an autonomous nawabi government into a region increasingly controlled through Company military, political, and fiscal power. The causal chain can be reconstructed in stages. The crisis of 1756 arose from disputes over privileges, private trade, fortification, and sovereignty, which led to the fall of Calcutta and the Company's military response. The Treaty of Alinagar restored the Company's position but did not resolve the underlying issue of who held authority. A conspiracy among Bengal's elites, assisted by Company officers, produced the political revolution of 1757, whose military expression was Plassey. Mir Jafar's dependent regime proved unstable under the weight of payments and conflicting demands. Mir Qasim attempted to rebuild nawabi authority through fiscal and military reform and by challenging the Company servants' commercial privileges, and war followed. Buxar defeated the broadest regional coalition that opposed the Company. The Allahabad settlement kept Awadh as a buffer, and the Diwani gave the Company the legal and financial foundation for its power. Dual government kept Indian administrators in place while shifting real authority to the Company.

The grant of the Diwani by Shah Alam II on 12 August 1765, after the victory at Buxar and in conjunction with the Allahabad settlement.

The Company acquires a legal claim to the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha; dual government begins; Awadh remains a buffer state; the nawab loses real control.

Long-term consequences

Revenue-funded Company armies; the end of silver imports for the "investment"; parliamentary concern; the move toward direct administration in 1772; the foundation for further Company expansion.

Historical complexity

The outcome depended on contingent events, Bengali choices, Mughal legal forms, and the Company's finances; it cannot be explained by superior arms alone.

The transformation was historically significant for several reasons. For Bengal, it ended the era of the autonomous nawabi and placed one of South Asia's richest regions under a foreign corporate authority, with consequences for revenue, trade, and the lives of its producers. For South Asia, it provided the Company with revenue and a military base from which to expand, as the following chapters on Mysore, the Marathas, and Punjab show. For the British state, it created a problem without precedent: a joint-stock company that governed millions, whose conduct raised questions about corruption, sovereignty, and the relationship of commercial enterprise to the Crown.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the English East India Company, between 1756 and 1772, moved from being a privileged trader dependent on Bengal's rulers to holding decisive military, political, and fiscal power in one of South Asia's richest regions. Its central argument is that the change was a staged transformation. Plassey changed the government, Buxar defeated the coalition that could have reversed the change, and the Diwani supplied the legal and financial foundation for the Company's position. Only in 1772 did the Company begin to administer the revenue directly.

The chapter began with Bengal under Siraj ud-Daulah. His government was an established regional state, derived from the nawabi of Murshid Quli Khan and Alivardi Khan, and it rested on a balance among military officers, revenue officials, zamindars, and bankers such as the Jagat Seths. Siraj's accession in April 1756 opened rivalries within the family and court. His relations with the Company deteriorated over the extent of its trading privileges under the 1717 farman, the private trade of its servants and the use of dastaks, the fortification of Calcutta, and the protection of a fugitive, all of which concerned sovereignty as well as commerce.

The fall of Calcutta in June 1756 and the Black Hole episode followed. The chapter treated the episode cautiously, noting the weakness of Holwell's account, the disputed numbers, and the later political uses of the story. The Company's response from Madras, led by Clive and Admiral Watson, recaptured Calcutta in January 1757, produced the Treaty of Alinagar, and removed the French from Chandernagore. These successes left the question of authority unresolved and encouraged some on both sides to consider a change of nawab.

The conspiracy of 1757 involved Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, the Jagat Seths, and others in the Bengal court, with Company officers acting as partners. The deceptive treatment of Omichand illustrates the methods used. The Battle of Plassey on 23 June 1757 combined limited fighting, the effect of rain, the death of Mir Madan, and the inaction of much of the nawab's army. The result was neither a simple military triumph nor a purely political event, but an interaction of both. Siraj was killed, Mir Jafar installed, and the Company obtained large payments and land near Calcutta, while the administration continued under the nawab.

Mir Jafar's regime proved unstable because the Company's demands undermined his capacity to govern, and he was replaced in 1760 by Mir Qasim. Mir Qasim pursued a coherent program of fiscal reform, military modernization at Munger, and assertion of nawabi authority over trade. The conflict over dastaks and inland customs, culminating in his abolition of duties in 1763, turned a commercial dispute into war. The Company reconquered, Mir Qasim retreated to Awadh, and the killings at Patna in October 1763 deepened the conflict.

The coalition of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula, and Shah Alam II was an alliance of different interests. At Buxar on 22–23 October 1764 the Company army defeated it. The date illustrates the care needed with source chronology. The defeat made the Company the dominant military power in the region and led to the separation of the emperor from the coalition, the defeat of Shuja at Kora, and negotiations in which Clive chose a settlement over annexation.

The agreements of August 1765 included the Treaty of Allahabad with Shuja-ud-Daula, who was restored as an ally and buffer, and the Diwani grant of 12 August, which gave the Company the right to collect the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha in return for payments to the emperor and the nawab. The Diwani gave the Company a Mughal legal title and a fiscal base without immediately replacing Indian administrators.

Dual government, from 1765 to 1772, left the nizamat with the nawab and the diwani with the Company, but concentrated power and money in the Company's hands while leaving responsibility to the nawab and his deputies. Revenue was used for military costs and for the purchase of export goods, and it increased the pressures on zamindars, weavers, and cultivators. These pressures formed part of the background to the famine of 1770, reserved for Chapter 63.

Historians have moved away from narratives of inevitability and decadence. Marshall, Bayly, Chaudhury, Sen, and others show that Company power grew through collaboration with Bengali elites, the logic of military fiscalism, contingent decisions, and the use of Mughal legal forms. The debates over Plassey and the date of territorial power show that the answers depend on how one defines sovereignty. The chapter's account of the Company's transformation provides the basis for Chapter 63.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a profound change in political power could occur through a sequence of ordinary-looking steps: a commercial dispute, a conspiracy, a battle, a treaty, and an imperial grant. None of them alone made a colonial state. Together they converted a trading corporation into the holder of a province's revenue, and they did so largely through the existing institutions of Mughal Bengal.

The chapter also shows the importance of Bengal's own history. The actors who shaped these events included bankers, officers, zamindars, nawabs, and an emperor, each pursuing aims that were reasonable in their own terms. Understanding their choices guards against both triumphalist and fatalist accounts, and it recognizes the responsibility of those who made decisions with consequences they could not fully foresee.

Many of the problems of later chapters began here: revenue demands that outran local conditions, a state whose officials were also traders, an army financed by the territory it controlled, and a corporation answerable to shareholders as well as subjects. The Company's reliance on Mughal forms, and its use of Indian administrators, also anticipate later patterns of colonial rule. Within the six-volume sequence, this chapter opens Volume IV by showing how a regional conquest became the foundation of a wider empire.

Chapter 63 now turns to what the Company did with the powers it had acquired: the famine of 1770, the reforms of Warren Hastings, parliamentary regulation, and the building of an administrative state.
