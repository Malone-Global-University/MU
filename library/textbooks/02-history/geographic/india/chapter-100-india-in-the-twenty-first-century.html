India in the Twenty-First Century

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the morning of 13 May 2004, as votes were counted across a country of more than a billion people, the government that had campaigned under the slogan “India Shining” discovered that a large part of the electorate did not recognise the description. Twenty-two years later, in November 2026, India's rural employment guarantee had been legally replaced, its census was under way for the first time by digital means, and its prime minister governed at the head of an alliance whose partners his party could no longer ignore. Between those two moments lies the history this chapter examines.

Chapter 100 covers the period from 2004 to 19 November 2026, the factual cutoff of this textbook. It is the longest stretch of continuous national government in this volume to be treated within a single chapter, and it was shared between two political formations: the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance (UPA), which governed from 2004 to 2014 under Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, and the Bharatiya Janata Party-led National Democratic Alliance (NDA), which has governed since 2014 under Prime Minister Narendra Modi. Chapter 98 explained the economic restructuring that began in 1991 and the coalition politics of the 1990s; Chapter 99 traced the Mandal, Ayodhya, and BJP developments that brought the Hindu nationalist party to office in 1998 and out of it in 2004. This chapter begins where those chapters end.

The chapter's central argument is that twenty-first-century India combined considerable continuity with important change. Continuity is visible in sustained economic expansion, in the steady growth of welfare programmes, in the spread of mobile telephony and digital identification, and in the persistence of competitive elections and a federal structure. Change is visible in the shift from a decade of coalition government led by Congress to a decade of single-party BJP majorities, in a more centralised and personalised style of executive leadership, in sharp constitutional controversies over citizenship and the status of Jammu and Kashmir, and in a changed vocabulary of public policy that moved from legal entitlement toward administrative delivery. The 2024 general election complicated any simple narrative: the BJP lost its own majority, though the NDA retained a majority of Lok Sabha seats.

The chapter deliberately avoids two temptations. The first is to treat 2014 as a clean break, as though nothing in the governmental practice of the Modi years had precedent in the UPA decade. The Aadhaar identification system, direct benefit transfers, the Goods and Services Tax proposal, and the major highway programmes all had roots in earlier governments. The second temptation is to reduce twenty years to the biographies of two prime ministers. Parliament, state governments, opposition parties, courts, firms, farmers' organisations, labour migrants, and households made this history too, and they appear throughout.

The chapter also asks students to work with kinds of evidence that differ from those of earlier chapters. Government reports, statutes, judicial judgments, Election Commission statistics, Reserve Bank publications, and household surveys survive in abundance. Their abundance can mislead. Official statistics carry definitions that changed over time, such as the revisions to national-accounts base years and the redesign of employment and consumption surveys, and the same figure can be presented as achievement or failure depending on the comparison chosen. Statements by governments, oppositions, and commentators are evidence of what actors claimed, not necessarily of what happened.

Because the chapter's later sections concern events within months of its cutoff, a further caution applies. Historians cannot yet know the long-term consequences of the 2025 rural-employment legislation, the 2026 census, or the state elections of 2026. This chapter therefore distinguishes completed historical processes from ongoing ones, avoids prediction, and describes laws by their provisions rather than their results where outcomes are not yet documented.

Finally, the chapter observes the division of labour in the Authoritative Chapter Outline. Kashmir and regional security are examined in Chapter 105; comparative economic and urban transformation in Chapter 106; caste, gender, and religion as social institutions in Chapter 107; and India's external relations in Chapter 110. Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives are the subject of Chapters 101–104. Chapter 100 is the national political history of India from 2004 to 2026, and it ends by pointing outward to those chapters.

Research-as-of date: 19 November 2026. Events after this date are treated as unknown. Developments in 2025–2026 rest on official notifications and contemporary reporting, not on settled scholarship, and are marked accordingly.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the NDA lost the 2004 general election and how the UPA coalition was formed and governed.
  2. Analyze the shift in welfare policy from discretionary schemes to statutory entitlements, distinguishing a legal right from its implementation.
  3. Assess the relationship between economic growth, globalization, and the expansion of the middle classes.
  4. Evaluate how corruption controversies, inflation, and civic mobilization weakened the UPA, distinguishing allegation, audit finding, and judicial outcome.
  5. Trace Narendra Modi's rise and the BJP's 2014 and 2019 victories, distinguishing party majority from alliance majority.
  6. Compare UPA and NDA approaches to welfare, identity systems, infrastructure, and digital government, identifying continuities.
  7. Interpret demonetization and the Goods and Services Tax as policy experiments, separating intention, implementation, and contested outcomes.
  8. Contextualize the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, the 2019 changes in Jammu and Kashmir, and the debates over secularism and federalism.
  9. Describe the COVID-19 pandemic and the farm-law protests as tests of governance, federalism, and social inequality.
  10. Distinguish growth, poverty reduction, employment, and inequality as separate measures, and explain limits of statistical comparison across periods.
  11. Evaluate competing arguments about democratic institutions, political finance, and civil society after 2014.
  12. Explain the 2024 election result, the renewed coalition dependence of the BJP, and the limits of what can be concluded about developments through 2026.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a government presiding over rapid growth lose power in 2004, and what does that suggest about the relationship between economic performance and electoral behaviour?
  2. How did rights-based welfare legislation change the relationship between citizens and the state, and where did implementation fall short of the statute?
  3. In what respects did BJP-led governments after 2014 continue UPA-era programmes and institutions, and in what respects did they depart from them?
  4. How should historians evaluate policies such as demonetization when intentions, measurable outcomes, and causal interpretations diverge?
  5. Did centralisation of executive authority after 2014 strengthen governmental effectiveness, weaken liberal-democratic safeguards, or both?

  8. What does the contrast between 2014–2019 and 2024 reveal about the distribution of political power?
  9. How should historians treat events that occurred within months of the chapter's cutoff?
  10. How did states, communities, firms, and ordinary citizens shape the transformation attributed to national leaders?

Historical Context

Three earlier developments frame this chapter. First, the economic liberalisation begun in 1991, examined in Chapter 98, had by the early 2000s produced a services-led economy, a booming telecommunications and software sector, and an urban middle class whose size and aspirations were new in Indian history. It had also left large regional and sectoral disparities and a workforce still predominantly employed in agriculture and the informal economy.

Second, coalition politics had become the normal condition of national government. Since 1989 no single party had governed with a Lok Sabha majority on its own, and regional parties, whose growth is traced in Chapter 94, held the balance. The BJP's own governments of 1998–2004 under Atal Bihari Vajpayee, discussed in Chapter 99, had governed through the National Democratic Alliance. The BJP's rise reflected the political consequences of the Mandal and Ayodhya movements, and the competitive position of Congress, once dominant, had been reduced to that of one large party among several.

Third, the constitutional framework established in 1950 (Chapter 88) remained intact: a parliamentary system, an independent judiciary with the power of judicial review, an Election Commission supervising polls, and a federal division of powers between the Union and the states. The twenty-first century tested that framework through new pressures: fiscal centralisation and fiscal federalism, the digitisation of public administration, controversies over citizenship, and changing relations among executive, legislature, courts, and press.

The chapter's geographic scope is the Republic of India as a political and administrative unit, with attention to regional variation among states. Where the history of India touches Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, or the Maldives, the relevant later chapters (101–104) and Chapter 110 provide fuller treatment.

Section 100.1

The 2004 Election and the Formation of the United Progressive Alliance

The Vajpayee government called the general election of April–May 2004 several months early, confident of success. The economy was growing, foreign exchange reserves were high, the information-technology sector had become a national symbol, and the party's campaign, widely known by the slogan “India Shining,” celebrated these achievements. The result surprised nearly every commentator. The BJP won 138 Lok Sabha seats, down from 182, and the NDA as a whole won roughly 181. Congress won 145 seats, only a few more than the BJP, but it and its pre- and post-poll allies assembled a larger bloc.

Explaining the result requires attention to variation. Growth since the early 1990s had been concentrated in services and in certain states and cities; large parts of rural India, including agricultural regions affected by slow farm-income growth, indebtedness, and in some states farmer suicides, did not experience the 2000s boom as improvement. Regional parties and state-level calculations mattered greatly: the NDA lost ground in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, where its allies were defeated, and Congress's alliances with regional parties, notably in Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, and Bihar, converted modest vote shares into seats. Historians therefore generally interpret 2004 less as a national rejection of liberalisation than as the product of state-level coalitions and the electoral weight of voters who felt excluded from the growth the government celebrated. The relative importance of these factors remains debated, and national survey evidence on voter motivation is open to differing readings.

The Congress president, Sonia Gandhi, was chosen as the leader of the parliamentary party and was expected to become prime minister. On 18 May 2004 she declined the office, a decision whose motives have been variously explained by participants and commentators, including controversy over her foreign birth and a wish to avoid divisive confrontation. She nominated Manmohan Singh, the economist who had been finance minister under P. V. Narasimha Rao and whose reputation was associated with the 1991 reforms discussed in Chapter 98. Singh was sworn in on 22 May 2004. The arrangement created a distinctive structure: a prime minister without a strong independent electoral base, a party president who held the political leadership of the largest party, and a coalition of allies with their own demands.

The UPA was a governing coalition rather than simply a Congress government. Its largest external prop was the Left Front, whose roughly sixty Lok Sabha members provided outside support, meaning that they sustained the government in votes without joining the ministry. Allies in the ministry included the Rashtriya Janata Dal of Lalu Prasad Yadav, the Nationalist Congress Party of Sharad Pawar, and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. A Common Minimum Programme, drafted through negotiation among partners, set the government's declared priorities. The National Advisory Council, established under Sonia Gandhi, became a space in which activists, economists, and civil-society figures proposed legislation, and its relationship to the ministry was debated: supporters saw a healthy channel for social-movement expertise, while critics argued that it created a second centre of authority.

The Left's support shaped the first UPA government's economic orientation. Left parties resisted rapid privatisation and pressed for rural employment, social-security measures, and caution on foreign investment and pension reform. The partnership ended in July 2008, when the Left withdrew support over the India–United States civil nuclear agreement. The government survived a confidence vote on 22 July 2008 with the help of the Samajwadi Party and others, a dramatic episode that confirmed the importance of parliamentary arithmetic. Foreign-policy aspects of the agreement are covered in Chapter 110.

The Common Minimum Programme must be read with care. It listed commitments, including employment guarantees, increased public spending on education and health, protection of farmers, and preserving secularism, but a statement of intent is not a record of implementation. Some commitments became law quickly, as the next section shows. Others, such as public expenditure on health reaching the targeted share of national income, were not met. The programme is therefore evidence of the coalition's bargaining and declared goals, and it should be compared with what the government actually enacted.

Section 100.2

Rights-Based Welfare Legislation and the Expansion of Social Entitlements

The most distinctive policy achievement of the UPA's first decade was a set of laws that recast welfare as legal entitlement. Earlier governments had run subsidy and poverty-alleviation schemes whose budgets and benefits depended on executive discretion. The UPA's statutes created claims that citizens could in principle enforce: a right to request information from public authorities, a right to demand work, a right of tribal and forest communities to their forest holdings, a right of children to schooling, and a right to subsidised grain.

The Right to Information Act of 2005 gave citizens a legal means to seek records from public authorities and established information commissions to hear appeals. Its origins lay in grassroots campaigns, especially the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan in Rajasthan, which had demanded access to records of local public works, and in earlier state laws. The Act became one of the most widely used transparency laws in the world, and its users included ordinary villagers seeking ration records as well as journalists and activists. Its effectiveness varied across states and over time, depending on the independence of commissions, backlogs, and risks faced by users, some of whom were attacked.

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, enacted in 2005, offered every rural household whose adult members were willing to do unskilled manual work up to one hundred days of employment per year at a statutory wage, with an unemployment allowance if work was not provided. It was introduced in selected districts in 2006 and extended to the whole rural country by 2008. It was demand-driven: in principle, work was to be provided on request, financed largely by the Union government. The law drew on advocacy by the NAC, economists, and activist networks, including the Right to Food campaign. Evaluations found that the scheme raised rural wages in some regions, provided income support in drought years, and gave women substantial participation, while also documenting delays in wage payment, inflated or fictitious muster rolls, uneven demand registration, and variation among states. States such as Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu performed comparatively well; others, including parts of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, lagged. Social audits and, later, electronic payment to accounts were among the responses to corruption.

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act of 2006 recognised individual and community rights of forest-dwelling communities and sought to correct what the Act's preamble described as a historical injustice arising from colonial and postcolonial forest administration. Implementation was slow and contested, with bureaucratic resistance, rejection of many claims, and disputes between conservation and livelihood interests. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act of 2009, effective 1 April 2010, made elementary education a legal right for children aged six to fourteen, built on the 2002 constitutional amendment, and required private schools to reserve a share of seats for disadvantaged children. It produced a major expansion of enrolment, though evidence from annual household education surveys indicated that learning outcomes lagged behind attendance. The National Food Security Act of 2013 gave statutory backing to subsidised foodgrain for up to two-thirds of the population, with maternity benefits and meals for children, extending the earlier public distribution system.

The distinction between a statutory right and effective implementation is essential. Rights existed on paper but depended on state capacity, on local officials, on documentation such as ration and job cards, and on the political will of state governments, which administer most of these programmes. Critics from the right questioned fiscal cost and the risk of dependency, and some economists argued that growth would reduce poverty more effectively than transfers. Critics from the left argued that funding remained inadequate and that exclusion from beneficiary lists denied rights in practice. Both arguments rest on evidence that varied by state and year.

The original MGNREGA framework described here was legally replaced, effective 1 July 2026, by a different statute that is discussed in Section 100.19. Students should keep the two periods distinct: the UPA-era law is part of the historical record of 2005–2026, and the 2026 law is too recent for assessment of its effects.

Historical Vocabulary
Statutory Entitlement

A statutory entitlement is a benefit that a law gives eligible individuals the right to claim, rather than a scheme that officials may fund, reduce, or discontinue at their discretion. In India after 2005 it described laws such as MGNREGA and the Food Security Act. The term matters because it explains a central difference between the policy philosophies of the period: entitlements stress legal claims and accountability, while delivery-oriented schemes stress administrative reach and targeting. The distinction is analytic, since many programmes combine both elements, and a statutory right does not guarantee practical enforcement.

Section 100.3

Economic Growth, Globalization, and the Expansion of India's Middle Classes

The UPA decade coincided with the fastest sustained growth in India's recorded economic history. Real gross domestic product grew at rates averaging in the vicinity of 7 to 8 percent annually across 2004–2014 in official series, with peaks above 9 percent in 2005–2008. The precise figures depend on which series and base year are used, since national-accounts revisions in 2011 and 2015 changed estimates, and economists argued over how the revisions altered comparison between periods. The broad point is not disputed: growth accelerated after 2003 and was high by Indian and global standards.

The foundations lay in the reforms of 1991, examined in Chapter 98, which opened sectors to private and foreign investment, reduced industrial licensing, and lowered tariffs. In the 2000s, high global liquidity, rising investment rates, rapid growth in the services sector, and expansion of credit supported the acceleration. Telecommunications transformed everyday life: mobile subscriptions grew from tens of millions at the start of the decade to hundreds of millions by 2010 as prices fell and private operators competed. Information-technology services and business-process outsourcing expanded in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, Pune, and the National Capital Region, and Indian firms such as Tata and Infosys became global names. Indian companies also invested abroad, with acquisitions such as Tata's purchase of Jaguar Land Rover in 2008.

Out of this growth emerged what commentators called the new middle class, though the term covers a wide range of incomes and statuses. It included salaried professionals in software and finance, owners of small businesses, upper-ranking government employees, and a growing body of urban households with consumer credit, private schooling for children, car and housing loans, and access to shopping malls and branded goods. Sociological studies emphasised its diversity by caste, region, and language, and its anxiety about status and employment. Its expectations mattered politically: it demanded better infrastructure, honest administration, and economic opportunity, and it became a powerful audience for political messages.

Most Indians, however, still lived outside this world. Roughly half the workforce remained in agriculture, which contributed a much smaller share of national output. Large numbers worked in the informal economy, with no written contract, social security, or protection from sudden loss of income. The growth of manufacturing employment, which had been expected to absorb labour as in East Asia, was modest, so the gap between productivity in the services sector and the livelihoods of most workers persisted. Measured poverty fell, as discussed in Section 100.15, but inequality also became a major subject of public debate.

The global financial crisis of 2008 tested India's integration. Capital outflows and falling exports slowed growth sharply in 2008–2009, but the effect was milder than in many advanced economies. The government and the Reserve Bank responded with fiscal stimulus, tax cuts, monetary easing, and a large agricultural loan waiver announced in 2008. Growth recovered quickly in 2009–2010. Many commentators credited the relative insulation of a domestically driven economy and a cautious banking sector, though the stimulus later contributed to fiscal deficits and inflation. Comparative economic detail across South Asia belongs to Chapter 106.

The central historical issue is how growth changed expectations. Rising incomes, spreading information, and visible examples of upward mobility encouraged demands for jobs, schooling, and public services. Governments were increasingly judged by the delivery of employment and infrastructure as much as by traditional identity-based loyalties, though caste, religion, and region retained great political weight. When growth faltered after 2011, as Section 100.4 shows, the disappointment of those expectations became politically potent.

Section 100.4

UPA-II, Corruption Controversies, Civic Mobilization, and the Crisis of Political Confidence

The UPA returned to office in the 2009 election with a stronger position: Congress won 206 Lok Sabha seats, its best result since 1991, and the BJP fell to 116. Observers credited the rural employment guarantee, the 2008 farm-loan waiver, and the Left's weakened position in West Bengal and Kerala, as well as the BJP's leadership difficulties. The second Singh government freed from dependence on the Left, gained room to legislate, and enacted the Right to Education Act in 2009 and the Food Security Act in 2013, as well as the Land Acquisition Act of 2013. Yet its second term was dominated by growing difficulties.

Economic conditions worsened. Food inflation reached high levels between 2009 and 2013, growth slowed to about 5 percent by 2012–2013, the rupee depreciated sharply in 2013, and investment decisions stalled, in part owing to regulatory and land-acquisition obstacles and to a perception of policy paralysis. Critics blamed inflationary stimulus and fiscal laxity; defenders pointed to global conditions after 2008 and to the difficulty of coalition management.

A series of controversies concerned the allocation of public resources. In the telecommunications scandal, commonly called the 2G case, the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) estimated in 2010 a presumptive loss to the exchequer, in a figure widely reported as ₹1.76 lakh crore, from the allocation of spectrum licences at prices set in 2001. The Supreme Court in 2012 cancelled 122 licences, finding the allocation process arbitrary. A special CBI court in December 2017 acquitted all accused in the criminal cases, finding that the prosecution had not proved its charges. Students must distinguish these stages: the CAG's figure was a notional estimate and its methodology was challenged; the court's cancellation concerned the process; the criminal acquittal concerned guilt. The Commonwealth Games held in Delhi in October 2010 generated allegations of irregularities in contracts and expenditure, leading to investigation and the resignation or arrest of some officials. In the coal-block allocation case, CAG reports estimated large presumptive losses from the allocation of blocks without competitive bidding; in 2014 the Supreme Court declared allocations since 1993 illegal and cancelled most blocks, and several prosecutions and convictions followed in later years.

The controversies fed a mass movement. In April 2011 the social activist Anna Hazare began a fast in Delhi demanding a strong ombudsman, the Lokpal, with powers to investigate high officials. Hazare's fasts, supported by lawyers, former civil servants, and a broad urban constituency, drew large crowds and intense television coverage. The government negotiated, introduced a Lokpal bill, and ultimately Parliament passed the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act in December 2013. The movement split: one wing, led by Arvind Kejriwal, formed the Aam Aadmi Party in November 2012 and won a striking showing in the Delhi assembly election of December 2013, forming a short-lived government. Another current of the movement drifted toward other parties. After the December 2012 gang rape of a young woman in Delhi, mass demonstrations also demanded stronger laws on sexual violence, resulting in the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 2013.

The anti-corruption movement itself has been interpreted in different ways. Supporters saw a legitimate civic response to genuine grievances and weak accountability. Critics noted that its leadership was socially narrow, that it targeted elected politicians while leaving other sources of corruption relatively unexamined, and that its moral absolutism sat uneasily with parliamentary processes. The Lokpal's practical effect after its eventual appointment in 2019 has been modest, and its limited use is part of the historical assessment.

By 2013–2014, the UPA's standing had weakened: its leader was seen as lacking authority, the party as divided, and the government as unable to act decisively. Political communication changed as well: television news, social media, and professional campaign management began to play a larger role. The opposition's message emphasised governance and growth. Chapter sections that follow show how the BJP, led by Narendra Modi, used these themes in 2014.

The Weakening of the UPA, 2010–2014

Long-term causes

Coalition constraints on decision-making; dual centres of authority between government and party; a political economy in which licences, land, and natural resources were allocated administratively; rising expectations created by a decade of growth.

Short-term causes

Slowing growth and high food inflation after 2010; the 2G, Commonwealth Games, and coal controversies; the 2011 anti-corruption movement; the 2012 protests over sexual violence; the perception of policy paralysis.

New political formations, notably the Aam Aadmi Party; a campaign environment centred on governance, decisiveness, and growth.

Section 100.5

Narendra Modi's Political Ascendancy and the 2014 General Election

Narendra Modi was born in 1950 in Vadnagar, Gujarat, and joined the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) as a young man. He became a full-time worker (pracharak) and moved into the BJP as an organiser in the 1980s, rising to the position of national general secretary. In October 2001 the party appointed him chief minister of Gujarat, and he held that office until May 2014, winning three successive state elections. His career therefore combined organisational work within the Hindu nationalist movement, whose development Chapter 99 traced, with more than twelve years of executive office in one of India's most industrialised states.

Gujarat under Modi became a subject of national argument. Supporters pointed to high state growth, investment-promotion summits, improvements in electricity supply and roads, and an image of administrative efficiency, often described as the “Gujarat model.” Critics noted that Gujarat's growth predated his tenure, that its social indicators, including child nutrition and some measures of education and health, did not match its income, and that the model's relationship to public welfare was uneven. These economic questions were inseparable from the political ones. In 2002, shortly after he took office, communal violence following the burning of a train at Godhra killed large numbers of people, most of them Muslims. Chapter 99 treats that violence. For this chapter, it is enough to note that Modi's role was investigated by courts and by a Supreme Court-appointed Special Investigation Team, which in 2012 filed a report that a magistrate and subsequently the courts accepted, and that critics continued to dispute its adequacy. The subject remained central to debate over his public record, and the historian's task is to distinguish judicial findings from political assertion.

In September 2013 the BJP named Modi its prime ministerial candidate, a decision that reflected pressure from the RSS and from party workers and displaced older leaders such as L. K. Advani. His campaign was notable for its scale and organisation. It used large rallies, which were broadcast and in some cases transmitted by hologram, intensive social-media outreach, professional communication specialists, and a presidential style that centred the leader rather than the party or the coalition. Its message combined themes of development, jobs, decisive administration, and an end to what it portrayed as the corruption and inertia of Congress rule, while also appealing to national pride and, in some regions, to Hindu identity. The slogan “acche din” (good days) captured the promise of improvement.

The result was decisive. The BJP won 282 of 543 Lok Sabha seats, the first single-party majority since Congress's 1984 victory, on about 31 percent of the national vote. Congress fell to 44 seats, the lowest in its history. The broader NDA, including allies such as the Shiv Sena, Telugu Desam Party, and Lok Janshakti Party, won about 336 seats. The distinction between the BJP's own majority and the NDA's alliance matters: the government continued to operate formally as an NDA coalition, and the BJP's majority allowed it to govern without dependence on allies for the passage of legislation in the Lok Sabha, though the party lacked a majority in the Rajya Sabha, which remained a constraint until later state victories raised its strength.

Scholars have analysed the 2014 outcome in terms of the first-past-the-post system, which converted a plurality of votes into a large majority, the fragmentation of opposition votes, strong performance in northern and western India, especially Uttar Pradesh, where the BJP won 71 of 80 seats with its ally, and the mobilisation of first-time and younger voters. Voter turnout of about 66 percent was the highest to that date. The BJP's gains came substantially from regions where it had been weak, and its expansion drew on non-dominant OBC and Dalit communities, a coalition-building strategy examined in later sections.

Section 100.6

The First Modi Government: Executive Leadership and Administrative Reorganization, 2014–2019

The first Modi government quickly established a distinctive governing style. Authority was concentrated in the Prime Minister's Office (PMO), which coordinated ministries, set targets, and monitored implementation, while the prime minister became the principal public voice of the government. Large national campaigns, announced personally and branded with memorable titles, gave visibility to policies: Make in India (2014) for manufacturing, Swachh Bharat (2014) for sanitation, Skill India, Smart Cities, and others. Regular broadcast addresses, notably the radio programme Mann Ki Baat begun in October 2014, reinforced direct communication with citizens.

One of the earliest institutional changes was the abolition of the Planning Commission, which had since 1950 drafted five-year plans and allocated resources to states (see Chapter 89). On 1 January 2015 it was replaced by NITI Aayog, a policy think tank with advisory rather than allocative functions. The change had several consequences. Plans were no longer drafted in the earlier manner, and the allocation of funds fell to the finance ministry and, in the case of states, to the Finance Commission, whose fourteenth report, accepted by the government, increased the share of central taxes devolved to states from 32 to 42 percent. Supporters argued that this strengthened cooperative federalism by increasing state autonomy over spending. Critics noted that an increase in untied devolution coexisted with growth in centrally sponsored schemes, cesses and surcharges not shared with states, and discretionary central influence.

Relations among institutions also changed. Cabinet ministers were seen to have limited independent scope relative to the PMO, and civil servants were encouraged to take part in presentations to the prime minister and to answer for targets. The government introduced lateral entry of specialists into senior civil-service posts in 2018 and promoted digital monitoring. Parliament functioned with significant disruptions in some sessions, though the government also pushed major legislation through, including the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016, the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act of 2016, and the constitutional amendment for GST. A major institutional change in monetary policy, the establishment in 2016 of a Monetary Policy Committee and a formal inflation target, was undertaken in agreement with the Reserve Bank and had its roots in recommendations made under earlier governments.

Interpretations of this style differ. Supporters emphasised decisiveness, speed of execution, reduced policy paralysis, and a clearer line of accountability. They pointed to improved coordination between ministries and a focus on measurable outcomes. Critics argued that concentration of authority in the PMO and in the prime minister personally weakened cabinet government, parliamentary scrutiny, and the autonomy of institutions such as the Reserve Bank, the investigative agencies, and the information commissions, and that it created risks of error when decisions were insulated from consultation. Debates also concerned the appointment of governors, the use of ordinances, and central intervention in state governments, as in the contested imposition of President's Rule in Uttarakhand and Arunachal Pradesh in 2016, which were reversed by courts.

Historians should avoid assuming that centralisation produced either effectiveness or democratic decline by definition. Some centralised initiatives achieved their administrative goals, such as the rapid spread of bank accounts. Others, discussed below, had costly consequences. The cabinet system and Parliament continued to function, and the opposition retained state governments and judicial recourse. The evaluation must therefore rest on specific institutional changes and their documented effects, which Section 100.16 revisits.

The BJP's organisational strength also altered how government operated. Under party president Amit Shah from 2014, the party expanded its membership and electoral machinery and won a series of state elections, including Maharashtra, Haryana, Jharkhand, and, in 2017, Uttar Pradesh, where it formed a government under Yogi Adityanath. These results increased its representation in the Rajya Sabha and eased the passage of national legislation. The alignment of party and government gave the executive greater capacity, which is one reason the period is often described as one of consolidation.

Section 100.7

Financial Inclusion, Welfare Delivery, and Household Security

The welfare architecture of the Modi governments relied on programmes that emphasised delivery systems, bank accounts, and executive schemes. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched in August 2014, aimed to give every household access to a bank account. Within a few years, hundreds of millions of accounts had been opened, and by the mid-2020s the total exceeded half a billion, a substantial portion of them held by women and in rural areas. Many accounts initially had low balances or were dormant, and evaluations noted problems of overdraft facilities, usage, and the use of accounts for actual savings. Nevertheless, account ownership rose markedly in national surveys such as the Global Findex.

Bank accounts were the base for direct benefit transfers (DBT), which pay subsidies and wages directly into accounts. DBT had been introduced in 2013 under the UPA, and the new government expanded it greatly, linking it to Aadhaar numbers. The system, in the government's account, reduced leakage and duplication in programmes such as the LPG cooking-gas subsidy, rural employment wages, and scholarships. The claims about savings from removing “ghost” beneficiaries were contested: some economists and the government calculated large savings, while others argued that estimates conflated elimination of fraud with exclusion of genuine beneficiaries.

Other flagship programmes addressed specific household needs. Ujjwala (2016) provided subsidised LPG connections to poorer households, particularly women; refill rates were an issue, as many households continued to use traditional fuels for reasons of cost. Swachh Bharat, launched on 2 October 2014, financed toilet construction in rural and urban areas and declared the country open-defecation free by 2019; surveys found a large expansion of toilet access, though usage and the accuracy of official claims were debated. Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana supported housing; the Saubhagya scheme (2017) pushed electricity connections to remaining households; Ayushman Bharat, launched in 2018, offered health insurance cover for hospitalisation to a large poorer population; and PM-KISAN, from 2019, provided direct income support of ₹6,000 a year to farmers in instalments, initially to small and marginal farmers.

The relationship between these programmes and earlier welfare institutions was one of both continuity and difference. Many schemes built on earlier models: sanitation campaigns on the UPA's Nirmal Bharat Abhiyan, LPG subsidy transfers on a pilot of DBT, and rural housing on the Indira Awas Yojana. The food-security law of 2013 and the rural employment guarantee continued, and the government expanded foodgrain distribution during the pandemic. The change lay partly in emphasis. UPA-era laws framed welfare as legal entitlement, enforceable through rights-based mechanisms, whereas NDA-era schemes stressed delivery through technology, administrative targeting, and the association of benefits with the central government and the prime minister. Supporters regarded this as a pragmatic modernisation that reached people more reliably. Critics argued that entitlements provided stronger accountability and that executive schemes were more vulnerable to discontinuation. Both descriptions oversimplify, since the NDA also relied on statutory frameworks and the UPA also ran executive schemes.

Aadhaar, the twelve-digit biometric identity number, became the connecting technology. Established by the UIDAI in 2009 under the UPA, it was given legal basis by the Aadhaar Act of 2016. Advantages included identity verification for people lacking other documents and reduced duplication. Risks included exclusion when fingerprint authentication failed or numbers were not linked, data-security and privacy concerns, and the compulsory linking of Aadhaar to services. In August 2017 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court recognised privacy as a fundamental right in Puttaswamy v. Union of India, and in September 2018 a five-judge bench upheld the Aadhaar scheme while striking down some provisions and limiting its mandatory use by private entities. Evidence of exclusion came from household surveys and field studies, for instance in ration distribution in Jharkhand, and should be weighed with official figures on authentication success.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Bank Account and a Cooking-Gas Cylinder: Welfare in Household Life

Household-level evidence about welfare comes from surveys such as the National Family Health Survey and the National Sample Survey, official evaluations, and independent field studies. They suggest that the introduction of a bank account changed a household's relationship to the state: wages and subsidies could be received without a middleman, and women in some households gained their first account in their own names. They also show persisting constraints. Banking agents and branches could be distant, mobile-network failures could block biometric authentication, and cash withdrawals often required repeated visits.

The LPG programme shows both effects. National surveys recorded a large rise in the share of households with a clean-fuel connection, reducing exposure to smoke from wood and dung, with health benefits particularly for women. Yet surveys also reported that many poor households used cylinders sparingly because refills required a cash outlay, and combined gas with traditional fuel. These findings come from aggregated surveys, not individual testimony, and should be read as patterns rather than representative stories.

Section 100.8

Infrastructure, Digital India, and the Expansion of Digital Government

India's twenty-first-century infrastructure combined projects begun by earlier governments with accelerated construction after 2014. The national highway network had been transformed by the Golden Quadrilateral and the National Highways Development Project under Vajpayee. UPA governments continued highway construction, expanded airports and power capacity, and launched the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission. After 2014, the government raised capital expenditure on roads, railways, ports, and logistics, promoted programmes such as Bharatmala and Sagarmala, built dedicated freight corridors that had been planned earlier, and expanded the average rate of national-highway construction, while the length of the rural road network under the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana, started in 2000, also grew. Electrification of villages and households, completed in official terms in 2018 for villages and in 2019 for households under Saubhagya, left questions about the reliability and quality of supply.

The digital dimension was more novel. The Digital India programme, launched in 2015, aimed to provide digital infrastructure, online services, and digital literacy. Its elements included the Aadhaar system, the BharatNet project to link village councils by optical fibre, the DigiLocker for documents, the Common Services Centres, and numerous central and state portals. Mobile telephony underpinned these developments. The entry in 2016 of Reliance Jio, which offered very cheap data, triggered a collapse in data prices and a surge in mobile-internet use; by the mid-2020s India had over a billion mobile subscriptions and among the lowest data prices in the world.

The most celebrated element was the Unified Payments Interface. The National Payments Corporation of India, an organisation promoted by the Reserve Bank and the banking sector, launched a pilot of UPI in April 2016 and opened it more widely in August of that year. UPI allowed instant, low-cost transfers between bank accounts using a mobile phone and a virtual address or QR code. Its use grew explosively after demonetization (Section 100.9), the COVID-19 pandemic, and the entry of private payment applications such as PhonePe and Google Pay, which came to dominate UPI transactions. By 2025 monthly transactions were on the order of twenty billion, and small shopkeepers, street vendors, and households routinely used QR codes. The government promoted UPI abroad through agreements with other countries (see Chapter 110).

Analysts debated the importance of these systems. Supporters described “digital public infrastructure,” meaning open, interoperable public platforms on which private actors build services, as a distinctive Indian contribution, reducing transaction costs and widening formal financial participation. Researchers documented effects on small businesses, which gained access to digital payment records and, potentially, credit, and on government administration, where payments could be tracked. Critics raised concerns about privacy, as large databases linked identity, finances, and welfare; cybersecurity, as breaches of data and fraud were reported; the concentration of the market in two private applications; and digital exclusion, since access depended on smartphones, connectivity, and literacy.

The technological capacity of the state and universal access were different things. In 2024–25 surveys, internet use and smartphone ownership were much higher in urban than rural areas, higher among men than women, and higher in southern and western states than in states such as Bihar, Jharkhand, and Uttar Pradesh. Digital services therefore reached some citizens more readily than others, and in the case of welfare benefits, failure of the technology could deny entitlements. The state's digital reach was real and growing, but it overlaid, rather than replaced, older administrative and social inequalities.

Historical Vocabulary
Digital Public Infrastructure

Digital public infrastructure (DPI) refers to shared, standardised digital systems, such as digital identity, payment networks, and data-exchange protocols, designed to be used by many government and private services. In India the term is applied above all to Aadhaar, UPI, and related platforms. It matters because it describes a particular approach to technological development: building common rails rather than individual applications. The term is sometimes used promotionally, and it should not be confused with universal access to the services built upon it.

Section 100.9

Demonetization, November 2016: Objectives, Implementation, and Economic Consequences

On the evening of 8 November 2016, the prime minister announced in a televised address that ₹500 and ₹1,000 banknotes would cease to be legal tender from midnight. The two denominations accounted for about 86 percent of the value of currency in circulation. Citizens were told they could deposit the notes in bank accounts or exchange them within a fixed period, while new ₹500 and ₹2,000 notes would be issued. The decision was prepared in secrecy, announced with a few hours' notice, and implemented by the Reserve Bank of India and the banking system.

The government gave several objectives. The principal ones were to attack undeclared wealth, or “black money,” by forcing holders of unaccounted cash to disclose it or lose it; to eliminate counterfeit notes; to cut off financing of terrorism and other illicit activity; and to push the economy toward digital and formal transactions. Other goals, particularly the promotion of digital payments, were emphasised as the policy unfolded. Students should note that stated objectives shifted in emphasis, an observation made by critics and often rebutted by supporters who argued that the aims were complementary from the start.

Implementation proved difficult. India's economy was heavily cash-dependent, and the supply of new notes took months to restore, as printing capacity was limited and the new ₹2,000 note did not fit many automated teller machines until recalibrated. People queued for hours at banks and ATMs, and the government imposed limits on withdrawals. Reports recorded deaths associated with queues, though the causal attribution of individual cases was disputed. The disruption fell hardest on the informal sector: daily-wage labourers, small traders, farmers selling produce in cash-based markets, construction workers, and small manufacturers in clusters such as the textile centres of Tiruppur and Surat. Rural cooperative banks, which were restricted from exchanging notes, faced particular difficulties. The effect on aggregate output was disputed, with some analysts estimating a loss of one or two percentage points of growth in the affected quarters and the official data, which poorly capture the informal sector, showing a smaller slowdown.

The Reserve Bank of India's annual report for 2016–17 reported that approximately 99.3 percent of the demonetized notes by value, about ₹15.31 lakh crore of ₹15.44 lakh crore, had returned to the banking system. Critics argued that this showed that little black money had been extinguished, since the expected gain, a windfall from notes not returned, did not materialise, and that cash had been converted into deposits or exchanged. Supporters argued that the return of notes did not mean the money was clean, that deposits brought unaccounted wealth into the tax net and triggered investigations, and that the number of tax filers and the volume of digital transactions rose. The Supreme Court in January 2023, by a 4–1 majority, upheld the legality of the decision-making process, with one judge dissenting on the use of statutory powers. The ruling addressed legality, not the policy's wisdom.

Outcomes were mixed and contested. Counterfeit notes detected by the banking system were a small fraction of circulation. Cash in circulation recovered and, by the early 2020s, exceeded pre-demonetization levels in absolute terms, though the ratio to GDP followed a more complex path. Digital payments increased steadily, but analysts disagreed on how much of the increase was due to demonetization as opposed to the broader technological trend visible in UPI. The decision also had political effects: the BJP did very well in the 2017 Uttar Pradesh election, which many interpreted as evidence that the poor did not blame the government, a view disputed by some survey data.

Section 100.10

The Goods and Services Tax and the Reorganization of India's Tax System

Before 2017, indirect taxation in India was divided between the Union and the states. The Union levied excise duties on manufacture and service tax; states levied value-added tax on sales, entry taxes, and octroi on goods entering cities. The result was a cascade of overlapping taxes, border checkposts that delayed trucks, and varied rates across states. Proposals for a unified goods and services tax went back to the late 1990s; a first constitutional amendment bill was introduced in 2011 under the UPA, and states, particularly those governed by opposition parties, resisted it over loss of fiscal autonomy and compensation. The BJP in opposition also initially opposed the proposal and later supported it in government.

The Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016, received assent in September 2016 after passage by both houses and ratification by states. It permitted Parliament and state legislatures to levy GST concurrently and created the GST Council, a constitutional body under Article 279A comprising the Union finance minister and state finance ministers. In the Council, the Union government holds one-third of the votes and the states two-thirds collectively, and decisions require a three-fourths majority, meaning that neither the Union nor the states can act alone. The GST took effect on 1 July 2017 following a midnight session in the Central Hall of Parliament. Goods and services were taxed at slabs of 5, 12, 18, and 28 percent, with higher rates on luxury and “sin” goods, plus a compensation cess, and some items were exempt. The tax was destination-based, with an integrated tax levied on inter-state transactions.

The reform was both an economic and a federal experiment. It aimed to create a single national market, eliminate cascading taxes through input-tax credit, remove checkposts, and widen the tax base. It also transferred control over a major part of states' own revenue to a joint body. States were promised compensation for revenue shortfalls for five years, a guarantee that later became a source of conflict.

Implementation was difficult. The technology platform, the GST Network, suffered early failures; businesses faced multiple returns, frequent rule changes, and delays in input-tax refunds. Small firms, which already struggled after demonetization, complained of compliance costs, while larger firms with accounting systems adapted more readily. Revenue collections gradually grew, and monthly collections surpassed ₹1 lakh crore regularly from 2019 and rose substantially in later years, though in early years they fell short of the expectations of the Union and the states. The tax base widened, with registered taxpayers increasing severalfold, and logistics times fell as border barriers were removed.

Compensation became a flashpoint during the pandemic. In 2020, with collections depressed, the Union government told the Council that it faced legal limits on its ability to pay the promised compensation, and it proposed that states borrow, with the Union making arrangements to ease terms. Opposition-ruled states objected strongly, and the dispute illustrated tensions in fiscal federalism. Compensation cess collections were later used to repay borrowing. In September 2025 the Council approved a major rate rationalisation, reducing the multiple slabs largely to two principal rates, 5 and 18 percent, with a higher rate on a small set of luxury and sin goods, effective later that month. This change, too recent for full assessment, is evidence of continuing adjustment.

The central analytical question is whether GST strengthened coordination between governments while changing states' fiscal autonomy. Supporters point to the Council as a unique instance of institutionalised cooperation in which states shared sovereignty over tax policy and, because of the three-fourths rule, hold collective veto power. Critics note that states gave up independent power to set rates on most goods and services, that the Union's one-third share gives it effective veto, that the compensation guarantee expired in 2022, and that cesses and surcharges on income and other taxes that are not shared with states have grown. Both observations can be correct, and the long-term balance depends on how the Council functions.

The 2019 General Election and the Second BJP Majority Government

By 2018, the BJP's position appeared less secure than in 2014. Farm distress, unemployment, the economic after-effects of demonetization and GST, and losses in the December 2018 assembly elections in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh, which Congress won, led some analysts to predict a closer contest. The government responded with welfare initiatives, including PM-KISAN income support for farmers in the interim budget of February 2019 and a ten percent reservation in government jobs and education for economically weaker sections outside existing caste reservations, enacted through the 103rd Constitutional Amendment in January 2019.

Events in February 2019 changed the political environment. On 14 February a suicide bombing killed more than forty paramilitary personnel in Pulwama, Jammu and Kashmir, and on 26 February the Indian air force carried out an attack on a target in Balakot in Pakistan. The security dimensions are treated in Chapter 105. For electoral history, the importance of these events lay in the BJP's decision to place national security at the centre of its campaign, and in analysts' continuing disagreement over how much they altered voting intentions, since the party had already been performing strongly in many states.

The BJP's campaign was leadership-centred. It stressed Modi's personal appeal, welfare programmes such as Ujjwala, toilets, housing, and bank accounts, and national security, backed by a vast organisation and substantial financial resources. The opposition was fragmented. Congress, led by Rahul Gandhi, campaigned on unemployment, agrarian distress, and allegations about the Rafale aircraft contract, and proposed a minimum income guarantee scheme. In Uttar Pradesh, the Samajwadi Party and Bahujan Samaj Party formed an alliance that failed to prevent a large BJP win. Regional parties contested in their own states and no national opposition alliance took shape.

The result was a larger victory than in 2014. The BJP won 303 seats, up from 282, with about 37 percent of the vote, and the NDA won about 353. Congress won 52, only slightly more than in 2014, and failed again to reach the ten percent of seats required to claim the post of Leader of the Opposition. The BJP's gains came from states where it had previously been weaker, including West Bengal, where it won 18 seats, and Odisha, and it won nearly all seats in states such as Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh. Turnout, about 67 percent, was a record. The regional opposition held on in the south and in states such as Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Punjab, so that the result reflected a deepening north–south and east–west variation.

The second majority altered the government's capacity. A larger BJP strength in the Lok Sabha and a growing presence in the Rajya Sabha through state victories and cooperation from parties such as the YSR Congress and the Biju Janata Dal allowed it to legislate quickly in 2019 on measures including the prohibition of instant triple talaq, amendments to the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, the changes in Jammu and Kashmir, and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act. The Supreme Court's November 2019 judgment on the Ayodhya dispute, treated in Chapter 99's account of the Ram Janmabhoomi movement, resolved a long litigation and was followed by the construction of a temple, consecrated in January 2024. The renewed mandate thus coincided with the pursuit of long-standing items of the Hindu nationalist agenda, a development that supporters regarded as fulfilment of promises and critics as a majoritarian turn.

The result also affected the opposition. Congress's leader resigned and the party endured defections and leadership uncertainty, and regional opposition parties pursued separate strategies. The internal balance of the NDA also shifted: allies such as the Shiv Sena, long a partner, broke with the BJP after the 2019 Maharashtra election over power-sharing, showing that even a strong majority did not eliminate alliance politics. This section establishes the political conditions for the second administration; Section 100.12 turns to its constitutional initiatives.

Section 100.12

Citizenship, Constitutional Change, Federalism, and Political Contestation

The second Modi government's most controversial legislation concerned citizenship and the constitutional status of one state. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act (CAA), passed by Parliament in December 2019, amended the Citizenship Act of 1955 to provide a faster path to citizenship for members of six religious communities, Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians, who had entered India from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, or Pakistan before 31 December 2014 and who faced religious persecution. Muslims were not included. The government argued that the Act protected persecuted minorities in three Muslim-majority neighbouring states, and that it did not affect the citizenship of any Indian. Critics argued that religion was made a criterion of citizenship for the first time in Indian law, in tension with the constitutional guarantees of equality and secularism, and that it excluded groups such as Ahmadiyyas and Rohingya who faced persecution.

The controversy was linked to a proposed National Register of Citizens (NRC). In Assam, an NRC updated under Supreme Court supervision had been published in August 2019 and excluded about 1.9 million people, many of whom were Hindus, which complicated expectations on all sides. Concerns arose that a national NRC combined with the CAA could expose Muslims lacking documents to the loss of citizenship, while the government stated that no nationwide NRC had been decided. Protests spread in December 2019 and early 2020, from Assam, where objections concerned the burden of migrants on indigenous communities, to Delhi and other cities, where demonstrations such as the sit-in at Shaheen Bagh, led largely by women, drew national attention. Police action at universities, deaths in protests in several states, and in February 2020 large-scale violence in northeast Delhi, which killed more than fifty people, most of them Muslims, produced investigations and prosecutions that remained controversial. Petitions challenging the Act's constitutionality were filed in the Supreme Court, and as of this chapter's cutoff they remained pending.

The Rules needed to implement the CAA were not framed for four years. The Ministry of Home Affairs notified them on 11 March 2024, shortly before the general election, and applications began to be received through an online portal. The distinction is important: passage of the law in 2019 and its operation from 2024 were separate events, and the numbers of persons granted citizenship under it were small in the early period.

In August 2019 the government also revoked the special constitutional provisions of Article 370 relating to Jammu and Kashmir and reorganised the state into two Union territories, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh. The legal reasoning, the Supreme Court's December 2023 upholding of the revocation, the security measures and communications restrictions that accompanied it, and the strategic implications are the subject of Chapter 105. For this chapter, the significance is domestic and constitutional. The measure was supported by those who saw it as completing integration and ending a regime of separate status, and opposed by those who argued that it bypassed the consent of the state's representatives and downgraded a state to a Union territory, which raised questions about the federal structure.

Arguments about these measures extended beyond their specifics. Government supporters argued that they fulfilled electoral promises, rectified historical anomalies, and protected national security and persecuted minorities. Opposition parties and many civil-society organisations argued that they weakened secular constitutionalism, the protection of minorities, and the federal balance, and that a majority in Parliament had been used to make fundamental changes with limited consultation. Constitutional scholars analysed questions such as whether classification by religion violates Article 14, the scope of Parliament's power over state boundaries, and the doctrine of the basic structure of the Constitution. Judicial decisions, where they exist, establish legal status; they do not settle the political and moral arguments.

For the study of federalism, these years showed that Union–state relations were contested not only through fiscal institutions such as the GST Council but through law-making, the role of governors, and the central handling of regional questions. Several states governed by opposition parties, including Kerala, West Bengal, and Punjab, passed resolutions against the CAA or contested other measures, though citizenship is a Union subject.

Section 100.13

The COVID-19 Pandemic and the Crisis of Public Health, 2020–2022

The first cases of COVID-19 in India were reported in Kerala in late January 2020. After a voluntary “Janata curfew” on 22 March, the prime minister announced on 24 March a national lockdown for twenty-one days, to begin at midnight, with about four hours' notice. It was imposed under the Disaster Management Act of 2005 and extended in stages until the end of May, after which restrictions were eased in phases (“unlock”). Passenger trains, flights, and road transport stopped, schools and most businesses closed, and movement was restricted. It was among the most stringent lockdowns in the world and the largest restriction on mobility in India's history.

Public-health preparedness was limited. India's public health spending was low by international standards, hospital and intensive-care capacity was thin, and diagnostic testing initially scarce. Responsibility for health lies largely with the states, so the Union government's lockdown order and the states' administration of hospitals, quarantine, and relief interacted in ways that varied across the country. Kerala, with a stronger public-health system and prior experience with the Nipah virus, achieved early containment; Maharashtra, Delhi, and Tamil Nadu faced early large outbreaks. Debates continued over whether the lockdown, which delayed the spread and gave time to prepare, was too abrupt and too long. Supporters emphasised the time gained; critics, including many economists and public-health experts, argued that the lack of notice and planning magnified suffering and that targeted measures might have been better.

The social effects were severe and unequal. Tens of millions of migrant workers, employed in construction, factories, and services in cities, lost their jobs and income overnight. With transport halted, large numbers began to walk hundreds of kilometres to home villages in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha, and elsewhere; some died on the way from exhaustion and accidents. The government began special “Shramik” trains in May. The Supreme Court took up the matter and directed measures for transport and food. The episode exposed the invisibility of internal migrant workers in official data and welfare systems, a fact acknowledged in later efforts such as “One Nation One Ration Card.” The government announced a relief package, including additional free foodgrain under the food-security system, cash transfers to some accounts through Jan Dhan, and credit measures, and the free-grain scheme was extended for several years. Informal-sector households, small enterprises, and street vendors suffered large income losses; the economy contracted by roughly six percent in 2020–21 by official estimates, the first annual contraction in decades. School closures lasted far longer in India than in many countries, with evidence of learning loss particularly among children without access to digital devices, which widened existing inequalities of class, gender, and region.

The second wave, driven by the Delta variant, struck in April–May 2021 after political rallies, religious gatherings such as the Kumbh Mela, and relaxation of precautions. Hospitals in Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, and elsewhere ran out of beds and medical oxygen, families searched for supplies through social media, and crematoria and burial grounds were overwhelmed. Rivers, notably the Ganges, saw bodies washed ashore in some areas. Courts, including the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court, intervened on oxygen supply. The scale was far above what official statistics recorded.

Mortality is a contested subject. The official count of COVID-19 deaths was about 530,000. Estimates of excess mortality, derived from civil-registration data, household surveys, and statistical modelling, were much higher, ranging in various studies from roughly one to more than four million deaths, and the World Health Organization's estimate for 2020–21 of about 4.7 million was disputed by the Indian government on methodological grounds. Historians should distinguish the confirmed official count from modelled excess-death estimates, recognise that the latter vary with method and data, and avoid presenting any single figure as settled.

Public-health achievements and failures both need recording. India developed vaccines domestically and manufactured them at scale, the Serum Institute of India producing the Covishield vaccine and Bharat Biotech the Covaxin vaccine. Vaccination began on 16 January 2021, accelerated after policy changes in mid-2021, and passed one billion doses in October 2021, with a digital registration platform, CoWIN. The government also exported vaccines early in 2021 before the second wave and then restricted exports. Critics charged that earlier exports and slow procurement left the country exposed; defenders pointed to the later pace of vaccination. Reopening of economic and educational institutions followed in 2021–22 and the economy rebounded strongly in 2021–22. The evaluation should consider decisions in light of contemporary knowledge, including the uncertainty about the virus, as well as the documented failures in implementation, such as the lack of preparation for migrants and for oxygen supply.

Section 100.14

Agriculture, the Farm Laws, and the Farmers' Protest Movement, 2020–2021

Indian agriculture was long regulated through state-run wholesale markets (mandis), under Agricultural Produce Market Committee laws, where farmers sold produce through licensed traders and commission agents. For wheat and rice, the government procured grain at announced minimum support prices (MSP), a system most effective in Punjab, Haryana, and parts of western Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and some other states. Economists across the political spectrum had criticised the mandi system as restrictive and the procurement system as distorting crop choices, and expert committees under earlier governments had proposed reform.

In June 2020 the government issued ordinances, which Parliament replaced with three Acts in September 2020: the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, allowing sale outside the mandis; the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, for contract farming; and an amendment of the Essential Commodities Act, relaxing stock limits. The government argued that they would give farmers more choice, attract investment, and raise incomes. The bills were passed in the Rajya Sabha amid disorder over a contested voice vote, and the process of passage, without committee scrutiny, became part of the grievance.

Farmers' organisations, notably in Punjab and Haryana, raised objections. Their central fear was that the laws would weaken the mandi system and, by extension, public procurement at MSP, leaving farmers dependent on large private buyers with greater bargaining power, and that contract-farming disputes would be resolved by administrators rather than courts. They also objected that agriculture is a state subject and that the laws were passed without consultation. Commission agents, who would lose their role, and state governments dependent on mandi fees shared the opposition. The government replied that MSP and mandis would continue and that the laws gave additional options.

From late 2020, farmers began the “Delhi Chalo” march. After being stopped at the Delhi border, tens of thousands set up encampments on highways at Singhu, Tikri, and Ghazipur, which lasted more than a year. The protest was coordinated by the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, an alliance of dozens of unions, and its participants included Sikh farmers from Punjab, Jat farmers from Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, and women and labour groups. The Supreme Court in January 2021 stayed the implementation of the laws and appointed a committee. Talks between the government and farmer leaders failed to reach agreement. Violence occurred on 26 January 2021 when some protesters entered the Red Fort during a tractor rally, and in October 2021 a vehicle in Lakhimpur Kheri in Uttar Pradesh killed protesters and others, leading to prosecutions. Organisers claimed that several hundred protesters died during the protest, through exposure, illness, and accident; precise numbers are contested.

The prime minister announced on 19 November 2021 that the laws would be withdrawn, and Parliament passed the Farm Laws Repeal Act at the end of the month. Explanations have included the proximity of elections in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh in early 2022, concerns over the unrest, and the strength of the movement; the government said that it had been unable to persuade a section of farmers. The repeal was a rare reversal of a major central initiative.

The episode is a case study in policymaking and federalism. It showed the limits of enacting economic reform without consultation among states and stakeholders, the capacity of organised and well-resourced groups, concentrated geographically, to resist, and the role of the courts and public opinion. It also showed regional variation: the protests were strongest in Punjab and Haryana, where procurement matters most, while farmers in many other states were less mobilised, and some farmers' organisations supported the laws. Analysts therefore caution against treating “the farmers” as a single bloc.

Section 100.15

Growth, Employment, Inequality, and the Changing Indian Middle Classes

Assessment of economic results from 2004 to 2026 requires attention to four distinct measures: growth of output, reduction of poverty, employment, and distribution of income and wealth. They do not move together, and each is measured imperfectly.

Growth was high for most of the period. Official data show an average above 7 percent in the UPA's first decade, with a slowdown to around 5 percent in 2012–13; growth recovered to between 6 and 8 percent in 2014–2019 and then slowed to about 4 percent in 2019–20, the quarters of the financial-sector stress involving non-bank lenders and rising bad loans. The pandemic caused a contraction in 2020–21, followed by strong rebound growth of about 9 percent in 2021–22 from a low base, and growth of roughly 7 to 8 percent in the following years. By nominal measures India moved up the world ranking and by the mid-2020s was among the five largest economies, with projections that it would become the fourth. Two methodological cautions apply. National-accounts series were revised, with a new base year in 2011–12 adopted in 2015, producing disagreement about comparisons across series; and growth in output per head is a different matter from growth in the aggregate.

Poverty declined substantially. Official poverty estimates using the Tendulkar line fell from about 37 percent of the population in 2004–05 to about 22 percent in 2011–12. After 2012 the government did not publish a comparable poverty line, and a household consumption survey of 2017–18 was withheld by the government, partly over data-quality concerns. Later estimates, including those of the World Bank from the 2022–23 consumption survey, suggest a further fall of extreme poverty to the low single digits at the international line, though the choice of line and survey comparability are disputed, and multidimensional poverty indices published by NITI Aayog report large reductions based on non-income indicators such as sanitation, cooking fuel, and housing. Historians should treat these as different measures and avoid attributing observed change to the party in office, since long-term growth, state-government policy, and global conditions all contributed.

Employment is the most contested domain. India's workforce is dominated by self-employment and informal work, and open unemployment is a poor measure of labour-market distress. The Periodic Labour Force Survey, begun in 2017–18, recorded a rise in the unemployment rate to above 6 percent in 2017–18, then lower rates in the early 2020s; at the same time, female labour-force participation, which had fallen for decades, rose in the survey, largely owing to self-employment in agriculture and household enterprises. Youth unemployment, especially among educated young people, remained high, and the share of workers in agriculture rose during the pandemic, reversing some structural shift. Manufacturing's share of employment grew little despite the Make in India programme and later production-linked incentive schemes, and the creation of enough stable jobs for a large cohort of young entrants remained the central economic challenge.

Inequality has been debated. Studies by economists including Thomas Piketty and colleagues, using tax and survey data, reported that the share of national income going to the top one percent reached very high levels by the early 2020s, while the government and some economists criticised the data and methods, and alternative consumption-based measures suggest lower inequality. The consensus is that the top of the distribution captured a large share of gains while data limitations make precise comparison uncertain.

The middle classes changed in this period. Urban professional households benefited from the growth of services and technology, from the expansion of private schools, colleges, and hospitals, and from digital consumer markets. At the same time, many middle-class households faced insecurity from rising costs of education and health care, competition for public jobs, and the dependence on informal or contract work. Regional differences were large: the southern and western states had higher incomes, better education, and higher urbanisation than much of the Gangetic plain. These changes mattered politically, because expectations about jobs and mobility, whether fulfilled or disappointed, shaped voting behaviour, as the 2024 election suggested.

Section 100.16

Democratic Institutions, Political Finance, Civil Society, and Public Debate

A prolonged period of single-party dominance at the centre, combined with strong executive leadership, raised questions about the health of India's democratic institutions. Elections continued to be held on schedule with high participation, and the opposition won state governments and, as Section 100.17 shows, large numbers of seats in 2024. Yet analysts and international indices, which are themselves contested, recorded concern about the functioning of institutions, and government supporters rejected these assessments as biased. The historian's task is to examine specific institutions.

Parliament functioned with a mixture of intense legislative productivity and frequent disruptions. Bills were sometimes passed with limited debate or committee scrutiny, as with the farm laws, and a smaller share of bills was referred to committees than in earlier periods. In December 2023, a large number of opposition members of Parliament were suspended in a single session, which critics regarded as an extraordinary measure; the government stated that the suspensions followed breaches of decorum and a security breach in the Lok Sabha. The post of deputy speaker of the Lok Sabha remained unfilled for the 17th Lok Sabha, in contrast to constitutional convention.

The judiciary acted both as a check and, according to critics, as a reluctant one. The Supreme Court issued landmark rulings on privacy (2017), on the decriminalisation of consensual same-sex relations (2018), on the electoral-bond scheme (2024), and on other questions, and on the status of Jammu and Kashmir (2023) it upheld the government's action. Debates arose over the appointment of judges, with the government and the collegium disputing the process, over delays in hearing important constitutional challenges, including those to the CAA, and over how often cases were heard in a timely manner. Election administration, including the revision of electoral rolls in 2025 in Bihar, which raised disputes over documentation and the deletion of names, was another matter of controversy and judicial scrutiny. Investigative agencies such as the Enforcement Directorate and the Central Bureau of Investigation were accused by opposition parties of selective targeting, while the government said they acted against corruption without regard to party. Journalism faced pressures from advertising, ownership concentration, legal cases, and in some instances intimidation, and rankings of press freedom placed India low, a verdict that the government rejected.

Political finance was a major issue. In 2018 the government introduced electoral bonds, bearer instruments that donors could buy from the State Bank of India and give to parties, with the donor's identity not disclosed to the public. The government argued that this reduced cash donations and encouraged formal channels; critics argued that it enabled opaque corporate funding and that most funds went to the ruling party. On 15 February 2024 a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India held the scheme unconstitutional as a violation of the citizen's right to information under the freedom of speech, and struck down related amendments that had removed limits on corporate donations and disclosure requirements. It directed the State Bank of India to stop issuing bonds and to disclose details to the Election Commission, which published them. The data revealed the purchasers and recipients of bonds and set off further debate and investigations.

In September 2023, Parliament passed the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, providing for one-third reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies. The reservation was made operative only after a census and subsequent delimitation of constituencies. As a result, it had not been applied in any general election as of November 2026; its implementation depends on the census now under way and on a delimitation exercise whose form, including the treatment of the freeze on seat allocation among states, is a politically sensitive question for southern states fearing a loss of share.

Civil society remained active but faced constraints. Regulations on foreign funding under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, tightened in 2020, led to the cancellation of registrations of several organisations, and laws such as the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act were used in prosecutions of activists, as in the Bhima Koregaon case, while protest movements such as the farmers' agitation succeeded in changing policy. Public debate through social media also reshaped political communication.

Historical Debate

Strengthened Government, Weakened Safeguards, or Both?

The question: Did the period after 2014 strengthen effective democratic government, weaken liberal-democratic safeguards, or produce both?

Interpretation A — Effective, accountable majority government: Supporters argue that decisive majorities delivered welfare, infrastructure, and reforms that earlier coalitions could not, that elections remained free and competitive, that courts struck down government measures such as the electoral bonds, and that opposition parties won many states and, in 2024, many seats.
Interpretation B — Erosion of safeguards: Critics argue that executive dominance weakened parliamentary scrutiny, that investigative agencies, election administration, and parts of the media were used or pressured in favour of the government, that minority rights and protest rights were curtailed, and that financial advantages of the ruling party distorted competition.
Interpretation C — Mixed and contested outcomes: Many scholars argue that features of both are visible, with institutions varying in independence by sector and period, and that the 2024 result shows the continued capacity of voters to constrain power.

Evidence: Parliamentary records, judicial decisions, election data, political-funding disclosures, press-freedom indices, case records, and comparative indices of democracy, whose methodology is itself debated.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how the period is placed within the longer history of Indian democracy, including the Emergency (Chapter 93), and how recent events are connected to future possibilities.

The third Modi government, current challenges, and long-term synthesis

Section 100.17

The 2024 General Election and the Return to Coalition Dependence

The general election of 2024 was held in seven phases between 19 April and 1 June, and votes were counted on 4 June. The BJP entered the campaign with high expectations, setting for the NDA a public target of 400 seats, and many commentators and exit polls predicted a larger majority. Its campaign centred again on the prime minister's leadership, on welfare delivery, infrastructure, the construction and January 2024 consecration of the Ram temple at Ayodhya, and the theme of a “developed India” by 2047. It was fought also on the changes of the preceding years: the CAA rules, the treatment of opposition leaders, and the use of investigative agencies against opponents, which the opposition emphasised.

The opposition had formed in 2023 the Indian National Developmental Inclusive Alliance, called the INDIA alliance, which brought together Congress and many regional parties, including the Samajwadi Party, the Trinamool Congress, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Rashtriya Janata Dal, and the Aam Aadmi Party. It was an uneasy alliance: partners contested against each other in some states, such as Kerala and West Bengal, and some parties left or joined, notably the Janata Dal (United), which returned to the NDA in January 2024. Rahul Gandhi, who had led the Bharat Jodo Yatra in 2022–23 and a second march in 2024, was Congress's principal campaigner. The opposition emphasised unemployment, price rises, the Agnipath short-term military recruitment scheme, the demand for a caste census, and warnings, which the BJP denied, that a larger majority would be used to amend the Constitution. Many of these themes were framed in terms of social justice and the defence of reservations, bringing to the fore the Mandal-era politics discussed in Chapter 99.

The result was a setback for the BJP. It won 240 seats, a loss of 63 compared with 2019, and fell short of the 272 required for a majority in the 543-member house. The NDA won 293 seats, comfortably above the majority threshold, and the INDIA alliance won 234, with Congress winning 99, nearly doubling its 2019 total. The BJP's vote share was about 36.6 percent, close to its 2019 level, while Congress's share rose to about 21 percent. Turnout was about 66 percent. The pattern shows a notable stability in vote share combined with a large loss of seats, which arose from changes in alliances and the effect of the first-past-the-post system in particular states.

The distinction between party-level and alliance-level results is essential. The BJP did not lose the election in the sense of losing power: the NDA retained a clear parliamentary majority, and Narendra Modi was sworn in on 9 June 2024 for a third consecutive term, the first prime minister since Jawaharlal Nehru to do so. What changed was the BJP's dependence. In 2014 and 2019 the NDA had existed as an alliance, but the BJP did not need its partners for a majority; in 2024 it did. That is the significant shift in the distribution of parliamentary power. It was a change in the balance within the governing alliance, and not a replacement of the alliance.

The geography of the result was uneven. The largest BJP losses occurred in Uttar Pradesh, where it fell from 62 seats to 33 and the Samajwadi Party, in alliance with Congress, won 37, and in Rajasthan, Haryana, Maharashtra, and West Bengal. Analysts attributed the Uttar Pradesh result to a combination of unemployment, anger over the Agnipath scheme in recruitment regions, caste coalitions among Other Backward Classes and Dalits, and a shift of some Dalit voters away from the BJP over constitutional worries; these explanations remain subject to survey-based analysis. The BJP gained elsewhere: it won almost all the seats in Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, and Delhi, formed a government in Odisha for the first time, and with its allies swept Andhra Pradesh, where the TDP, Jana Sena, and BJP formed a coalition. It also won its first Lok Sabha seat in Kerala. In the south, the BJP's presence grew in vote share in some states, but its principal opponents remained strong in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Karnataka.

The election should be interpreted as a redistribution of power within a continuing governing arrangement and as evidence that voters could constrain a dominant party. It should not be treated as a wholesale replacement of the governing alliance nor as proof that the BJP's long-term position had collapsed, as later state elections, discussed in Section 100.18, showed.

Section 100.18

Modi's Third Government: Coalition Bargaining, Federalism, and Governance, 2024–2026

The third Modi government was formed on 9 June 2024 with Modi as prime minister and a ministry in which the BJP held the major portfolios, including home, finance, defence, and external affairs, while allies received a number of ministries. The government's parliamentary base was the NDA's 293 seats. Among the allies, the Telugu Desam Party (TDP) of N. Chandrababu Naidu won 16 seats and the Janata Dal (United) of Nitish Kumar won 12; both therefore became the most consequential partners, followed by the Shiv Sena of Eknath Shinde with seven and the Lok Janshakti Party (Ram Vilas) with five. Their support was essential: without either the TDP or the JD(U), together with other smaller parties, the NDA would have been much closer to the majority line.

Both parties had interests tied to their states. The TDP, which formed a coalition government in Andhra Pradesh in June 2024, sought financial support for the planned capital at Amaravati and the Polavaram irrigation project, and Bihar, governed by the JD(U) in alliance with the BJP, sought special assistance for infrastructure. The Union budget of July 2024 included substantial allocations for both states, which opposition parties characterised as a price of coalition support and which the government defended as part of normal development policy. Historians should not assume that every budgetary decision resulted from coalition pressure, but the pattern of allocation provides evidence of the bargaining.

The coalition's influence on policy is clearest where the government altered or slowed proposals. A bill to reform the law on waqf properties was referred to a joint parliamentary committee in August 2024 after objections, including from allies, and the Waqf (Amendment) Act was passed in April 2025 with support from the TDP and JD(U) following revisions. Its constitutionality was challenged in the Supreme Court. On 15 September 2025 the Court issued an interim order: it declined to suspend the entire Act but stayed or restricted the operation of particular provisions pending further adjudication, including aspects of the five-year religious-practice requirement and property-status determinations. An interim ruling does not conclusively settle the constitutional merits. The judgment itself is available in the Documentation and References section. A proposal for simultaneous national and state elections, introduced in December 2024, was referred to a committee, and a scheme of lateral entry into senior civil-service posts advertised in 2024 was withdrawn after criticism from allies and others. Such episodes suggest that the government was more responsive to partners and the opposition than in 2019, but they do not demonstrate that coalition dependence caused every adjustment; internal government calculations, court rulings, and public criticism also played roles.

The government's political position was also shaped by state elections after 2024. The BJP won Haryana in October 2024, against many predictions, and its Mahayuti alliance prevailed in Maharashtra in November 2024. The BJP won the Delhi assembly election in February 2025, ending the Aam Aadmi Party's rule in the capital, and the NDA won the Bihar assembly election later in 2025. In April 2026 assembly voting took place in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Assam, and Puducherry, with the principal results declared on 4 May; one West Bengal constituency, Falta, required a later poll on 21 May. In West Bengal the BJP displaced the Trinamool Congress and formed the state government for the first time; in Assam the BJP retained power. In Kerala the Congress-led United Democratic Front won a majority. Tamil Nadu experienced a different realignment: the actor Vijay's Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam won 108 of 234 seats and became the largest single party, although it fell short of the 118-seat majority threshold. In Puducherry the AINRC–BJP alliance retained government. These are verified electoral outcomes, not yet a settled explanation of voters' motives. The results indicate continued BJP strength in important regions alongside enduring regional, coalition and newly founded parties. Seat totals should be checked against final Election Commission statistical reports, rather than election-night projections.

Compared with its predecessors, the third government operated in a different setting. The first Modi government (2014–19) had a BJP majority and a favourable economy and could legislate with minimal negotiation; the second (2019–24) had a larger majority and used it for constitutional initiatives. The third had a smaller BJP contingent, a stronger and more energetic opposition led in the Lok Sabha by Rahul Gandhi as Leader of the Opposition, a post vacant since 2014, and a requirement for coalition management. Yet the government retained firm control of the executive, a united party, and stable allies, and the opposition remained divided in many states, with several INDIA partners contesting against Congress. Parliamentary stability continued, and elections remained the arena in which the balance of power could shift.

Historical Vocabulary
Coalition Dependence

Coalition dependence describes a situation in which the leading party in a governing alliance cannot command a parliamentary majority without the votes of partner parties. It differs from the existence of a coalition: the NDA was a formal alliance throughout 2014–2024, but the BJP's own majority meant allies were not needed to pass legislation. In 2024 that changed. The term matters because it identifies a change in bargaining power, not a change in the governing alliance, and it should not be used to assume that partners determine every policy.

Section 100.19

India through 2026: Welfare Reform, Population Policy, Development, and Unresolved Challenges

Historical cutoff: 19 November 2026. This section describes developments close to that date. They are best understood as ongoing processes, and the descriptions below rest on official documents and contemporary reporting, not on scholarly assessment.

The government's declared priorities included employment generation, infrastructure investment, industrial modernisation, and technology policy under the long-term slogan of a “Viksit Bharat,” or developed India, by 2047. Measures included production-linked incentives for manufacturing, public capital expenditure, a focus on semiconductors and electronics assembly, and, in the 2025 budget, income-tax relief for middle-income taxpayers. The September 2025 GST rationalisation, discussed in Section 100.10, reduced rates on many goods. Public finance, in which the Union government pursued a path of gradual fiscal consolidation after the pandemic, remained constrained by interest payments and debt, and the relationship between Union and states over shares of revenue was reconsidered by the Sixteenth Finance Commission.

The most significant social-policy change concerned rural employment. Parliament enacted the Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 (No. 36 of 2025, the VB–G RAM G Act), which received presidential assent on 20 December 2025. A subsequent notification brought it into force nationally on 1 July 2026 and repealed the 2005 MGNREGA framework. Section 5 sets a statutory entitlement of not less than 125 days of guaranteed wage employment in a financial year for eligible rural households whose adult members volunteer for unskilled manual work, compared with the former statutory guarantee of not less than 100 days. A legal guarantee must be distinguished from the number of days of employment actually delivered. Section 22 establishes central–state cost sharing of 60:40 for most states with legislatures and 90:10 for the specified northeastern and Himalayan jurisdictions; Union territories without a legislature are centrally financed. It also uses state-wise normative allocations, with specified expenditures above an allocation falling to the state. Section 6 requires states to notify, in advance, an aggregate 60-day period covering peak sowing and harvesting seasons during which work under the Act shall not be undertaken, with scope for different local schedules. Supporters argued that the Act extends the legal guarantee while linking works to rural infrastructure and livelihood planning. Critics questioned the fiscal shift, the allocation rules, the interruption of work during agricultural seasons, and the removal of Mahatma Gandhi's name. These competing assessments must be evaluated against the statutory wording and later implementation data, not treated as established results by November 2026.

The census is a second major development. The census due in 2021 was postponed following the pandemic. On 30 April 2025 the government announced that the forthcoming census would include caste enumeration, renewing a long-running argument about caste statistics and representation. Census 2027 is a two-phase, digitally administered exercise with a self-enumeration option. The houselisting and housing phase ran on staggered schedules from April through September 2026 and, according to the Home Ministry's 16 November update, was complete in 35 states and Union territories, except Manipur. Contrary to a single nationwide February 2027 timetable, population enumeration had already begun in selected areas before this chapter's cutoff: field enumeration was scheduled for September 2026 in Ladakh and designated snow-bound areas of Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, with an October 2026 reference date. From 16 November 2026 self-enumeration was operating in Goa, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh and the non-snow-bound areas of Uttarakhand, ahead of their house-to-house count from 1 December 2026 through 4 January 2027, with a 5 January reference date. For most other jurisdictions, the notified self-enumeration window was 10–24 January 2027, and house-to-house enumeration was scheduled for 25 January–28 February, with a 1 March reference date; Manipur remained subject to a separate notification. As of 19 November 2026 the later field operations had not occurred, but the population-enumeration phase was already under way in parts of the country. How the new caste data will be interpreted and used for policy, including reservations, remains unresolved. These developments extend the issues examined in Chapter 99, including reliance on the 1931 caste census and the implications of Bihar's 2023 state-level survey.

The census is also linked to unresolved questions of representation. The women's reservation amendment of 2023 is to take effect after the census and a subsequent delimitation of constituencies, so that it cannot apply before the post-2027 timetable. Delimitation raises the further question of how seats are apportioned among states with different rates of population growth; southern states fear a loss of relative influence, while northern states with larger populations stand to gain. These are scheduled or proposed matters and should not be presented as settled.

Other challenges are evident. Youth employment remains the central economic problem: a large number of young people enter the labour market each year, and the creation of productive formal jobs has not matched this. Regional disparities in income, health, and education remain large. Climate-related stress, including heat and water scarcity, affects agriculture and cities (see Chapter 109). The balance between Union and state authority continues to be contested through taxation, centrally sponsored schemes, governors, and the delimitation question. And the relationship between economic growth and social well-being, as expressed in nutrition, health, and learning outcomes, remains a matter of evidence and debate. Scheduled events, such as the 2027 enumeration and forecasts of economic rankings, should not be presented as outcomes.

Section 100.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

Between 2004 and 2026, India changed under two political formations, and five long-term transformations organise the evidence of this chapter. The first is the development of welfare rights and delivery systems. The UPA created statutory entitlements to information, work, forest rights, education, and food; the NDA built delivery systems of bank accounts, identity numbers, direct transfers, and national schemes, and in 2026 replaced the rural-employment statute with a new framework. The two models were neither opposed nor identical. Direct benefit transfers began under the UPA, and the NDA preserved the food-security law and expanded free grain. The common feature was the growth of the state's role in household security, a growth measured by the rise of programmes and budgets, though the quality of delivery varied by state and by social group.

The second transformation is the expansion of economic and technological capacity. Growth, with interruptions, remained high; telecommunications, digital identity, and payments created infrastructure on which businesses and households relied; highways, electrification, and logistics improved. Yet the distribution of benefits was uneven, employment lagged, and large parts of the workforce remained informal. Capacity and access were not the same thing, and the pandemic made the difference visible.

The third is the transition from Congress-led coalition government to BJP-majority government and renewed alliance dependence. The UPA governed through negotiation among allies and with outside Left support. The BJP in 2014 and 2019 achieved a single-party majority for the first time in three decades, and in 2024 it lost that majority while the NDA retained power. The sequence demonstrates the flexibility of the electoral system and the capacity of voters to alter the balance of power without ending the government.

The fourth concerns executive authority and democratic institutions. After 2014 executive leadership became more centralised and personalised; major decisions, including demonetization, the lockdown, and the farm laws, were announced by the prime minister; constitutional changes followed in 2019. Courts, states, protest movements, and elections nevertheless constrained government, as the repeal of the farm laws, the electoral-bond judgment, and the 2024 result showed. Whether the period strengthened effective government or weakened safeguards depends on the institution examined, and historians have not reached agreement.

The fifth is the changing expectation of citizens. A growing, more connected, more educated population expected jobs, services, and accountability, expressed through demands for transparency, anti-corruption action, welfare, and representation. The politics of caste, in the demand for a census count, and of gender, in women's reservation, were part of these expectations, as were disputes about religion and citizenship. Citizens were not merely recipients of policy; they mobilised in movements from the Lokpal agitation to the farmers' encampments and expressed preferences in elections.

The continuities between the UPA and NDA governments are as important as the differences. Both pursued market-oriented growth with state-led welfare; both expanded digital administration; both faced the challenge of employment; both governed a federal system in which states implemented most social policy. The differences lay in political organisation, since Congress governed as the centre of a diverse coalition and the BJP as a cadre-based party with a disciplined organisation and an ideological programme; in ideology, since the BJP emphasised cultural nationalism and the UPA emphasised secular and rights-based language; in administrative style; and in economic emphasis.

The central question was how successive governments transformed India and what 2024 revealed about the capacity of Indian democracy to alter the distribution of power. The answer is that governments changed the institutions of welfare, finance, identity, and administration, altered the constitutional landscape in several respects, and presided over unprecedented digital and infrastructural expansion, and that the electorate retained the ability to reduce a dominant party's majority and require it to bargain, while in other contests, including state elections in 2025 and 2026, voters favoured the party. Democratic competition thus coexisted with a strong ruling party, which is a more accurate description than either triumph or decline.

India's twenty-first-century transformation cannot be understood only through national leaders. State governments implemented welfare and contested fiscal authority; firms and entrepreneurs built the economy; farmers, workers, students, and women's groups shaped policy through protest; courts and the Election Commission set limits; and households adapted to new technologies and uncertainties. The history of this period is therefore also a history of regional diversity, from Kerala's public health to Punjab's procurement politics and Bihar's migration. The chapters that follow place India in its region. Chapters 101 to 104 examine Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the Himalayan and Indian Ocean states; Chapter 105 returns to Kashmir and security; Chapter 106 to the region's economic and urban transformation; and Chapters 107 to 111 to society, culture, environment, international relations, and historical memory.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined India's transformation from 2004 to 19 November 2026 under the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance and the BJP-led National Democratic Alliance. Its central argument is that the period combined continuity in economic expansion, welfare growth, and technological modernisation with significant changes in political leadership, executive style, constitutional controversy, and the relationship between government and society.

The UPA came to power in 2004 after an election in which the Vajpayee government's “India Shining” campaign failed to persuade many voters, particularly in rural areas and in states where regional alliances favoured Congress. Manmohan Singh became prime minister, Sonia Gandhi held the party's leadership, and the Left provided outside support until 2008. The coalition's character shaped its policy, and the National Advisory Council contributed to the drafting of welfare legislation.

The UPA's most distinctive achievement was a series of rights-based laws: the Right to Information Act, the rural employment guarantee, the Forest Rights Act, the Right to Education Act, and the Food Security Act. They recast welfare as legal entitlement, though implementation depended on states and on administrative capacity and varied widely. In parallel, the economy grew rapidly, a new middle class emerged, and globalisation and telecommunications changed daily life, while most workers remained in agriculture or the informal sector.

UPA-II, elected with a stronger mandate in 2009, was weakened by slowing growth, high inflation, controversies over spectrum, the Commonwealth Games, and coal allocation, the anti-corruption movement of 2011, and a perception of indecision. The controversies had different legal outcomes, and students must distinguish audit estimates, court rulings, and criminal verdicts. These conditions prepared the BJP's victory in 2014, when Narendra Modi led the party to 282 seats, the first single-party majority in three decades.

The Modi governments after 2014 centralised executive leadership around the Prime Minister's Office, replaced the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog, and emphasised delivery through bank accounts, Aadhaar, direct transfers, and national schemes, while building on earlier programmes. They expanded infrastructure and digital public systems, including UPI. Policy experiments such as demonetization in 2016 and the Goods and Services Tax in 2017 produced lasting debate about objectives, costs, and federal fiscal relations.

The 2019 election gave the BJP 303 seats and a renewed majority, which the government used for major constitutional and legal initiatives, including the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, whose rules were notified in 2024, and the changes in the status of Jammu and Kashmir. These measures generated protest, litigation, and sharp argument over secularism, citizenship, and federalism. The COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 imposed a severe test: the abrupt lockdown, the hardship of migrant workers, the second wave of 2021, and disputed mortality estimates exposed weaknesses in public health and social protection, while vaccine production showed capacity. The farm laws of 2020 and the year-long protest ending in repeal in 2021 demonstrated the limits of executive initiative.

Debates about democratic institutions centred on Parliament, courts, election administration, investigative agencies, the media, civil society, and political finance. The Supreme Court's 2024 electoral-bond judgment and the 2023 women's reservation amendment illustrate both constraints on and initiatives of the government. The question of whether the period strengthened effective government, weakened safeguards, or both remains contested.

In 2024 the BJP won 240 seats and the NDA 293, so that the party, though not the alliance, lost its majority; the BJP governed with dependence on the TDP, JD(U), and other allies. Subsequent state elections, including in 2026, showed continued BJP strength in several regions alongside the strength of regional and new parties. By November 2026 the VB-G RAM G Act had replaced MGNREGA, and Census 2027, including caste enumeration and a population count staggered across September 2026–February 2027, was under way. The long-term effects of these developments cannot yet be judged.

The chapter's synthesis identifies five long-term transformations: welfare rights and delivery systems, economic and technological capacity, the change from coalition to majority to renewed alliance dependence, executive authority and democratic institutions, and changing citizen expectations. It argues that India's twenty-first-century history cannot be explained solely by national leaders, and it leads to the country-specific and regional chapters that follow.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it covers the period in which the history in this textbook becomes the present. The questions it raises, about the relation between growth and well-being, about entitlement and delivery, about executive power and institutional limits, are being decided as this is written, and the tools of historical judgment, careful distinction among kinds of evidence, attention to the date and source of a claim, and refusal to treat intention as outcome, are tools students need now.

The chapter also reveals continuities with earlier ones. The coalition politics of Chapter 98 gave way to single-party dominance and back again, as the Congress dominance traced in Chapter 88 gave way to competitive multiparty politics. Debates about federal authority recall the reorganisation of states discussed in Chapter 89. Disputes over caste and reservation return to Chapter 99. The census, caste and all, links the colonial classification of Chapter 73 with the politics of the twenty-first century.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter shows India as one among several South Asian states with distinct trajectories. It does not end a story but points to its regional continuation. The chapters that follow examine Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives, and then the shared themes of security, economy, society, environment, and memory, in which India's history is bound to that of its neighbours.

Chapter 101 now turns to Pakistan from the 1990s to the present.
