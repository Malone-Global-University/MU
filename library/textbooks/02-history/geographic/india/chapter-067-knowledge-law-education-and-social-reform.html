Knowledge, Law, Education, and Social Reform

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1776 an English translation appeared in London of a body of law that no single Indian community had ever possessed in exactly that form. Its Sanskrit original had been compiled by a group of learned pandits at the request of the Company's governor in Bengal; their text was read aloud and rendered into Persian, because no Company official could yet read Sanskrit; and the Persian version was then turned into English by a young Company servant, Nathaniel Brassey Halhed. The book called itself A Code of Gentoo Laws. It was a landmark of scholarship and an instrument of government at once, and the layered process behind it, with Indian expertise at every stage and European authority at the end, is a compact picture of the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 66 examined how Company rule restructured land, agriculture, and commerce. This chapter turns from the colonial economy to the intellectual and social foundations of colonial government. A state that collected revenue, decided property disputes, punished crimes, and educated its own servants needed to know the languages, laws, and customs of the people it ruled. It also needed to decide what kinds of knowledge it would sponsor and what kinds of society it wished to see. The Company's answers to these questions shaped scholarship, legal categories, schools, printing, and the terms on which reform was argued across roughly the period 1772 to 1857.

The chapter's governing claim is that this was not a one-way transfer of European ideas into a passive India. South Asia already possessed sophisticated traditions of grammar, law, philosophy, medicine, astronomy, and pedagogy, carried by pandits, maulavis, munshis, teachers, and thousands of village schoolmasters. The Company relied on these specialists, sometimes honored them, sometimes dismissed them, and often reshaped their knowledge by translating, selecting, and codifying it. Indian intellectuals in turn used the institutions the Company created, including colleges, presses, courts, and petitions, to pursue projects of their own, which sometimes coincided with British aims and often did not.

Three sets of relationships run through the chapter. The first is between Orientalist scholars, who valued classical Indian learning and sponsored its study, and Anglicists, who argued that public funds should promote English and European science. The second is between the colonial state and Indian reformers such as Raja Rammohun Roy, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, and Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule, whose arguments drew on Indian scripture, reason, and experience. The third is among South Asians themselves: reformers, defenders of established custom, teachers of traditional learning, converts, students of the new colleges, and communities for whom none of these debates offered much practical change. Reform, resistance to reform, and resistance to colonial authority were different things, and the chapter keeps them distinct.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partial. Printed codes, minutes, despatches, regulations, statutes, mission reports, newspapers, and letters survive in quantity, but nearly all were produced by the literate, the official, or the privileged. Women, laborers, communities subjected to untouchability, and rural families appear mostly through the descriptions of others. Historians reconstruct their experience by reading official and elite sources against the grain, and this textbook asks students to do the same, noting at each stage whose voice is present and whose is not.

The chapter's geography is concentrated but not limited. Calcutta, the capital of Company India, was the hub of Orientalist institutions, missionary printing at nearby Serampore, Hindu College, and the Brahmo movement. But Benares, Delhi, Bombay, Madras, Pune, and Tranquebar all mattered, and the Phules' work in Pune shows that social reform did not originate only in Bengal. Treating Bengal's urban intelligentsia as representative of the whole subcontinent is one of the errors the chapter is designed to avoid.

The chronology is similarly layered. The Orientalist enterprise began under Warren Hastings in the 1770s and 1780s. The educational and legal debates reached decisive moments in 1813, 1833–35, and 1854. The great reform controversies over sati (1829) and widow remarriage (1856) bracket the period's most visible legislation. The universities of 1857 and the Penal Code of 1860 fall at or beyond the end of Company rule, and the chapter marks that boundary carefully, because the draft code of 1837 and the enacted code of 1860 are not the same event.

By the end of this chapter students should be able to explain how colonial power and South Asian intellectual agency shaped one another, and why the resulting changes expanded opportunities for some people while reinforcing, or newly creating, inequalities for others. Chapter 68 then examines the forms of resistance to Company authority that unfolded alongside these developments, setting the stage for the rebellion of 1857 discussed in Chapter 69.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the East India Company required linguistic, legal, and geographic knowledge to govern, and how it depended on existing South Asian scholarly traditions.
  2. Describe the work of the Asiatick Society and British Orientalists, and identify the Indian scholars whose expertise made that work possible.
  3. Analyze how surveys, archives, printing, and classification shaped colonial representations of territory, religion, and society before 1857.
  4. Trace the translation chain behind Halhed's Code of Gentoo Laws and assess how Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan law reorganized legal concepts.
  5. Distinguish the 1837 draft penal code from the Indian Penal Code of 1860 and explain the tension between uniform criminal law and differentiated personal law.
  6. Compare Orientalist and Anglicist positions in the educational controversy, and distinguish Macaulay's Minute (2 February 1835) from Bentinck's resolution (7 March 1835).
  7. Evaluate the ambitions and limits of Wood's Despatch of 1854 and the presidency universities of 1857.
  8. Assess the roles of Christian missionaries, Indian printers, and translators in the expansion of schooling, printing, and vernacular literature.
  9. Interpret the emergence of an urban colonial public sphere, including its press restrictions and its exclusions.
  10. Contextualize Raja Rammohun Roy, the Brahmo Sabha, and their intellectual sources without reducing them to imitations of European thought.
  11. Explain the debates over sati and widow remarriage, distinguish legal change from social change, and connect both to gender and property.
  12. Evaluate the evidence for women's education, caste, and marginalized communities, and differentiate reform, resistance to reform, and resistance to colonial authority.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a commercial corporation turned territorial government come to invest in scholarship, translation, and the codification of law?
  2. How did the expertise of Indian pandits, maulavis, and munshis shape what European scholars thought they had discovered?
  3. In what ways did translating Indian legal and religious texts into European categories clarify, and in what ways distort, the societies they described?
  4. Was the Orientalist–Anglicist controversy a dispute about education, about language, or about the legitimacy of Indian intellectual traditions?
  5. Why did government educational policy reach so few people in this period, and who was systematically excluded?
  6. How did missionaries, printers, and newspaper editors create new publics, and how did Indians use these new media for their own purposes?
  7. What intellectual traditions informed Rammohun Roy's arguments, and why is it misleading to call him simply a Westernizer?
  8. How did the legal prohibition of sati and the legalization of widow remarriage differ in their origins, in the opposition they met, and in their social effects?
  9. Whose interests did reform movements represent, and how did caste and gender limit the reach of elite reform?
  10. How should historians distinguish reform, resistance to reform, and resistance to colonial rule when the same individuals could take different positions on each?

Historical Context

By the 1770s the East India Company had become, in effect, a territorial power in Bengal. As discussed in Chapters 62 and 63, the grant of the diwani in 1765 gave it control of revenue collection, and the Regulating Act of 1773 and the administrative experiments of Warren Hastings and Lord Cornwallis created a court system, a revenue bureaucracy, and a military-fiscal state. Chapter 66 showed how land settlements and commercial policy reshaped rural society. This chapter asks what the Company's rulers needed to know in order to govern, and what happened to South Asian knowledge when it entered the Company's offices and courts.

The Company's servants arrived in a region with thick traditions of learning. Persian had been the language of Mughal administration and remained the language of the Company's courts and diplomacy for decades. Sanskrit scholarship flourished at centers such as Nadia and Benares, with specialized schools called tols. Arabic and Persian learning was taught in madrasas, and regional-language instruction in pathshalas and maktabs reached many boys in villages and towns, though far fewer girls. Law was plural: it combined Islamic jurisprudence, Sanskrit legal texts such as the dharmashastras and their commentaries, regional and caste custom, and the discretionary justice of rulers and local assemblies. The judicial plan Hastings introduced in 1772 directed that Hindu law and Muslim law should govern Hindus and Muslims respectively in matters such as inheritance, marriage, and caste, a decision that made religious identity a legal category in new ways.

Company rule was also shaped by contest inside Britain. Evangelical Christians, utilitarians, and liberal reformers pressed for moral and intellectual "improvement" of India, while many officials, with an eye to political stability, were wary of provoking religious controversy. Officials were divided among themselves, and these divisions appear repeatedly in this chapter. The Company's Charter Acts of 1813 and 1833 renewed its charter and restructured its authority, and both contained provisions on education, religion, and law that framed the debates examined below.

The evidence for this chapter comes from published codes, translations, parliamentary papers, mission records, school reports, newspapers, private correspondence, and the writings of Indian reformers. Students should keep three cautions in mind. Official documents record what the government intended and justified, not necessarily what happened on the ground. Translations are interpretations. And the "reformers" prominent in the sources were a small, literate, mostly male, mostly urban group. They were important but never represented the whole of South Asian society, whose rural and laboring majority, women, and subordinated castes experienced these years largely as changes in taxation, courts, and employment rather than as intellectual debates.

Section 67.1

Knowledge and Colonial Government: Languages, Institutions, and the Problem of Understanding India

The Company that acquired Bengal in the 1760s was staffed by merchants and soldiers who, for the most part, could not read the documents on which the country's administration depended. Revenue accounts, land records, judicial opinions, and diplomatic correspondence were written in Persian, in regional languages such as Bengali, and in specialist scripts and shorthand used by clerks and accountants. To collect taxes, make treaties, and decide lawsuits, Company officials had to find people who could read, write, and interpret these materials, and they found them in the existing scribal and scholarly classes of Mughal and successor-state administration.

Those intermediaries had many titles. Munshis were Persian secretaries and language teachers, often from families of Kayastha or Muslim service gentry that had staffed Mughal and regional courts for generations. Pandits were scholars trained in Sanskrit grammar, philosophy, and law. Maulavis and qazis were learned in Arabic and Persian and in Islamic jurisprudence. Alongside them worked translators, accountants, record-keepers, and village headmen. The Company did not replace these specialists. It hired them, paid them, and made its own decisions depend on their reading of texts and customs.

This dependence created both opportunity and risk. For Indian scholars it meant new patronage at a time when older courts were losing wealth and authority. It also gave them power as gatekeepers of information that their employers could not easily verify. Company officials were well aware of that power, and complaints about corrupt or unreliable interpreters recur in the records. A good deal of what looks like European "discovery" of Indian law and literature was in fact a reorganization of knowledge supplied by these experts.

Knowledge also served administration directly. Taxation required surveys of land and revenue histories. Judicial decision-making required knowledge of which text or custom applied to which litigant. Diplomacy required fluency in Persianate court etiquette. Yet knowledge also allowed officials to classify, and classification was never neutral. To decide that a dispute fell under "Hindu law" or "Muslim law" was to assume that each body of law was a coherent, identifiable system applicable to a defined community, and that assumption simplified a landscape in which custom, caste practice, and regional tradition often mattered more than a single textual authority.

It is therefore important not to imagine that India's intellectual life began when Europeans started studying it. Sanskrit grammarians had analyzed language with extraordinary rigor since antiquity, as Volume I of this series showed. Commentarial traditions on law and philosophy stretched across a millennium. Persian and Arabic scholarship had its own historians, lexicographers, and jurists, and regional literatures in Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Urdu, and other languages had thriving audiences. What the Company introduced was not learning as such but a new set of purposes, institutions, and hierarchies for learning, all operating under political power that Indian scholars did not control.

The remainder of Division I traces how these purposes shaped scholarship (§67.2), the recording of territory and society (§67.3), and the construction of the legal categories that structured colonial courts (§67.4).

An important distinction was between knowledge possessed by a scholar and the authority to use it. A court pandit could explain the reasoning behind several possible rules, but the judge decided which opinion would govern the case and enter the record as precedent. Likewise, a Persian accountant could identify a local usage, while the collector determined whether it fitted an official revenue category. This division of labor gave Indian intermediaries substantial practical influence without equal control over the institutions employing them. Their salaries, positions, and ability to secure continued patronage also depended on officials whose own command of the languages was often incomplete. Knowledge production therefore involved negotiations over employment and credibility as well as intellectual disagreement. An analysis of colonial archives must ask not only what information was recorded, but who was allowed to certify it and whose interpretations were excluded from the final administrative order.

Section 67.2

British Orientalism: William Jones, the Asiatic Society, and the Study of Indian Civilization

The term Orientalism has two meanings that students must keep apart. In the period of this chapter it described a school of scholarship and a policy preference: the study of Asian languages and literatures and the belief that the Company should govern India through, and patronize, its own learned traditions. In later critical scholarship, especially since Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), the word has also come to describe the broader European habit of representing Asian societies as fixed, exotic, and different from an active, rational West. This chapter uses the term in the first sense and discusses the second in the Historical Debate below.

Orientalism as a policy had a champion in Warren Hastings, who argued that governing Indians by their own laws required understanding them and that scholarly patronage would win the loyalty of the learned classes. It found its intellectual leader in Sir William Jones, who arrived in Calcutta in 1783 as a judge of the Supreme Court. Jones, already known as a linguist, studied Sanskrit with Indian teachers, and on 15 January 1784, he and a group of Company servants founded the Asiatick Society in Calcutta, devoted to inquiry into the history, antiquities, arts, sciences, and literature of Asia. Hastings was invited to be its patron. Its early membership was overwhelmingly European, and Indian scholars, despite their indispensable contributions, long participated mainly as assistants and informants rather than as members.

The scholarly achievements were genuine. Charles Wilkins translated the Bhagavad Gita (1785). Jones translated Kalidasa's play Shakuntala (1789), a work that deeply impressed readers in Europe, and wrote on Indian music, botany, and law. Henry Thomas Colebrooke produced pioneering studies of Sanskrit grammar, philosophy, mathematics, and law. Jones's famous observation that Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and other languages shared a common source helped launch comparative philology, a development whose long and sometimes troubled aftermath for ideas about "Aryan" origins students encountered in Chapter 5. The Society's journal, Asiatick Researches, circulated findings in Britain and Europe.

Behind every translation stood Indian scholars. Jones studied with pandits in Bengal. Colebrooke relied on the learned Jagannatha Tarkapanchanana for the legal digest he translated. Persian munshis and Muslim scholars supplied manuscripts, explanations, and corrections. Persian-language scholar Mirza Abu Taleb Khan and others traveled and wrote their own accounts, and Indian engagement with European learning in this period was real if less visible in the published record. The scholarship was collaborative, but credit and authority were distributed unequally.

The Orientalists' respect for classical learning had political and intellectual limits. They concentrated on Sanskrit and Persian texts, on the "classical" past, and on the learned elite who preserved it, and they often treated contemporary practice as a degraded survival of an earlier golden age. Translating texts as though they stood for living custom could obscure regional and popular traditions. Nor was Orientalist patronage disinterested: officials who sponsored scholarship expected it to help in governing. Even so, Orientalists preserved manuscripts, trained translators, and created institutions that gave Indian learning a recognized place in colonial government, a recognition that their Anglicist critics would later challenge.

The circulation of Orientalist scholarship created consequences beyond its original administrative purpose. Translations enabled readers outside South Asia to encounter texts previously difficult to access, while the prestige conferred on selected works could influence arguments among Indian scholars about education and religious authority. At the same time, European editions were often presented without an account of their Indian teachers, manuscript variants, or the decisions required to choose one reading. Sanskrit learning was not a single intellectual system: different schools contested grammar, logic, philosophy, law, and ritual. Persianate scholarship likewise included histories, poetry, statecraft, and theological controversy, much of which escaped the narrow interests of administrators. Students should therefore compare the richness of the traditions with the small, curated portion that entered English publication. What appeared in Europe as an authoritative description of India was usually an artifact of selection, collaboration, translation, and an institutional audience.

Historical Vocabulary
Orientalism and Anglicism

Orientalism, in the context of Company policy, referred to the view that British rule should patronize Sanskrit, Persian, and Arabic scholarship and govern through Indian legal and literary traditions. Anglicism referred to the opposing view that public funds should promote English-language instruction and European literature and science. The labels describe policy factions, not nationalities or levels of respect for India: some Orientalists were deeply condescending, and some Anglicists argued from genuine, if paternalistic, ideas of improvement, while Indian advocates of English education had motives of their own.

In later scholarship, "Orientalism" also names a critical concept: the claim that Western scholarship constructed an image of the East that supported domination. Students should note which sense is being used in any given source.

Section 67.3

Colonial Knowledge Production: Surveys, Archives, Printing, and the Classification of Society

Orientalist scholarship was only one part of the colonial effort to know India. A second was practical and administrative: mapping, surveying, collecting, recording, and counting. The Company needed to know where its territory lay, how land was held and what it produced, who collected revenue, and how many people lived where. Out of these needs grew an expanding archive of reports, registers, maps, and manuscripts.

Geographical knowledge came first. James Rennell's surveys of Bengal in the 1760s and 1770s produced the first detailed maps of the Company's territory. The Great Trigonometrical Survey, begun in 1802 under William Lambton near Madras, aimed to measure the subcontinent with precision by extending a chain of triangles across it; under George Everest and his successors, the survey continued for decades. It rested on Indian labor: surveyors, calculators, chain-bearers, and mathematicians, among them men such as Radhanath Sikdar, whose later work as a computer is well known, and countless uncredited assistants. Regional surveys also generated descriptive knowledge. Francis Buchanan's surveys of eastern India in the early nineteenth century recorded agriculture, crafts, castes, and antiquities, and Colin Mackenzie assembled a vast collection of manuscripts, inscriptions, and local histories with the help of Indian assistants, notably Kavali Venkata Borayya and his brothers.

Printing and the archive mattered too. The Company produced regulations, reports, and gazetteers, and it collected manuscripts into libraries at Fort William College and elsewhere. Dictionaries and grammars standardized languages that had been highly variable in oral and manuscript practice; translating, printing, and teaching a standard Bengali or Hindustani prose helped shape what later generations treated as "the language." Statistical inquiries, such as local population counts and revenue surveys, began to quantify society, but they were uneven and local. The systematic all-India census belongs to the later nineteenth century and is discussed in Chapter 73; it should not be read back into the period before 1857.

The central historical question is what such knowledge did. It enabled administration, and it often reflected careful observation and Indian expertise. But it also required decisions about categories: which groups counted as a "caste," which customs counted as "law," which rulers counted as legitimate, which religious communities were distinct. Officials tended to prefer clear, textually grounded categories, and they often treated Brahmanical texts as the key to the whole society. Local realities of overlapping religious practice, fluid occupational groups, and negotiated authority were harder to record and so were often simplified. Once recorded in official reports, simplified categories could take on a life of their own, shaping policy and, eventually, shaping how communities understood themselves.

This is not to say colonial knowledge invented Indian social categories wholesale. Caste, religious community, and custom existed long before 1757, and Indian informants contributed to every report. The better view, developed in later sections and in the Historical Debate, is that colonial recording interacted with preexisting hierarchies, sometimes hardening them and sometimes opening them to new forms of competition and claim.

The time and expense required to produce administrative knowledge also matter. Survey teams depended on seasonal conditions, local guides, the willingness of rulers and villagers to cooperate, and the survival of earlier records. A boundary line drawn on a Company map might conceal disputed grazing access, seasonal cultivation, or overlapping rights to forest products and water. Similarly, a table of occupations might compress a household's several livelihoods into one permanent classification. A record could be reliable for a particular administrative question but misleading when later historians treated it as a complete description of society. The Great Trigonometrical Survey supplied precise measurements of terrain; it did not on its own explain political allegiance or communal identity. Students studying the archive should distinguish the accuracy of a measurement from the interpretation attached to it and look for evidence of local negotiation, correction, or refusal.

Section 67.4

Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan Law: Translation, Custom, and Colonial Legal Knowledge

The most consequential application of colonial knowledge was in law. Following the judicial plan of 1772, Company courts were to apply Hindu law to Hindus and Muslim law to Muslims in matters of inheritance, marriage, caste, and religious institutions. But British judges could not read the relevant texts, so they relied on court-appointed pandits and maulavis to state the law. This dependence soon seemed unsatisfactory. Officials suspected that these experts might be biased, and they wanted authoritative written sources they could consult directly. The solution was translation.

The first major result was Halhed's A Code of Gentoo Laws, published in 1776. At Warren Hastings's direction a group of pandits compiled a digest of Sanskrit legal opinion on topics such as inheritance, debt, and contract. Because no Company servant could then read Sanskrit with confidence, the compilation was read out and rendered into Persian, and Halhed translated the Persian into English. The process is a case study in how knowledge changed as it traveled. Meanings shifted at each stage of translation. Texts that offered competing opinions were presented as a unified code. And a compilation arranged by scholars for a patron became, in colonial courts, an authority to be cited.

Later projects followed the pattern with improvements in direct access to Sanskrit. William Jones, convinced that Indian law must be understood in the original, studied Sanskrit and began a digest of Hindu and Muslim law, a project left unfinished at his death in 1794, though his translation of the Manusmriti (the "Institutes of Manu") was published that year. Colebrooke translated a digest compiled by Jagannatha Tarkapanchanana. The emphasis on a handful of authoritative texts, especially the Manusmriti, privileged Brahmanical normative literature and tended to assume that these texts described what Hindus actually did.

Islamic law received similar treatment. Company officials sponsored the translation of the Hanafi legal work al-Hidaya into English, and the Calcutta Madrasa trained maulavis to serve as law officers. In criminal law the Company at first retained Islamic criminal law, modified by its own regulations, a topic treated under court administration in Chapter 63. The result of all these efforts was what scholars call Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan law: bodies of law shaped by textual translation and judicial precedent in Company courts, not simply continuations of precolonial practice.

The distinction between textual prescription, local practice, and judicial outcome is crucial. Dharmashastra texts were scholarly and normative, and their application in village disputes was mediated by custom, caste councils, and local authority. Women's property claims, regional inheritance systems such as Dayabhaga in Bengal and Mitakshara elsewhere, and the customary practices of artisan, pastoral, and tribal communities did not always map onto the authoritative texts. By choosing certain texts as authoritative and enforcing them in courts, the Company made written Brahmanical law more influential, and made litigation, appeal, and precedent central to resolving disputes. Legal knowledge had become an instrument of government, and the question of how much of it should be codified, and how, came to the fore in the 1830s (§67.5).

The emerging legal system also redistributed the costs of obtaining justice. A litigant now needed to navigate petitions, translated testimony, interpreters, court fees, delays, and appeals. Wealthier parties or those familiar with official language could use these procedures to their advantage, even when the underlying substantive rules appeared unchanged. Conversely, an appeal to a written rule could provide a vulnerable litigant with a resource against an adversary who controlled local custom. The outcome depended on the judge, the record, and the available legal expertise. There was no single uniform Anglo-Hindu code binding every community in the same way: textual schools, regional inheritance traditions, customary evidence, and changes in judicial practice continued to matter. The colonial tendency to organize law under large religious headings should therefore be evaluated through actual cases, not simply through the titles of the translated treatises.

Historical Debate

Did Orientalist Scholarship Preserve Indian Intellectual Traditions or Reconstruct Them for Colonial Government?

The question: Was the Orientalist enterprise primarily a scholarly recovery and preservation of Indian learning, or a process that selected, translated, and classified that learning in ways that served colonial rule?

Interpretation A — Preservation and intellectual exchange: Scholars such as David Kopf emphasized that Orientalists collected and preserved manuscripts, trained students, produced grammars and dictionaries, and encouraged Indians to take pride in their classical heritage. On this view Orientalist institutions provided a foundation on which Indian intellectuals could build, and some of them, in Bengal especially, engaged productively with it.
Interpretation B — Selective authority and colonial utility: Critics building on Said's work and on studies by Bernard Cohn, Nicholas Dirks, and others argue that Orientalism privileged classical texts and Brahmanical authority, treated contemporary practice as degenerate, and produced categories that facilitated administration and control. On this view the scholarship was never separable from the political purposes of its patrons.

Evidence: Both sides draw on institutional records, prefaces, translations, and correspondence. Defenders point to manuscript collections and long-term scholarly contributions; critics point to the use of selected texts in courts and to the dependence of Orientalist authority on Company power. Many historians accept elements of both positions and focus on the contributions and constraints of Indian scholars.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the origins of modern Indian ideas about tradition, law, and identity, and whether those ideas were largely inherited, largely colonial, or the product of interaction.

Division II Codification, Education, and the Colonial State Sections 67.5–67.8
Section 67.5

Codification of Law: The Charter Act of 1833, the Law Commission, and Legal Uniformity

By the early 1830s the Company's legal system was a patchwork. As Chapter 63 explained, the presidencies had different regulations, the Supreme Courts in the presidency towns applied English law to British subjects and others, and the Company's own courts in the interior applied regulations, Islamic criminal law as modified by the Company, and personal law drawn from Hindu and Muslim sources. Many officials thought the result was confusing and unpredictable. Utilitarian thinkers, inspired by Jeremy Bentham and James Mill, who had written a major History of British India, argued that clear, systematic, written law would produce predictability and good government.

The Charter Act of 1833 responded by centralizing legislative power in the Governor-General in Council, adding a Law Member to the Council, and directing the creation of a commission to inquire into the laws of India. The same Act declared that no person should be barred from Company office by reason of religion, place of birth, descent, or colour, a principle of formal equality whose real application was far more limited. The First Indian Law Commission was appointed in 1834 with Thomas Babington Macaulay, the first Law Member, as its leading figure.

The Commission's principal product in this period was a draft Indian Penal Code, submitted in 1837. Macaulay and his colleagues argued for a code that was clear, comprehensive, and uniform, replacing the mixture of Islamic criminal law, regulations, and English common law then in force. The draft defined offences and penalties in concise terms and used illustrations to explain them. It was not enacted at once. Criticism, revisions, and administrative caution delayed it for more than two decades, and the Indian Penal Code became law only in 1860, after the end of Company rule, as Act XLV of that year. Students should not date the Code itself to 1837; what belongs to the Company period is the draft and the intellectual project.

Codification also raised a basic tension. A criminal code could be uniform because it rested on the state's power to define and punish crime. Personal law, covering marriage, inheritance, adoption, and religious institutions, was tied to communal identity and religious authority, and the Company had committed itself since 1772 to leaving it to religious norms. The Law Commission accordingly left personal law largely alone, even as it pursued uniformity elsewhere. The consequence was a durable legal structure in which criminal, procedural, and commercial law were increasingly unified, while family law remained differentiated by religion and community. The effect of this arrangement in shaping later political debates over religious identity extends far beyond the period of this chapter.

Other legislation of the period signalled the state's changing willingness to intervene. In 1837 vernacular languages replaced Persian as the language of the lower courts, a change that opened some roles to those literate in regional languages while diminishing the position of Persianate service families. In 1850 the Caste Disabilities Removal Act (Act XXI) provided that a person's change of religion or loss of caste should not deprive him or her of rights of inheritance, a measure with consequences for converts that §67.18 discusses. Law, in short, was both a tool of centralization and a site where social and religious boundaries were redefined.

The creation of the Law Commission did not eliminate the distinction between legislation and enforcement. A proposed code could specify a general rule, but district magistrates, police officers, and the varying jurisdictions of presidency and interior courts determined how people encountered criminal authority. Drafting required compromises over definitions of intention, evidence, and punishment; it also confronted the Company's dependence on judges who were already working within older procedures. The long delay between 1837 and 1860 matters precisely because it prevents students from using the enacted code as a description of the courts of the 1840s. The legal reforms unfolded alongside disputes over public employment and the language of the courts, in which new formal eligibility requirements could displace established clerical families. An official claim of equal treatment must be compared with persistent exclusions from high office and with differences in access to counsel, translation, and appeal.

Historical Vocabulary
Codification and Personal Law

Codification is the process of gathering, systematizing, and enacting law as a single written code. The Law Commission's work aimed at a uniform criminal code. Personal law refers to rules governing family relations, marriage, inheritance, adoption, and religious institutions, which colonial courts applied according to the litigant's religion, as understood by the court.

The distinction matters because the colonial period made the line between "public" uniform law and "private" communal law a central structure of Indian legal life. Students should not assume that either category was fixed or that communities themselves had always defined their family law in such terms.

Section 67.6

Indigenous Education and the Orientalist–Anglicist Controversy, 1781–1835

Before 1835 formal education in South Asia was neither absent nor centrally organized. Boys of many communities learned to read, write, and calculate in village or neighborhood schools, called pathshalas in Bengal and maktabs in Persianate settings, often taught by a single teacher paid in cash, grain, or service. Advanced Sanskrit learning took place in tols run by individual scholars, often with students living in the teacher's household, while Arabic and Persian higher learning took place in madrasas. Professional training, such as accounting or medicine, was often hereditary or apprenticeship-based. Girls' formal schooling was rare, though some girls learned at home, and learned women, courtesans, and members of religious communities sometimes acquired literacy.

Company officials soon began to survey this landscape. Thomas Munro in Madras and Mountstuart Elphinstone in Bombay ordered inquiries into local schooling in the 1820s. In 1835–1838 William Adam reported on education in Bengal and Bihar. His frequently repeated figure of approximately 100,000 indigenous elementary schools was an inference from preliminary materials and assumptions, not a comprehensive census; his later district investigations provide more specific but geographically limited evidence. The schools also varied in curriculum, attendance, and facilities. Adam's reports show that elementary instruction in regional languages reached more people than government institutions, but he also found that it was deeply uneven by caste, class, and gender.

The Company's first educational institutions were Orientalist in design and political in purpose. Warren Hastings founded the Calcutta Madrasa in 1781 to train legal officers for Company courts and to win support from Muslim elites. Jonathan Duncan, the Resident at Benares, established the Sanskrit College there in 1791 for similar reasons. Lord Wellesley founded Fort William College in 1800 to teach newly arrived Company servants Indian languages and law, employing Indian scholars, including Sanskrit pandits and Persian munshis, as teachers and authors of textbooks; its role in developing prose in Hindustani and Bengali is discussed in §67.10. The Calcutta Sanskrit College opened in 1824 under the Company's General Committee of Public Instruction.

The Charter Act of 1813 gave this activity a legislative basis. Among other provisions, it directed that a sum of not less than one lakh of rupees a year, from any surplus in territorial revenues, should be set aside for the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of Indian learned persons, and for the promotion of knowledge of the sciences among the inhabitants of British territories. The wording was vague, and the money was slow to be spent. The Act also opened India more widely to missionary activity (§67.9). The General Committee of Public Instruction, established in 1823 to oversee education spending, was soon divided between Orientalists such as H. H. Wilson and H. T. Prinsep, who favored supporting Sanskrit, Arabic, and Persian institutions, and critics who favored English and European sciences.

Indians took positions on both sides, and not along simple lines. Some Hindu patrons wanted English for its career value, a motive that would become increasingly strong as English became the language of higher office. Others defended Sanskrit and Arabic learning as the core of cultural and religious identity. Rammohun Roy, as §67.13 shows, opposed a Sanskrit-centered scheme not because he scorned Sanskrit but because he wanted useful science and rational inquiry taught. Missionaries, for their own reasons, favored English instruction as a means of intellectual influence. By 1834 the Committee was evenly split, and the stage was set for a decision from the Governor-General's Council.

William Adam's inquiries offer a particularly useful exercise in historical measurement. His first report repeated an estimate of approximately one hundred thousand indigenous elementary schools in Bengal and Bihar, but it was not a census of schools visited. Adam derived the claim from earlier opinions, assumptions about population and villages, and a broad definition of local instruction; he acknowledged that the premises could yield only distant approximations. His later reports investigated selected districts in greater detail, documenting schoolmasters, pupils, fees, materials, and methods. They cannot establish a precise province-wide enrollment rate. Their strongest contribution is qualitative: they show a wide range of locally financed instruction, including teaching in temporary spaces and arrangements tied to agricultural seasons. The question for historians is not whether education existed before government schools—it clearly did—but who attended, what they learned, and why specific forms of schooling became more or less viable under changing state and market conditions.

Section 67.7

Macaulay's Minute of 1835: English Education, Language Policy, and Intellectual Hierarchy

The deadlock was broken by Thomas Babington Macaulay, who had arrived in India in 1834 as Law Member and was also made president of the General Committee of Public Instruction. His Minute on Indian Education, dated 2 February 1835, argued that the 1813 Act did not oblige the government to promote Sanskrit and Arabic learning specifically, that the "native" learning supported by public funds was of limited practical value, and that the government should promote English literature and European science instead. Macaulay, who admitted that he did not know Sanskrit or Arabic, nevertheless dismissed their literatures in sweeping terms, and he argued that the government should create a class of people, Indian by birth, who would be culturally English and could act as interpreters between rulers and the population, enriching the vernaculars from the new learning.

The Minute was a recommendation, not a policy. Governor-General Lord William Bentinck adopted its main argument in a resolution on 7 March 1835, which stated that the object of the British government should be the promotion of European literature and science among the natives of India, and that educational funds should be best employed on English education alone. Students must keep the two documents distinct: Macaulay advised, and Bentinck's government decided. The resolution also said that existing colleges and teachers were not to be abolished and that stipends of current students would continue; what it halted was new expenditure on printing Oriental works and the expansion of Orientalist institutions.

The consequences unfolded slowly. The resolution did not abolish Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, or vernacular teaching, much of which continued outside government support. English education developed first in a handful of government colleges, such as Elphinstone Institution in Bombay and Hindu College in Calcutta (which became Presidency College in 1855), and in missionary and private institutions. In 1837 Persian ceased to be the official language of the courts and revenue offices, replaced by vernaculars at lower levels and by English at higher ones, and the Hardinge resolution of 1844 gave preference in public employment to those with English education, a policy that attached material rewards to English and made it attractive to ambitious families. The Minute's vision of a class of mediators was partly realized, but the middle class thus created became in time a source of nationalist criticism of colonial rule, a development discussed in Chapter 76.

Macaulay's arguments reveal an intellectual hierarchy typical of the period's liberal imperialism. He treated Indian learning as inferior in content and method, ignoring centuries of sophisticated work in grammar, logic, mathematics, and medicine, and presented English education as the road to progress. The assumption that cultural improvement justified political rule was shared, in differing forms, by evangelicals and utilitarians. Yet some Indians who supported English education did so on different premises: to acquire new scientific knowledge, to gain access to jobs, or, as Roy argued, to make Indian reform arguments intelligible to a wider world. The shared support for English did not imply shared views about Indian civilization.

It is also important to note what the policy did not do. It did not address elementary education for the mass of the population in their own languages; proponents of "downward filtration" assumed that learning would trickle down from the educated few. Funds were scarce, and the result was a system that produced a small, urban, mostly male, mostly upper-caste English-educated elite, while the great majority of children received little or no formal schooling.

English education also operated through access to credentials and employment. A young man trained in English might qualify for work in a government office, a commercial firm, a school, or a newspaper, while his contemporary educated in Persian could find a familiar career narrowing. The benefits were concentrated where schools existed and where families could finance prolonged study. The change did not instantly produce a wholly English-language administration: Indian-language records and translators remained indispensable, and Persianate literary culture continued. Nor did the Anglicist policy settle the question of scientific teaching. The quality of laboratories, mathematics instruction, books, and teachers varied sharply by institution. Students should examine whether the curriculum promised by a resolution was delivered in classrooms, what social groups could reach those classrooms, and how Indian patrons used English-language instruction to pursue projects that colonial officials had not intended.

Section 67.8

Wood's Education Despatch of 1854: Schools, Universities, and the Expanding Educational State

By the 1850s the limits of the post-1835 approach were evident. English education was concentrated in cities, vernacular schooling was mostly private, missionary institutions competed with government colleges, and the Company's expenditure remained small relative to the population. The renewal of the Company's charter in 1853 prompted a parliamentary inquiry into Indian education, and its result was the Education Despatch of 19 July 1854, drafted under Sir Charles Wood, President of the Board of Control, and often called "Wood's Despatch."

The Despatch proposed a comprehensive system. It called for a Department of Public Instruction in each province, headed by a Director, with inspectors to supervise schools. It recommended universities modeled on the University of London, to examine and confer degrees, at the presidency capitals. It advocated a graded structure from vernacular primary schools through anglo-vernacular middle schools to colleges, and it endorsed teacher training and the promotion of female education. It introduced a system of grants-in-aid, by which the government would support private and missionary schools that met secular standards and accepted inspection, an approach that encouraged private effort while preserving the principle of religious neutrality in government institutions. And it stated that vernacular languages should be used along with English, rather than English alone, while continuing to treat English as the language of higher education.

The Despatch's practical effects were gradual. Departments of public instruction were created in the years after 1854. The universities of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras were established in 1857 by legislative act. They were originally examining and degree-granting bodies rather than teaching universities; colleges affiliated with them prepared students for their examinations. The first cohorts of graduates were very small, and the universities served a narrow constituency of urban, English-educated, mostly upper-caste men. Their expansion, and their social consequences, belong largely to the later nineteenth century and to Chapters 73 and 76.

The Despatch also exposed a gap between aspiration and reach. Policy statements spoke of educating the people, yet funding for primary schooling remained limited, and the system favored the groups who could pay fees and who valued English for employment. Girls' education received official endorsement but little money. Communities subjected to untouchability were often excluded from schools in practice, even when no law barred them, as §67.18 discusses. Regional differences were also sharp: Bengal, Bombay, and Madras developed much faster than the Punjab, central India, or the newly annexed territories. Education in this period expanded the state's reach into society, and it created new channels of opportunity, but it also established a new hierarchy based on English, examinations, and certification.

The grant-in-aid system required administrative choices that deserve attention. Inspectors had to decide which private or missionary schools qualified for support, and those judgments gave the state an indirect influence over curriculum, attendance records, teacher qualifications, and examinations. Institutions serving languages or communities with fewer wealthy patrons could struggle to satisfy the same standards as better-financed urban schools. The Despatch's endorsement of female and elementary education was consequential as a statement of policy, but it did not immediately resolve the cost of constructing schools, training women teachers, overcoming local exclusions, or providing instruction beyond the cities. The 1857 universities were designed chiefly to examine students taught elsewhere, so their foundation should not be equated with a sudden increase in places to study. This distinction between system-building and mass access links the educational policy of the 1850s to later questions of social mobility and political participation.

Division III Missionaries, Print, and the New Intellectual Public Sphere Sections 67.9–67.12
Section 67.9

Christian Missionaries, Company Policy, and the Expansion of Educational Activity

Christianity was not new to South Asia in 1813. Syrian Christian communities had existed on the Malabar coast for many centuries, Catholic missions had operated since the Portuguese arrival in the sixteenth century (Chapter 49), and Protestant missionaries had worked in India since the early eighteenth century. The Danish-Halle mission at Tranquebar, on the Coromandel coast, was established in 1706, and its missionaries produced Tamil printed works and schools. What changed in the early nineteenth century was the scale of missionary effort and the legal environment in which it operated.

The Company's attitude had long been cautious. Its officers feared that missionary activity, and particularly attacks on Hindu and Muslim belief, might provoke unrest, as the Vellore mutiny of 1806, in which religious grievances played a part, seemed to confirm. The Company had at times excluded missionaries from its territories, and the Baptist William Carey, who reached Bengal in 1793, had to settle in Danish Serampore in 1800 with Joshua Marshman and William Ward. After evangelical pressure in Britain, the Charter Act of 1813 altered the framework by allowing licensed missionaries to enter and by affirming a general policy of tolerance, and later acts continued this opening. It did not introduce Christianity or missionary education, but it lessened official obstruction.

Missionaries founded schools at many levels. Serampore established a college in 1818 to teach theology and the arts and sciences to Indian Christians and others. Alexander Duff, a Scottish Presbyterian, arrived in Calcutta in 1830 and opened an institution teaching English and European science alongside Christian instruction, with the aim of undermining belief in Hindu scripture by rational education. Missionary societies also ran vernacular and girls' schools, an area where they were often ahead of the government. Indian Christians, including catechists, teachers, and translators, were essential to this work, though their contributions are less documented than those of European missionaries.

Missionary schooling produced conflict as well as opportunity. Some Indian students and families valued instruction in English and science and tolerated or ignored the religious message; others withdrew children when conversion seemed likely. A small number converted, sometimes at great social cost, including exclusion from family and caste. Hindu and Muslim leaders produced rebuttals and organized their own schools. The controversy over religious authority linked missionary education to the broader debates of the period, as Rammohun Roy's response to Baptist critiques of Hindu practice (§67.13) shows. In this way the missions helped create the arena of public religious argument, even where they won few converts.

The relationship between missions and the Company was not stable across all regions. Officials concerned with military discipline and revenue feared that aggressive proselytizing would create suspicion, but they also found missionary teachers and translators useful. A mission operating in Danish Serampore or Tranquebar did not face precisely the same rules as one seeking a base in Company territory. Missionaries themselves disagreed over whether evangelism should proceed through preaching, printed scriptures, vernacular literacy, or elite English schooling. Duff's strategy differed from the Serampore Baptists' emphasis on translation and regional-language work, although neither can be reduced to a single method. Indian Christian communities and teachers shaped decisions about language, curriculum, and local needs. Conversion was sometimes a religious choice made through sustained intellectual engagement and sometimes a source of family and employment conflict; it should not be treated merely as the mechanical result of Western teaching. The Company's declared religious neutrality was thus tested constantly by the activities of institutions it licensed, restrained, or indirectly supported.

Section 67.10

Missionary Printing, Vernacular Literature, Translation, and the Growth of a Reading Public

Before the nineteenth century, South Asian texts circulated mainly in manuscript, copied by scribes and reproduced in limited numbers, and print was largely confined to a few mission presses. The Serampore Mission Press, established in 1800, became one of the most important printing centers in Asia. It printed the Bible in dozens of languages, as well as grammars, dictionaries, school texts, and editions of Sanskrit and vernacular works, in a wide range of scripts.

The press depended on Indian skill. Panchanan Karmakar, a blacksmith and engraver trained by Charles Wilkins, cut types for Bengali and other scripts, and his work was continued by his son-in-law Manohar. Without such artisans, the press could not have produced the range of fonts that made vernacular printing practical. Indian scholars also contributed as writers and editors. Ram Ram Basu, a Bengali munshi working with Carey, wrote one of the early works of Bengali prose, a life of the king Pratapaditya (1801), and pandits at Fort William College, among them Mrityunjay Vidyalankar, composed Bengali prose texts for the college's students, helping shape a literary prose where earlier writing had been dominated by verse.

Print also changed periodicals. Serampore missionaries published Samachar Darpan in 1818, one of the first newspapers in Bengali, which offered news, essays, and commentary alongside religious material. Other presses, owned by Indians as well as Europeans, soon followed, publishing almanacs, devotional texts, and polemics. Printed books were cheaper than manuscripts, though still expensive for most families, and distribution through teachers, booksellers, and schools reached mainly towns. Literacy rates remained low, and reading was frequently collective, with texts read aloud to those who could not read themselves.

Printing enabled standardization of languages and the growth of vernacular literature, but also contributed to new religious and social controversies, since printed tracts and counter-tracts allowed arguments to circulate widely. It is worth emphasizing that print was not a European gift handed to Indians; Indians were printers, editors, authors, and patrons from the start, and they soon used the new technology for their own ends, as the following sections show.

A printed page did not erase the social relationships through which writing reached readers. Type had to be cut for scripts with complex combinations of characters; paper and presses had to be financed; and editors needed proofreaders conversant with local idiom. A scripture, grammar, or schoolbook could pass through several translators before it appeared in print. Those who supplied the language and those who controlled the press were not always the same people, making attribution a historical issue as well as a technical one. Indian publishers increasingly used print to produce calendars, literary works, religious argument, and news that had little to do with missionary goals. Print was also not a guaranteed means of persuasion: readers evaluated the social position and motives of authors, and a pamphlet might prompt an equally vigorous rebuttal. Where one copy was read aloud to a group, the audience could greatly exceed the count of literate subscribers. Students should separate numbers of printed copies, paying readers, and people participating in an argument.

Commercial and missionary presses also affected the relationship between religious communities. A Bengali translation of a Christian text could trigger a response written by a Hindu or Muslim author in the same language, and both writers might draw upon newly standardized prose conventions. These exchanges were not simply disputes between Europe and India: they could expose differences within Indian religious traditions over doctrine, authority, and how to address new audiences. Material constraints remained decisive. A press required access to fonts, skilled compositors, credit, and networks to transport bundles of printed sheets. The emerging periodical market was especially vulnerable to government regulations and the financial risks of small circulation. Publishers who survived developed relationships with booksellers, colleges, associations, and patrons; those without support could disappear quickly. A narrative of print culture therefore needs to include the business of printing as well as the ideas it carried.

Section 67.11

Hindu College, Young Bengal, and New Intellectual Associations

Hindu College was founded in Calcutta in 1817 by Indian patrons and supporters, including prominent Bengali Hindus, with the cooperation of Europeans such as the watchmaker and educator David Hare and Sir Edward Hyde East, chief justice of the Supreme Court. It aimed to give sons of respectable Hindu families an education in English and European learning. Its founding illustrates a point easily lost: demand for English education in Bengal came partly from Indians themselves, who saw it as a route to careers in commerce and administration. Alexander Duff, sometimes mistakenly connected with Hindu College, founded a separate institution in 1830, and he did not found Hindu College in 1817.

The college charged fees and drew its students largely from Hindu families of means in Calcutta, mostly of the Brahmin, Kayastha, and other high-status groups. Its curriculum combined English literature, history, and, increasingly, science, and its management committee included orthodox figures who were wary of religious challenge. In 1826 Henry Louis Vivian Derozio, a young teacher of mixed Indian and Portuguese descent, joined the faculty. Derozio encouraged his students to question received authority, discuss religion, politics, and science, and form debating societies. His pupils, known as Young Bengal, pursued rationalist critique, sometimes shocking their families with open disregard for food taboos and ritual.

The reaction was swift. Parents and college managers, alarmed by reports of irreverence, forced Derozio's dismissal in 1831; he died of cholera that same year, aged twenty-two. His students continued to meet, publish periodicals, and debate, and some became prominent in later public life. Their experience shows both the intellectual ferment among young Calcutta men and the social limits on dissent: families, caste networks, and employers could punish those who defied convention.

It is important to keep Hindu College in perspective. It served a tiny, privileged, urban constituency, while the vast majority of Bengal's children, if they were in school at all, attended village pathshalas or no school. The Young Bengal circle was influential in the history of Bengali intellectual life but not representative of the society as a whole.

The intellectual impact of the college was mediated through unequal relationships between parents, pupils, teachers, and trustees. Students who studied European history or science might accept new methods of argument without abandoning family obligations, while others deliberately challenged inherited religious practices. A few highly public controversies made Young Bengal appear more representative of the student body than it was. Derozio's removal in 1831 illustrates how a privately supported institution could tolerate inquiry in classrooms yet retreat when influential patrons feared social scandal. It is also important to distinguish formal associations of students from later recollections that turned a diverse circle into a unified movement. The college's long-term influence involved habits of debate, writing, and professional preparation, not simply dramatic gestures of defiance. The students' ability to publish or organize rested on family resources, urban social networks, and access to the English-language press. Similar opportunities were far less accessible to girls, agricultural laborers, and many people excluded by caste.

Section 67.12

Newspapers, Public Associations, Religious Debate, and the Emerging Colonial Public Sphere

By the 1820s Calcutta and, more slowly, Bombay and Madras had a growing press. Newspapers had begun in the 1780s with James Hicky's Bengal Gazette, but the early press was mostly European and often in conflict with officials who feared criticism. From 1818 newspapers in Bengali and Persian appeared, alongside English-language journals, and Indians increasingly edited and owned them. Rammohun Roy, for example, published the Bengali Samvad Kaumudi (1821) and the Persian Mirat-ul-Akhbar (1822).

The government kept a watchful eye. In 1823, the acting Governor-General John Adam issued a regulation requiring a licence to publish, which could be revoked. Roy and others petitioned against it, arguing that restrictions on the press would harm the cause of knowledge and good government, though the petition failed and Roy ceased publishing the Mirat-ul-Akhbar in protest. The regulation remained in force until 1835, when Sir Charles Metcalfe, as acting Governor-General, enacted legislation easing restrictions on the press, a measure that Indian and European publicists celebrated.

Alongside newspapers grew a wider public life of pamphlets, petitions, and voluntary associations: debating societies, learned societies, and bodies formed to press specific causes. The Dharma Sabha, formed in 1830 to oppose the abolition of sati, and the Brahmo Sabha, formed to promote a reformed theism, show how religious argument shaped associational life. The Landholders' Society (1837), a political association of landed interests in Bengal, indicates how the new forms of organization could also be used to petition the government.

Who took part in this emerging public sphere? Mostly literate men of the towns, usually from groups with education, income, and leisure: Brahmins, Kayasthas, merchants, and some Muslim and Christian elites. Women, the rural poor, communities excluded by caste, and those who did not read English or the major vernaculars were largely absent. Newspapers could criticize officials, yet they also expressed the interests of particular communities, from orthodox Hindu publications defending custom to reformist and missionary periodicals. The public sphere was thus a plural, contentious, and exclusive space, and its debates, including those between Roy and his critics, provided the background for the reform campaigns of Division IV.

Public debate did not occur in a single national arena. A controversy printed in English for Company officials could differ from the same issue framed in Bengali for merchants and students, or in Persian for courtly and administrative readers. Newspapers addressed particular urban constituencies, and their proprietors often had commercial or political interests. The ability to write to an editor or petition a government officer did not necessarily convey equal influence: petitions required signatures or recognizable patrons, while censorship and licensing affected the risks of publication. The 1823 restrictions heightened these difficulties, and the liberalization in 1835 altered but did not eliminate government oversight. Local discussion also continued in settings not preserved as printed texts, including religious assemblies, market conversation, and teaching circles. A historian reading a newspaper dispute must therefore ask who financed the publication, whose language it used, how copies circulated, and whether people outside the recorded debate interpreted the controversy differently. The emergence of new publics was a transformation in participation, not the creation of a representative democracy.

Historical Vocabulary
The Colonial Public Sphere

The term public sphere, associated with the German theorist Jürgen Habermas, describes a space in which private persons debate matters of common concern, using print and associations. Historians of South Asia use the term cautiously: the colonial public sphere was shaped by censorship, restricted literacy, and exclusion by gender, caste, and language, and it coexisted with other forms of public life such as caste councils, temple assemblies, and religious gatherings.

Applying the concept helps explain how newspapers, associations, and petitions created new arenas of argument, but it should not be taken to mean that a single, inclusive public existed.

Section 67.13

Raja Rammohun Roy: Religious Interpretation, Education, Public Debate, and Reform

Rammohun Roy (born c. 1772, though the date is debated) came from a Bengali Brahmin family with ties to the Mughal and Company administrations. He studied Persian and Arabic as well as Sanskrit and Bengali, learned English as an adult, and served as a Company official and moneylender before turning to writing and public life in Calcutta around 1815. His intellectual world was multilingual and cosmopolitan. He read the Upanishads and Vedanta commentaries, Islamic rationalist theology, and Christian scripture and European philosophy, and his earliest known work, a Persian treatise written in the early 1800s, criticized idol worship and priestly authority from a monotheist standpoint informed by Islamic as well as Hindu thought.

Roy argued that the oldest Hindu scriptures, especially the Upanishads, taught the worship of one formless God and that later ritual practices, including image worship and some customs, had departed from that teaching. He translated Upanishadic texts into Bengali and English and defended Hinduism against missionary attacks, while also rejecting what he saw as superstition. In The Precepts of Jesus (1820), he presented the ethical teaching of the Gospels while omitting miracles and doctrines of divinity, provoking a debate with Baptist missionaries at Serampore. His position drew on multiple sources and cannot be reduced to imitation of Europeans: he often used Sanskrit and Persian scholarship against both missionaries and orthodox opponents.

He was also active in public causes. He campaigned against sati (§67.15), argued for women's property rights, and opposed restrictions on the press (§67.12). He supported the introduction of modern education and was a patron of institutions such as the Anglo-Hindu School he helped found and of efforts to teach science. In 1830 he traveled to Britain as an envoy of the Mughal emperor Akbar II, who conferred on him the title "Raja," to present the emperor's claims on the Company, and also to observe the debates over the renewal of the Company's charter. He died in Bristol on 27 September 1833.

His letter to Lord Amherst of 11 December 1823 is the most famous statement of his educational views. When the government planned to establish a Sanskrit college in Calcutta, Roy wrote to the Governor-General objecting that a curriculum centered on traditional Sanskrit learning would perpetuate speculative and ritual subjects rather than provide useful knowledge. He urged instead the teaching of mathematics, natural philosophy, chemistry, and anatomy, taught by Europeans, as the basis of "improvement." The letter demonstrates that Roy spoke as an Indian advocate seeking to influence colonial policy, not as a passive recipient of European ideas, though his proposals and those of later Anglicists overlapped, and the Committee did not follow his advice in 1824.

Roy's career raises questions about categories. He was a reformer but also a defender of Vedantic tradition; he cooperated with officials but criticized them; he drew on Christian ethics but rejected Trinitarian doctrine. Some contemporaries, orthodox and missionary alike, found his position unsettling, and later historians have disagreed on whether to call him the "father of modern India" or a figure whose influence has been exaggerated. What is clear is that his work was independent and multi-sourced, and that he helped shape the idiom of public religious argument for generations.

Roy's position cannot be reconstructed by selecting only his English-language writings. His arguments were directed at different audiences and drew on distinct forms of authority: close reading of the Upanishads, Persianate monotheistic thought, debates with missionaries, and practical knowledge of courts and administration. When he challenged practices he regarded as indefensible, he often sought to show that a reform could be justified within Indian sources rather than imposed against them. His educational petition likewise criticized the proposed allocation of public funds, not the existence of Sanskrit learning as such. The breadth of his work helps explain why contemporaries interpreted him in conflicting ways—as a religious critic, a defender of scriptural truth, an ally on particular reforms, or an inconvenient opponent of government restrictions. His intervention in debates over the press was particularly revealing: he could seek legislative action against sati while insisting that Company authority be open to public criticism. Students should avoid treating these positions as inconsistent merely because they combined cooperation with dissent.

Section 67.14

The Brahmo Sabha and Brahmo Samaj: Monotheism, Religious Criticism, and Institutional Reform

Roy's religious ideas took institutional form gradually. From 1815 he gathered a circle in Calcutta called the Atmiya Sabha (Friendly Society), which met to discuss Vedantic texts and the worship of one God. In 1828 he and his associates founded the Brahmo Sabha, a congregation that met weekly for worship consisting of Upanishadic recitation, Sanskrit and Bengali hymns, and a sermon, without images. A trust deed of January 1830 established the congregation's building at Chitpur Road in Calcutta as a place of worship of the one eternal, unsearchable, and immutable Being, open to all without distinction of sect, and prescribed that no image or sacrifice be allowed. The deed is an important documentary foundation for the movement's early principles.

The early movement's character was reformist but not separatist. Members saw themselves as recovering the true teaching of the Vedas and Upanishads, and many continued to live as Hindus. The congregation's worship, a weekly service and a theistic creed, was modeled partly on Protestant and Unitarian practice, though its textual sources were Indian, and its hymns drew on regional devotional traditions. The group's membership was small, mainly of educated Bengali men, including the wealthy Dwarkanath Tagore, a friend of Roy and a patron of many causes.

After Roy left for Britain in 1830 and died in 1833, the Sabha faltered. Ram Chandra Vidyabagish, the learned Brahmin who had served as its first minister, kept the services going, and in the late 1830s Debendranath Tagore, Dwarkanath's son, revived the movement. In 1839 he founded the Tattwabodhini Sabha to study scripture and promote monotheist theology, and its journal, Tattwabodhini Patrika, begun in 1843 under the editorship of Akshay Kumar Datta, became an influential vehicle for Bengali prose on religion, science, and social issues. In 1843 Debendranath and his associates formally joined the Brahmo Samaj, and the movement's membership and organization expanded during the 1840s, with branches beyond Calcutta.

These developments belong to the early history of the movement. Its later nineteenth-century divisions, notably the schism under Keshub Chunder Sen in the 1860s and the formation of new societies, as well as its relationship to other reform and revival movements, are treated in Chapter 74. For the period before 1857, students should note that the Brahmo movement was small, urban, and Bengali, with influence far beyond its numbers, through publishing, education, and the model it offered to reformers elsewhere. The Prarthana Samaj in Bombay (1867), for example, took inspiration from it later.

Institutional continuity after Roy's death is essential to the chronology. A meeting association formed around one distinguished reformer did not automatically become a large, continuous social organization. Worship practices, leadership, the use of a trust property, and the involvement of new adherents all changed over time. Debendranath Tagore's later role, the Tattwabodhini circle, and the development of sermons and periodicals helped give the movement new intellectual and organizational resources. But the more prominent later divisions of the Brahmo movement should not be anticipated as if they were already fixed in the 1830s. Students should also consider what the movement's social profile meant for its reach: urban educated patrons could sponsor public debate, yet people without comparable education or access to Calcutta's associations were much less likely to participate. The Brahmo example shows a reform movement evolving through religious interpretation, institutional organization, and relations with colonial civil society, rather than advancing through a single founding declaration.

The trust arrangements of 1830 also raise a useful legal question: who could control property dedicated to an association, set conditions for worship, and decide whether later participants remained faithful to its purposes? The answer could shape religious practice for generations, even when relatively few people attended particular meetings. In the Company's courts, property held in trust acquired a written legal form that did not exactly reproduce older arrangements of collective worship and patronage. The Brahmo movement thus stood at the intersection of indigenous religious criticism and colonial legal institutions. Its adherents argued over how scripture should be interpreted, which practices were optional, and how far a new community should differentiate itself from its Hindu social environment. Those arguments helped produce different reform programs over time. They should be reconstructed from the documents and institutions of each decade rather than read backward from the larger movements of the late nineteenth century.

Section 67.15

Sati, Religious Controversy, and Bengal Regulation XVII of 1829

Sati (the Anglicized spelling "suttee" appears in many sources) was the practice by which a Hindu widow was burned, or in some cases buried alive, with her husband's body. It was a historically specific and regionally variable practice, not a universal Hindu custom. Evidence suggests it was practiced mainly among certain upper-caste and warrior or elite groups and was more common in some regions, such as Bengal and parts of Rajasthan, than in others, and many Hindu communities and texts did not endorse it. Textual authority was contested: some Sanskrit sources praised it, while others discouraged or restricted it.

Company officials began collecting statistics on sati in the Bengal Presidency in 1815, recording several hundred cases a year in the early 1820s, concentrated in the districts around Calcutta, though the figures were collected by police and are probably incomplete. These records show the practice was far from universal even in Bengal, but they also reveal that many widows died, and that the reasons were complex. Pressure from relatives, expectations of honor, the widow's precarious position in the household, and, in some cases, outright coercion were involved. Property was relevant: in Bengal's Dayabhaga system, a childless widow could inherit her husband's share, which gave some relatives an interest in her death. Historians therefore reject simple claims that sati was either wholly voluntary or wholly forced; the evidence points to a range of circumstances, with consent difficult to establish when a widow's alternatives were so limited.

Indians debated the practice. Rammohun Roy wrote tracts in Bengali and English arguing from Sanskrit sources that sati was not commanded by the highest Hindu authorities and that widows should instead live ascetic lives or, as he sometimes argued, be allowed property. He had been moved, according to his own and others' accounts, by the death of his brother's widow. Other Bengalis argued that sati was sanctioned by custom and scripture and that the state should not interfere with religion. The Company's officials were divided, too: some feared political reaction, while evangelical and liberal opinion in Britain pressed for abolition.

Governor-General Lord William Bentinck resolved the question. Bengal Regulation XVII of 4 December 1829 declared sati, the burning or burying alive of Hindu widows, illegal and punishable by the criminal courts, and it required police officers to prevent it and to report attempts. Bentinck acted after consulting officials and reviewing the arguments, and he cited, among other considerations, that the practice was not an essential part of the Hindu religion. The regulation was extended to Madras and Bombay in 1830. Orthodox leaders, led by Radhakanta Deb and the Dharma Sabha, petitioned against the law, and appealed to the Privy Council in London, which rejected the appeal in 1832; Roy's circle presented an address in support of the regulation.

The legislation was a major assertion of colonial legal power over a religious practice, and its meaning has been debated. Supporters credit it with ending a cruel practice. Critics note that the debates treated widows as objects of rescue rather than as persons with views of their own, that the focus on sati diverted attention from the broader conditions of widowhood, and that the argument that Hindu texts did not require sati rested on selective scriptural authority. Enforcement was uneven, especially in princely states outside direct Company control, where abolition came later. The law did not end the problems of widowhood, which the next section examines.

The regulation itself provides a striking lesson in the difference between a government's legal justification and the social facts it purported to describe. Bentinck's council argued that sati was not an imperative religious duty and that many Hindus opposed it; these were arguments used to defend intervention, not neutral findings about every community. Reports of individual cases were collected by officials through police and local intermediaries, often with uneven geographical coverage. The figures cannot simply be added into a precise count of all women who died across the subcontinent, nor can their recording establish the degree of willingness or coercion in each case. Opposition to abolition included arguments about the limits of foreign government and the authority of scripture, while supporters advanced moral, legal, and interpretive objections to the practice. The requirement that landholders notify police of an intended burning and the power to arrest participants demonstrated how the statute attempted to convert a previously tolerated ritual into an enforceable criminal prohibition. Whether these mechanisms worked depended on local officials and communities.

Section 67.16

Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Widow Remarriage, and the Limits of Legal Reform, 1856

If the sati regulation addressed a widow's death, the next reform campaign addressed her life. Many upper-caste Hindu widows, particularly in Bengal, faced severe restrictions: they were expected to remain unmarried, wear plain clothing, follow strict dietary rules, and live as dependents in the households of in-laws, with limited control over property. Child marriage meant that some were widowed in childhood. Practices varied by region and caste, and in many lower-caste and some regional communities widow remarriage was already customary, so the reform debate concerned mainly elite groups that had prohibited it.

Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891), a Bengali Brahmin scholar and educator, became the movement's leader. Trained at the Sanskrit College in Calcutta, where he later served as professor and, from 1851, principal, he was a Sanskritist who also promoted modern education, wrote influential Bengali primers, and supported women's schools. His reform argument was scriptural: in works published from 1855, he argued, from texts including the Parashara Smriti, that Hindu law permitted widows to remarry. Using the learned idiom of the pandits, he aimed to answer those who defended prohibition on religious grounds.

The campaign combined scholarship, public argument, and petitioning. Vidyasagar and supporters submitted a signed petition with a draft bill in October 1855; the 1979 Law Commission historical appendix describes roughly a thousand signatories. Orthodox opponents, including Radhakanta Deb and learned pandits, organized counterpetitions. Reports of very large opposition totals differ by source and should not be treated as a census of popular opinion. Disputes among pandits, newspaper articles, satirical poems, and sermons fueled the controversy. The Governor-General's Council, with the support of Lord Dalhousie, passed the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, Act XV of 1856, dated 25 July 1856 in the Bangladesh government legal text and in the Indian Law Commission’s historical review (passed by the Legislative Council on 19 July; one later biography gives 26 July for assent). The Act declared that no marriage contracted between Hindus should be invalid, and that the issue of such a marriage should not be illegitimate, by reason of the woman's previous widowhood.

The statute also contained a significant restriction. A widow who remarried lost any interest she held in her deceased husband's property by way of a limited estate, so remarriage could cost her economic security. The Act therefore protected the legal validity of remarriage but at the same time reflected the property interests of the deceased husband's family. Some scholars see this as a limit imposed by the legislators' concern for orthodox sensibilities and for family property; the law's text thus shows both reform and constraint.

Social acceptance was a different matter. The first reported remarriage under the Act took place in Calcutta in December 1856, with Vidyasagar's support, and he went on to subsidize several others, at personal cost; remarrying couples often faced ostracism, and Vidyasagar himself faced hostility. In the following decades only a small number of widows remarried, mostly among a few reformist families, and widowhood remained a harsh condition for many women. The history of widow remarriage thus shows the gap between legislation and practice: law could remove a legal obstacle, but families, caste councils, and public opinion determined whether widows could in fact remarry.

The legislative record shows that petition politics mattered, but reported signature totals require attention to the document and to who was counted. Vidyasagar and his supporters submitted petitions and a proposed bill in 1855, while opponents, including prominent Sanskrit scholars and conservative patrons, organized counterpetitions in 1856. Later histories give different totals for supporting and opposing signatures, and large numbers do not establish either the balance of public opinion or the preferences of women whose lives the legislation concerned. The Act itself did more than remove a single bar: it addressed the legitimacy of children, certain guardianship questions, the consent required for minors, and property interests on remarriage. A widow could gain the ability to contract a legally valid marriage while losing some rights connected to her deceased husband's estate. These provisions illustrate reform conducted through the language of family law and inheritance, and the concessions reflected the legislators' competing priorities. Actual remarriages remained rare, so the reach of legal change must be assessed separately from its statutory significance.

Division V Women, Caste, Resistance, and Historical Synthesis Sections 67.17–67.20
Section 67.17

Women's Education, Savitribai Phule, and the Expansion of Social Reform beyond Bengal

In the early nineteenth century most South Asian women had no access to formal schooling. Exceptions existed: some women from elite households learned at home, some religious communities taught girls, and courtesans and other women performers had their own training. But custom, early marriage, and concerns about seclusion and propriety discouraged girls' schooling in many communities, and the educated woman was widely regarded with suspicion, a point that varied by region and group.

Missionary and reform efforts began to change this slowly. Missionary societies, including a society for female education founded by Englishwomen in the 1820s, opened girls' schools in Calcutta and other towns, often teaching poor or Christian girls. Indian reformers, such as Vidyasagar and the associates of the Tattwabodhini Sabha, supported girls' education, and in 1849 John Elliot Drinkwater Bethune opened a school for Hindu girls in Calcutta, with Vidyasagar's help. Wood's Despatch endorsed female education, though funding remained slight. The pupils were few, and families often withdrew girls at marriage.

In western India, Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) and Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890) began a distinctive initiative. Jyotirao, from a family of the Mali caste, was educated in a missionary school and became critical of Brahmanical dominance. The Phules’ first educational initiatives in Pune are commonly dated to 1848; school reports and later accounts provide more detailed evidence of their organized girls’ schools in 1851–1852. Savitribai taught in these initiatives and was among the pioneering Indian women teachers of the period. In the early 1850s they expanded their efforts, opening further schools, including schools for girls and for children of communities subjected to untouchability such as the Mahars and Mangs, and reorganized the institutions under new committees; sources differ on details of these early schools, and students should distinguish the founding of 1848 from the later reorganizations. Fatima Sheikh is also reported by historians to have been an important associate in the work.

The obstacles were formidable. Savitribai reportedly faced harassment from hostile neighbors, and the Phules were eventually forced out of Jyotirao's father's home because of the opposition of orthodox relatives. Women who sought education or employment as teachers risked their reputations. Yet their effort shows that educational reform was not limited to Bengal or to upper-caste men, and that women were active agents of change, even though the sources, which are mostly official or reform publications, record their work imperfectly.

The early school chronology needs an explicit evidentiary qualification. The Phules' first initiatives are commonly dated to 1848, but later accounts and contemporary school reports also describe the organization and inspection of distinct institutions in 1851–1852. These may refer to different stages and schools rather than a single easily dated founding event. A historian should not combine the figures reported for several institutions into a count for one school. Fatima Sheikh's participation is an important part of the surviving history: Savitribai referred to her by name in a letter, but many details of Sheikh's life and precise duties are known principally through later recollection. Their collaboration illustrates how anticaste education could depend on reciprocal assistance between households. The strongest claim is that the Phules and their associates organized sustained educational activity for girls and excluded communities, not that every celebrated anecdote has the same documentary status.

Section 67.18

Caste, Social Hierarchy, and the Limits of Elite Reform

Colonial law, education, and religious debate interacted with caste in several ways. The Company's courts, relying on Brahmanical legal texts and pandits, often gave weight to Brahmanical ideas of rank. Government and missionary schools, though nominally open, drew mostly from groups with literacy traditions, such as Brahmins, Kayasthas, and merchant communities, while families subjected to untouchability and many artisan and laboring castes were excluded by custom, poverty, and the hostility of other parents. Missionary schools sometimes admitted lower-caste children more readily, which was one reason for conflict with orthodox groups.

Legislation also touched caste. The Caste Disabilities Removal Act of 1850 (Act XXI) aimed to protect the inheritance rights of those who lost caste or changed religion, a measure aimed especially at Hindu converts to Christianity. Orthodox Hindus saw it as an attack on caste discipline; it nonetheless suggests how colonial law could weaken certain social sanctions. Meanwhile, the reliance on Brahmanical texts for Hindu law, and the later census classification of castes, discussed in Chapter 73, tended to reinforce hierarchical readings of society.

Reform movements differed on caste. Rammohun Roy and the Brahmo movement criticized some caste practices, but in this period most prominent reformers came from upper castes and did not directly challenge their communities' privileges, and some defended caste distinctions as part of social order. Vidyasagar's campaign concerned primarily upper-caste widows. By contrast, Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule explicitly addressed the exclusion of communities treated as low or untouchable from education, directing their schools toward them. Their later organizations and writings, notably the Satyashodhak Samaj (1873), belong to the next period and should not be read back into the 1840s and 1850s.

The evidence for marginalized communities' experiences is scarce and indirect. Few members of these communities wrote in the period, and the surviving records are mostly produced by officials, missionaries, and reformers. Historians must read school registers, mission reports, and later recollections with care, noting what they omit. The central lesson is that the priorities of prominent urban reformers did not represent all South Asians, and that the benefits of colonial-era educational and legal change were distributed unequally by caste as well as by gender and class.

Caste exclusions were also experienced differently in an urban college, a village school, and a household apprenticeship. An institution might lack a written rule of exclusion yet remain inaccessible because students needed money, social protection, or permission to share classroom space. Some missionaries and reformers challenged these barriers but carried their own assumptions about civilization, religion, and respectability. Conversely, some Indian patrons defended familiar social hierarchies while sponsoring substantial educational investments. The Caste Disabilities Removal Act dealt with a particular legal consequence of religious conversion or loss of caste; it did not abolish caste or make everyday discrimination unlawful. The contrast between its narrow legal protection and the Phules' educational practice demonstrates why institutional reform must be examined at several levels: formal statute, social opportunity, and the response of communities. The sources offer more about reformers' intentions than about the everyday experiences of those who entered the new schools.

Section 67.19

Reform, Resistance, and Colonial Power: Competing Visions of Social Change

The preceding sections show that responses to colonial intervention in knowledge, law, education, and social practice varied widely. The first point is that British officials disagreed among themselves. Orientalists and Anglicists clashed over education; evangelicals urged intervention in religious practice while cautious administrators feared unrest; utilitarians pressed for codification while others defended customary law. Policy often emerged from compromise or the preferences of individual Governors-General.

Indian communities held competing positions as well. Some welcomed English education for its opportunities; others defended classical learning or feared cultural loss. Some supported the abolition of sati; others petitioned against it as interference with religion. Some Hindus supported widow remarriage; others mobilized against it. Muslims debated the new educational policies and the displacement of Persian, and many looked to their own institutions. Christian converts were treated by families and caste groups in ways that varied from acceptance to ostracism. In none of these cases did a community speak with one voice.

Three kinds of response should be distinguished. Reform refers to efforts, by Indians or others, to change social or religious practices. Resistance to reform refers to defense of existing practices, as in the Dharma Sabha's opposition to the sati regulation or opposition to widow remarriage. Resistance to colonial authority refers to opposition to the Company's rule or specific policies, which could be armed or peaceful and which Chapter 68 examines. These categories can overlap: an orthodox leader might resist reform and also criticize Company interference, while a reformer might cooperate with the Company on law and criticize its political inequality. Treating them as interchangeable obscures the real divisions among South Asians.

A fundamental contradiction ran through the Company's claim to improve society. Officials proclaimed the aims of progress and humanity while maintaining a government in which Indians had almost no political power and from which they were largely excluded from the highest offices, despite the formal language of the Charter Act of 1833. Indian reformers could use colonial institutions, including courts, presses, schools, and legislative power, to advance their goals, while also criticizing colonial policy, as Roy did in his campaigns for press freedom. This combination of cooperation and critique helped shape later nationalist politics.

The intensity of the debates over religion, education, and law also contributed to anxiety about the Company's intentions. Many Indians suspected that the combination of missionary activity, legislative intervention, and educational change aimed to undermine their religions, a fear that would resonate in the rebellion of 1857 examined in Chapter 69. Chapter 68, however, first considers the longer history of military, peasant, tribal, and religious resistance to Company rule before 1857.

The temporal sequence is important when considering the relationship between reform and the crisis of 1857. Religious unease did not arise solely from the prohibition of sati or from missionary teaching. Military regulations, rumors, treatment of soldiers, annexations, taxation, and local conflicts all mattered, and different regions understood the Company's intentions differently. A government might insist that it would not interfere with religion while sponsoring legal measures that altered particular customs; Indians could therefore distinguish the formal principle of noninterference from specific policies. Some regarded reforms as valuable protection or expanded opportunity; others interpreted the same acts as signs of encroaching power. Public arguments conducted by a small literate minority did not translate directly into the reactions of rural communities or army units. This complexity requires moving beyond a simple opposition between progressive reformers and conservative resisters and toward a study of power, trust, evidence, and political agency.

Section 67.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced how the East India Company governed through knowledge, law, language, and education, and how South Asians engaged, shaped, and challenged those instruments. Several threads converge.

The first thread is colonial knowledge. From Halhed's Code in 1776 to the surveys of the early nineteenth century, the Company built an archive of translations, maps, and reports that enabled administration and also classified society. That knowledge depended throughout on Indian pandits, maulavis, munshis, surveyors, and printers, whose contributions were crucial and often unacknowledged. Whether Orientalism is best seen as preservation or reconstruction is a question on which historians divide, and the evidence suggests elements of both: it saved and studied manuscripts and also privileged some texts, and some groups, over others.

The second thread is law and education as instruments of the state. Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan law translated plural traditions into court-applicable categories; codification pursued uniformity in criminal law while leaving personal law communal. The Orientalist–Anglicist controversy, resolved in favor of English in 1835 and extended by the educational system of 1854, produced institutions that served a narrow constituency but would become consequential. The difference between Macaulay's advice and Bentinck's decision, and between the draft code of 1837 and the enacted code of 1860, shows the need to separate proposals from policies.

The third thread is Indian agency. Rammohun Roy drew on multiple traditions to argue for monotheism, education, and press freedom. Vidyasagar used the learned language of Sanskrit scholarship to argue for widow remarriage. Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule opened schools for girls and for excluded communities. Indian patrons founded Hindu College, Indian printers and writers helped create vernacular print, and Indian opponents of reform organized petitions and counter-institutions. These figures were not mere recipients of European ideas; they pursued their own projects, sometimes in cooperation with and sometimes in opposition to the Company and missionaries.

The fourth thread is unequal outcomes. Colonial institutions opened opportunities for some men in the cities and created new channels of employment, but they excluded or marginalized many others by gender, caste, region, and class. The same legal and educational systems that enabled reformers could reinforce Brahmanical authority, concentrate English education in a small elite, and leave the majority without schooling.

The chapter's central question can now be answered. Colonial power and South Asian intellectual and social agency shaped one another through interaction under conditions of unequal political power. The Company's needs and assumptions framed what could be done, but Indians transformed the institutions and arguments that came their way, and their disagreements, as well as their collaborations, produced both reform and resistance. Chapter 68 turns to the forms of resistance to Company rule that ran alongside these developments, from military mutinies to peasant and Adivasi uprisings, preparing the ground for the rebellion of 1857.

The historian's task is to keep several kinds of evidence in conversation without confusing them. A regulation reveals how government justified intervention, while a petition reveals an organized constituency and a school report records a limited administrative encounter. None establishes the views of all Indians, still less the experiences of people absent from the archive. Comparison across Calcutta, Serampore, Benares, Madras, Bombay, and Pune exposes the uneven geography of change. It also reveals a central paradox: systems that made education and legal appeal more accessible to certain groups could create new barriers through language, cost, credentialing, or the authoritative use of elite textual traditions. The course of colonial reform was therefore neither an uncomplicated triumph of progress nor a story in which Indians lacked initiative. Its institutions were continually reshaped by the people who used them, the conflicts they generated, and the limits of the Company's political order.

Historical Debate

Was Nineteenth-Century Social Reform Primarily an Indian Intellectual Movement or an Instrument of Colonial Power?

The question: Did reform in sati, widow remarriage, education, and caste arise mainly from Indian intellectual initiative, or was it largely shaped and enforced by colonial authority?

Interpretation A — Indian initiative: Historians emphasizing Indian agency point to Roy's tracts against sati, Vidyasagar's scriptural arguments, the Phules' schools, and Indian patronage of Hindu College, and argue that reformers drew on indigenous intellectual resources and that colonial law responded to, and was legitimated by, their arguments.
Interpretation B — Colonial power and its limits: Others, including scholars such as Lata Mani in her study of the sati debates, stress that the decisive acts were legislative and that the discourse of rescue and civilization served colonial claims; in this view the terms of debate, and the place of women within it, were shaped by colonial authority and by selective use of texts.

Evidence: Both draw on regulations, petitions, tracts, newspapers, and official correspondence. The evidence shows reformers and officials cooperating, as with Roy and Bentinck, and also diverging, as with the Phules' work outside government institutions and the orthodox opposition to law.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians understand the origins of modern Indian reform and nationalist politics, and the roles of women, who were central subjects of debate but rarely authors.

Student task: Reject a false choice between "Indian" and "colonial": explain, using sati and widow remarriage, how reform developed through interaction, and identify where participants' objectives diverged.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Knowledge, Law, and Learning under Company Rule

What changed? Scholarship acquired new institutions and printing; Persian gave way to vernaculars and English in administration; law became increasingly written, translated, and centralized; English education created a new urban elite; public debate moved into print and associations; the state legislated on religious practices such as sati.

What remained continuous? Indigenous elementary schools, tols, and madrasas continued; customary and caste-based law remained important; many families preserved existing marriage and widowhood practices; Persianate and Sanskritic scholarly traditions persisted outside government patronage.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban, literate men who could access English education and official employment; Persian-educated service families whose position declined; Sanskrit and Arabic scholars dependent on patronage; widows and others directly affected by legislation.

Who experienced greater continuity? Rural families, laboring communities, many women, and communities excluded from schooling.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that colonial change was real but uneven, and warns against treating the experience of Calcutta's intelligentsia as that of the subcontinent.

Cause and Effect

Why the Company Intervened in Education, Law, and Social Practice

Long-term causes

The Company's transformation into a territorial state needing legal and linguistic knowledge; British evangelical, utilitarian, and liberal ideas of improvement; preexisting Indian traditions of learning and law that the Company drew upon.

Short-term causes

The 1813 and 1833 Charter Acts; the deadlock in the General Committee of Public Instruction; campaigns by Roy, Vidyasagar, and others; the governors-general's preferences, notably Bentinck and Dalhousie.

Immediate effects

Legislation on sati and widow remarriage; the 1835 resolution; a draft penal code; new colleges, presses, and associations.

Long-term consequences

A narrow English-educated elite; a legal system with uniform criminal law and communal personal law; standardized vernaculars and print culture; public debates that shaped later reform and nationalist politics; anxiety about religious interference.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains these developments; economic need, ideology, Indian initiative, and political calculation operated together, and officials disagreed among themselves.

Everyday Life and Social History

Learning, Reading, and Widowhood in Company India

For most South Asians in this period, the Company's debates about knowledge and reform arrived indirectly. A boy in a Bengal village might learn letters and arithmetic from a local teacher paid in kind, using palm-leaf or paper and chalk on the ground, while a Brahmin student might live with a teacher in a tol studying grammar and law. Girls were most often taught domestic skills, and only a few learned to read. Where English education reached a family, it could mean new employment as a clerk, translator, or lawyer's assistant, along with tension between older and younger generations.

Reading was often communal: a newspaper or tract might be read aloud in a gathering. For a widow, the debates over sati and remarriage translated into daily questions of dress, food, household authority, and property, which varied by caste and region, and which the law changed only slightly. Evidence for these everyday experiences is thin, and historians rely on school surveys, mission records, memoirs, and later recollections, reading them with attention to who recorded them and why.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the East India Company governed through knowledge, law, education, and the regulation of social practice between about 1772 and 1857, and how South Asians shaped and contested those interventions. Its central argument is that colonial power and Indian intellectual and social agency developed through interaction under conditions of unequal political authority, not through a one-way transfer of European ideas.

The Company needed linguistic, legal, and geographic knowledge to rule, and it depended on Indian munshis, pandits, maulavis, surveyors, printers, and translators. These specialists belonged to older traditions of scholarship that long predated European study, and their expertise underlay the work of the Orientalists associated with Warren Hastings and William Jones, including the Asiatick Society founded on 15 January 1784.

Orientalist scholarship produced translations, grammars, and a comparative philology, and preserved manuscripts, but it also concentrated on classical, mostly Brahmanical and Persianate sources and treated them as authoritative for the whole society. The Historical Debate showed that historians disagree on whether this amounted to preservation, reconstruction, or both. Surveys such as the Great Trigonometrical Survey, regional descriptions, archives, and printing similarly enabled administration while simplifying local realities into categories.

In law, the Company's reliance on pandits and maulavis led to translated digests such as Halhed's Code of Gentoo Laws (1776), which passed through Sanskrit, Persian, and English, and to the construction of Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan law. The Charter Act of 1833 and the First Law Commission pursued codification, producing a draft penal code in 1837 that became law only in 1860, while personal law remained differentiated by religion.

Education policy was shaped by a dispute between Orientalists and Anglicists. Indigenous schools, tols, and madrasas continued, while the Company founded Orientalist colleges and the 1813 Charter Act provided a modest sum for learning. Macaulay's Minute of 2 February 1835 recommended English education, and Bentinck's resolution of 7 March 1835 adopted that policy. Wood's Despatch of 1854 and the universities of 1857 created a state-supervised system whose reach remained narrow by gender, caste, class, and region.

Christian missionaries, from Tranquebar to Serampore, founded schools, printed books, and encouraged vernacular literature, depending on Indian printers and scholars such as Panchanan Karmakar and Ram Ram Basu. Hindu College, Young Bengal, newspapers, and associations formed a colonial public sphere that was plural, contentious, and restricted by literacy, censorship, and exclusion.

Raja Rammohun Roy drew on Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, and European sources to argue for monotheism, education in useful science, press freedom, and an end to sati, and his Brahmo Sabha (1828) and its successors, including the Tattwabodhini Sabha (1839), founded an enduring reform movement. Bengal Regulation XVII of 1829 prohibited sati, and Vidyasagar's campaign led to the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856, which validated remarriage but restricted a widow's property rights, illustrating the gap between legal and social change.

Women educators such as Savitribai Phule and reformers such as Jyotirao Phule showed that reform extended beyond Bengal and upper-caste men, although the available sources record marginalized communities' experiences only partly. Reform, resistance to reform, and resistance to colonial authority were distinct and sometimes overlapping responses.

Colonial institutions expanded opportunities for some, notably urban literate men, while reinforcing or creating inequalities for others. The chapter's developments prepared the ground for later nationalism and communal debate and form the background of Chapter 68, which examines resistance to Company rule before 1857.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that colonial rule was exercised not only through armies and revenue but through dictionaries, translations, courts, schools, and printing presses. It also shows how South Asians were active participants: scholars who supplied the learning, printers who made the books, reformers who made arguments, and communities who defended or rejected change.

The developments traced here shaped later history. The legal division between uniform public law and communal personal law, the dominance of English in higher education, the emergence of a print-based public sphere, and the arguments over religion, gender, and caste were all part of the inheritance of later nationalist and social movements, as Volume V will show.

The chapter also connects regions: Calcutta, Pune, Madras, Benares, and Serampore were linked by networks of scholars, missionaries, and printers, though their histories differed. Within the six-volume sequence, it marks the point where Company rule becomes a government of ideas as well as of territory and revenue, preparing for Chapter 68's account of resistance and Chapter 69's account of 1857.

For students, the chapter's lesson is methodological as much as historical: official minutes, statutes, and reform writings are essential evidence, but they speak for the powerful and the literate. Careful historians ask whose voices are missing and resist treating any single group, whether the colonial state or the urban reformers, as the sole author of change.
