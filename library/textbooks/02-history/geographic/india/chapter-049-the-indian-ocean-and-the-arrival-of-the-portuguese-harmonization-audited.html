The Indian Ocean and the Arrival of the Portuguese

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 20 May 1498 a small Portuguese fleet anchored off Calicut, one of the busiest ports of the Indian Ocean. The visitors imagined they had reached the edge of a world; in fact they had entered one of the oldest and most sophisticated commercial systems on earth.

This chapter argues that the Portuguese did not create Indian Ocean commerce. They entered an existing system and tried to redirect parts of it through naval coercion, alliances, fortified enclaves, and licensing. Their intervention changed maritime relations without ending Asian commercial predominance.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions. It first reconstructs the pre-1498 ocean, then the South Asian ports and merchants that anchored it. It then follows Portuguese expansion from Atlantic Portugal to Calicut, Cochin, and Goa, examines naval warfare and the cartaz, and considers the Estado da India, religion, resistance, collaboration, and the limits of European power.

It also asks whose history is being told. Portuguese writers produced the most detailed narratives, but coastal rulers, shipowners, sailors, and laborers shaped outcomes, and the chapter tries to keep them visible rather than treating the Portuguese arrival as the beginning of the story.

Three questions run through the chapter. What kind of commercial world did the Portuguese enter? What did they actually achieve, and by what means? And how did coastal rulers, merchants, sailors, and communities respond? Answering them requires holding together an economic system, a series of naval and political campaigns, and the lives of people who rarely appear in the sources.

Chapter 47 treated Gujarat and the regional sultanates, and Chapter 46 the Deccan; here the focus is the sea. Chapter 50 returns to northern India. Evidence is uneven: Portuguese sources are abundant but partisan, while Asian merchant voices survive in fragments.

Historical Context

By the fifteenth century Gujarat, Malabar, and the eastern coasts were integrated into trade running from East Africa to China. Regional states such as Vijayanagara, the Bahmanis, and the Gujarat Sultanate depended on maritime revenue, while Mamluk Egypt and Hormuz controlled western gateways.

Religiously the coast was plural. Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Christian, and Jewish communities lived and traded together, and Islam spread through trade, intermarriage, and Sufi networks (see Chapter 48). Politically, no empire ruled the ports: small kingdoms and sultanates competed. This fragmented setting, together with the wealth of the trade, shaped how the Portuguese could act.

Chapter 48 examined devotional and Sufi movements that shaped religious life on land; this chapter returns to religion only where it affected maritime communities, missions, and colonial policy. Chapter 50 begins the Mughal story, which would later bring an inland empire into contact with the Gujarat coast and its European neighbors.

Environment mattered as much as politics. Monsoon timing, coastal harbors, river mouths, and the pepper-growing Ghats shaped which ports flourished. Climate and geography set limits for every power, including Portugal, that tried to organize the ocean.

Division I — The Ocean System before 1498

Section 49.1

The Indian Ocean before the Portuguese: Geography, Sources, and Commercial Organization

The Indian Ocean before 1498 was not an empty highway awaiting European discovery but a densely used commercial world. It linked the Swahili coast and Arabia with Iran, the western and eastern coasts of South Asia, the Maldives and Sri Lanka, maritime Southeast Asia, and Ming China. Historians such as K. N. Chaudhuri have described it as a set of interlocking circuits rather than a single system: ships, merchants, and goods moved along overlapping segments, and many cargoes changed hands several times before reaching a final market. No empire administered the whole, and no one merchant community controlled it.

The evidence for this world is varied and uneven. Arabic and Persian geographies and travelogues, including Ibn Battuta's account of the fourteenth century, describe ports and customs. Chinese records, such as the narratives associated with the Ming voyages of Zheng He, mention Calicut and other Indian ports in the early fifteenth century. Inscriptions, coins, and shipwreck and port archaeology document material exchange. Merchant letters, above all the Cairo Geniza documents of roughly 1080 to 1240, reveal how traders communicated, borrowed, and formed partnerships, though for an earlier period than the one this chapter concentrates on.

This documentary landscape has a distortion built into it. After 1498 the Portuguese produced large quantities of letters, chronicles, fort records, and descriptions, and these became the most accessible record of the western Indian coast. Asian sources from the same decades survive in fewer numbers or have been less studied, so a researcher can easily mistake the density of Portuguese writing for the importance of Portuguese activity. Reading the chapter's evidence therefore requires asking, each time, who wrote it, for whom, and what the writer could not see.

The organization of the commercial world must be reconstructed with the same caution. Merchant communities, port authorities, shipowners, brokers, and rulers each had distinct roles, and those roles varied by port and period. The rest of this division examines the winds, gateways, regional connections, merchants, ships, and goods that made up the system the Portuguese entered.

Section 49.2

Monsoon Winds, Seasonal Navigation, and the Geography of Maritime Exchange

The rhythm of the ocean was set by the monsoons. During the southwest monsoon, which begins in early summer, steady winds from the southwest blow across the Arabian Sea and carry sailing ships toward the Indian west coast. During the northeast monsoon, in winter, the pattern reverses and winds carry ships back toward Arabia and East Africa. In the Bay of Bengal and across to Southeast Asia, trading voyages likewise followed seasonal wind patterns. This regularity allowed merchants to plan year-long cycles of departure, trading, and return.

The same seasonal pattern shaped port life. On the Malabar coast, heavy summer rains and surf made the sea dangerous for ocean-going ships, and ports were effectively closed to long voyages for part of the season. Departures for the west clustered in the months after the rains. Merchants who arrived with the southwest monsoon therefore had months in port, in which they sold imports, bought return cargoes, borrowed money, and waited for the winds to turn. This waiting generated demand for warehouses, lodgings, brokers, and credit, and it made resident merchant communities valuable.

Monsoon timing also encouraged relay trade. Rather than every ship traveling the whole distance, merchants often sailed one segment of the route and transferred goods at intermediary ports such as Calicut, Cambay, Hormuz, or Malacca. The pattern gave South Asian ports a central position, since they stood at the meeting point of the western and eastern monsoon circuits.

Regularity did not mean safety. Storms at the monsoon's onset, shoals and reefs including those around the Maldives, and piracy along parts of the Gujarat and Malabar coasts threatened voyages. Pilots accumulated and recorded knowledge of winds, coasts, and stars; the fifteenth-century navigator and writer Ahmad ibn Majid composed influential navigational works. The environment was thus both a framework for trade and a source of risk that merchants spread through partnerships and shared ownership.

Section 49.3

The Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and Western Indian Ocean Gateways

The western gateways connected Indian Ocean goods to Mediterranean consumers. At the mouth of the Red Sea, Aden, under the Rasulid dynasty of Yemen in much of the period, served as a transfer point where goods from India and East Africa were reloaded. Jeddah, port of the holy cities, received ships and pilgrims and forwarded goods to Egypt. From there, caravans and river routes carried merchandise to Cairo and Alexandria, where European merchants, notably Venetians, purchased spices and other Asian goods.

Egypt and the Hijaz were under the Mamluk sultanate, whose rulers drew substantial revenue from the transit trade. Egyptian merchants, including the group often called Karimis in the sources, were prominent in the spice trade in earlier centuries, and during the fifteenth century the Mamluk state intervened more directly in it, at times attempting to control particular commodities. This state interest explains why later Portuguese attempts to divert spices around Africa mattered to Cairo and to Venice.

The Persian Gulf formed a second gateway. Hormuz, an island kingdom at the Gulf's entrance, was a major transshipment center and a principal supplier of Arabian and Iranian horses to Indian rulers. Gulf routes connected to Basra and Iraq and, by land, to Iran and Anatolia. Hormuz's rulers prospered from customs and from their position between the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.

These gateways were not controlled by a single group. Arab, Persian, Indian, Jewish, Egyptian, and later Italian merchants all took part, and rulers at each point taxed and sometimes protected traffic. The combination of multiple routes, multiple intermediaries, and multiple rulers means that any claim to control a single chokepoint, as the Portuguese would later attempt, had to contend with alternative paths and with states that depended on the revenue.

Section 49.4

East Africa, Arabia, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asian Connections

The ocean's rims were as important as its Indian center. On the East African coast, the Swahili city-states, including Kilwa, Mombasa, and Malindi, were Muslim-majority trading towns linked by language, architecture, and commerce. They exported gold, which reached the coast through Sofala from the interior, along with ivory and timber, and imported Indian textiles, beads, and other manufactures. Their ruling houses and merchants dealt with Arabian and Indian traders and drew prestige and wealth from this trade.

Arabia contributed ports, shipping, and merchants. Aden and the coasts of Oman and Hadramawt provided harbors, pilots, and commercial communities that settled in Indian and African ports. Arabian horses were among the most valuable exports to South Asia.

South Asia itself extended beyond the subcontinent's mainland. Sri Lanka supplied cinnamon, gems, and pearls, and its harbors lay on routes between the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea. The Maldives produced cowrie shells, used as currency in parts of Bengal and in Africa, and coir fiber for ship cordage. These island societies were part of South Asian commerce, and their inclusion corrects maps that end at the mainland coast.

To the east, Malacca rose around the early fifteenth century at the straits between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra. With support from Ming China and a policy of welcoming merchants, it became a major entrepot where Indian cloth was exchanged for spices from the eastern archipelago and goods from China. Gujarati and other South Asian merchants were prominent there. South Asia's position was therefore that of an intermediary between two great commercial zones, the eastern one centered on China and Southeast Asia and the western one on Arabia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean.

Section 49.5

Arab, Persian, Jewish, Gujarati, and South Indian Merchant Networks

Long-distance trade depended on trust, and trust was built through institutions. Merchants relied on family and kin networks, partnerships in which one traded abroad with another's capital, agents stationed in distant ports, and shared religious and community institutions. Resident merchant communities lived in foreign ports under the protection of local rulers and often under their own customary or religious law. Such communities included Arab and Persian Muslims, Jewish traders, Gujarati and other Indian merchants, Tamil Chettis of the Coromandel coast, and Chinese traders in some ports.

The Cairo Geniza letters of about 1080 to 1240 provide a rare close view of this world. Written largely in Judeo-Arabic by Jewish merchants, they discuss shipments, prices, debts, and the difficulties of long-distance communication. They include the papers of Abraham Ben Yiju, a trader who lived for years on India's southwest coast, at Mangalore, and whose records show cooperation with local and Muslim associates. They do not describe the fifteenth century, and Jewish merchants were one community among many, so the letters illustrate how such networks could work rather than defining the system around 1498.

Within South Asia, specialized groups handled particular roles. Shipowners and captains, brokers who mediated between foreign and local traders, bankers and money-changers, and interpreters formed a service economy around the ports. Gujarati and Chettiar merchants financed voyages and trading ventures, and Malabar's Muslim communities supplied shipping and crews. These roles overlapped and changed, and religion did not rigidly determine occupation.

Commercial cooperation often crossed religious lines. Hindu rulers granted privileges to Muslim merchants, Muslim merchants worked with Hindu brokers and financiers, and port authorities mediated disputes. This cooperation, together with the diversity of merchant groups, helps explain why the arrival of a newcomer who claimed religious and political superiority would disturb established arrangements.

Section 49.6

Ships, Navigation, Maritime Technology, and Commercial Risk

Ships of the Indian Ocean were diverse. In Arabia and western India, many large merchant vessels were built with planks sewn together with coir, a technique well suited to flexible hulls and landing on open beaches. Other vessels used nails and iron fastenings, and large Chinese junks, which visited Indian ports in the early fifteenth century, had distinctive multiple watertight compartments. In Southeast Asia, builders produced very large ships. Describing all Asian ships as one type, or as technologically simple, would be inaccurate.

Navigation combined practical and written knowledge. Pilots used star observation, instruments such as the kamal, which measured stellar altitude, the magnetic compass in use by the fifteenth century, and rutters, or written sailing directions. Ibn Majid's navigational writings are the best-known example of this tradition. Coastal knowledge was equally important: experienced pilots recognized landmarks, depths, and seasonal winds.

Ownership and risk were organized collectively. Vessels were often owned by several partners, and cargoes belonging to many merchants were carried on one ship, so a wreck did not ruin a single trader. Rulers and port authorities sometimes provided protection or required payments, and in places coastal chiefs or pirates demanded tribute. Maritime trade was therefore a managed risk rather than a gamble.

Armament requires careful statement. Merchant ships typically carried limited defensive weapons, since commerce was usually conducted under the protection of rulers and customary arrangements rather than state-organized naval force. Firearms and artillery were present in the region by the fifteenth century and spread quickly, but the Portuguese introduced a more systematic combination of heavy ship-mounted guns and vessels built for fighting. That difference mattered in specific encounters without making Portuguese ships superior in every respect.

Section 49.7

Commodities, Money, Credit, and the Economic Integration of the Ocean

The ocean's goods can be grouped by origin and function. Black pepper came chiefly from Malabar. Cloves, nutmeg, and mace came from the eastern archipelago, cinnamon from Sri Lanka, and ginger and other spices from several regions. Cotton textiles from Gujarat, Coromandel, and Bengal were among the most widely exchanged manufactured goods. East Africa supplied gold and ivory; the Maldives, cowries; and China, porcelain and silk. Horses flowed from Arabia and Iran to Indian rulers, and metals such as copper, tin, and silver moved across the ocean.

Trade in enslaved people also formed part of the system. Africans, South Asians, and others were bought and sold across the ocean, and enslaved labor served in households, ships, and armies. Slavery did not begin with the Portuguese, though they expanded and reorganized it.

Exchange relied on money and credit. Gold and silver coins of different states circulated, along with local currencies such as the Malabar fanam, and cowries served as small change in some regions. Merchants used partnerships, advances, and credit instruments, and bankers and money-changers in Indian ports facilitated payments across distances. Differences in prices between ports created profits for those who could carry goods across seasons.

Distinguishing kinds of trade matters. Luxury goods such as spices were valuable relative to their weight and drew the attention of rulers and, later, Europeans. Bulk goods such as rice, grain, salt, and timber moved in large quantities on regional routes and supported coastal populations. Historians cannot easily compare the total commercial value of these categories, because comparable quantitative evidence is lacking, so statements about which was larger should be avoided or carefully qualified. Both mattered to the economic integration of the ocean.

Section 49.8

Gujarat and the Western Indian Ocean: Cambay, Bharuch, and Maritime Enterprise

Gujarat's coast faced the western ocean and drew on some of South Asia's most productive manufacturing districts. Its ports included Cambay on the gulf of Khambhat, Bharuch to the south, and harbors such as Gogha and Diu on the Kathiawar coast. Cambay's gulf was subject to strong tides and silting, which complicated navigation, but the port long remained a principal outlet for Gujarat's goods. Diu, on an island off the southern coast, became increasingly important as an easily defended harbor. The Gujarat Sultanate, whose political history belongs to Chapter 47, ruled these ports from the early fifteenth century, and rulers such as Mahmud Begada (r. 1458-1511) had strong fiscal reasons to protect them.

Gujarati ships and merchants reached Aden and Jeddah on the Red Sea, Hormuz in the Gulf, the Swahili coast, and Malacca. They carried textiles, indigo, and other goods outward and brought back horses, gold, spices, and other imports. Gujarat's importance in the ocean therefore exceeded its political size: its merchants were found in nearly every major port from East Africa to Southeast Asia, and their commercial networks shaped how others experienced the trade.

This position explains Gujarat's later reaction to Portuguese expansion. A power that sought to license shipping, inspect cargoes, and station fleets at Gujarat's entrances directly threatened the revenue of the sultanate and the profit of its merchants. The conflict at Chaul and Diu, the later cession of Bassein and Diu, and the sieges of Diu in 1538 and 1546 followed from the high stakes of Gujarat's maritime economy. At the same time, many Gujarati merchants and officials, including governors of Diu, were willing to negotiate with the Portuguese when it served their interests. The pre-1498 commercial order thus gave Gujarat both the capacity to resist and strong incentives to adapt.

Section 49.9

Gujarati Textiles, Merchants, Finance, and Shipping Networks

Gujarat's wealth rested on manufacturing as well as shipping. Weavers, dyers, and printers of western India produced cotton cloth in many grades, from coarse goods for ordinary consumers to fine and patterned textiles for elite markets. Indigo, produced in Gujarat, was another important export, and the bead industry of Cambay, which worked agate and carnelian, supplied ornaments for Indian, Arabian, and African consumers. These goods were exchanged in many ports, and textiles in particular served almost as a currency in some trades: in Southeast Asia, Indian cloth was the usual means of buying spices, and in East Africa it was exchanged for gold and ivory.

Production was linked to ports through chains of intermediaries. Merchants advanced funds to artisans, brokers arranged purchases, and carters and boatmen moved cloth from inland towns to harbors. Financiers and money-changers, sometimes called shroffs in Indian sources, discounted bills, exchanged coins, and lent capital for voyages. Bills of exchange such as the hundi allowed payments to be made across distances without moving coin. These financial instruments sustained trade over seasons and sailing cycles, and they connected inland production to maritime profits.

The merchants who organized this system came from many communities, including Vania and Jain traders, Muslim groups such as the Bohras and Khojas, Arab and Persian residents, and others. Roles overlapped and shifted, and historians caution against assigning rigid economic functions by religion. A Muslim merchant might own ships and a Hindu or Jain merchant might finance them, but families of every community also acted as brokers, bankers, and shippers, and partnerships crossed religious lines. This pluralism of merchant groups is essential background for understanding why Portuguese policies framed in religious terms met a commercial world that did not divide neatly along them.

Section 49.10

The Malabar Coast: Pepper Cultivation, Hinterlands, and the Spice Economy

The Malabar coast was the main source of black pepper. The pepper vine grew in the humid hills and gardens of the Western Ghats foothills and coastal plain, and the crop was harvested and dried by cultivators before moving to markets. Landholding was shaped by local elites, including Nayar and Brahmin landholders, and the Syrian Christian community of Malabar, which long predated the Portuguese, took a prominent part in pepper cultivation and trade. Other regional products, including ginger, cardamom, coconut, and coir, were also exported. Black pepper must be distinguished from the cloves, nutmeg, and mace that came from the eastern archipelago, and from cinnamon, which came from Sri Lanka.

Pepper reached the coast through river routes, paths, and coastal craft. Intermediaries collected supplies from growers, stored them in warehouses, and sold them to merchants arriving by sea. Prices varied with harvests, demand, and the arrival of ships, and credit advances secured future supplies. Brokers, including local merchants and members of resident foreign communities, mediated between cultivators and overseas buyers. The system involved many small producers and several tiers of middlemen, which made it hard for any single actor to control the whole.

Rulers benefited from pepper through taxation and tribute and used it to build alliances. The Zamorin, the rulers of Cochin and Cannanore, and lesser chiefs each sought a share of the trade, and the relationship between inland producers and coastal ports was a political as well as an economic matter. The Portuguese would soon understand that whoever could secure pepper supplies and control the port outlets could gain leverage over coastal politics. They would also discover that the fragmented structure of production and the independence of many suppliers made monopoly more a claim than a practice.

Section 49.11

Calicut and the Zamorin: The Political Organization of a Commercial Port

Calicut was the leading commercial port of the Malabar coast in the fifteenth century. It was ruled by the Zamorin (Samutiri), a Hindu ruler whose title is often interpreted as meaning lord of the sea. The port exported pepper and ginger and received textiles, horses, Chinese goods, and other imports. Visitors recorded its importance: Ibn Battuta described it in the fourteenth century, and Chinese fleets under Zheng He called there in the early fifteenth century. Its prosperity was a political achievement as well as a geographical one.

The Zamorin's policy was to attract merchants. He offered protection, a predictable customs regime, and privileges to foreign traders, and the port was open to merchants of many origins, including Arab, Persian, Gujarati, and Chinese traders. Local Muslim communities, often called Mappilas, supplied shipowners, sailors, and merchants, and some became powerful, while port officials managed disputes and collected duties. Customs revenue and gifts from merchants sustained the court and the Nayar military elite on which the Zamorin's authority depended. The ruler's position also involved ceremonial and ritual obligations to local lords.

This arrangement made the ruler dependent on merchants and merchants dependent on the ruler's protection. A threat to merchant interests threatened royal income, and a ruler who failed to maintain order risked losing traders to other ports such as Cochin and Cannanore. For this reason the arrival of the Portuguese, who proposed to take a share of trade and to exclude rivals, raised a direct political problem for the Zamorin, not only a commercial one. Calicut's later history of resistance and negotiation follows from this structure.

The Zamorin's position was also regional. He competed with neighboring rulers, including the rulers of Cochin and Venad, and maintained claims over smaller chiefs. The fortunes of Calicut therefore depended on both maritime commerce and the shifting politics of the Malabar coast, which the next section examines.

Section 49.12

Cochin, Cannanore, Quilon, and Competition among Malabar Ports

Calicut's rivals shared its commercial interests but not its political ambitions. Cochin rose in importance after the fourteenth century, when changes to the Periyar river's mouth are commonly understood to have opened a harbor and shifted trade away from older sites. Its rulers, who were nominally subordinate to the Zamorin, resented that subordination and welcomed merchants who might strengthen their position. Cochin was also home to Jewish, Syrian Christian, and Muslim merchants.

Cannanore, to the north, was the principal port of the Kolathiri rulers and a center for the import of horses and the export of pepper and ginger, with a substantial Muslim merchant community. Quilon, to the south, had been a major port in earlier centuries, linked to China and Arabia, but its relative importance declined by the fifteenth century as other ports grew. Smaller harbors and local chiefs completed a patchwork of competing jurisdictions.

The rivalry among these ports and rulers involved dynastic claims, tribute, and control of the pepper hinterland. Alliances formed and dissolved, and small kingdoms used merchants and foreign powers to improve their positions against their neighbors. This competitive setting explains why the Portuguese found willing partners. Cochin's ruler, who wanted protection from Calicut, and the rulers of Cannanore offered anchorage, supplies, and a base. Their cooperation was not simple submission but a strategy within a long-standing regional contest, and it brought dependence on the Portuguese that these rulers later had to manage.

Section 49.13

Coromandel, Sri Lanka, Bengal, and the Bay of Bengal Trading System

The eastern coasts formed a second major commercial pole. On the Coromandel coast, ports such as Pulicat, Mylapore, and Nagapattinam exported cotton textiles to Southeast Asia and received spices, metals, and other goods in return. Tamil merchant communities, including the Chettis, were active in this trade, and textile-producing districts inland supplied weavers. Vijayanagara, whose political history is treated in Chapter 45, depended on these ports and on access to western ports for horse imports, and its rulers took an interest in maritime revenue.

Bengal's ports, including Satgaon and Chittagong, linked the Ganges delta to the Bay of Bengal. The Bengal Sultanate, discussed in Chapter 47, ruled a productive agrarian and manufacturing region that exported textiles, rice, and other goods and imported cowries from the Maldives, which circulated as small currency. Sri Lanka, ruled by the Kotte kingdom in the early sixteenth century, exported cinnamon, elephants, and gems, and the pearl fisheries of the Gulf of Mannar drew divers and merchants.

These regions were connected to the west through the Indian peninsula's own internal and coastal trade, through Sri Lanka and the southern tip of the peninsula, and through shared merchant networks. Textile exchange between the eastern coasts and Southeast Asia was among the largest flows in the ocean, and its operation did not depend on the western routes where the Portuguese first established themselves. This is one reason that Portuguese success on the Malabar coast and at Goa did not give them control of the Bay of Bengal's commerce, a point developed later in the chapter.

Section 49.14

Port Cities as Social Worlds: Merchants, Sailors, Laborers, and Religious Communities

Port cities were social worlds that functioned through the labor of many people. Dockworkers and porters loaded and unloaded cargo, shipbuilders and carpenters constructed and repaired vessels, sailors crewed ships, and brokers and translators arranged transactions between merchants who spoke different languages. Food suppliers, water carriers, boat operators, and domestic servants sustained the port's population. Fishing communities, such as the Mukkuvar of Malabar and the Paravas of the southeastern coast, were also tied to maritime life, and the Paravas controlled aspects of the pearl fishery.

Residential patterns reflected commercial life. Merchant communities often lived in distinct quarters with their own mosques, temples, or churches, and customary or religious law governed disputes within communities. Rulers appointed officials to oversee relations between foreign merchants and local authorities. These arrangements allowed groups with different languages, laws, and religions to live in close proximity, and they were maintained because ports depended on their trade.

Inequality was a structural feature of this world. Wealthy merchants and shipowners profited most, while laborers, sailors, and enslaved people had little bargaining power, and caste and status distinctions shaped who could do which work. Women appear in the sources in limited ways, as household managers, traders, and workers in textile production, though the evidence is thin and mostly written by men. Because most surviving records were produced by elites, the experience of ordinary port residents must be inferred from fragments.

Division III — Portuguese Expansion and First Encounters

Section 49.15

Portugal's Atlantic Expansion and the Search for a Sea Route to Asia

Portuguese expansion began as a combination of crusade, commerce, and statecraft. In 1415 King John I and his sons, including Prince Henry, captured the North African city of Ceuta, a Muslim trading town at the end of caravan routes carrying Saharan gold. The conquest had religious meaning for a crusading monarchy, but it also reflected hopes of profit, access to grain and gold, and noble ambitions for status. From this base, Portuguese expeditions moved along the Atlantic coast of Africa.

Over the following decades Portuguese ships settled Madeira and the Azores, passed Cape Bojador in 1434, and reached the Senegal and Gambia regions and Cape Verde. The Portuguese began buying and seizing enslaved Africans in the 1440s, and the trade became a lasting source of profit. In 1482 they built the fortress of Sao Jorge da Mina on the Gold Coast to secure gold. Technical learning accompanied these voyages: improved ships, such as the caravel, knowledge of Atlantic winds and currents, and navigational tables and instruments. Royal sponsorship concentrated funds, charts, and information.

The idea of reaching India by sea was driven by commercial and religious aims. Portuguese rulers wanted a share of the spice trade that passed through Egypt and Venice, and they hoped for allies against Muslim powers, including the legendary Christian ruler Prester John. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa and showed that the Atlantic and Indian Oceans were connected by sea. Meanwhile the Crown sent agents overland, including Pero da Covilha, who traveled to the Indian west coast and East Africa and reported on the trade. The accumulated knowledge made a voyage to India feasible once King Manuel I (r. 1495-1521) chose to attempt it, after Columbus's voyages and the Spanish-Portuguese Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 had pushed the rivalry to the oceans.

Section 49.16

Vasco da Gama's Voyage, 1497-1499: Navigation, Pilots, and Arrival at Calicut

Vasco da Gama left Lisbon in July 1497 with four vessels. The fleet sailed far into the South Atlantic to find favorable winds, then rounded the Cape in November. On the East African coast, the Portuguese met mixed receptions. They were able to obtain supplies at some ports, but at Mozambique and Mombasa relations deteriorated into suspicion and clashes. At Malindi, whose ruler was a rival of Mombasa, they were received more favorably and obtained a pilot to guide them across the Arabian Sea.

Accounts describe this pilot as an experienced navigator, often said to have come from Gujarat, but his identity is uncertain, and the popular identification with the famous navigator Ibn Majid is unsupported by good evidence. What matters historically is that the crossing depended on local knowledge. The Portuguese reached the Indian coast in about three weeks and anchored near Calicut on 20 May 1498. The voyage was a notable achievement of Atlantic seamanship, but its final stage relied on the expertise of the Indian Ocean's pilots.

The return was costly. Leaving Calicut in August 1498, the fleet struggled against unfavorable winds, and the crossing took about three months. Scurvy and other diseases killed many of the crew, and the surviving ships reached Portugal in 1499 with only perhaps a third of the men who had set out. Nonetheless, the Crown judged the expedition a success, since it had reached the spice markets of Malabar and brought back samples, and it showed that spices could be obtained without passing through the Red Sea and Egypt.

The Portuguese concluded that a direct route existed, but also that obtaining a commercial position would not be simple. They had found a rich port with established rulers and merchants who had no need of what they offered. Their later policy was shaped by this realization.

Section 49.17

The First Calicut Encounter: Diplomacy, Commerce, and Misunderstanding

The first encounter at Calicut was an exchange between parties with different expectations. The Portuguese sent men ashore, among them a convict who was lodged in the city, and they found a local Muslim who spoke a European language, a Tunisian merchant called Monçaide in the sources, who acted as an interpreter and guide. Da Gama was then received by the Zamorin, and he presented a letter from King Manuel and gifts.

The gifts proved a problem. Contemporary accounts report that officials and merchants regarded them as modest and unsuitable for a royal court, and the goods the Portuguese had brought were not those in demand at Calicut. Local custom expected substantial presents as part of diplomatic ritual. The Zamorin, who had much experience of foreign envoys, probably regarded da Gama as one visitor among many, whereas the Portuguese, proceeding from the logic of Iberian monarchy and crusade, sought a formal alliance and privileges.

Resident Muslim merchants had good reason to be wary of a newcomer who came armed, declined to pay customary dues, and framed his mission in terms of religious rivalry. Disputes about customs and the landing of goods led to a period in which some Portuguese were detained before being released. In the end the Zamorin sent a letter to King Manuel inviting trade in goods such as gold, silver, and coral, but da Gama left without a formal treaty and with a small cargo.

The encounter shows that misunderstanding was not simply cultural. Both sides pursued interests, and both interpreted the other through established categories: the Zamorin through the practice of hospitable commerce, the Portuguese through the experience of Atlantic conquest. The ambiguities of the meeting set the stage for later conflict.

Section 49.18

Reading the Portuguese Arrival: The Roteiro, Commercial Interests, and Historical Perspective

Much of what is known about da Gama's first voyage comes from the Roteiro, an anonymous narrative traditionally attributed to Alvaro Velho, who sailed in the fleet. It survives in a single manuscript preserved in Porto and was first published in 1838. It is a valuable record of the voyage's daily events and of the participants' impressions of ports and peoples, written near the events by someone who was present.

Its limitations are equally important. The author wrote from the viewpoint of a Portuguese participant, expecting Christians in India and interpreting local religions through that expectation. It reports what the Portuguese could see and understand, mediated by interpreters, and it expresses suspicion and hostility toward the Muslim merchants of Calicut. It says little about the Zamorin's reasoning or about how Calicut's merchants understood the visitors.

Later Portuguese chroniclers, including Joao de Barros, Gaspar Correia, and Fernao Lopes de Castanheda, wrote more extensive histories, but they wrote decades afterward, from official or literary perspectives, and sometimes embellished or reorganized earlier accounts. Indian and Asian perspectives survive more thinly. A prominent example is the Tuhfat al-Mujahidin, composed in the 1580s by Zainuddin Makhdum, a Muslim scholar of Malabar, which presents the Portuguese as an oppressive force and calls for resistance. It, too, was written later and for a purpose.

Historians therefore work comparatively, reading Portuguese narratives against Arabic, Malayalam, and other sources where available, and acknowledging the difficulty of reconstructing the Zamorin's and local merchants' views directly. The student's task is to identify what each source can and cannot show.

Section 49.19

Pedro Alvares Cabral, the Return to Calicut, and Escalating Conflict in 1500

The second Portuguese expedition, under Pedro Alvares Cabral, was much larger. It left Portugal in March 1500 with thirteen ships, reached the coast of Brazil in April, and lost several vessels on the way around the Cape. It arrived at Calicut in September 1500. This time the Portuguese came with greater force and clearer commercial intentions, and they sought an agreement to establish a trading post, a feitoria, to buy spices.

The Zamorin initially allowed a post to be set up, with a Portuguese factor in charge. Competition for cargo soon produced conflict. Portuguese forces seized a merchant ship in a dispute over loading, and in December 1500 a crowd attacked the trading post, killing many of the Portuguese, perhaps about fifty, including the factor. The sources disagree on the sequence of provocations, and the Portuguese account naturally stresses Muslim merchant hostility.

Cabral responded by seizing Arab ships in the harbor and bombarding Calicut for a day or more. He then sailed south to Cochin, where the ruler received him and allowed the Portuguese to obtain pepper, and he also made contact at Cannanore. The episode combined commercial rivalry, diplomatic failure, and a decision to use coercion. Historians debate how much weight each element deserves, but the pattern is clear: Calicut, with the strongest interest in maintaining existing trade, became the Portuguese opponent, while its rivals saw an opportunity.

Historical Vocabulary

Feitoria

A feitoria was a trading factory or station through which agents bought and sold goods, such as Cabral's post at Calicut. It need not be fortified, though the Portuguese often added forts. Equating every feitoria with a fortress misrepresents how Portuguese commerce first operated.

Section 49.20

Vasco da Gama's Second Voyage, 1502, and the First Portuguese Footholds

Da Gama returned to India in 1502 with a much larger fleet and an openly coercive policy. On the East African coast he forced tribute from the ruler of Kilwa. In the Arabian Sea his ships attacked merchant vessels, and in October 1502 they captured the ship Meri, which carried Muslim pilgrims returning from Mecca. The Portuguese plundered it and burned it with heavy loss of life. This action, described in Portuguese chronicles, demonstrates the readiness to use violence against unarmed shipping.

Off Calicut, da Gama demanded that the Zamorin expel Muslim merchants, and when he did not comply, bombarded the city. Portuguese chronicles describe the killing and mutilation of captives. These actions were intended to impose Portuguese terms through terror, but they did not break Calicut's resistance. They did strengthen the determination of the Zamorin and many merchants, and they added to the Portuguese reputation for brutality.

At Cochin, the ruler welcomed the Portuguese and allowed them to buy pepper. In 1503, after da Gama's departure, Portuguese forces under Afonso and Francisco de Albuquerque built a fortified position, the first fort in India, with the ruler's agreement. The Zamorin attacked Cochin in 1504, and the Portuguese, under Duarte Pacheco Pereira, helped defend it, which bound the Portuguese more closely to their ally.

By 1503 the Portuguese had a foothold on the pepper coast. It rested on the cooperation of a local ruler whose own interests were opposed to Calicut's, and it committed them to a continuing military presence. The shift from isolated expeditions to a lasting base marks the transition from the first encounters to the organized campaigns discussed in the next division.

Division IV — Naval Warfare and Strategy

Section 49.21

Portuguese Oceanic Warfare: Armed Ships, Artillery, and Coastal Fortresses

Portuguese power in the Indian Ocean rested on a particular combination of ships, guns, and bases. Their larger vessels, the carracks, carried heavy artillery mounted along the sides, while caravels and galleys provided mobility. Portuguese captains preferred to fight at a distance with cannon fire rather than board high-sided merchant ships, and in a series of encounters this gave them advantages over fleets of merchant vessels and the lightly armed oared boats of coastal powers. The advantage was real but specific: Asian states also used artillery, and their fleets could be formidable when organized for war.

Ships required bases. Fortified positions provided anchorage, repair, supplies, and refuge, and they allowed the Portuguese to project power along a coast. The forts at Kilwa, Cochin, and Cannanore were built in 1503 to 1505 and later bases followed. Convoys protected cargoes on the Lisbon route and in the Asian trade, and patrols guarded key waters. This system worked best in narrow waters where traffic had to pass within reach of a fortress or fleet.

Its limits were equally clear. The Portuguese in Asia numbered only a few thousand at any time, and they relied on local recruits, enslaved crews, and supplies bought from local suppliers. Naval strength could harass shipping and blockade ports, but it could not occupy inland territory or compel producers to sell. The Portuguese commanded the sea in particular places and times; they did not command the commerce that depended on thousands of vessels, ports, and producers beyond their reach.

Section 49.22

Francisco de Almeida, the First Viceroyalty, and the Struggle for Maritime Control

Francisco de Almeida, appointed viceroy in 1505, set out with a large fleet and instructions to establish Portuguese authority in the Indian Ocean. He built forts on the East African coast, sacked Mombasa, and established the fortresses at Cannanore and Cochin, binding the Cochin ruler to the Crown as ally and subordinate. His son Lourenco explored the waters off Sri Lanka and the Maldives, and in 1506 a Portuguese squadron defeated a fleet of the Zamorin off Cannanore.

Almeida's central policy, often called the blue-water strategy, was to control the sea with fleets and a minimum of land bases rather than to conquer territory. He argued that fortresses beyond a few strategic points would drain resources and manpower, and that dominance at sea would secure the Crown's interests. This position reflected a realistic assessment of Portuguese capacities and an understanding of costs.

The viceroyalty created the first official Portuguese administration in the East, with a viceroy, captains of forts, and commercial officials, answering to the Crown. Almeida's approach was opposed by Afonso de Albuquerque, who favored seizing key ports as permanent bases, and the disagreement reflected a persistent dilemma: whether a small power could maintain a maritime hegemony without territorial footholds, and how much territory it could afford. The question would shape the course of Portuguese expansion in the next decade.

Section 49.23

Chaul, 1508, and Diu, 1509: Gujarat, Egypt, and Portuguese Naval Power

The conflict with Gujarat and Egypt arose from the threat that Portuguese interference posed to the spice trade through the Red Sea. The Mamluk sultan, whose revenues depended on the transit trade, sent a fleet to the Indian Ocean under Amir Husain al-Kurdi, and Gujarat, whose governor of Diu, Malik Ayaz, controlled a major port, cooperated with it. Historians have also noted Venetian interest in Mamluk preparations, since Venice depended on spices arriving through Egypt.

In January 1508 the combined forces defeated a Portuguese squadron at Chaul, and Almeida's son Lourenco was killed. The Viceroy, determined to avenge his son, sought out the allied fleet, and in February 1509 he destroyed it at Diu. The Mamluk commander escaped, and the result established a Portuguese advantage in the western ocean. The Mamluk state was soon weakened, and the Ottomans conquered Egypt in 1517.

Diu was a strategic victory but not a decisive one. Malik Ayaz proved willing to negotiate with the Portuguese, and Gujarati shipping continued to trade. Calicut remained independent, and other routes remained open. The battle showed that Portuguese naval power could destroy a fleet in a set-piece engagement and shape the balance of power; it did not give Portugal command of the whole Indian Ocean or the capacity to eliminate Asian trade.

Section 49.24

Afonso de Albuquerque and the Conquest of Goa in 1510

Afonso de Albuquerque succeeded Almeida as governor in late 1509 and quickly turned to his plan for permanent bases. Goa, a island port on the west coast, belonged to the Sultanate of Bijapur, whose political history is treated in Chapter 46. It was important as a harbor and as an entry point for horses imported for Deccan armies, including those of Bijapur and Vijayanagara. Capturing it threatened the sultanate's supply of cavalry mounts.

Albuquerque took Goa in February 1510 with the help of local intermediaries, notably the Hindu privateer Timoji, and Bijapur's forces drove the Portuguese out in May. He returned in November 1510 with reinforcements and retook the city. Portuguese accounts record a massacre of Goa's Muslim inhabitants after the capture. Albuquerque also encouraged Portuguese men to marry local women, offering land and positions, a policy that created the settler class later called casados, and he retained local officials and village communities to collect revenue.

Goa soon acquired a customs house, a mint, a shipyard, and fortifications. Its conquest represented a strategic shift from a sea-control strategy to a combination of fleet and territorial base, and it gave the Portuguese a place to administer, tax, and settle. The conquest also involved local collaboration and exploited political divisions, which is a reminder that the Portuguese acquired Goa in part because some local actors chose to assist them.

Section 49.25

Malacca in 1511 and Hormuz in 1515: Expanding Portuguese Strategic Ambitions

Albuquerque's strategy envisioned control of the chokepoints through which Asian trade passed. In 1511 he led an expedition of perhaps a thousand men and a fleet of some seventeen ships against Malacca, the great entrepot of the straits. The city fell in August after a fight, and the Sultan, Mahmud Shah, fled and established a rival base in the south. The Portuguese built a fortress there, A Famosa.

Tome Pires's account emphasizes Malacca's role as a meeting point for merchants from many regions and the value of holding it. In practice, Portuguese control of the city did not give them control of the surrounding trade. Many Asian merchants, including Gujarati and Tamil traders, shifted to other ports, and the Sultanate of Aceh and the exiled Malacca court would later challenge Portuguese influence.

In 1515 Albuquerque seized Hormuz, at the entrance to the Gulf, and built a fort, reducing its ruler to tributary status while retaining his authority. An earlier attempt on Aden in 1513 had failed. Albuquerque died at Goa in December 1515, leaving a network of positions that stretched farther than Portuguese manpower could properly sustain. The attempt to control chokepoints demonstrates both the ambition of Portuguese strategy and the practical limitations of maintaining power across enormous distances.

Section 49.26

The Red Sea, Arabian Coasts, and the Limits of Portuguese Maritime Expansion

The Red Sea was the Portuguese goal that proved most elusive. Albuquerque's attack on Aden in 1513 failed, and he sailed into the Red Sea but withdrew. Another expedition under Lopo Soares failed at Aden in 1517. The Portuguese did penetrate the Red Sea region by 1520, when an embassy reached the Ethiopian highlands, but they could not close the route to commerce.

The political context changed in 1517 when the Ottomans conquered Egypt and took over the Mamluk position, and in 1538 they took Aden. The Ottomans became major rivals of the Portuguese, with their own interests in the spice trade and in Muslim pilgrimage routes. During the sixteenth century the spice trade through the Red Sea recovered, and Asian and Middle Eastern merchants continued to ship goods that the Portuguese had hoped to divert.

On the East African and Arabian coasts, the Portuguese built forts and raided ports, including Mozambique, Sofala, and Muscat, and they allied with Malindi against its rivals. These efforts gave them stations along the route to India but did not establish full control of the regional trade. The Red Sea case shows the limits of naval strategy against an entrenched commercial network supported by capable states.

Section 49.27

The Cartaz System: Shipping Licenses, Inspection, Blockades, and Coercion

The cartaz system was the Portuguese attempt to turn naval presence into a regulatory regime. Asian ships were expected to obtain a pass from Portuguese authorities, usually at a fort or customs house, naming the vessel and its voyage and specifying conditions. Ships were to call at Portuguese ports, pay duties, and accept inspection, and they were forbidden to carry certain goods, such as arms and, in some periods, spices reserved to the Crown. Vessels found without a pass could be seized, and their cargoes confiscated.

The system served several purposes. It generated revenue from fees and duties, it asserted a claim of sovereignty over the sea, and it allowed the Portuguese to monitor and restrict the trade of rivals. Purchase of a cartaz also carried a political meaning, since Asian rulers and merchants who accepted one acknowledged Portuguese authority, even if only for convenience.

Enforcement was uneven. Portuguese fleets patrolled key routes, particularly along the Gujarat and Malabar coasts, but the sea was too large and the number of vessels too great for continuous control. Merchants evaded patrols, used alternative ports, bribed officials, or obtained negotiated exemptions, and many ships carried a pass and smuggled goods. The system's effectiveness is debated, and the evidence suggests that the difference between claiming jurisdiction and enforcing it was large.

The cartaz illustrates the broader difference between military capacity, territorial possession, and commercial control. Portugal had considerable naval power, held strategic ports, and claimed regulatory authority, but these did not add up to command of the ocean's commerce. This is the central point of the chapter's analysis of the Portuguese system.

Historical Vocabulary

Cartaz

A cartaz was a pass issued by Portuguese authorities that gave an Asian ship permission to sail. It expressed a claim to regulate the sea, but a claim is not the same as consistent enforcement.

Division V — Goa and the Estado da India

Section 49.28

Goa as the Center of Portuguese Power in Asia

Goa grew from a captured island port into the capital of Portuguese Asia. The seat of the viceroy moved there from Cochin around 1530, and the city acquired the institutions that marked it as a center of power: a cathedral, churches and convents, a royal hospital, a charitable confraternity, a customs house, a mint, and a large shipyard on the river. The Portuguese gradually extended their holdings from the island of Tiswadi to the neighboring districts of Bardez and Salcete, acquired in 1543. The city's name became associated with the whole Estado.

Goa's prosperity depended on its hinterland and its connections. The surrounding villages supplied rice, coconuts, and other produce, and the port drew goods from the Deccan and from across the Arabian Sea. The horse trade remained central, since Arabian and Persian horses landed at Goa were bought by Deccan rulers. Village communities, which held land collectively and paid revenue, were largely retained, and local Hindu officials, clerks, and moneylenders continued to administer revenue and credit under Portuguese rule.

The population was mixed and unequal. Portuguese officials, soldiers, and clergy lived alongside Indian Christians, Hindus, Muslims, merchants of many origins, artisans, domestic servants, and enslaved people from Africa and Asia. Goa's slave markets and households reflected the extent of coerced labor in the Portuguese system. The city's size and wealth made it a stage for ceremony and display, but it depended on cooperation with regional powers and on the labor of many people whom Portuguese records mention only in passing.

Section 49.29

The Estado da India: Governors, Viceroys, Forts, and Trading Factories

The Estado da India was the Crown's name for its overseas possessions and administration in Asia. It was not a territorially continuous empire. It consisted of a chain of forts, ports, and settlements, including Cochin, Cannanore, Goa, Chaul, Diu, Hormuz, Malacca, and Colombo, and later Daman and the trading settlement of Macau, held together by fleets and officials. Some places were held outright, others through treaties with local rulers, and many Portuguese interests lay in cities they did not control.

At its head stood a viceroy or governor appointed by the Crown for a fixed term, typically three years. Fort captains, commercial agents called factors, judges, and financial officials such as the overseer of the treasury administered the system, and a high court was established at Goa in the mid-sixteenth century. In Lisbon, the Casa da India supervised the trade. Many offices were granted as rewards for service, which made the system a network of patronage as much as a bureaucracy.

Communication was slow. A letter took many months to reach Lisbon, and instructions took as long to return, so officials acted with considerable discretion. Rivalries among nobles, disputes between viceroys and captains, and the pursuit of private profit weakened central direction. The Estado functioned because it combined Crown authority with local alliances and with the initiative of individuals, and it relied extensively on Asian soldiers, sailors, clerks, and advisers.

Comparative Study

Asian and Portuguese Maritime Organization

Asian merchant shipping was mostly private. Partnerships spread risk, ships were lightly armed, and protection came largely from rulers' port policies and customary arrangements. The Portuguese system was Crown-directed, using armed fleets, fortified bases, and licensing, and it had to pay for military costs through revenue extraction.

Each had strengths. Asian arrangements were flexible, cheap, and adaptable to many ports. The Portuguese arrangement could concentrate force and claim sovereignty over routes but was expensive and thinly staffed. Questions: Which was better suited to long-term trade, and why? How did each depend on the other?

Section 49.30

Customs, Royal Monopolies, Private Merchants, and Imperial Finance

The Estado's finances combined several sources. The Crown collected customs duties at its ports, fees for cartazes, and revenue from land and villages in Goa and its dependencies. Customs at Hormuz were among the most lucrative. The Crown claimed monopolies in certain goods, notably pepper, which it bought at agreed prices from suppliers and shipped home. It also granted licenses for particular voyages, such as those to Bengal, Malacca, and later Japan and China, to officials and nobles as rewards.

Private traders were essential. Portuguese casados, nobles, and officials took part in intra-Asian commerce, shipping textiles, horses, and other goods between ports, and many of them also traded illicitly in goods reserved for the Crown. Asian merchants worked with them as partners, financiers, and agents. Corruption was widespread, and officials who held posts for a few years sought to enrich themselves.

Costs were heavy. Maintaining fleets and fortresses, paying salaries, and funding military campaigns strained resources, and the Crown's income from Asia did not always cover the expense. The imperial system depended on both coercion and cooperation, since forts and fleets required revenue that could only be obtained from merchants and rulers who found it advantageous to pay. A balance between extraction and accommodation was always fragile.

Comparative Study

Coastal State Revenue and Commercial Institutions

Calicut collected customs and gifts, offered protection, and relied on merchants' presence for income. Gujarat taxed a large manufacturing and shipping economy and used governors at ports such as Diu. The Estado da India combined customs, license and cartaz fees, land revenue from places such as Goa, and Crown trade in pepper.

The first two depended on attracting merchants; the Estado also used coercion to direct them. Questions: What incentives did each system create for merchants? Why did the Portuguese system cost more?

Section 49.31

The Carreira da India: The Cape Route and the Portugal-Asia Commercial Connection

The Carreira da India was the long sea route connecting Lisbon with Asia. Fleets left Portugal in the spring, sailed around the Cape of Good Hope, called at East African ports, and crossed to India in time to arrive before the end of the sailing season, and the return left Asia in the winter months. A round trip took more than a year. In the earliest decades the route linked Lisbon with Cochin and Cannanore, before Goa became the principal hub, so the Lisbon-Asia connection was not always a Lisbon-Goa one.

The ships were large carracks, often overloaded, and the voyages were dangerous. Shipwrecks, disease, and storms caused heavy losses, and crowded conditions bred illness. Outward cargoes included soldiers, officials, missionaries, and silver and other goods to pay for Asian products, and return cargoes carried pepper, other spices, cotton textiles, indigo, and gems.

The route tied Atlantic Europe to Asian networks, but did not displace them. For some decades the Portuguese sold spices at Antwerp, a major European market, and the Crown depended on this income to finance the system. Meanwhile spices also reached Europe through the Red Sea and Mediterranean, and Venice recovered part of its trade by the mid-sixteenth century. The Carreira thus created a new link between Europe and Asia without destroying the old ones.

Section 49.32

Diu, Bassein, and the Western Indian Coastal Network

Portuguese interest in Gujarat's coast grew through the first decades of the sixteenth century. After the defeat at Diu in 1509 and the founding of a fort at Chaul in 1521, the Portuguese sought a firmer position in the north. In the 1530s, Sultan Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, facing military threats from the north, agreed to terms. In December 1534 he ceded Bassein and its dependencies, and in 1535 he permitted the Portuguese to build a fortress at Diu in exchange for support. The political threats on his northern border belong to the history of the Mughals, treated in later chapters.

Relations deteriorated quickly. In February 1537, Bahadur Shah was killed during a confrontation with the Portuguese near Diu. The Portuguese gained a foothold but also a strong enemy in Gujarat, whose rulers wanted to expel them.

These possessions played distinct roles. Bassein, with its agricultural lands and shipbuilding resources, became the center of a northern province that supplied revenue and timber. Diu, on an island commanding the entrance to the gulf of Khambhat, was a strategic fortress whose value lay in controlling movement and extracting duties. Together with Goa, Chaul, and later Daman, these bases formed a western coastal network that gave Portugal a lasting presence in the waters of Gujarat, though not mastery of Gujarati trade.

Section 49.33

Catholic Missions, Conversion, and Portuguese Religious Policy

Portuguese religious policy was tied to the Crown through the padroado, the royal patronage of the Church in Asia, which gave the Crown responsibility for missions and the right to appoint officials. Franciscans arrived early in the sixteenth century, followed by Dominicans and, in 1542, the Jesuits, among them Francis Xavier. Churches, schools, and colleges were founded at Goa, and the city became the center of Catholic activity in Asia. Missionaries combined preaching with charitable work, and Crown officials supported conversion.

Christianity was not new to South Asia. The St Thomas Christians of Malabar, who used Syriac liturgy and had a long history, were in contact with Portuguese clergy, and relations were often tense. At the Synod of Diamper in 1599 the Portuguese archbishop imposed Latin practices on the community, creating a lasting division. Other communities converted in the Portuguese period, such as the Paravas of the southeastern coast, who sought protection and support, among other motives.

Conversion had varied causes, including belief, material incentive, social pressure, and political dependence, and the responses of local communities varied. In Goa and its dependencies, authorities restricted public Hindu and Muslim ceremonies and destroyed temples in the course of the sixteenth century. Missionary activity thus shaped coastal society, but it also met resistance, accommodation, and adaptation, and converts often kept many earlier customs, including caste distinctions.

Section 49.34

The Goa Inquisition, Religious Coercion, and the Limits of Colonial Assimilation

The Goa Inquisition was established in 1560, with inquisitors arriving that year and activity beginning in 1561. It was part of the Portuguese Inquisition, an ecclesiastical tribunal under royal supervision. Its jurisdiction covered baptized Christians, and it investigated suspected heresy and religious deviance, including accusations against converts from Hinduism and Islam who allegedly continued earlier practices, and against New Christians, converts from Judaism and their descendants. Hindus and Muslims who had not been baptized were not under its jurisdiction, though they faced other Portuguese restrictions.

Procedure involved secret denunciations, imprisonment, interrogation, confiscation of property, and public ceremonies of sentencing called autos-da-fe, and the most serious punishments could include execution. Historians estimate that thousands of people were tried over the tribunal's history, though the number of executions is debated, and the destruction of many records at the end of the tribunal's existence complicates assessment. Evidence of its practice comes also from later accounts, including that of a French physician who was imprisoned in the seventeenth century.

The tribunal was part of a wider pattern of religious pressure. Combined with decrees restricting non-Christian rites and the destruction of temples, it led some families to leave Goa for neighboring territories, taking temples and images with them. Yet local identities and institutions survived, since many converts maintained customs and social ties. The Inquisition was suspended from 1774 to 1778 and was permanently abolished in 1812. A precise account distinguishes its formal jurisdiction from the broader history of conversion and coercion, while recognizing that both caused suffering and changed Goa's society.

Division VI — Resistance and Collaboration

Section 49.35

Calicut, the Zamorin, and the Kunjali Marakkars: Maritime Resistance

Resistance on the Malabar coast lasted for most of the sixteenth century and involved changing partnerships. The Zamorin of Calicut, whose commercial position the Portuguese had challenged from the start, fought them repeatedly by land and sea, but his naval forces relied on Muslim seafaring families of the coast. Among these were the Marakkars, Muslim merchants and ship commanders of Cochin and Calicut, several of whom bore the title Kunjali. They commanded fast, oared vessels suited to coastal raiding, and used the Malabar coast's rivers and inlets to attack Portuguese shipping.

The relationship between the Zamorin and the Marakkars was cooperative but unstable. In 1571 the Zamorin's forces, with Marakkar naval support, captured the Portuguese fort at Chaliyam, a significant loss for Portugal. Later the Marakkars built a fortified base at Puthuppanam, near Kottakkal, and under Kunjali Marakkar IV they claimed a degree of independence that alarmed the Zamorin. Their commercial interests, maritime ambitions, and loyalty to the court did not align.

In 1599 the Zamorin allied with the Portuguese against Kunjali Marakkar IV, and in 1600 the fort fell after a siege. Kunjali surrendered to the Zamorin, was handed to the Portuguese, and was taken to Goa, where he was executed. Historians debate how to characterize the Marakkars, as a merchant clan pursuing its interests, as defenders of Malabar, or as early symbols of anticolonial resistance, a modern interpretation not shared by contemporary evidence. The episode shows that resistance was shaped by local rivalry as well as opposition to Portugal.

Section 49.36

Cochin, Cannanore, and the Politics of Collaboration and Dependence

Cochin and Cannanore illustrate the other side of coastal politics. The ruler of Cochin, resentful of Calicut's superiority, welcomed the Portuguese from 1500 and allowed them to build a fort in 1503. Cochin's rulers received protection against Calicut and the benefit of a powerful ally, and the port prospered as a pepper outlet. But dependence was costly. The Portuguese influenced succession disputes, demanded commercial preferences, and could not be easily dismissed.

Cannanore, whose ruler allowed a Portuguese fort in 1505, followed a similar path, though relations were strained. A fort at Cannanore was besieged in 1507 by local forces, and Portuguese reinforcements relieved it. Rulers of Cannanore and neighboring chiefs accepted Portuguese protection, sold pepper and other goods, and sometimes resisted Portuguese demands.

Collaboration was strategic rather than complete. Rulers allied with Portugal against regional rivals and calculated the costs and benefits of cooperation at each point. A ruler might negotiate commercial terms with the Portuguese while trading with their opponents, or turn against them when circumstances changed. The Portuguese gained local supporters but lacked control over them, and these alliances changed with shifting regional rivalries.

Section 49.37

Gujarat, Ottoman Intervention, and Indian Political Resistance to Portuguese Expansion

Gujarat and the Ottomans also challenged Portuguese power. In 1538 an Ottoman fleet under Hadim Suleiman Pasha arrived at Diu, where Gujarat had asked for help against the Portuguese after Bahadur Shah's death. The combined forces besieged the fortress, but the siege failed and the fleet withdrew. In 1546 a different campaign, a major Gujarati assault led by the commander Khwaja Safar, besieged Diu again. Safar was killed, and the Portuguese under Joao de Mascarenhas held the fortress. The second siege was primarily a Gujarati effort, not a repeat of the Ottoman expedition.

A larger coalition acted in 1570 and 1571. Bijapur besieged Goa, Ahmadnagar besieged Chaul, and the Zamorin's forces took the fort of Chaliyam. The Portuguese under Viceroy Luis de Ataide held Goa and Chaul, but they lost Chaliyam, a significant setback. The coalition did not coordinate effectively, and its members pursued separate aims, but its simultaneous pressure shows the range of opposition.

These events show that Indian and Ottoman powers could threaten Portuguese positions and did not accept their claims. They also show the limits of those efforts: the opponents had divided objectives, and the Portuguese retained their main bases. Portuguese survival rested on several factors, including fortifications, naval support, and the lack of sustained unity among opponents.

Section 49.38

Indo-Portuguese Society: Casados, Women, Labor, Slavery, and Cultural Exchange

Portuguese settlement created a distinct coastal society. In Goa, Cochin, Chaul, Diu, and other places, Portuguese soldiers, officials, and merchants lived alongside Indian Christians, converts, and people of mixed descent. A number of Portuguese men married local women, and the Crown at times encouraged this to build a settler population, offering land and office. Few Portuguese women came to Asia, and the resulting families produced communities with Portuguese names, Catholic practice, and local connections.

The labor force included sailors, soldiers, artisans, interpreters, clerks, servants, and enslaved people. Many soldiers and crews were recruited locally, and enslaved Africans and Asians served in households, shipyards, and fleets. Language contact produced Portuguese-based creoles, and cultural exchange affected food, dress, music, and architecture. Plants from the Americas, such as chili peppers and cashew, reached South Asia through Portuguese networks and were adopted into local agriculture and cooking.

Social relations were unequal and varied. Marriages and household arrangements ranged from negotiated partnerships to coercion, and enslaved women and men had no freedom to refuse. Historians distinguish these coercive relationships from the voluntary participation of local elites who chose to work with the Portuguese for profit or status, while acknowledging that choices were made under pressure and are recorded in sources written largely by Portuguese observers.

Section 49.39

Asian Merchants Respond: Commercial Adaptation, Evasion, and Continuing Networks

Asian merchants responded to Portuguese pressure through a range of strategies. Many obtained cartazes and paid duties in order to continue trading, while also using other ports, routes, and arrangements to avoid inspection. Gujarati merchants maintained their trade with the Red Sea, Hormuz, and Southeast Asia, often changing their voyages and cargoes, and shifted business to ports outside Portuguese control, such as Aceh in Sumatra. Muslim merchants of Malabar traded through smaller harbors, using the coastal knowledge that Portuguese fleets lacked.

In the east, Tamil and Bengali merchants continued to work across the Bay of Bengal, where the Portuguese had only scattered influence, and Armenian and Persian merchants traveled overland and by sea. Some Asian merchants worked with Portuguese officials and private traders, who themselves needed Asian partners and cargoes. Such cooperation could be profitable, and cooperating merchants gained protection and access.

These responses show that continuity of Asian trade did not mean passivity or isolation. Merchants adapted quickly, balancing risk and profit, and they used the weaknesses and divisions in Portuguese authority. The commercial system survived by changing, and the Portuguese had to operate within it.

Division VII — Maritime Change without European Monopoly

Section 49.40

The Limits of Portuguese Maritime Dominance

The limits of Portuguese dominance are visible in several areas. Their forts and fleets controlled particular ports and sea lanes but not the whole ocean. Asian shipping remained large throughout the sixteenth century, and by the middle of the century the Red Sea spice trade had recovered. Muslim merchant networks adapted and survived, and regional commerce within the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea, and the Indian coasts continued largely outside Portuguese reach.

The Portuguese themselves depended on Asian resources. Their trade relied on Asian shipping, textiles, labor, pilots, and credit, and the Estado's revenues depended on cooperation from merchants and rulers. Crown monopolies were frequently evaded, and officials participated in the trade they were supposed to regulate.

For these reasons the notion of a complete Portuguese trade monopoly is historically misleading. Portugal's claims were ambitious, and its coercion was real and damaging to many, but it operated within a system that it could not replace. Historians treat the Portuguese as a significant new participant that changed the terms of competition, not as the master of the Indian Ocean.

Section 49.41

The Indian Ocean around 1600: Continuity, Reorganization, and Long-Term Consequences

By about 1600, the Indian Ocean had been reorganized in important ways. The Cape route connected Asia to Atlantic Europe, and Portuguese forts and licensing practices had changed coastal politics and trade routes. Heavier artillery and fortification technology spread, new crops and goods circulated, and Goa and other Portuguese settlements created new religious and social institutions. Regional states adjusted their policies, and some ports lost importance while others, such as Aceh, gained.

Continuity was also strong. Gujarati, Malabari, Arab, Persian, and other merchants remained active, the monsoon system governed voyages, and regional states retained their authority. The Portuguese had changed maritime relations without removing the commercial structures on which they depended.

New competitors were emerging. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600 and the Dutch East India Company in 1602, and both would challenge Portuguese positions in the seventeenth century, a development outside this chapter's scope. On land, the expansion of the Mughal Empire into Gujarat in the 1570s, discussed in Chapter 51, began to bring larger imperial formations into contact with the coast. The chapter therefore ends at a moment of transition, when regional South Asian societies remained part of a wider and changing world.

Section 49.42

Review and Historical Synthesis

The pre-1498 Indian Ocean was a connected commercial world in which South Asian ports, merchants, and rulers played central roles. Monsoon winds, relay trade, merchant networks, and the demand for goods such as pepper and textiles organized it. Gujarat, Malabar, and the eastern coasts were integral parts, and no single group dominated.

The Portuguese entered this system through voyages, alliances, and violence. They used ships and artillery, fortified bases such as Cochin and Goa, and administrative institutions such as the Estado da India and the cartaz to claim authority over parts of the ocean. Local rulers and merchants cooperated or resisted according to their interests, and the Portuguese relied on Asian partners, labor, and markets.

Students should distinguish five things that are often confused: a claim to regulate a route, a naval victory, a treaty or alliance, a fortress, and actual commercial dominance. The evidence suggests that Portugal achieved the first four in places and times, but not the fifth across the ocean. Evaluate the proposition that Portuguese expansion altered an existing commercial system rather than replacing it, using the evidence and perspectives presented in this chapter.

Chapter Summary

The Indian Ocean before 1498 was a connected, monsoon-driven commercial world in which Gujarati, Malabari, Arab, Persian, and other merchants held substantial power. The Portuguese arrived through a long Atlantic expansion, met with diplomatic misunderstanding at Calicut, and turned to armed force, fortresses, alliances, and licensing. Goa and the Estado da India gave them a durable base, while missions and the Inquisition brought religious coercion. Resistance and collaboration coexisted, and Asian merchants adapted. By 1600 the ocean had been reorganized, not conquered.

The chapter's central lesson concerns the difference between intention and achievement. The Portuguese claimed monopoly, fought important naval battles, and built fortresses, yet they relied on Asian shipping, labor, and allies. Their intervention nevertheless had lasting consequences: armed competition at sea, the licensing of shipping, new connections between Asia and Atlantic Europe, religious coercion in Goa and neighboring territories, and the formation of Luso-Asian societies.

Asian coastal societies were neither passive nor uniform. Some rulers fought, others allied, and many did both over time. Merchants adapted by evasion, negotiation, and rerouting. The chapter thus closes Part IX by showing regional South Asian societies as part of a changing world, just as larger land empires, the subject of the following chapters, began to reshape the subcontinent.

Methodologically, the chapter illustrates how historians weigh abundant but partial sources, notably the Portuguese chronicles, against fragmentary Asian evidence. It also shows why a story of European arrival must be told alongside the long history of Asian commerce that made the arrival worthwhile.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows South Asian coastal societies as active participants in a wider world. Portuguese armed maritime competition was consequential, but it unfolded within, and depended on, Asian networks. It leads forward to the Mughal empire, whose rise (Chapter 50 onward) shifts attention back to the land while the sea remains central to the economy.

The chapter also changes how the sixteenth century is read: not as the moment Europe discovered Asia, but as a period when an existing, plural, commercially dynamic world encountered a new kind of armed competitor. Continuities of merchant enterprise, regional politics, and coastal society endured, and they remain visible in later colonial history.
