Part XVII — South Asia in an Age of Upheaval, 1971–1991

Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the Smaller States, 1971–1991

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 10 January 1972, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman returned to Dhaka after nine months in a Pakistani prison. Crowds estimated in the hundreds of thousands greeted a leader who had become, in the space of a year, the founding figure of a new state. Behind the celebration lay a country with shattered bridges and railways, millions of returning refugees, a depleted administration, and an army and civil service that had to be rebuilt almost from nothing. Less than four years later Mujib would be dead, murdered with most of his family in a military coup, and the constitutional order proclaimed at independence would be in ruins.

Chapter 92 told the story of the Bangladesh Liberation War and the creation of the state. This chapter begins where that account ends, with the question of what the victors of 1971 could build. Its answer is not confined to Dhaka. Between 1971 and 1991 five of South Asia's smaller states passed through very different crises of political authority, and set side by side they illuminate one another. Bangladesh moved from parliamentary democracy through a one-party experiment and two periods of military-led government to a restored parliamentary system. Sri Lanka, a parliamentary democracy with high literacy and a long electoral tradition, rewrote its constitution twice and descended into overlapping civil conflicts. Nepal, ruled under a partyless monarchy, experienced a popular movement in 1990 that restored multiparty politics. Bhutan, a monarchy undertaking cautious modernization, adopted citizenship and cultural-integration policies that provoked the flight of tens of thousands of Nepali-speaking residents. The Maldives, a small island republic dependent on fishing and an expanding tourist sector, centralized authority in the presidency and survived an attempted coup in 1988 only because India intervened at the government's request.

These states differed enormously in population, resources, and institutional inheritance. Bangladesh, with tens of millions of people crowded onto the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, and the Maldives, with a population measured in the low hundreds of thousands scattered across coral atolls, cannot be compared on every dimension. But every one of them confronted the same fundamental questions. Who held legitimate authority, and by what procedure was it conferred? How would the state treat communities that differed from the majority in language, religion, or citizenship status? What was the proper relationship between the armed forces, the executive, and elected institutions? How would impoverished economies be developed, and who would bear the costs? And how far did the presence of a large neighbor, above all India, constrain or enable domestic choices?

The chapter therefore proceeds comparatively, in five divisions. The first two follow Bangladesh from reconstruction and famine through coups and military-presidential rule to the democratic transition of 1990–1991. The third and fourth follow Sri Lanka from the constitutions of 1972 and 1978, through the hardening of Sinhalese and Tamil nationalisms, to Black July in 1983, the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord of 1987, and the multiple wars of the late 1980s. The fifth turns to Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives and closes with a systematic synthesis. Developments after 1991, including the end of Sri Lanka's war in 2009, Nepal's later insurgency and abolition of the monarchy, and the later history of Bhutanese refugees, are mentioned only as retrospective context, and several are treated in Chapters 101 through 104.

The evidence is as varied as the states. For Bangladesh, historians draw on constitutional texts, parliamentary and election records, government and World Bank documents, famine studies by economists and demographers, memoirs, newspapers, and later judicial and commission findings about the coups. For Sri Lanka there are constitutions, census and electoral data, parliamentary debates, party resolutions, reports by human-rights organizations and legal commissions, journalism, scholarly ethnography, and survivor testimony. For Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives, researchers rely on constitutional texts, royal proclamations, census documents, government and international-agency reporting, refugee testimony, and the work of regional specialists, though the evidentiary record for the smaller states is thinner and, on contested points, more dependent on partisan sources. Each source type has limits, and this chapter flags them where they matter most: mortality figures for the 1974 famine, casualty estimates for Black July, and competing accounts of Bhutan's citizenship policies.

Two cautions run through the chapter. The first is against inevitability. The breakdown of Bangladeshi parliamentary democracy, the emergence of armed Tamil separatism, and the displacement of Bhutan's Lhotshampa each had identifiable causes, but each also followed from choices made by particular people under particular constraints, and alternatives existed at each stage. The second is against collapsing categories. Sri Lankan Tamils are not the same as Indian-origin Tamils; Tamil political parties are not the same as militant organizations; Nepali-speaking residents of Bhutan did not all hold the same legal status; and in every state discussed, the armed forces, parties, and governments were internally divided. Understanding the period means keeping those distinctions in view.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain not only what happened in each country but why these small states diverged so sharply, and what their divergence reveals about the relationship among national identity, constitutional government, military power, minority rights, and regional intervention. The sections that follow supply the evidence for that comparison, and Section 96.20 draws it together.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the principles and institutions of Bangladesh's Constitution of 1972 and the pressures that undermined them between 1972 and 1975.
  2. Analyze the 1974 famine in Bangladesh as a crisis of food entitlement and state capacity, while distinguishing contested mortality estimates by source.
  3. Trace the sequence from the Fourth Amendment of 1975 through the coups of August and November 1975 to the emergence of Ziaur Rahman.
  4. Compare the political systems of Mujib, Ziaur Rahman, and H. M. Ershad, recognizing differences within military-led and presidential rule.
  5. Explain the democratic movement of 1990 and the restoration of parliamentary government in Bangladesh in 1991, while assessing the limits of that transition.
  6. Distinguish among Sinhalese, Sri Lankan Tamil, Indian-origin Tamil, and Muslim communities and explain how language and citizenship policies shaped their political positions.
  7. Compare Sri Lanka's Constitutions of 1972 and 1978 and evaluate their consequences for executive power and minority representation.
  8. Explain the shift in Tamil politics from federal autonomy to separatism and the emergence of armed organizations, including the LTTE, within a wider field of Tamil politics.
  9. Assess Black July 1983 as a turning point, using attributed evidence and distinguishing documented events from disputed estimates.
  10. Evaluate the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord of 1987, the Thirteenth Amendment, and the Indian Peace Keeping Force, including the multiple conflicts in Sri Lanka by 1989.
  11. Analyze Nepal's Panchayat system, the 1980 referendum, and the People's Movement of 1990, and compare Nepal's transition with Bangladesh's.
  12. Evaluate the causes and consequences of Bhutan's citizenship policies and the Maldives' 1988 coup attempt, and compare all five states by legitimacy, institutions, minority rights, and regional influence.

  2. Why do economists and historians describe the 1974 famine as a failure of entitlement and state capacity rather than only of food supply?

Historical Context

The states examined in this chapter emerged from very different pasts. Bangladesh was the former East Bengal and East Pakistan, a region that had been part of British India's Bengal Presidency until 1947, then the more populous but politically subordinate wing of Pakistan until 1971. Its politics had been shaped by the language movement of 1952, by the long exclusion of Bengali politicians from power in Rawalpindi and Karachi, and by the Awami League's six-point autonomy program, as Chapters 90 and 92 describe. The Awami League won an overwhelming majority in the 1970 election, and the refusal to transfer power led to the military crackdown of March 1971 and the war. Bangladesh therefore inherited a strong party, a charismatic leader, a nationalist movement united by language and grievance, and very little else: a bureaucracy partly trained under Pakistan, an army that had to integrate returning officers and former guerrillas, and a damaged economy.

Sri Lanka, known as Ceylon until 1972, followed a different path. It received independence from Britain in 1948 without a mass anticolonial struggle, inherited the Soulbury Constitution with a Westminster-style parliament, and enjoyed relatively high literacy and health indicators for a low-income country. Its society was multiethnic. The Sinhalese, mostly Buddhist, formed a large majority. Sri Lankan Tamils, mostly Hindu with a Christian minority, were concentrated in the north and east. Indian-origin Tamils, brought as plantation labor by the British in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, lived in the central highlands and were distinct from Sri Lankan Tamils in history, economic position, and legal status. Muslims, who usually spoke Tamil but were not generally regarded as ethnically Tamil, formed a third significant minority, especially in the east. Competition over language, access to education and public employment, and citizenship began soon after independence and shaped politics well before the period covered here.

Nepal had never been formally colonized. Its Rana family prime ministers ruled in the name of the Shah monarchs until the revolution of 1950–1951, after which the monarchy was restored to a central role and a brief experiment in multiparty politics followed. That experiment ended in 1960, when King Mahendra dismissed the elected government, an event examined in Section 96.17. Bhutan, a Himalayan kingdom with a Buddhist political tradition, had conducted its external relations through British and then Indian guidance and began planned modernization in 1961. The Maldives, a Muslim island nation that had been a British protectorate, became independent in 1965 and a republic in 1968.

Two features of the wider setting framed all five stories. The first was the shock of 1971. The division of Pakistan and the emergence of Bangladesh made India the unquestioned dominant power of the subcontinent and changed what smaller neighbors expected of it. Chapter 95 examines Pakistan's response to that shock, and Chapter 97 examines the new regional diplomacy and the formation of SAARC. The second was the economic setting. All five were low-income, aid-dependent or remittance-dependent economies, mostly agricultural, and vulnerable to price shocks, as the 1973 oil crisis and subsequent global food and fuel price changes demonstrated.

Students should also keep in mind the political language of the period. Terms such as socialism, secularism, nationalism, and development meant different things in each constitutional text. The Cold War, the Afghan conflict after 1979, and the growing economic importance of the Persian Gulf also shaped the choices of governments and oppositions. Finally, the chapter relies on sources of uneven reliability. Government documents, party resolutions, human-rights reports, and later memoirs each reflect their authors' purposes, and where scholars still disagree about numbers or motives the text says so.

Bangladesh after Independence: Reconstruction and Breakdown

Division I · Section 96.1

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and the Creation of the New Bangladeshi State

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman had been arrested by the Pakistani army in March 1971, held in West Pakistan, and released in early January 1972 under international pressure after Pakistan's defeat. He flew to London, then to New Delhi, and arrived in Dhaka on 10 January. The provisional government that had directed the war from exile in Calcutta, in which Tajuddin Ahmad had served as prime minister and Syed Nazrul Islam as acting president, had already begun working from the capital. Mujib briefly held the presidency under a provisional order and on 12 January 1972 became prime minister, with Abu Sayeed Chowdhury as president; Tajuddin Ahmad became a senior minister. The rapid transition reflected Mujib's popularity and limited institutional competition.

The new government faced a state that existed more as an idea than an apparatus. Many senior Bengali civil servants and officers had returned from internment in West Pakistan only after months of delay, and a large number of non-Bengali officials, industrialists, and residents (often called Biharis) had left, been detained, or fallen under suspicion because of alleged collaboration with the Pakistani army. Armed guerrillas, the Mukti Bahini, held weapons across the countryside. The regular army had to integrate officers who had served with the rebellion with those who had been in West Pakistan and returned after repatriation. Some of the division between these groups would resurface in the coups of 1975. India's armed forces withdrew in March 1972, an important symbolic point for a government that wished to demonstrate its sovereignty.

The constitution was drafted by a committee under the jurist Kamal Hossain and adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 4 November 1972, taking effect on 16 December, the first anniversary of the surrender. It declared four fundamental principles: nationalism, socialism, democracy, and secularism. Nationalism meant, in the language of the time, a Bengali nationalism rooted in language and culture, and not a religious identity. Socialism meant state ownership of major industry and an egalitarian development goal, though the text left room for private enterprise within limits. Democracy was embodied in a unicameral parliament, the Jatiya Sangsad, of 300 directly elected seats with reserved seats for women, and a cabinet responsible to parliament. The prime minister held effective executive power, and the largely ceremonial presidency was held by Abu Sayeed Chowdhury and, after December 1973, by Mohammadullah. Secularism barred religion-based political parties and the political use of religion, and equal citizenship was affirmed without distinction of religion. Those provisions reflected the experience of 1971, when Pakistani authorities had used religious language to justify repression and when sections of the religious parties had sided with the army.

The constitution also enshrined a large role for the Awami League. In the March 1973 general election, the first under the new order, the League won 293 of the 300 seats, a result that reflected genuine popularity but also the absence of a credible organized opposition, since the leftist and Islamist currents were fragmented, and some of the parties that had collaborated in 1971 were banned. A decisive majority made the formal separation between the executive and the legislature thin, and the party's own organization, which had grown rapidly, was filled with newcomers whose loyalties were often personal and local.

Behind the institutions lay a deeper challenge. A war of liberation produced a powerful consensus about what Bangladesh was against, but much less agreement about what it would be. Bengali nationalists, socialists, Maoists, devout Muslims, and former military men all claimed the legacy of 1971. Mujib's personal prestige papered over these differences while he was able to rely on it, but it also encouraged a style of rule in which he and a small circle took decisions that would ordinarily have passed through party and parliamentary channels. The constitutional order collapsed by 1975; historians dispute the relative roles of leadership, institutions, and circumstance.

Primary Source Window 1

The Constitution of Bangladesh, 1972

Source type: Constitutional text · Created: Drafted by the Constitution Drafting Committee; adopted by the Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1972; in force 16 December 1972

Context: The text was written by a committee of the ruling party and its allies within a year of independence, at a time when the Awami League held an overwhelming majority and the memory of Pakistani military rule was fresh.

Paraphrase of key provisions: The preamble and the early articles identify nationalism, socialism, democracy, and secularism as fundamental principles of state policy. They set out a parliamentary structure with a prime minister responsible to the legislature, guarantee fundamental rights, and prohibit communal political parties.

Analysis: The text shows how the founders sought to prevent a repeat of Pakistani experience: a representative legislature, civil guarantees, and a pluralist secular state. It also reveals the assumptions of the moment, including a broad commitment to nationalization and a belief that one dominant party could embody the national will. It does not show how the provisions worked in practice, nor the views of the groups that thought the constitution too secular, too centralized, or too socialist.

  2. Who wrote it, and what circumstances shaped their choices?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the aims of the new state?
  4. What can historians not establish from the text alone about how government actually functioned?

Division I · Section 96.2

Economic Reconstruction, Social Dislocation, and the Bangladesh Famine of 1974

Bangladesh began independence with a damaged infrastructure. Railways, ports, and roads had been attacked during the war, many bridges and river vessels were destroyed or disabled, and the Chittagong and Mongla ports required clearing of mines and sunken ships. Millions of people had fled to India as refugees and began to return in 1972, while many others had been displaced within the country. International agencies, notably the United Nations Relief Operation in Dacca, supplied food and transport at the outset, and the government became heavily dependent on external food aid and development assistance in the years that followed. Aid brought needed resources but also gave donors an influence over policy that reflected Bangladesh's weak bargaining position.

The economy the new government inherited had been shaped by Pakistan's East–West disparities. Industry, especially jute, had been owned largely by West Pakistani or non-Bengali business groups, many of whom left in 1971. The government nationalized abandoned and large enterprises, including jute and cotton mills, banks, and insurance companies, and created public corporations to manage them. Management was often inexperienced, political appointees mixed with technical staff, and the new corporations struggled with losses, shortages of spare parts, and smuggling across the border. At the same time the government sought to retain agricultural and small-scale private activity, and a shortage of capital goods made rebuilding slow.

Agriculture, on which the great majority depended, faced its own difficulties. Rice and jute were the principal crops, farms were small and fragmented, and yields were low. The war had disrupted seed, fertilizer, and irrigation supplies, and the 1971–1972 crop year was weak. Output recovered in the following years but without the dynamism of later decades, when high-yielding varieties and minor irrigation spread. Rural landlessness was already widespread, and many households depended on wage labor and on markets for food. This dependence mattered in 1974.

In mid-1974 heavy rains and a severe flood affected much of the country, damaging the aman rice crop, particularly in the northern districts of Rangpur and Mymensingh, and disrupting transport and employment. World prices of grain, fertilizer, and fuel had risen sharply after 1972–1973, and the price of rice rose steeply within Bangladesh, in part because of expectations, speculation, smuggling, and government difficulties in procuring and distributing grain. Food aid shipments were delayed, partly because of the diplomatic dispute about a Bangladeshi jute export arrangement with Cuba, which the United States government had objected to, though the extent to which this delayed deliveries is disputed. Rationing covered only part of the population, mostly urban and government-connected groups, and rural laborers were largely outside it. Wage employment and income fell as flooded fields stopped providing work.

People moved toward towns and relief centers (langarkhanas, or free kitchens, were opened) in search of food. Death from hunger and associated disease concentrated in the autumn months, particularly in September to November 1974, and the worst-affected were landless laborers, widows, and other households with little or no resources. Estimates of mortality vary widely. Official early figures were in the tens of thousands. Scholarly estimates, based on demographic reconstruction and local surveys, are much higher. Studies by economists such as Mohiuddin Alamgir and by demographers working in the Matlab area put excess deaths in the range of several hundred thousand up to figures cited as high as 1.5 million, and the figures depend on assumptions about baseline mortality and the reliability of data. Students should treat any single number as an attributed estimate and not as an established fact. What is not seriously disputed is that mortality was very high, that it was concentrated among the poor, and that it was caused by hunger and disease together.

The central analytical contribution comes from Amartya Sen, who argued in Poverty and Famines (1981) that the 1974 famine could not be explained simply by a decline in the aggregate supply of food. Food availability per person in 1974 was not exceptionally low compared to other years; it was people's ability to obtain food, their entitlement, that collapsed as rice prices rose and agricultural wages and employment fell. Other scholars, including Martin Ravallion and Alamgir, offered different emphases, stressing the role of market expectations, flood-related disruption of regional supply, and the importance of public distribution. The famine is best understood as a crisis of entitlement and of state capacity: a government lacking reserves, procurement and distribution systems reaching the rural poor, and timely relief responded late and unevenly. It was neither only a natural disaster nor only a policy failure; floods, prices, and institutional weakness interacted.

The consequences were also political. The famine damaged the Awami League's legitimacy, fed the opposition's claims that the government was corrupt and ineffective, and strengthened the argument, advanced from within and outside the government, that firmer central direction was needed. It was in this climate of hunger and unrest that the government declared an emergency in December 1974, a step examined in the next section.

Historical Vocabulary
Entitlement

In the analysis of famine associated with Amartya Sen, a person's entitlement is the set of commodity bundles they can command through the legal, economic, and social means available to them: what they can produce, what they can buy with wages, what they can obtain through exchange or public provision. A famine may occur when entitlements collapse even if total food supplies are adequate. In 1974 Bangladesh, landless laborers who depended on wages saw earnings fall as prices rose, and so lost command over food. The idea matters here because it shifts attention from the harvest to markets, income, and state action. It does not deny that supply shocks matter; it asks who is exposed to them and why.

A Bangladeshi Household during Reconstruction and the 1974 Famine

Consider a landless laborer's household in a northern district. Before the floods of 1974 its adult members earned their living by agricultural wage labor, paid partly in cash and partly in grain, and supplemented their income by gathering vegetables and fishing in seasonal waters. The family's rice purchases were made in small quantities from the local market. As the flood waters spread, field work stopped and wages in kind disappeared. At the same time the market price of rice rose, so that earnings bought less. Households responded by reducing the number of meals, selling small assets such as livestock, utensils, or the roof's corrugated sheeting, and borrowing at high interest. When these options ran out, some members migrated toward district towns or to Dhaka, where relief kitchens served gruel. Women and children, and the elderly, were among the most vulnerable. Rationing, which covered urban and government employees, did not reach this household, and relief was sporadic.

The example shows why the famine was a crisis of access. The grain was in the country; the household's ability to command it was not. It also shows the importance of social history: migration, family separation, and indebtedness shaped the aftermath as much as the mortality itself.

Division I · Section 96.3

Political Centralization, the Fourth Amendment, and BAKSAL

Between 1972 and 1975 the political space in Bangladesh narrowed. The initial popularity of the Awami League and of Mujib persisted, but the party's internal discipline weakened, corruption allegations spread, and opposition organizations became more militant. The principal left-wing opposition, the Jatiya Samajtantrik Dal (JSD), formed in 1972 by former student leaders who had been prominent in the independence movement, criticized the government's economic policies and demanded a more radical form of scientific socialism. Maoist and other underground groups also operated, and some, such as those led by Siraj Sikder, were suppressed violently. Islamist parties had been banned or discredited by their wartime associations, though they retained an informal following.

The government's response to disorder and dissent relied increasingly on security forces. The Jatiya Rakkhi Bahini, a paramilitary force created in early 1972 and loyal to the prime minister, was used against opposition activists, and critics, including some in the regular army, resented its privileges and the diversion of resources. Political killings, abductions, and extrajudicial violence became common, and the government passed legislation such as the Special Powers Act of 1974 that allowed preventive detention. The press also faced restrictions. These measures were defended by officials as necessary to deal with smuggling, hoarding, and armed insurgents, and their critics saw them as instruments of party rule.

On 28 December 1974 the government declared a state of emergency, suspending fundamental rights, including freedom of speech and association, and the right to move the courts for their enforcement. The declaration followed the famine, the economic crisis, and the political violence of the year. It paved the way for a still more far-reaching constitutional change. On 25 January 1975 parliament passed the Fourth Amendment to the Constitution, in a single sitting and with limited debate. The amendment replaced the parliamentary system with an executive presidency, with the president holding extensive powers including the appointment and removal of ministers and relative insulation from legislative control; Mujib was named as the first president for a five-year term. It reduced judicial protection of fundamental rights and permitted the creation of a single national party. Mujib took the presidency, and on 24 February he announced the formation of the Bangladesh Krishak Sramik Awami League, known by its acronym BAKSAL (formally launched in June), a national party that absorbed the Awami League and sympathetic groups, including the pro-Moscow Communist Party and the National Awami Party (Muzaffar), and required officials, and in some respects journalists, to align with it. Other parties were dissolved. The government also placed the press under tighter control, leaving only a few state-aligned newspapers.

Mujib's stated justification was that a period of national reconstruction, a "second revolution," required unity and discipline: an end to corruption, a campaign for greater production, administrative reform, cooperatives, and a new system of district governors to bring the administration closer to the people. Some of the amendment's supporters, including leftist allies, saw it as a means to advance socialist aims and to prevent further disorder. Critics, then and later, argued that it destroyed the pluralism that the independence movement had been fought to secure, that it displaced parliament, and that the formation of a single party contradicted the 1972 commitment to democracy. A fair account distinguishes between the leadership's stated objectives and the concentration of authority and suppression of opposition that actually resulted. Some close associates opposed the measures, and several senior Awami League leaders, including Tajuddin Ahmad, had been sidelined or were privately skeptical.

The experience illustrates a recurrent theme of postcolonial state-building: the temptation of a movement leader to treat dissent as obstruction when institutions are weak and crises acute. It also illustrates the danger of constitutional change that removes the very mechanisms through which unrest can be channeled. By mid-1975 Bangladesh had a single party, a powerful president, a compliant press, and a deeply disaffected set of institutions, including parts of the army. The new structure had existed for only a few months when it was overthrown.

Division I · Section 96.4

The Assassination of Sheikh Mujib and the Coups of 1975

In the early hours of 15 August 1975, a group of junior and mid-level army officers attacked the residence of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman at Dhanmondi in Dhaka and killed him, along with most members of his family who were present, including his wife, three sons, and two daughters-in-law. His daughters Sheikh Hasina and Sheikh Rehana, who were abroad, survived. Other relatives and aides were killed elsewhere in the city, and units moved to take control of key locations. The officers, who included Majors Syed Faruq Rahman, Abdur Rashid, and others who had been dismissed or disciplined or who had grievances, announced the establishment of a new government and martial law.

The coup leaders installed Khondaker Mostaq Ahmad, a senior Awami League minister who was considered conservative and had reportedly been skeptical of the Indian connection and the 1975 amendments, as president. The new administration abolished BAKSAL, restored a degree of party politics in theory, and announced elections, though it also issued an indemnity ordinance protecting those involved in the killing from prosecution, a measure that was later made part of the constitutional order and became a long-standing grievance. The army's chiefs, including Chief of Staff Major General K. M. Shafiullah and Deputy Chief Brigadier Ziaur Rahman, accepted the situation, and the military leadership was divided about what had occurred and what should be done.

The new order proved unstable. On 3 November a counter-coup led by Brigadier Khaled Musharraf, who represented a more professional and pro-Awami League tendency in the army, removed Mostaq and arrested Ziaur Rahman. On the same day, in Dhaka Central Jail, four of the leaders of the 1971 wartime government, Syed Nazrul Islam, Tajuddin Ahmad, Captain M. Mansur Ali, and A. H. M. Qamaruzzaman, were killed by a group of soldiers. The killing of these four, who were the most prominent surviving leaders of the independence struggle and who were imprisoned by the Mostaq government, deprived the Awami League of much of its senior leadership and became a central symbol of the destruction of the original order. Responsibility for planning the jail killings is a matter of continued argument, and the later judicial proceedings determined that serving and former officers of the August plot were involved.

Khaled Musharraf's government lasted only a few days. On 6–7 November, a mutiny broke out among soldiers in the Dhaka garrison, mobilized by Colonel Abu Taher and the left-wing Biplobi Shainik Sangstha (Revolutionary Soldiers' Organization), associated with the JSD. Musharraf was killed, Ziaur Rahman was freed and rose as the figure who restored order, and the rebellious soldiers, who had hoped to bring about a social revolution in the army, were themselves suppressed. Abu Taher was later tried and executed in July 1976. The chief justice, Abu Sadat Mohammad Sayem, was installed as the civilian president and chief martial law administrator, while real authority lay with the army chief of staff, Ziaur Rahman, together with the heads of the navy and air force.

Several lines of explanation have been proposed. Some stress factional divisions within the army: between officers repatriated from Pakistan and those who had fought in the war, between Mujib's critics and supporters, and between those who resented the privileges of the Rakkhi Bahini and its integration into the army. Others emphasize the effect of the economic crisis and the political closure of 1975, which left the army as an arbiter. Some writers have pointed to the role of foreign actors, and conspiracy accounts abound, but evidence for outside direction is contested and inconclusive. The later investigations and trials, including a Bangladeshi court's verdicts in the 1990s and 2000s after the indemnity law was repealed, established individual responsibility for the murders of Mujib and his family and the jail killings, but they did not settle the wider questions about conspiracy.

For the historian, the key point is to separate the documented sequence of events, which is well established, from competing political interpretations of what the coups meant. For supporters of the Awami League, August 1975 was a treasonous murder that terminated a democratic nationalist government; for others, it was a response to authoritarian drift. Neither reading should be allowed to conceal that the killing of a head of state and his family, and later of imprisoned leaders, was an act of political violence that destroyed the constitutional framework of 1972 and inaugurated more than fifteen years in which the army was a central actor in the state.

Division 2 · Sections 96.5–96.8

Bangladesh under Ziaur Rahman and H. M. Ershad

Division II · Section 96.5

Ziaur Rahman's Consolidation of Military and Presidential Authority

After the upheavals of November 1975, Bangladesh was governed by a martial-law administration in which a civilian president, Justice Abu Sadat Mohammad Sayem, served as the nominal head while Major General Ziaur Rahman gradually became its most powerful figure. Zia had been a young major when he announced the declaration of independence over the radio from Chittagong in March 1971 and had commanded a sector and a brigade in the liberation war, which gave him credentials that rivaled those of the Awami League's civilian leaders. In the chaotic months after the sepoy mutiny, he had restored discipline in the army, suppressed the left-wing soldiers' movement, and drawn the officer corps behind him. In November 1976 he took over as chief martial law administrator, and on 21 April 1977, with Sayem's resignation, he assumed the presidency.

Zia's task was to turn military control into a durable political order. A referendum in May 1977 asked voters to express confidence in his leadership and program; the official result, an approval of about 98.9 percent, was not a competitive contest and cannot be treated as a measure of free opinion, but it served to give the new presidency a plebiscitary mandate. In June 1978 he stood in a presidential election against a coalition candidate, the retired General M. A. G. Osmani, the wartime commander-in-chief, and won roughly three-quarters of the vote under conditions that critics said were favorable to the incumbent but that were more competitive than the 1977 referendum. In September 1978 he created the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP), which drew together former members of different parties, retired officers, local notables, and conservative and centrist politicians. The party elections of 18 February 1979 gave the BNP a large majority, about 207 of 300 seats, with the Awami League faction led by Abdul Malek Ukil in second place and several smaller parties returning. Martial law was lifted in 1979, and the Fifth Amendment to the constitution ratified the acts and proclamations of the martial-law period.

The restoration of elections did not mean complete civilian control. The president remained the center of power, and the president had been, and continued to be, the army's former chief of staff. Zia retained his military rank for a time, relied on the armed forces and intelligence agencies, and appointed many military and former military officers to administrative and public-corporation posts. The BNP functioned as an instrument of his political management as much as an independent party. At the same time, the system differed from simple military dictatorship. It reintroduced a multiparty setting, allowed a press that was freer than in 1975, and permitted opposition parties, including a revived Awami League, to compete. It might better be described as a military-backed presidential regime with electoral features, and it set a pattern that Ershad would later imitate.

Zia also altered the symbolic and ideological basis of the state. A martial-law proclamation of 1977 inserted the invocation "Bismillah" in the constitution's preamble, replaced the principle of secularism with a commitment to absolute trust and faith in Almighty Allah, and redefined socialism as economic and social justice. The ban on religion-based political parties was removed, allowing parties such as Jamaat-e-Islami, which had opposed independence, to reenter politics. Zia promoted what he called Bangladeshi nationalism, in place of the Bengali nationalism that had been the basis of 1972. The distinction was deliberate. Bengali nationalism, centered on language and culture, linked the state to the Bengali identity that Bangladesh shared with West Bengal in India; Bangladeshi nationalism, defined by territory and by a stress on Islam and distinctiveness from India, emphasized the independent sovereign nation and its Muslim majority, and it also had implications for non-Bengali minorities, such as the Chakma and other peoples of the Chittagong Hill Tracts, who did not fit either definition easily. These changes were controversial: for supporters they corrected an over-ambitious secular program and responded to popular religious sentiment, while critics regarded them as a repudiation of the principles of 1971 for political advantage.

Division II · Section 96.6

Economic Policy, Rural Development, Foreign Relations, and Zia's Assassination

Zia's economic approach reversed some of the priorities of the Mujib period. His government shifted from the expansive nationalization of 1972 toward encouragement of private enterprise, privatized or returned some industrial units, loosened controls, and sought aid and investment from Western countries, international financial institutions, and Gulf states. The "Nineteen-Point Program" announced in 1977 emphasized food self-sufficiency, rural development, population control, and mass mobilization for production. The regime sponsored canal digging and rural public works, often through food-for-work programs financed by donors, and promoted village-level institutions, including the gram sarkar (village government) experiment and local councils. The effects of these programs are debated: they gave rural employment and rebuilt small infrastructure, but they were also used to build a rural political base for the regime, and the gram sarkar scheme was not lastingly successful.

Agricultural performance improved gradually in this period. Fertilizer distribution, irrigation by shallow tube-wells and low-lift pumps, and high-yielding rice varieties began to spread, and foodgrain production grew, though not enough to eliminate import dependence or rural poverty. Population remained a central pressure. The 1981 census counted roughly 87 million people, and family planning was a government priority, with international funding, even though fertility fell only slowly until the later 1980s. A further feature of the period was the growth of labor migration: from the mid-1970s Bangladeshi workers began to travel to Gulf countries and elsewhere, remitting wages that supported households and, increasingly, the balance of payments. The wider regional dimension of labor migration is a theme for Chapter 97.

In foreign policy the government pursued diversification. Relations with Pakistan, which had recognized Bangladesh in 1974 under Mujib, were normalized further and became cordial, and Zia was welcomed in the Muslim world, strengthening ties with Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states, which provided aid and employment opportunities. Bangladesh established relations with China in 1975–1976, and Chinese military supply and political support became an important counterweight to India. Relations with India were strained by disputes over the sharing of Ganges water at the Farakka barrage, over border and enclave questions, and over the Chittagong Hill Tracts, where an insurgency by the Shanti Bahini, drawn from Chakma and other hill peoples, grew after 1975–1976 and where Bangladesh accused India of giving sanctuary. Relations with the United States improved as aid from Washington and from multilateral lenders increased, and Zia's government sought a balanced international position. Zia also floated the idea of a regional cooperation body in 1977 and 1980, a proposal that led to SAARC and that Chapter 97 examines.

Zia's presidency was also marked by internal military challenges. Between 1977 and 1981 there were several attempted coups and mutinies, including one in Bogra and one in Dhaka in 1977, which were suppressed with executions of officers and soldiers; their frequency shows that his consolidation was incomplete and that the army was still divided by factional loyalties and by grievances left over from 1971 and 1975. On the night of 29–30 May 1981, while visiting Chittagong, Zia was assassinated at the Circuit House by a group of army officers. Major General Abul Manzur, the local divisional commander, was blamed and was himself killed in custody shortly afterwards, and a military court later sentenced a number of officers to death. The motives of the plotters remain disputed, and accounts range from personal grievance and factional rivalry within the army to a reaction against Zia's treatment of officers.

Zia's death left a constitutional and political vacuum. Vice President Abdus Sattar, an elderly former judge, became acting president and, in November 1981, was elected president in a contest dominated by the BNP and its machinery, with the Awami League's Kamal Hossain a distant second. But the BNP was divided, civil-military relations were tense, the economy faced financial strain, and the army chief of staff, Lieutenant General H. M. Ershad, had long been a prominent figure, who spoke openly of a formal role for the military in governance. In early 1982 Ershad suggested that the army should participate in the government of the country. The sequence illustrates a recurrent point in this chapter: a regime built around a single dominant figure struggled to institutionalize succession.

Division II · Section 96.7

H. M. Ershad: The Coup of 1982, Military Government, and Political Opposition

On 24 March 1982 Lieutenant General Hussain Muhammad Ershad overthrew President Abdus Sattar in a bloodless coup, suspended the constitution, dissolved parliament, banned political activity, and declared martial law, with himself as chief martial law administrator. He justified the takeover by pointing to corruption, political disorder, and an economic slowdown, the usual formula of military governments of the period. Sattar was removed; a civilian, Justice A. F. M. Ahsanuddin Chowdhury, was installed as a figurehead president. Ershad took the presidency himself in December 1983 and did not abandon his army role until 1986.

Ershad's government differed from Zia's in important ways. It was a more direct form of military rule at the beginning, with martial-law regulations, military courts, and tight restrictions on the opposition, and the main parties, including the Awami League and the BNP, were forced to organize around demands for the restoration of democracy. One of Ershad's main institutional initiatives was administrative decentralization. A reform in 1982–1983 created upazilas (subdistricts) as the basic unit of local administration, to which resources and officials were assigned and whose chairmen were elected, intended to bring government closer to rural society and, critics said, to create a base of local patronage loyal to the regime. Decentralization of the judiciary followed in 1988, with permanent benches of the High Court Division established in several district centers, although a later court ruling in 1989 declared this scheme unconstitutional. The upazila system survived, with modifications, beyond Ershad's time, a sign of institutional continuity under a military ruler.

The regime also tried to acquire political legitimacy through party building and elections. A civilian front organization developed into the Jatiya Party, launched in its mature form in 1986, which gathered defectors, local notables, and military-linked politicians. Ershad promised elections and the lifting of martial law. Parliamentary elections in May 1986, in which the Awami League led by Sheikh Hasina decided to participate while the BNP led by Khaleda Zia boycotted, gave the Jatiya Party a majority, around 153 of 300 seats, with the Awami League as the leading opposition party; the results were widely criticized by observers and opponents for manipulation. In October 1986 Ershad was elected president in a poll boycotted by major opposition parties. Martial law was lifted in November 1986 after a constitutional amendment, the Seventh, retroactively approved the martial-law period's measures. When the Awami League resigned from parliament in December 1987 and the opposition escalated its demands, Ershad dissolved parliament and held another election in March 1988, boycotted by the main opposition parties, in which the Jatiya Party won a large majority. The elections of 1986 and 1988 were thus electoral in form but contested in legitimacy, and the extent of the boycott is one reason that raw seat totals should not be compared with those of freer elections.

In June 1988 parliament passed the Eighth Amendment, which declared Islam the state religion of Bangladesh. Ershad's motives are debated. Supporters presented it as an expression of the religious identity of the large Muslim majority, while critics regarded it as an effort to gain support from religious constituencies and to counter an opposition that held much of the secular nationalist inheritance. The amendment retained other constitutional guarantees of equal rights for people of all religions, but it altered the 1972 formula and became a long-standing point of contention for secularists and for religious minorities, particularly Hindus. It also needs to be set beside Zia's earlier changes, which had already removed the explicit principle of secularism from the constitution.

Opposition to Ershad took many forms. Students were prominent from the start: in February 1983 protests at Dhaka University against his education policies were met by force, and a student, Dipali Saha, was among those killed in a march that year. The Awami League and BNP organized separate alliances, an eight-party alliance led by the Awami League and a seven-party alliance led by the BNP, with a third group of five left-wing parties, and they sometimes cooperated in strikes (hartals) and in demands for a neutral caretaker government to oversee elections. Professional associations, lawyers, journalists, teachers, and later much of the middle class joined. The regime responded with arrests, restrictions on assembly, and use of security forces, and the opposition leaders were often confined to house arrest. In those years, protest in Bangladesh developed a recognizable repertoire, mass hartals, street marches, university mobilization, and demands for a caretaker administration, which survived beyond the end of military-led rule.

Division II · Section 96.8

The Fall of Ershad and the Restoration of Parliamentary Politics, 1990–1991

By the late 1980s Ershad's government was weakened by economic difficulties, corruption allegations, flooding in 1987 and 1988 that damaged rural livelihoods and strained relief, and the cumulative effect of years of electoral boycotts. In December 1987 an opposition mobilization had already led to the dissolution of parliament and a state of emergency, and the movement revived in 1990. The decisive development was the convergence of the opposition. In November 1990 the three main alliances, representing the Awami League, the BNP, and the left, issued a joint declaration setting out a common framework: Ershad's resignation, a neutral caretaker government headed by a nonpartisan figure, free elections, and a return to parliamentary government. Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia, who had long been rivals, were the principal leaders, and professional groups, including lawyers and university teachers, and student organizations of different tendencies, joined in a rare joint program.

Protests intensified through autumn 1990. Clashes intensified in October and November; physician and protest organizer Dr. Shamsul Alam Khan Milon was shot dead on 27 November 1990 and became a symbol of the movement. Ershad declared a state of emergency on 27 November, but the measure failed to quell the crowds, and officials, including some civil servants, and soldiers were reluctant to support further repression. Crucially, the army's leadership indicated that it would not intervene to maintain Ershad in power. On 4 December Ershad announced that he would resign once the opposition's nominee for acting president, Chief Justice Shahabuddin Ahmed, took office, and he formally resigned on 6 December 1990. He was later arrested and prosecuted on corruption and other charges.

Shahabuddin Ahmed headed a caretaker administration of nonpartisan figures, and the army returned to its barracks. The general election of 27 February 1991 was widely judged the freest and fairest in Bangladesh's history to that date, with a turnout of about 55 percent. The BNP won about 140 seats, the Awami League about 88, the Jatiya Party about 35, and Jamaat-e-Islami about 18, with other parties and independents holding the rest. The BNP, with parliamentary support from Jamaat, formed a government under Khaleda Zia, the widow of Ziaur Rahman, who became prime minister in March 1991. The Awami League, which had expected to do better, accepted the outcome and sat in opposition. Parliament passed the Twelfth Amendment Bill on 6 August 1991; a national referendum on 15 September approved the restoration of a parliamentary system with a prime minister responsible to parliament and a largely ceremonial president. The caretaker mechanism, created by consensus rather than by constitutional text in 1990–1991, became after 1996 a constitutional feature of Bangladeshi electoral politics, a subject for Chapter 102.

The transition was an important achievement, and the way it was achieved, through the unified action of opposition parties and a popular movement, mattered. But it did not resolve the underlying problems. The rivalry between the BNP and the Awami League, personal as well as ideological, continued and grew in the following decades. The armed forces remained a powerful institution with considerable autonomy and a legacy of political involvement, even if they withdrew from direct rule. Corruption, administrative weakness, and poverty were unresolved, and the debates about the constitutional role of Islam and the handling of the 1975 killings persisted. It is also worth stressing the contrast among the three regimes of this period. Mujib's government was a popular parliamentary regime that turned into a one-party presidential system; Zia's was a military-backed presidential regime with electoral and party features; Ershad's was a military government that tried to civilianize itself and never secured acceptance. Treating military government as an unchanging phenomenon obscures these differences.

Historical Debate 1

Why Did Bangladesh's Parliamentary Democracy Collapse after Independence?

The question: Why did the parliamentary system established in 1972 give way within three years to a one-party presidential state, and then to military coups?

Interpretation A — Structural and economic pressures: The war left a devastated economy, the 1974 famine eroded public trust, and dependence on aid constrained policy. A state with limited administrative capacity could not meet the expectations raised by independence, and political violence and corruption followed from scarcity.
Interpretation B — Party centralization and institutional weakness: The Awami League's overwhelming majority, the personalization of authority around Mujib, the creation of paramilitary forces answerable to the leader, and the dismantling of constitutional checks in 1974–1975 destroyed the mechanisms through which conflict could be managed, and the army became the arbiter.

Evidence: Economists' studies of the famine, government records, the text of the Fourth Amendment, accounts by participants, and the later court findings on the coups all inform the debate. Scholars also point to factional divisions in the army, which combined returned officers from Pakistan with wartime fighters, and to the legacy of Pakistani military rule.

Why it matters: If economic crisis is the main cause, democratic failure appears largely circumstantial; if centralization and institutional design are the key, then choices within the regime carried greater weight. Most historians treat these factors as interacting: war damage and famine created crises, centralization removed safeguards, and the army's internal divisions provided the means of overthrow.

Sri Lanka: Constitutional Change and Tamil Militancy

Division III · Section 96.9

Sri Lanka after Independence: Language, Citizenship, Religion, and Competing Nationalisms

Sri Lanka's crisis after 1971 grew from disputes that were already a generation old. Britain had ruled the island's coastal and then, after 1815, its entire territory, and had governed through a centralized colonial administration that drew educated Sri Lankans of several communities into English-language schooling and the professions. Under colonial rule, Tamils, especially from the Jaffna peninsula, where mission schools were strong, were prominent in the civil service, medicine, and clerical employment out of proportion to their numbers. This pattern was often exaggerated in nationalist argument; most people in both communities were rural cultivators. After independence in 1948, as the electorate expanded and vernacular-educated Sinhalese sought public employment, the question of which language would be used by the state became politically explosive.

The population divisions need careful handling. The Sinhalese, mostly Theravada Buddhists, formed roughly three-quarters of the population. Sri Lankan Tamils, historically settled in the north and east, were about one-eighth. Indian-origin Tamils, descendants of laborers brought to work on tea, coffee, and rubber estates in the highlands, were a separate community with a different economic position and set of grievances, and their numbers were also several percent of the population. Muslims, often called Moors, were around seven percent, mostly Tamil-speaking, scattered in the east and in towns across the island. Christians, among Sinhalese and Tamils alike, were a smaller group. Religion, language, and region overlapped, making the conflict more complex than a binary ethnic division.

Citizenship was the earliest flashpoint. The Ceylon Citizenship Act of 1948 and the Indian and Pakistani Residents (Citizenship) Act of 1949 made it difficult for Indian-origin plantation workers to prove the descent requirements for citizenship, leaving the great majority stateless and removing them from the electoral rolls. The leaders of the Ceylon Tamil Congress, notably G. G. Ponnambalam, were divided on this, and the Federal Party (Ilankai Tamil Arasu Kachchi), founded in 1949 by S. J. V. Chelvanayakam, emerged partly as a result of the Sri Lankan Tamil leadership's decision not to oppose the measure. Agreements between India and Ceylon in 1964 and 1974 provided for repatriation of some of the stateless to India and for the grant of Ceylonese citizenship to others, and over the following decades the issue was only partly resolved, with full resolution coming much later.

The language dispute followed. In 1956, in an election dominated by Sinhalese cultural politics, S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike's coalition won office on a promise to make Sinhala the sole official language, and the Official Language Act, popularly called the "Sinhala Only" Act, was passed that year. Its supporters argued that Sinhala, the language of the majority, had been marginalized by English and that it deserved the status that a national language should hold. For Tamils, the act implied that public employment and administration would be conducted in a language many of them did not know, and it threatened their prospects. Federal Party protests in 1956 were met with violence in Colombo and in the east, and communal riots broke out in 1958. A pact in 1957 between Bandaranaike and Chelvanayakam, which would have given some regional autonomy and recognition of Tamil as a language of a region, was abandoned under pressure from Sinhalese nationalist and Buddhist groups. A later agreement in 1965 under Dudley Senanayake and Chelvanayakam, to provide for district councils and use of Tamil for official purposes, also lapsed. Subsequent legislation allowed limited use of Tamil in administration in Tamil areas, but its implementation was weak.

Other grievances accumulated. State-sponsored colonization of dry-zone irrigation schemes brought Sinhalese settlers to the eastern and north-central regions that Tamils regarded as traditional homelands and where Muslims also lived, and the question of land and demography became a continual source of tension. In 1970–1971, the new United Front government introduced a "standardization" system of university admission by language medium, with different qualifying marks, and by district quotas, which Tamil students experienced as a reduction in their prospects; supporters said it corrected disadvantages for rural Sinhalese. At the same time a different insurgency erupted in the south. In April 1971 the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), a radical youth movement based among rural Sinhalese educated youth with limited prospects, rose in revolt, and the government suppressed it with considerable force. The event shows that the pressure on Sri Lanka's political order was not only an ethnic division, and that unemployment and frustrated expectations among Sinhalese youth were an independent source of instability.

None of this made later conflict inevitable. In the 1950s and 1960s there were agreements, alliances, and cooperation between Sinhalese and Tamil politicians, and some Tamil leaders served in cabinets. But the political logic of electoral competition rewarded Sinhalese leaders who took a firm line on language and religion and penalized those who compromised, while the structure of a unitary state with a Sinhalese majority left Tamil leaders with limited leverage. The sequence of agreements made and abandoned also eroded faith in constitutional bargaining among Tamils, a loss of trust that would matter in the 1970s.

Division III · Section 96.10

The Constitutions of 1972 and 1978: Republican Government and Executive Power

In 1970 the United Front, led by Sirimavo Bandaranaike's Sri Lanka Freedom Party with Marxist allies, won a large parliamentary majority and decided to replace the Soulbury Constitution of 1948. The Constituent Assembly, in which the Tamil Federal Party initially participated and then withdrew when its proposals for language parity and a federal structure were rejected, adopted the First Republican Constitution on 22 May 1972. The new text changed the island's name from Ceylon to Sri Lanka, declared it a republic, and ended the Crown's remaining connection and appeals to the Privy Council. It defined the state as unitary, made Sinhala the official language with Tamil provided for as a language of courts and administration in certain contexts, and, in a provision that had large symbolic and political weight, gave Buddhism the "foremost place" and committed the state to protect and foster it while securing freedom of religion for others.

For Tamil and other minority leaders, the 1972 Constitution was a double loss. It dropped Section 29(2) of the Soulbury Constitution, which had barred Parliament from passing laws that discriminated against communities or religions, and it did not replace that safeguard with an enforceable bill of rights of similar effect. It entrenched the unitary character of the state at the moment when Tamil leaders were arguing for federalism. And it gave constitutional status to the primacy of Sinhala and Buddhism. Defenders of the constitution, including many Sinhalese politicians, saw it as completing decolonization, expressing majority culture, and establishing parliamentary sovereignty, and noted that it recognized fundamental rights in a more limited way and provided for Tamil in some official uses. The Federal Party and other Tamil groups said that the document confirmed second-class status and rejected it.

Politics shifted again in 1977. The United National Party (UNP) under Junius Richard Jayewardene won a landslide in the July 1977 general election, taking about 140 of 168 seats, while the SLFP collapsed. The newly formed Tamil United Liberation Front (TULF), which had campaigned for a mandate for a separate state, won nearly all of the seats in the Tamil-majority areas of the north and east, a total of about 18 seats, becoming the second-largest party and the official opposition, with A. Amirthalingam as leader of the opposition. This outcome created the unusual situation of a government with a huge Sinhalese-majority mandate and an opposition whose electoral mandate was to represent a separatist platform, and it intensified the pressure on both sides. Anti-Tamil violence broke out in August 1977, after the election, in Jaffna and in other parts of the island, with deaths and displacement, particularly among Tamil plantation workers in the central highlands.

Jayewardene used his majority to change the political system and the economy. His government introduced a program of economic liberalization, including reduced import controls, encouragement of foreign investment, free trade zones, and a large infrastructure program, notably the accelerated Mahaweli irrigation and hydropower scheme, and the reduction of food subsidies. The program fostered export-oriented growth alongside inflation and inequality. He also redesigned the constitutional structure. The executive presidency first took effect on 4 February 1978 under the Second Amendment to the 1972 Constitution. The Second Republican Constitution, effective 7 September 1978, consolidated that presidential system, modeled in part on the French system. The president was directly elected for a six-year term, served as head of state and government, appointed the prime minister and cabinet, and held substantial powers, including the power to dissolve parliament after a year. The constitution also introduced proportional representation in the electoral system, which gave minorities greater legislative representation, created a chapter of fundamental rights, recognized Sinhala as the official language and Sinhala and Tamil as national languages (the latter provision strengthening the status of Tamil), and retained the foremost place of Buddhism and a unitary state. It renamed the country the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka.

Assessment of the 1978 Constitution divides scholars. Supporters argued that a strong executive was needed to carry out economic reform and provide stability, and that proportional representation and a charter of rights improved on 1972. Critics, including legal scholars and the opposition, argued that it concentrated power in a president insulated from parliamentary accountability, made the president hard to remove, and left minority concerns about devolution unaddressed. Consequences soon followed. Jayewardene extended the life of parliament through a referendum in December 1982, which postponed the next general election to 1989, and he used emergency powers extensively. The executive presidency also shaped the later decision-making on the Tamil question, since a single office could make rapid and often unilateral choices, whether for settlement or for repression. Division III · Section 96.11

Tamil Nationalism, Political Demands, and the Breakdown of Constitutional Opposition

Tamil politics in the 1970s moved along a path from a demand for federal autonomy to one for a separate state, and this movement cannot be understood apart from the experience of the previous two decades. The Federal Party, founded in 1949, argued that the Tamil-speaking north and east should form an autonomous unit within a federal Ceylon, with Tamil language rights and control over land settlement. It practiced nonviolent protest, including satyagraha campaigns inspired by Gandhi, and negotiated agreements with Sinhalese leaders in 1957 and 1965. The collapse of each agreement strengthened those who said that Sinhalese-dominated parliaments would never concede meaningful autonomy. After the Federal Party left the 1972 Constituent Assembly, several Tamil parties, including the Federal Party, the Tamil Congress, and the Ceylon Workers' Congress representing Indian-origin Tamils, formed the Tamil United Front in 1972. In 1976 it was renamed the Tamil United Liberation Front (TULF), with Chelvanayakam as its leader until his death in 1977.

On 14 May 1976, at Vaddukoddai in the Jaffna peninsula, the TULF adopted a resolution declaring that the Tamil nation had the right to self-determination and calling for the restoration of a free, sovereign, secular, socialist state of Tamil Eelam, comprising the northern and eastern provinces. The resolution argued that successive Sinhalese governments had deprived Tamils of equality, and that the 1972 Constitution had finally closed the constitutional path. It also called on Tamil youth to take up the struggle, which signaled the leaders' recognition of rising youth militancy and their wish to channel it. The TULF fought the 1977 election on this platform and won a decisive mandate in the Tamil areas. Even so, it remained a parliamentary party, took its seats, and was willing to negotiate. Its leaders, including Amirthalingam, repeatedly said that they would settle for a satisfactory regional autonomy, and this ambiguity is central to understanding the period: the movement for a separate state was in part a bargaining position and in part a statement of despair, and its parliamentary leaders were increasingly criticized by younger activists as ineffective.

A series of grievances explained the radicalization of Tamil youth. Educational standardization and district quotas for university admissions reduced the proportion of Tamil students in science and medical faculties. Employment in the public service and state corporations, in which Tamils had once been well represented, declined as Sinhala became the language of administration. State-aided colonization in the north-central and eastern provinces altered the demographic balance in some areas. And security forces, particularly the army and police, which were overwhelmingly Sinhalese in composition, were seen by many Tamils as an alien force. The Prevention of Terrorism Act of 1979, initially temporary and made permanent in 1982, allowed detention without trial for long periods and admission of confessions to police officers, and it came to be associated with arbitrary arrests and torture of Tamil suspects. In July 1979 Jayewardene sent the army to Jaffna with instructions to eliminate terrorism within a short period, and a heavy security presence followed.

Anti-Tamil violence before 1983 formed part of this background. Riots in 1956 and 1958 had killed Tamils, and in 1977 and again in 1981, there were attacks across the island, with deaths, displacement, and destruction of property. Particular importance attaches to the burning of the Jaffna Public Library on the night of 31 May to 1 June 1981, during a period of violence after a District Development Council election campaign, when police and paramilitary personnel were reported to have run amok in Jaffna, burning shops, a newspaper office, the home of a TULF member of parliament, and the library. The library, one of the largest in South Asia, held tens of thousands of books and rare manuscripts, estimated at roughly 90,000 to 97,000 volumes, and its destruction was a profound blow to Tamil intellectual life. Many Tamils interpreted it as an act of cultural destruction. Government ministers were reported to have been in Jaffna at the time, and the extent of official responsibility has remained a matter of controversy, with commissions and observers pointing to the role of security personnel.

Students should distinguish carefully between several different positions. Demands for federal autonomy, for a separate state, and for armed struggle were not identical, and different groups held each. Federal autonomy sought a division of power within a single state. Separatism sought independence and could be pursued by constitutional campaigning, as the TULF did, or by force. Armed militancy added the use of violence, which was directed at security forces, at political rivals, and sometimes at civilians. By the early 1980s the belief that parliamentary politics could secure Tamil objectives was in retreat, and the monopoly of the TULF over Tamil politics was being challenged by militant groups, a development examined in the next section.

Division III · Section 96.12

The Rise of Tamil Militancy and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam

Armed Tamil groups emerged in the 1970s from a generation of young people, many of them educated, from the northern peninsula and the east, who regarded the parliamentary leadership as unsuccessful. The roots of the militant movement lay in student organizations and small secret groups, and its early actions included attacks on police and on Tamils who were regarded as collaborators with the government. A significant event was the killing of Alfred Duraiappah, the Jaffna mayor and a member of the SLFP, in July 1975 by a young militant, Velupillai Prabhakaran, then in his early twenties. Over the next years numerous groups formed, often small and personalized, with differing ideologies and relationships to the TULF.

The Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) took its name in 1976 from the earlier Tamil New Tigers, which Prabhakaran had founded in 1972. It was a highly disciplined organization with a clear military focus, a strong cult of leadership, and an emphasis on separation as the sole objective, along with a rhetoric of socialism that was secondary to nationalism. Other groups included the Tamil Eelam Liberation Organization (TELO), which was also strongly separatist, had links with Tamil Nadu, and lost its early leaders to arrest and killing before Sri Sabaratnam took over; the Eelam People's Revolutionary Liberation Front (EPRLF), a Marxist-oriented organization led by K. Pathmanabha and Douglas Devananda; the People's Liberation Organisation of Tamil Eelam (PLOTE), led by Uma Maheswaran; and the Eelam Revolutionary Organisation of Students (EROS). They differed in ideology, social base, and strategy, and their rivalry was as important to the history of the 1980s as their conflict with the state.

Militant organizations recruited from young men and, increasingly, women, drawing on feelings of humiliation and rage after anti-Tamil violence and on the lack of employment and educational prospects. They financed themselves through donations, local taxation, robberies, and, increasingly, support from the Tamil diaspora and external sponsors. Their operations in the late 1970s and early 1980s included bank robberies, ambushes of police and army patrols, and assassinations. The Sri Lankan government responded with emergency regulations, the Prevention of Terrorism Act, and security operations, which hurt many civilians, fueled recruitment, and widened the gap between the state and the Tamil population. The TULF leaders condemned violence on some occasions and tried to remain in contact with the militants on others, which left them politically exposed.

India became increasingly significant. Tamil Nadu, across the narrow Palk Strait, had close linguistic and cultural ties with the northern Sri Lankan Tamils, and public opinion there was sympathetic. After 1983 especially, militant groups established training camps and bases in Tamil Nadu and elsewhere in India, and refugees crossed the strait in large numbers. The Indian central government, led by Indira Gandhi, tolerated and in some accounts assisted the militants, partly for domestic political reasons, partly as a way to pressure Jayewardene, whose government was perceived as tilting toward the United States and Pakistan, and partly to retain the ability to mediate. The issue of Indian support for the militants, and the ambiguity of India's policy, were to have major consequences, as Section 96.15 shows.

The LTTE was one organization within a broader history of Tamil politics, and it did not speak for all Sri Lankan Tamils. Many Tamils supported constitutional parties, some supported other militant groups, and many were more concerned with safety and livelihoods than with any political program. Over the 1980s the LTTE moved to establish its supremacy through military discipline, strong leadership, and, notably, the violent suppression of rival organizations, especially TELO in 1986 and later the EPRLF and others, a process that killed many Tamil militants and political figures and later left the LTTE with a near-monopoly in Tamil armed politics. The history of this period thus includes violence directed by Tamil militants against other Tamils as well as against the Sri Lankan state and Sinhalese and Muslim civilians, and a responsible account recognizes both the legitimacy of Tamil grievances and the harm inflicted by militant organizations.

Sri Lanka: Black July, Civil War, and Intervention

Division IV · Section 96.13

Black July, 1983: Anti-Tamil Violence and the Transformation of Political Conflict

On the night of 23 July 1983, an LTTE unit ambushed an army patrol at Thirunelveli, near Jaffna, killing thirteen soldiers. It was the largest single loss the army had suffered at militant hands, and it came after months of attacks and reprisals in the north. The government decided to bring the soldiers' bodies to Colombo for a funeral at the main cemetery at Kanatte, rather than return them to their families in the south. The decision, together with the political context, helped create conditions for mass violence: a large crowd gathered, and on the evening of 24 July attacks on Tamil homes and businesses began in the Colombo suburbs. Over the following days violence spread through Colombo and to Kandy, Trincomalee, Nuwara Eliya, Badulla, and other locations, continuing in some places until the end of the month.

Eyewitness accounts, journalism, and later human-rights reports agree on several features. Groups of men, often in vehicles and carrying lists, identified Tamil-owned houses, shops, and factories, using electoral registers and business records, and set fire to them. Tamils were dragged from buses and cars and killed, some by burning. In poorer districts and among Indian-origin Tamil workers, there were attacks as well. Many Sinhalese and Muslim neighbors sheltered Tamils at personal risk, and there were also instances of police and soldiers protecting individuals, which shows that the participants in the violence did not include all Sinhalese and that responses varied. At the same time, many witnesses reported that police and soldiers stood by, and that in some cases security personnel participated or encouraged attacks. Allegations also pointed to involvement by individuals associated with the governing party, including some local politicians and trade union groups, in organizing the violence. The government blamed leftist parties and the JVP, banning three organizations, a claim that has not been supported by the preponderance of independent evidence. President Jayewardene took several days to address the nation, and when he did on 28 July, his remarks were criticized by many for stressing Sinhalese resentment and showing little sympathy for the victims.

Among the worst episodes occurred in Welikada prison in Colombo, where on 25 July and again on 27 July, prisoners, most of them Tamils detained under the Prevention of Terrorism Act, were killed in their cells by other inmates. In total fifty-three Tamil prisoners died. The inadequacy of prison security, and allegations about the role of guards and officials, have been the subject of continued controversy, and no thorough accountability followed. After the violence, thousands of Tamils took refuge in camps or fled by air and sea, and the government evacuated many to the north by ship.

Estimates of the scale of the violence differ. The government's official count of deaths was a few hundred, commonly given as around 400. Other estimates, from Tamil organizations, some journalists, and researchers, range substantially higher, from about a thousand to as many as two or three thousand. The discrepancy reflects differences in documentation: official figures counted bodies and registered deaths, while higher estimates relied on local testimony and extrapolation, and some bodies were disposed of without registration. The numbers of homes and businesses destroyed and of people made homeless, estimated in the tens of thousands, also varied by source. Students should treat any specific number as an attributed estimate. What is not disputed is that the violence was large in scale, that it was targeted at a particular ethnic community, and that the state failed to protect its citizens.

The legislative response worsened matters. In August 1983 the government passed the Sixth Amendment to the constitution, which prohibited advocacy of separatism and required members of parliament to take an oath renouncing it. The TULF members, who had been elected on a platform that included self-determination, refused and lost their seats, so that Tamil parliamentary representation effectively disappeared at the moment of greatest crisis. For many Tamils the combination of mob violence, official failure, and the removal of their elected representatives destroyed confidence in the Sri Lankan state, and the numbers who left the island or joined militant organizations increased. Black July therefore marks a turning point, but it should be seen not as an undifferentiated or spontaneous communal outburst but as organized violence, in which political actors, local organizers, and security forces had roles, and for which the state bore responsibility. It should also not be read as the collective act of all Sinhalese civilians.

Everyday Life Study 2

A Sri Lankan Tamil Family during Black July and Displacement

Note: this is a clearly identified composite example based on patterns documented in survivor testimony and reports; it contains no invented quotations or personal histories.

Imagine a Tamil family living in a Colombo suburb in July 1983, with a father who is a clerk in a commercial firm, a mother who runs a small shop from their house, and school-age children. As violence spread, neighbors of different communities warned them to leave. Some families sheltered with Sinhalese or Muslim friends for a night or two; others saw their houses burned and their shop looted. Seeking safety, they moved to a temporary refugee camp in a school or temple, where food and sanitation were limited, and from there the family decided to leave for Jaffna by ship, losing most of their property. Children's schooling was interrupted, and the father's employment ended with the destruction of his workplace. Relatives who had been working in Colombo became dependent on kin in the north, and later some family members left for Tamil Nadu, Europe, Canada, or the Gulf, sending remittances and forming the beginnings of diaspora communities. In the north and east they encountered a new form of insecurity, from security operations and from militant demands for recruits and funds.

The example illustrates how violence transformed housing, livelihood, education, and family structure, and how displacement created networks that later shaped the conflict's international dimension.

Cause and Effect

Black July and the Transformation of the Conflict

Long-term causes

Language and citizenship disputes after 1948, the failure of autonomy agreements, the 1972 Constitution, educational and employment grievances, and earlier riots in 1958 and 1977 created deep distrust.

Short-term causes

The LTTE ambush of 23 July, the decision to bring the soldiers' bodies to Colombo, rumors, organized groups with lists, and the failure of security forces to act decisively.

Immediate effects

Hundreds or more killed, widespread destruction of Tamil property, mass displacement, the Welikada killings, and the Sixth Amendment removing TULF members from parliament.

Long-term consequences

Tamil flight abroad and to India, growth of militant organizations, deeper Indian involvement, and a shift from political bargaining to protracted war.

Historical complexity: The violence cannot be reduced to the ambush alone, which triggered but did not by itself cause it, or to ancient hatred, which does not explain why the violence took this form at this time. Political organization, state failure, and a decades-long record of unresolved grievance all contributed.

Division IV · Section 96.14

The Sri Lankan Civil War, 1983–1987: Armed Conflict, Civilian Displacement, and Political Polarization

After 1983 the confrontation between the Sri Lankan state and Tamil militant groups became a sustained armed conflict. The Sri Lankan army, which before 1983 had been small and untrained for counterinsurgency, expanded rapidly, as did the police and a newly created Special Task Force, and the government acquired weapons and training from abroad, including from Pakistan, Israel, the United Kingdom, and others. Militant organizations recruited in large numbers, operated from camps in Tamil Nadu and in the north, and mounted attacks on military and police posts, ambushes, and bombings. The Northern Province, and parts of the Eastern Province, became the principal theatre, with towns such as Jaffna, Vavuniya, Mannar, and Trincomalee as foci of operations.

The conflict affected different communities in different ways. Tamil civilians in the north and east experienced military operations, curfews, checkpoints, arbitrary detention, disappearances, and shelling, along with demands by militants for contributions, recruits, and compliance. Among the incidents widely reported were army reprisals after attacks, including in Valvettithurai and Point Pedro, and killings of civilians in the east in 1984–1987. Sinhalese civilians were also targeted, notably in the massacre at Anuradhapura in May 1985, when LTTE gunmen killed well over a hundred people, mostly pilgrims and bus passengers, and in attacks on Sinhalese border villages and farm settlements in the north and east in 1984–1986. Muslim communities, who lived intermingled with Tamils and Sinhalese in the east, were caught between them, were victims in several incidents, and increasingly felt that neither the state nor the Tamil militants represented them. In the central highlands, Indian-origin Tamil plantation workers, who had suffered in 1977 and 1983, remained outside the northern and eastern core of the conflict but were affected by displacement and insecurity.

Within the Tamil community, the LTTE moved to eliminate rival organizations. In April and May 1986 it attacked TELO, killing hundreds of its members, including its leader Sri Sabaratnam, and later it suppressed the EPRLF and other groups in the north, so that by the late 1980s the LTTE dominated armed Tamil politics. This was a violent process that reduced pluralism within the Tamil movement and, in the eyes of many Tamil civilians, demonstrated its willingness to eliminate dissent. The government, for its part, used emergency regulations, the Prevention of Terrorism Act, and security operations, and there were widespread allegations of torture, extrajudicial killing, and disappearance by the security forces, documented by Amnesty International, the International Commission of Jurists, and other groups. It is important to distinguish between legitimate counterinsurgency operations against armed groups and attacks against civilians, and to hold both state forces and militant organizations to the same standards.

Political efforts continued. India, increasingly engaged, sponsored talks in Thimphu, Bhutan, in July–August 1985 between the Sri Lankan government and Tamil groups, including the LTTE, TELO, EPRLF, and TULF, at which the Tamil groups put forward four principles, including recognition of Tamils as a distinct nation, a traditional homeland, the right of self-determination, and citizenship rights for all Tamils in Sri Lanka. The government rejected them, and the talks collapsed. Ceasefires in 1985 failed. Jayewardene explored the idea of provincial councils, and a proposal in 1986 based on devolution to provinces was discussed but not agreed. In May 1987 the army launched Operation Liberation in the Vadamarachchi region of the Jaffna peninsula, a large offensive against the LTTE. India, which was unwilling to see the Tamil insurgents crushed and was under pressure from Tamil Nadu, air-dropped relief supplies over Jaffna on 4 June 1987, a violation of Sri Lankan airspace that made clear its willingness to act. The incident led to negotiations that produced the 1987 Accord.

Displacement was a defining feature. By the mid-1980s large numbers of Tamils had been displaced inside Sri Lanka or had crossed to India, where Tamil Nadu hosted camps for refugees. Others sought asylum in Europe, Canada, and elsewhere, which began the formation of an international Tamil diaspora whose political and financial support later became significant. Estimates of the numbers displaced, of civilians killed, and of refugees vary by agency and year, and students should use dated and consistently defined figures, as the following study illustrates.

Division IV · Section 96.15

The Indo-Sri Lanka Accord of 1987 and the Indian Peace Keeping Force

India's involvement in Sri Lanka grew from several motives. Tamil Nadu's political leaders pressed the central government to act on behalf of Sri Lankan Tamils, and the regional parties, whose support Delhi needed, made this a matter of domestic politics. The central government also saw Jayewardene's relations with the United States, Pakistan, and Israel as hostile to Indian regional interests, and it did not want a foreign military presence or a Tamil insurgency out of control on its southern flank. Indira Gandhi, and after her assassination in 1984 her son Rajiv Gandhi, therefore pursued a mixed policy: supporting the Tamil militants with training and sanctuary, mediating, and pressing the Sri Lankan government to offer devolution. By mid-1987, with the army's offensive threatening the LTTE and with India's airdrop having shown its resolve, Jayewardene concluded that an agreement with India was preferable to continued confrontation.

The Indo-Sri Lanka Accord was signed in Colombo by Rajiv Gandhi and J. R. Jayewardene on 29 July 1987. Its principal provisions can be summarized as follows. Sri Lanka would devolve power to provincial councils, with the Northern and Eastern Provinces temporarily merged into a single unit, subject to a referendum in the Eastern Province to be held later on whether the merger should continue. Tamil would be recognized as an official language alongside Sinhala, with English as a link language. Hostilities would cease, militants would surrender their arms, and an Indian Peace Keeping Force (IPKF) would supervise the ceasefire at Sri Lanka's request. In addition, the two governments exchanged understandings that Sri Lankan territory would not be used in ways prejudicial to Indian interests, concerning foreign military and broadcasting facilities and personnel. The Sri Lankan Parliament enacted the Thirteenth Amendment to the constitution and the Provincial Councils Act in November 1987, creating provincial councils with a governor, a board of ministers, and defined powers over listed subjects, although with the retention of a unitary state and with the central government keeping control over key areas, with police and land powers remaining contested.

The accord was controversial. In Sri Lanka, Sinhalese nationalists, the JVP, and sections of the SLFP and the UNP condemned it as a surrender of sovereignty to India; riots broke out in Colombo, and a Sri Lankan naval rating struck Rajiv Gandhi with a rifle butt during a ceremonial inspection. Prime Minister Premadasa and some other UNP figures were reluctant. Among Tamils, many welcomed the promise of devolution and an end to fighting, but the LTTE, which had not been a party to the negotiations, opposed an arrangement that fell short of independence and left it with no guaranteed role, and it distrusted India's intentions. Prabhakaran accepted the accord only under pressure and handed over a limited number of weapons. Within a few months the cooperation between the LTTE and the IPKF collapsed. In October 1987, after a dispute over the arrest of several LTTE members by the Sri Lankan navy and their suicide by cyanide, violence resumed, and the IPKF began a large offensive, known as Operation Pawan, to take control of Jaffna.

The IPKF grew to tens of thousands of troops, commonly cited at 50,000 or more at its peak, and it fought the LTTE in the north and east for more than two years. The campaign cost the Indian army more than a thousand dead and many wounded, and it caused a high toll among civilians and militants. There were documented abuses by multiple actors: the LTTE used civilians as cover, conscripted fighters, and killed opponents; some IPKF units were accused of killings, including in a widely reported incident at Jaffna hospital in October 1987, of torture, and of sexual violence; and the Sri Lankan government and its associated forces were responsible for other violations, including in the south. The IPKF also sponsored the Tamil National Army and supported the EPRLF-led administration of the merged North-Eastern Provincial Council, elected in November 1988 in an election that the LTTE boycotted and that was criticized for irregularities. The council's chief minister, Varadaraja Perumal, found his authority limited, and in March 1990, as the IPKF left, the council's leaders declared a form of independence, a gesture that was quickly overtaken by events.

The policy also changed with politics in both countries. Ranasinghe Premadasa, elected president in December 1988 after campaigning for the removal of Indian forces, demanded that the IPKF leave and opened talks with the LTTE, which received weapons from the Sri Lankan government in a tacit alliance against the Indian army. In India, the new government of V. P. Singh, which took office in December 1989 after Rajiv Gandhi's electoral defeat, agreed to withdraw. The last IPKF units left Sri Lanka in March 1990. The intervention had failed to disarm the LTTE or produce a settlement, and it left a legacy of Tamil bitterness and Sinhalese resentment, as well as the Thirteenth Amendment, which remained part of the constitution and the base for later debates over devolution. The LTTE's assassination of Rajiv Gandhi in May 1991, which lies beyond this chapter, was shaped by that history.

Historical Vocabulary
Devolution

Devolution is the transfer of specified powers from a central government to regional institutions, such as provincial councils, while the central government retains ultimate authority and sovereignty. It differs from federalism, in which the constitution divides sovereignty between levels of government, and from separatism, which seeks an independent state. In Sri Lanka, the 1987 Accord and the Thirteenth Amendment created provincial councils within a unitary state. For Tamil advocates of federalism, devolution could be a first step; for the LTTE it was inadequate; for many Sinhalese nationalists it threatened national unity. Which powers, especially police and land, were devolved is central to understanding why the settlement was disputed.

Division IV · Section 96.16

Sri Lanka's Multiple Conflicts, 1987–1991: The JVP Insurgency and Renewed War

By 1987 Sri Lanka was not facing a single civil war but several overlapping conflicts, with different participants, causes, and logics: the Tamil insurgency in the north and east, the Indian military intervention and its confrontation with the LTTE, and a second insurrection by the JVP in the south. Conflating these conflicts obscures the pattern of the late 1980s.

The JVP, which had been banned after 1971 and was then released in 1977, reorganized in the early 1980s and was proscribed again after the 1983 riots, which the government blamed on it. It drew on the grievances of rural Sinhalese youth, unemployment, and the perceived failures of the post-1977 economic order, and combined a Sinhalese nationalist and Marxist rhetoric. Under its leader Rohana Wijeweera it opposed the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord and the Indian presence, and from 1987 it mounted a campaign of assassinations, strikes, and intimidation against supporters of the government, including UNP politicians, local officials, and relatives of members of the security forces. Its military wing and associated front organizations enforced stoppages by threats of death and attacked state institutions.

The government answered with an extremely severe counterinsurgency. Security forces and associated paramilitary groups, sometimes called death squads, were responsible for large numbers of abductions, killings, and disappearances, with bodies found on roadsides and rivers and mass graves later documented. Estimates of those killed between 1987 and 1990 vary widely, from tens of thousands upward, and range in some accounts to 30,000 or more, though they are uncertain. Rohana Wijeweera was captured and killed in November 1989, and the insurrection was largely crushed by early 1990. Human-rights organizations documented abuses by both the JVP and the state, and later commissions investigated disappearances in the south, the survivors of which remain a significant component of memory in the Sinhalese-majority region.

Ranasinghe Premadasa, who had been prime minister under Jayewardene, won the presidential election of December 1988 narrowly against the SLFP's Sirimavo Bandaranaike, in a contest in which the JVP threatened voters and the Tamil north and east were largely unable to take part. His government sought to end the Indian presence, to reach an understanding with the LTTE, and to promote antipoverty programs and housing. In 1989 he initiated talks with the LTTE, which won legitimacy from the contact and received arms and recognition while the Indian army was still in the field. The talks failed to produce a settlement, partly because of disagreements over the status of the provincial councils, the future of the North-East merger, and the LTTE's insistence on its own political role.

After the IPKF left in March 1990, relations between the government and the LTTE collapsed. In June 1990 the LTTE attacked police stations in the east, and large-scale hostilities resumed, a phase often called the Second Eelam War. The resumption of war also brought violence against Muslim civilians. In August 1990 gunmen, widely attributed to the LTTE, attacked mosques in Kattankudy in the east, killing well over a hundred worshippers. In October 1990, the LTTE ordered the expulsion of Muslim residents from Jaffna and other northern areas on short notice, an event that displaced tens of thousands of people, commonly cited at around 75,000 across the north, and that remained a central grievance of northern Muslims. Muslim politicians in the east, meanwhile, increasingly organized a separate political identity, which reflected the unwillingness of Muslims to be absorbed into either a Sinhalese or Tamil national project.

By 1991 the situation in Sri Lanka was that the state had defeated the southern insurrection but at great human cost and with a legacy of unresolved atrocities; it faced renewed war in the north and east against a more dominant and militarized LTTE; the provincial council system established in 1987 existed on paper but was weakened by the war and the collapse of the North-East council; and the island's communities were more divided than in 1971. Subsequent developments, including the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi in 1991 and of Premadasa in 1993, and the war's eventual end in 2009, are treated in Chapter 103.

Historical Debate 2

Could Sri Lanka's Ethnic Conflict Have Been Resolved through Constitutional Reform?

The question: Would language equality, federalism, regional autonomy, or power-sharing, adopted earlier or more fully, have prevented the violent conflict of the 1980s?

Interpretation A — Missed opportunities: The 1957 and 1965 agreements, the minority safeguards of the Soulbury Constitution, and proposals for devolution in the 1970s and 1980s could have addressed core grievances. Their repeated abandonment under pressure from majoritarian politics, and decisions such as the 1972 and 1978 constitutional choices and the Sixth Amendment, narrowed moderate options and strengthened militants.
Interpretation B — Structural and political obstacles: Electoral competition within the Sinhalese majority rewarded hard lines, and the unitary state tradition, plus fears of partition, limited what leaders believed they could concede. Moreover, once militant organizations pursued independence and eliminated rivals, they had little incentive to accept devolution, so that even a better offer might not have ended the war.

Evidence: The texts of the agreements and constitutions, parliamentary debates, party resolutions, accounts of the Thimphu talks and the Accord negotiations, and the later record of the Thirteenth Amendment all bear on the question. Comparative studies of other multiethnic states are often used.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether ethnic violence was avoidable and on what institutional design might manage diversity. Most scholars regard the failures of constitutional bargaining as an important cause but also stress the role of political incentives and violence.

Student task: Distinguish political demands, such as autonomy or independence, from attacks on civilians, which are not matters of factual dispute and cannot be treated as legitimate positions. Then explain which constitutional reforms, if any, you think might have changed outcomes, and what further evidence would help you decide.

Division V · Section 96.17

Nepal's Panchayat System: Monarchical Authority and Restricted Political Participation

Nepal's political system in the early 1970s was the product of a royal decision taken more than a decade earlier. After the overthrow of the Rana prime ministers in 1950–1951, King Tribhuvan returned to a central role, and under his son Mahendra, Nepal held its first general election in 1959. The Nepali Congress won a clear majority and B. P. Koirala became prime minister. On 15 December 1960, Mahendra dismissed the government, dissolved parliament, arrested Koirala and other leaders, and banned political parties, justifying the step by pointing to corruption, disorder, and the alleged unsuitability of Western-style party politics for Nepal. The Constitution of 1962, promulgated by the king, established the Panchayat system, described as a "partyless democracy."

The system was organized as a pyramid. At its base were village and town panchayats, elected by adult residents, which chose representatives to district panchayats, which in turn sent members to zonal panchayats and then to the national legislature, the Rashtriya Panchayat. Alongside this were "class organizations" for peasants, youth, women, laborers, and former soldiers, designed to channel social groups into the system. Political parties were prohibited, and candidates stood as individuals. Sovereignty was vested effectively in the king, who appointed the prime minister, could nominate members of the legislature, controlled the army, and could proclaim emergency, and the constitution gave him powers to amend the text. The official justification emphasized national unity, development, and an indigenous political tradition, with the monarchy as the unifying symbol of a diverse state. Mahendra's government promoted a nationalist program emphasizing the Nepali language, a common dress, and the Hindu monarchy, while its "Back to the Village" campaign sought to mobilize rural development.

Social and regional inequalities limited participation. Political and administrative power was concentrated in a small elite drawn mainly from hill Brahmin and Chhetri families and the court, and the benefits of development projects, funded heavily by foreign aid from India, the United States, China, and international agencies, were uneven. The Land Reform Act of 1964 set ceilings and attempted to regulate tenancy, but it was weakly implemented and left structures of landholding largely intact. Literacy and access to education were low and expanded gradually, and the growth of schools and colleges would in time produce the students who challenged the system. Regional differences were significant: the Terai plains in the south, with their Madhesi populations and links to India, the hill ethnic groups, often called Janajati, and the Himalayan communities were underrepresented in state institutions, and cultural policies privileging Nepali and Hindu symbols created resentments that would surface after 1990.

Mahendra died on 31 January 1972 and was succeeded by his son Birendra, who was educated in part in Britain and the United States and was formally crowned in 1975. Birendra inherited a system in which the monarchy was the center of authority, and he sought at first to continue it, with adjustments, and to present himself as a modernizing king. His reign began with a foreign policy proposal in 1975 to declare Nepal a "zone of peace," which won support from China, Pakistan, and others but not from India, and illustrates Nepal's concern to balance its two large neighbors. The system's legitimacy rested on the monarchy's popularity, the absence of an alternative organized opposition inside the country, and the suppression of exiled parties, but it was always open to the charge that it excluded most citizens from real decision-making.

Historical Vocabulary
Panchayat

The word panchayat traditionally refers in South Asia to a council of village elders. In India it was adopted for elected local government institutions. In Nepal after 1962 it became the name of an entire political system, in which councils at village, district, zonal, and national level formed a ladder under royal authority, and political parties were prohibited. Students should not assume it meant the same thing as Indian panchayati raj: the Nepali Panchayat was a framework of monarchical government, not a local-government layer within a multiparty democracy. Its significance lies in how it combined the language of grassroots participation with strict limits on political organization.

Division V · Section 96.18

Nepal's Democratic Movement: The 1980 Referendum and the People's Movement of 1990

Pressure on the Panchayat system built through the 1970s and 1980s. The outlawed Nepali Congress, with leaders in exile in India or in prison inside Nepal, kept alive the demand for multiparty democracy, and small communist groups, divided into many factions, operated underground. Student organizations, which had grown as education expanded, were the catalyst of a major crisis in 1979. Demonstrations that began over a dispute about education and international events grew into broader protests against the political system, with clashes with police in Kathmandu and elsewhere. In May 1979 King Birendra, seeking to defuse the movement, announced that a referendum would be held to let the people decide between the Panchayat system, with suitable reforms, and a multiparty system.

The referendum was held on 2 May 1980 and the Panchayat option won, with roughly 55 percent against about 45 percent for the multiparty alternative. The result was close, but historians question how free the process was. The campaign took place with restrictions on the opposition, who were not allowed to organize openly as parties and had limited access to the state-controlled media, while the Panchayat side enjoyed the state's resources and the king's backing, and there were allegations of irregularities. The outcome nevertheless allowed the monarchy to claim a popular mandate, and the constitution was amended in 1980 to introduce direct elections to the Rashtriya Panchayat on the basis of adult franchise and to make the prime minister formally accountable to it. Elections followed in 1981 and 1986, but the party ban and the king's powers remained, and the Nepali Congress, which boycotted some of them, launched a civil disobedience movement in 1985.

The final crisis was produced by a combination of domestic and external factors. Economic difficulties, charges of corruption in the court and the Panchayat establishment, and growing political awareness fed discontent. In March 1989 the trade and transit treaties between India and Nepal expired and were not renewed, in a dispute that involved Nepal's purchase of Chinese weapons and the status of Indian workers, and India closed most of the border crossings, creating severe shortages of fuel and other goods in Nepal. The blockade, which lasted for more than a year, damaged the economy, increased hostility to the system, and signaled that India would not defend the existing order, although how far India deliberately supported the democratic movement is debated.

The Nepali Congress and United Left Front launched the Jana Andolan, or People's Movement, on 18 February 1990. The cooperation between the Nepali Congress and left-wing organizations was crucial: it combined the Congress's national network with the organizing capacity of leftist student and professional groups. The movement mobilized students, lawyers, doctors, teachers, journalists, and ordinary townspeople in demonstrations, strikes, and marches in Kathmandu, Patan, Bhaktapur, and other towns. Security forces responded with arrests and gunfire, and dozens of people were killed, particularly in early April. Faced with escalating protest and a sense of isolation, the king on 8 April 1990 lifted the ban on political parties, dissolved the Panchayat structures, and invited the opposition to form an interim government, headed by the Nepali Congress's Krishna Prasad Bhattarai, which took office in April with representatives of the left, the king's nominees, and human rights figures.

A Constitution Recommendation Commission drafted a new constitution, which was promulgated on 9 November 1990. It established a constitutional monarchy under a multiparty parliamentary system, with a bicameral legislature, an independent judiciary, and fundamental rights, and it declared sovereignty to be vested in the Nepalese people. At the same time it kept Nepal a Hindu kingdom, designated Nepali as the language of the nation while recognizing other languages as national languages, and left the king as head of state with command of the army through a defense council and with certain emergency powers. Critics later argued that these provisions left unresolved questions of religion, language, and ethnic and regional inclusion, which would matter in the decades that followed. In the first general election held under the new constitution, in May 1991, the Nepali Congress won an outright majority, with the communist party, the Communist Party of Nepal (UML), the second force, and Girija Prasad Koirala became prime minister.

Why did Nepal's monarchy accept a constitutional transition? The question can be answered by considering the king's choices. Birendra faced a widespread movement whose cost to repress by force was high, in a context where his own legitimacy as a symbol of national unity was at stake; the army leadership was not prepared to carry out massacres on the scale that would have been needed; and the Indian blockade and the international climate after the end of the Cold War in Europe reduced external support for authoritarian arrangements. The king retained a significant constitutional role, which made compromise more acceptable. The later abolition of the monarchy lies outside this chapter's chronology, and the transition of 1990 should not be read as an inevitable step toward it. It is, however, an example of a monarchy surrendering executive power under pressure, in contrast with the army-led politics of Bangladesh and the violent conflicts of Sri Lanka.

Bhutan and the Maldives: Monarchy, Citizenship, Development, and Security

Bhutan: Monarchy, Modernization, and the Citizenship Crisis

Bhutan, a Himalayan kingdom with a Buddhist political tradition, had been unified under a hereditary monarchy established in 1907, and had conducted its foreign relations under the guidance of British India and then independent India under treaties of 1910 and 1949. Under the third king, Jigme Dorji Wangchuk, the country opened to planned development beginning in 1961, built roads, schools, and hospitals with Indian assistance, created a National Assembly (the Tshogdu) in 1953 and a Royal Advisory Council in 1965, and joined the United Nations in 1971. When he died in July 1972, his son Jigme Singye Wangchuck, then a teenager, succeeded him; he was formally crowned in 1974.

The king pursued planned modernization, hydropower, district administration, and education under an ethos of cultural preservation and "Gross National Happiness." The government stressed national integration around a Drukpa Buddhist, Dzongkha-speaking identity, and in the late 1980s it introduced the Driglam Namzha code of etiquette and dress and made Dzongkha more prominent in schooling, in a campaign associated with the slogan "One Nation, One People." These measures were presented as a defense of a small state's identity against demographic and cultural pressure, including from the settled Nepali-speaking population of the south, estimated by the government to be growing quickly and, in its view, including large numbers of illegal immigrants.

The conflict that followed concerned citizenship. Nepali-speaking Bhutanese, commonly called Lhotshampa ("southerners"), had settled in the southern foothills from the late nineteenth century, and many acquired citizenship under the Nationality Law of 1958, which gave citizenship to those who had resided in Bhutan for a set period. The 1958 Nationality Law had recognized citizenship by descent through a Bhutanese father. The 1985 Citizenship Act changed the rules: citizenship by birth required both parents to be Bhutanese citizens; separate provisions addressed citizenship by registration for people permanently domiciled by 31 December 1958 and recorded in the census, and naturalization under additional conditions. The 1988 southern census applied strict documentary classifications, from citizens to migrants and non-nationals. Those classified as non-nationals faced loss of civil rights, restrictions on education, employment, and movement, and the threat of eviction. Different families faced different circumstances: some held documents of 1958 residence, some had lost or never held them, some were recent arrivals, and some had been long-established but could not prove it. Students should therefore not assume that all Nepali-speaking residents had the same legal status.

Protest developed from 1989 and peaked in September 1990, when demonstrations in southern districts were met with arrests, army and police action, and reports of abuses. A Bhutan People's Party was formed in exile, and activists such as Tek Nath Rizal, arrested in 1989, became symbols. The first Bhutanese refugees reached Nepal around the end of 1990 and through 1991; approximately 5,000 were reported there by September 1991. Most mass displacement and major UNHCR-supported camp expansion followed in 1992. The government said that most departures were voluntary migration by people who had no legal right to remain, or were the result of antinational agitation, and that some of the unrest involved violence by militants. Human-rights organizations and refugee testimonies said that many people were forced out, their property confiscated, and their citizenship arbitrarily denied. The legislation describes legal rules; testimony and human-rights investigations illuminate enforcement. The numbers grew in later years to well over 100,000, and their later resettlement and diplomacy lie outside this chapter.

The Maldives: Executive Authority, Tourism, and the Coup of 1988

The Maldives, a coral-atoll state southwest of India, became independent from Britain in 1965 and a republic in 1968. President Ibrahim Nasir expanded schooling, administration, and tourism, with the first resorts opening in 1972. Economic and political controversy preceded his departure for Singapore in 1978. The Majlis nominated Maumoon Abdul Gayoom, educated at al-Azhar in Cairo, and he was confirmed in a referendum. Single-candidate referendums reconfirmed him in 1983 and 1988.

Gayoom's government combined strong executive authority with economic modernization. Political parties were not permitted, and the system depended on the president's dominance, the Majlis's nomination role, and a close circle of officials and business families. Tourism grew rapidly, from a few thousand visitors in the early 1970s to well over a hundred thousand arrivals by the late 1980s, and became the principal source of foreign exchange and government revenue, alongside fisheries. Education and health services expanded. Administration was centralized in the capital, Malé, where much of the population and nearly all political power was concentrated, and outlying atolls depended on the capital for services, transport, and employment, and had fewer resources and less political voice. Islam was the state religion, with the president's religious authority supporting his political position.

The government was shaken by attempts to unseat it. After reported plots in 1980 and 1983, on 3 November 1988 a group of several dozen armed men, mostly Tamil mercenaries from Sri Lanka associated with the militant organization PLOTE, landed in Malé and seized parts of the capital, with the support of Maldivian opponents of the government, including a businessman, Abdullah Luthufi. President Gayoom took refuge in a safe location and appealed for help to several countries, including India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, the United States, and the United Kingdom. India responded within hours. In Operation Cactus, Indian paratroopers flown from Agra landed at Hulhule airport in the evening of 3 November, secured the airport and the capital, and restored the government's control by the next day, while Indian naval vessels intercepted a vessel carrying some of the mercenaries and hostages. Casualties included about a score of people, most of them Maldivians, and the mercenaries were captured and later handed over for trial.

India intervened at the Maldivian government's request, an important element of its legal and diplomatic justification. It also illustrates the security vulnerability of a small island state with minimal armed forces, and the capacity of a regional power to respond quickly. The dependence also raised questions about sovereignty and regional power. For Gayoom, the failed coup reinforced his control for another two decades, and it influenced India's self-image as a regional security provider, a theme developed in Chapter 97.

Review and Historical Synthesis: State-Building, Democracy, Ethnic Conflict, and Regional Power

The five states confronted related questions of political legitimacy, development, minority rights, and regional influence, yet followed different paths.

Why did Bangladesh experience coups? Famine, conflict, and the Fourth Amendment narrowed the political space of a dominant-party state. A divided army then became the strongest organized arbiter. Zia and Ershad prolonged military-backed rule through referendums and controlled electoral competition, while the 1990–1991 popular transition restored parliamentary government without abolishing military influence.

Why did Sri Lanka descend into prolonged conflict? Majoritarian politics, language policy, the loss of constitutional safeguards, failed autonomy agreements, anti-Tamil violence, armed militancy, and Indian intervention reinforced one another. The resulting war was not inevitable; specific political decisions narrowed peaceful alternatives. The contemporaneous JVP insurgency also demonstrated grievances among southern Sinhalese youth.

Why did Nepal's monarchy accept a constitutional transition? The Panchayat monarchy restricted political participation yet retained room for negotiated transition. In 1990 the combination of a broad coalition of the Nepali Congress and the left, widespread urban mobilization, economic pressure from the Indian blockade, and the reluctance of the army to engage in mass repression made compromise the least costly option. The 1990 constitution preserved the monarchy and many of its powers, as well as the Hindu and Nepali-language identity of the state, and so left structural questions of inclusion for later.

Why did Bhutan's citizenship policies produce displacement? A small state with a monarchy that regarded cultural continuity as critical to survival, and that feared demographic change in its south, tightened the legal definition of citizenship in 1985, carried out a census in 1988 that classified southern residents by documentary proof, and promoted a single national culture in 1989. These measures, in the context of a lack of institutional channels for dissent, turned a legal and cultural dispute into protest, repression, and mass flight. Legal categories and their enforcement require separate scrutiny.

Why was the Maldives' stability so closely connected to executive authority and regional security? The Maldives' centralized presidency lacked strong countervailing institutions; the 1988 coup demonstrated its vulnerability and reliance on India.

Across the five cases, constitutional texts mattered only in relation to political practice. Economic changes could sustain or undermine legitimacy; minority protection and inclusion were essential to stability. India's influence varied from diplomacy and intervention to economic pressure and security assistance. Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives therefore demonstrate divergent responses to shared problems, not one regional political destiny.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Authority in Bangladesh, 1972–1991

What Changed

  • The structure of government moved from parliamentary to presidential, military-backed, and back to parliamentary.
  • The constitutional definition of identity shifted from secular Bengali nationalism toward Bangladeshi nationalism and an acknowledged role for Islam.
  • Economic policy moved from nationalization toward liberalization and rural development.

What Stayed the Same

  • Personalized leadership and a strong executive remained central.
  • The armed forces remained a major political institution.
  • Poverty, dependence on aid and overseas labor, and flood vulnerability endured.
  • Rivalry between the Awami League and the BNP and its leaders continued to define politics.

Who experienced change most directly, and who continuity? Urban politicians, students, and professionals experienced regime changes most directly, whereas rural landless laborers experienced more continuity of insecurity, as the 1974 famine showed. Why the pattern matters: It shows that changes at the top do not automatically transform the material conditions or the institutional habits of a state.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has compared the political development of five smaller South Asian states between 1971 and 1991: Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives. Its central argument is that these states followed different trajectories of state-building, constitutional change, military intervention, ethnic conflict, and democratic mobilization, and that their divergence shows how national identity, centralized authority, minority rights, and regional intervention shaped postcolonial politics.

Bangladesh began independence with a popular government, a parliamentary constitution built on four principles, and an overwhelming Awami League majority, but also with a devastated economy. The famine of 1974, best understood as a collapse of entitlements and state capacity interacting with floods and price rises, undermined the government's legitimacy. The emergency of December 1974, the Fourth Amendment, and the creation of BAKSAL then replaced parliamentary government with a single-party presidential system, and the coups of August and November 1975 destroyed the order of 1972.

Under Ziaur Rahman, Bangladesh acquired a military-backed presidential regime that held a referendum and elections, created the BNP, and redefined the constitutional emphasis from secularism and Bengali nationalism toward Islam and Bangladeshi nationalism. His assassination in 1981 led to Ershad's coup in 1982, a more direct military government that decentralized administration, built the Jatiya Party, held contested elections, and in 1988 made Islam the state religion. A united opposition and mass movement forced Ershad's resignation in December 1990, and the election of February 1991 and the Twelfth Amendment restored parliamentary government, though the rivalry of parties and the armed forces' autonomy persisted.

Sri Lanka's crisis grew from older disputes over citizenship, language, and religion. The Constitutions of 1972 and 1978 removed minority safeguards, retained a unitary state, and created an executive presidency, and the Tamil United Liberation Front's 1976 Vaddukoddai Resolution shifted Tamil political demands toward self-determination. Educational and employment grievances, security practices, and anti-Tamil violence, including the burning of the Jaffna library in 1981, widened the gap between the state and Tamil society and fed the growth of militant organizations, of which the LTTE became the dominant one by eliminating its rivals.

Black July in 1983 was a turning point: organized anti-Tamil violence, official failure to protect civilians, killings at Welikada prison, and the Sixth Amendment which removed Tamil parliamentarians transformed political conflict into civil war and prompted large-scale displacement and the growth of a diaspora. The war of 1983 to 1987 harmed Tamil, Sinhalese, and Muslim civilians, and all armed actors committed abuses. India's involvement, which moved from sheltering militants to mediating and then to military intervention, produced the Accord of 1987, the Thirteenth Amendment, and an IPKF deployment that ended in confrontation with the LTTE.

By 1989 Sri Lanka faced overlapping conflicts, including the second JVP insurrection in the south, which the government suppressed with extreme violence, and the renewed war with the LTTE in 1990 accompanied by violence against Muslim civilians. The provincial council system survived on paper but not in practice. The chapter stressed throughout that the roles of different communities, parties, and organizations must be distinguished.

In Nepal, King Mahendra's 1960 dismissal of an elected government produced the partyless Panchayat system, which King Birendra inherited and defended through the 1980 referendum. The People's Movement of 1990, built on cooperation between the Nepali Congress and the left, backed by economic pressure from the Indian blockade, led the king to accept a constitutional monarchy under the Constitution of November 1990, though questions of language, religion, and inclusion remained.

Bhutan modernized under a monarchy that emphasized cultural integration, but its Citizenship Act of 1985, the 1988 census, and national-dress and language policies produced protest and the flight of tens of thousands of Lhotshampa, with competing accounts from government and refugees. The Maldives, a presidential republic without parties and with a tourism-based economy, survived the November 1988 coup attempt through Operation Cactus, an Indian intervention requested by its government that revealed both the vulnerability of small states and the capacity of a regional power.

Comparison shows several shared structures: strong executives, weak institutional checks, uneven development, difficult relations between majorities and minorities, and the presence of India. It also shows decisive differences in how armies, monarchies, parties, and constitutional texts operated. No single factor explains the period, and the chapter's cases demonstrate that political outcomes depended on the interaction of structures and choices. The next chapter turns to the wider regional systems of nuclear politics, SAARC, and migration.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the period after 1971 was not a single story of decolonization completed but a set of experiments in how to organize political authority in small, poor, and diverse states. The achievements were real: Bangladesh restored parliamentary politics through a popular movement, Nepal made a negotiated transition, and Sri Lanka and the Maldives sustained functioning states under severe strain. But the human costs of failure, including famine, political killings, ethnic violence, civil war, and displacement, were also real, and a serious history must hold both in view.

The chapter also shows how often regional and domestic politics were entwined. India's interventions took different forms, as sponsor, mediator, intervenor, and guarantor, and each shaped what was possible in the smaller states without determining it. Chapter 97 turns to the regional systems of nuclear competition, SAARC, labor migration, and economic interdependence that framed these relationships.

In the longer view of the six-volume series, the issues examined here, the relationship between majorities and minorities, between armies and elected governments, and between center and region, are older than the postcolonial period but took new forms within the nation-state. The Bangladesh of 1991, the Sri Lanka of the Second Eelam War, the Nepal of the new constitution, and the Bhutan of the refugee camps all lead directly into the later chapters of this volume.

Students who have studied this chapter should be wary of simple explanations. Neither culture, nor leadership, nor economics, nor foreign intervention alone accounts for the outcomes, and the careful use of evidence, attributed estimates, and distinctions among communities is the method by which historians avoid both hindsight and partisanship.
