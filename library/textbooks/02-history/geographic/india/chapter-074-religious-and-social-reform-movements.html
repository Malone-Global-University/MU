Religious and Social Reform Movements

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Imagine four rooms in nineteenth-century South Asia. In a Calcutta hall, a congregation assembles for worship of one formless God, with Upanishadic verses, a sermon in Bengali, and hymns, a pattern of worship that no earlier Hindu temple tradition had quite resembled. In a school and college compound at Aligarh, young Muslim men study English, mathematics, and the natural sciences alongside religious instruction, under the sponsorship of a reformer who insists that faith and modern knowledge need not conflict. In a madrasa at Deoband, a different kind of reformer organizes teaching around the Quran, the sayings of the Prophet, and Islamic law, funded by small public donations rather than by a court or by government. And in a Pune school, children from groups long excluded from formal education learn to read under teachers who regard literacy itself as a challenge to caste hierarchy. All four rooms belong to the same historical moment. They reflect strikingly different answers to the same underlying question: what should religion and society become under colonial rule?

Chapter 73 examined how colonial institutions, through the census, ethnography, law, schooling, and the press, classified South Asian society and, in doing so, helped reshape the social categories people inhabited. This chapter turns to the other side of that encounter. South Asians were not merely classified; they organized. Across the nineteenth century, and especially between 1858 and about 1905, thousands of men and a growing number of women founded associations, schools, presses, charitable institutions, and religious communities aimed at renewing, defending, reinterpreting, or fundamentally challenging the religious and social worlds they had inherited. Some drew on older devotional and scholarly traditions; some drew on European rationalism, Unitarian theology, or the idiom of humanitarian reform; many drew on both; and some, like the anti-caste thinkers examined in Sections 74.14 and 74.15, started from the experience of people whom the inherited order had placed at its bottom.

The central historical question of the chapter is therefore comparative: how did South Asian religious and social reform movements respond to colonial rule, inherited inequalities, intellectual change, and competition among religious communities, and why did their visions of reform differ so profoundly? Two tempting shortcuts must be avoided. The first assumes that reform meant Westernization, as if reformers simply imported European ideas into a passive society. The second assumes that religious revival was inherently anti-modern, as if scriptural authority and institutional innovation were opposites. The movements examined here refute both assumptions. The Arya Samaj appealed to the supreme authority of the Vedas while building schools, presses, and voluntary associations of a distinctly modern kind. Deoband defended classical Islamic learning through a new, donation-supported, examination-based institutional form. Ramakrishna's devotional pluralism had deep roots in older Bengali religious practice, even as his disciples organized it into a monastic mission with hospitals and relief work.

Historians reconstruct these movements from a rich but uneven body of evidence: the founding statements, rules, and annual reports of associations; religious treatises and sermons; vernacular and English newspapers and journals; school and college records; autobiographies and memoirs; petitions to government; legislative records; court cases; and, increasingly, the writings of women and anti-caste activists that earlier historiography neglected. This evidence has characteristic biases. Institutional reports tend to emphasize success; leaders' writings may misrepresent their followers; and the voices of ordinary participants, of women, of marginalized communities, and of orthodox critics survive more thinly. Each section below asks whose perspective a source represents and whose is missing.

The chapter moves through five clusters of movements. Sections 74.1 through 74.7 examine Hindu religious reform and institution-building, from the Brahmo Samaj and Prarthana Samaj to the Arya Samaj and the Ramakrishna movement. Sections 74.8 through 74.11 examine Muslim reform, from Sayyid Ahmad Khan and Aligarh to Deoband and the wider range of Islamic networks. Sections 74.12 and 74.13 examine Sikh renewal. Sections 74.14 and 74.15 examine anti-caste thought and organization. Sections 74.16 through 74.18 place women's education, marriage reform, and women's own writings at the center. Section 74.19 compares additional traditions, namely Parsi, Christian, and Buddhist, and Section 74.20 synthesizes the whole.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter belongs to the social and intellectual history of the high colonial period. Chapter 67 introduced Rammohun Roy, early Brahmo thought, and the campaign against sati; Chapter 73 described colonial classification and the new middle classes; Chapter 75 turns to cities, labor, and migration; and Chapters 76 and 77 turn to organized nationalism and Muslim political organization. Reform and politics overlapped, but they were not identical. The later political uses of religious identity must be established through documented connections, not read backward from outcomes the nineteenth-century reformers could not have foreseen.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why nineteenth-century South Asian reform movements cannot be reduced to Westernization or to simple religious revival.
  2. Trace the development of the Brahmo Samaj from Rammohun Roy through Debendranath Tagore and Keshab Chandra Sen, including its schisms of 1866 and 1878.
  3. Compare the Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, and Arya Samaj in their sources of authority, methods, and social bases.
  4. Analyze Dayananda Saraswati's claims about Vedic authority and distinguish them from historians' reconstruction of Vedic society.
  5. Distinguish the teachings of Ramakrishna from the institutional program of Vivekananda and the Ramakrishna Mission.
  6. Assess the educational and intellectual program of Sayyid Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh movement, and contrast it with Deoband without reducing the difference to modernity versus tradition.
  7. Identify the diversity of Muslim religious networks, including Ahl-i Hadith, the circle of Ahmad Riza Khan, Sufi affiliations, and the Nadwat al-Ulama.
  8. Explain how the Singh Sabha movement, Sikh education, and Gurmukhi print culture contributed to the articulation of Sikh identity after 1849.
  9. Evaluate Jyotirao Phule's critique of caste and Brahmanical authority and explain why anti-caste organizing was not simply an extension of elite reform.
  10. Analyze women's education, widow remarriage, and the Age of Consent controversy as contested legal and social questions.
  11. Interpret the writings of Pandita Ramabai and Tarabai Shinde as intellectual contributions in their own right.
  12. Assess how reform both challenged inequality and reproduced other hierarchies, and how it contributed to both cooperation and sharper community boundaries.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did "reform" mean to different movements, and why did reformers so often disagree with one another?
  2. In what ways did colonial institutions such as schools, courts, printing presses, and legislation shape the possibilities for reform, and in what ways did South Asians act on their own intellectual initiatives?
  3. Why did some movements appeal to ancient scripture while others appealed to reason, devotional experience, or social equality?
  4. How did new organizational forms, including associations, journals, schools, public meetings, and donation-based institutions, change religious life?
  5. How did the social base of a movement (Bengali middle classes, Punjabi urban groups, Muslim elites, ulama, peasants, or marginalized castes) shape its priorities?
  6. How did anti-caste thinkers critique both the inherited hierarchy and the limits of elite-led reform?
  7. Who spoke for women in debates about marriage, education, and law, and how did women speak for themselves?
  8. How did reform movements contribute to the sharpening of religious boundaries, and how did they also foster cooperation and shared public debate?
  9. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct these movements, and what perspectives does that evidence leave out?
  10. Why should historians avoid treating later religious nationalism as the inevitable outcome of nineteenth-century reform?

Historical Context

South Asia's religious institutions before 1858 were numerous, varied, and often locally rooted. Hindu life was organized around temples, monasteries (maths), sectarian lineages (sampradayas), Brahmin schools of Sanskrit learning, pilgrimage centers, caste councils, and devotional communities, patronized by kings, landholders, and merchants. Muslim religious life rested on mosques, shrines, Sufi lineages, networks of scholars (ulama), and madrasas supported by courts, endowments (waqf), and local donors. Sikh life centered on gurdwaras, many managed by hereditary custodians, and on the memory of the Gurus and of the Sikh polity that Chapter 65 showed was annexed by the East India Company in 1849. Parsi, Jain, Buddhist, Christian, and other communities maintained their own institutions. None of these worlds was static: Chapters 44 and 48 showed centuries of change in Sufi, Bhakti, and Sikh traditions.

Colonial intervention altered the setting in which religious change occurred. Company and Crown officials claimed religious neutrality, a stance given formal expression in the Queen's Proclamation of 1858 (Chapter 70), yet colonial courts, codification, land policy, and legislation shaped religious institutions and family law, and legislation such as the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856 plainly intervened in community practice. English-language education, introduced and expanded as Chapter 67 explained, produced new educated groups. Christian missionaries ran schools, preached publicly, and attacked Hindu and Muslim practice, provoking defensive and reformist responses. The printing press, the postal system, railways, and the telegraph enabled religious debate to occur across long distances, in newspapers and pamphlets, and before audiences that earlier scholarly debate rarely reached.

Social hierarchies were also in motion. As Chapter 73 showed, new middle classes emerged in port cities and provincial towns; censuses and ethnography hardened categories of caste and religion; and competition for education, government employment, and public recognition raised the stakes of community definition. The abolition of Mughal and other courts removed patrons on whom religious scholars, artists, and literati had relied. Famines, epidemics, and migration disrupted rural communities. Against this background, associations became a characteristic nineteenth-century form: groups of like-minded individuals, often outside kinship and caste networks, who raised funds, held meetings, published journals, and founded schools.

This chapter concentrates on the period from 1858 to about 1905, with necessary earlier background. It does not treat the movements as one family of "Indian Renaissance" initiatives marching together toward a single modern ideal. Some of their goals converged, notably on female education and on the value of schooling. Others conflicted sharply: scriptural revivalists and rationalists disagreed over authority; caste reformers and orthodox traditionalists disagreed over hierarchy; Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, and Christian movements competed for adherents and for public recognition. Understanding these tensions is the chapter's central task.

Section 74.1

The Brahmo Samaj after Rammohun Roy: Debendranath Tagore and Religious Renewal

Chapter 67 introduced Rammohun Roy and the association he helped found in Calcutta in August 1828, first called the Brahmo Sabha and later the Brahmo Samaj. Its premises were open to worshippers of the one formless God, without images or sacrifice, and its worship combined Upanishadic recitation, Sanskrit chanting, and sermons. Roy left for England in 1830 and died at Bristol in 1833. After his departure the association survived chiefly through a small circle of supporters, among them members of the wealthy Tagore family of Jorasanko, but it lacked a settled membership or a clear doctrine. Its revival in the 1840s is the true beginning of the movement this chapter traces.

The revival was led by Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905), son of Roy's associate Dwarkanath Tagore. In 1839 he founded the Tattvabodhini Sabha, a society for the study of the Upanishads and the pursuit of religious inquiry, and from 1843 its journal, the Tattvabodhini Patrika, edited by the rationalist writer Akshay Kumar Datta, became one of the leading Bengali-language periodicals of its day. Debendranath joined the Brahmo Samaj in 1843, took a formal covenant, and began to give it institutional shape by defining membership, organizing regular worship, and compiling a body of devotional and doctrinal texts, the Brahmo Dharma. Historians have noted that this was a decisive shift from an informal circle toward a self-conscious religious community.

Debendranath's Brahmoism rested on a monotheistic reading of the Upanishads, but the question of scriptural authority proved unsettled. In the mid-1840s the Tattvabodhini group sent students to Banaras to study the Vedas, and in about 1850, after discussion within the movement, Debendranath abandoned the view that the Vedas were infallible revelation. The Brahmo Samaj instead looked to reason and conscience, together with selected Upanishadic texts, as sources of religious truth. This was a distinctive position: it criticized image worship and polytheism, as Roy had, but it also refused to accept scripture as an unquestionable authority, a stance that put it at odds both with orthodox Hindus and, later, with the scriptural revivalists of the Arya Samaj (Section 74.4).

Brahmo worship adopted features that distinguished it from temple-centered Hindu practice: regular congregational meetings, vernacular sermons, collective hymn singing, and a printed liturgy. Historians have often seen in this a creative adaptation of Protestant and Unitarian congregational forms, to which Roy had been exposed, although the content was Vedantic. The movement also engaged Christian missionaries in public debate, defending monotheism as a Hindu inheritance while rejecting missionary claims about Hindu "idolatry."

The social base of this early Brahmo community was narrow. Its members were largely educated, urban, upper-caste Bengali men, the group commonly called the bhadralok, including lawyers, teachers, clerks, and writers. Its reach in rural Bengal and outside Calcutta, Dhaka, and a handful of other towns was limited. Women and children participated mainly through family connection and domestic observance. The movement generated much more debate and publication than mass conversion, and its influence on Bengali literary and intellectual life exceeded its numbers.

Brahmos also faced opposition from Hindu traditionalists. Conservative groups such as the Dharma Sabha, founded in 1830 in part to oppose the abolition of sati, regarded reformers as threats to orthodoxy. Debendranath himself was cautious on social questions: he wished to keep Brahmo practice rooted in Hindu forms, and the movement's formal rejection of caste distinctions in its ministry was far from complete. This caution would become a source of conflict with a younger generation, led by Keshab Chandra Sen, who wanted a faster pace of social change. The movement's story after 1857, therefore, is as much about its internal disagreement as about its confrontation with orthodoxy.

Historical Vocabulary
Samaj, Sabha, and Anjuman

Samaj (Sanskrit, "society" or "assembly"), sabha ("assembly"), and anjuman (Persian and Urdu, "association") were the characteristic terms by which nineteenth-century reformers named their voluntary associations. The Brahmo Samaj, Arya Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Satyashodhak Samaj, Singh Sabha, and Anjuman-i Himayat-i Islam were all founded on the principle that individuals could join a body by choice, pay subscriptions, attend meetings, and participate in governance, rather than inheriting membership through birth.

These associations were new in scale and function even where the vocabulary was old. They borrowed from, and competed with, the voluntary societies of Protestant Britain and the mission societies working in India. Their constitutions, elections, and schisms (the Brahmo Samaj divided twice) show that associational life brought its own forms of disagreement.

Section 74.2

Keshab Chandra Sen, Brahmo Divisions, and the Politics of Social Reform

Keshab Chandra Sen (1838–1884) joined the Brahmo Samaj in 1857, impressed Debendranath with his eloquence and commitment, and rose quickly. A graduate of the Hindu College milieu, he combined religious fervor with a strong interest in practical social change, and by the early 1860s he led a younger group that urged the movement beyond theological debate. They advocated women's education, widow remarriage, the abolition of child marriage, and the relaxation of caste restrictions, and they were impatient with what they regarded as Debendranath's reluctance to break with Hindu social convention.

Tension grew over several matters. Among them were inter-caste marriage; the expectation that Brahmin ministers (acharyas) wear the sacred thread, which Keshab's group regarded as incompatible with a movement that rejected caste; and the broader question of whether Brahmoism was a reformed Hinduism or a new universal religion. In 1866 Keshab and his supporters left to form the Brahmo Samaj of India, while Debendranath's group continued as the Adi (original) Brahmo Samaj. Both retained the Brahmo name and a common commitment to monotheism; the division concerned pace, authority, and the social meaning of the faith.

Keshab's Brahmo Samaj of India became an energetic reform organization. Following a visit to England in 1870, Keshab helped establish an Indian Reform Association with sections devoted to women's improvement, education, cheap literature, temperance, and charity, and the movement supported a vernacular journal for women and inexpensive newspapers. Keshab also drew increasingly on devotional (bhakti) and Christian themes, and his religious language placed growing emphasis on emotional devotion and on divine inspiration. His campaign for civil marriage for Brahmos bore fruit in the Native Marriage Act (Act III of 1872), which allowed persons who declared that they did not profess Hinduism, Christianity, Islam, or certain other religions to marry under civil law, with minimum ages of eighteen for men and fourteen for women. The Adi Brahmo Samaj, which regarded itself as part of Hinduism, objected to the declaration the Act required.

The second schism, in 1878, turned on the marriage of Keshab's daughter Suniti Devi, then about thirteen, to the minor Maharaja Nripendra Narayan of Cooch Behar. Their ages conflicted with the minimum-age standards Keshab had helped secure for marriages under Act III of 1872. The princely marriage was not solemnized under that Act; the dispute was over a reform leader's consistency and the rites used, not a demonstrated statutory offense. Keshab defended his decision by appeal to divine command and to the political and personal circumstances of the match. A large group of dissenters, among them Ananda Mohan Bose, Sivanath Sastri, and Dwarkanath Ganguli, left to found the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj in 1878, with a constitution based on elected representation. Keshab, increasingly inclined to prophetic language, proclaimed a "New Dispensation" in 1880 and died in 1884.

These divisions reveal that reformers differed not only from traditional religious authorities but also among themselves. The disputes involved doctrinal questions (was inspiration individual or institutional?), governance (should a charismatic leader or an elected committee decide?), and social reform (should principles bind leaders as strictly as followers?). The Cooch Behar case also shows the complicated interaction between reform and colonial law: the Brahmos had sought legal recognition for their marriages, and the law then became a standard against which their own leader's conduct was measured. After 1878 the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj, with its participatory organization, became the most active Brahmo body in public life, linking religious reform to emerging political associations such as the Indian Association of 1876, a connection Chapter 76 will take up.

Primary Source Window

The Native Marriage Act (1872) and the Cooch Behar Controversy (1878)

Source type: Colonial legislation and the public statements of Brahmo factions · Place: Calcutta and Cooch Behar · Dates: 1872 and 1878 · Where to read it: The text of Act III of 1872; Brahmo periodicals such as the Indian Mirror; Sivanath Sastri's later history of the Brahmo Samaj

Documentary witnesses: Act III of 1872, especially §2(2), which applies its age thresholds to marriages celebrated under that Act; and Suniti Devi, The Autobiography of an Indian Princess (London: John Murray, 1921), chapter IV, digitized memoir, an interested retrospective account of the wedding's legal basis. Contemporary Indian Mirror issues and Sivanath Sastri's later account remain supplementary witnesses; their individual passages have not been collated here. The study below is an editorial comparison, not an original quotation.

Context: The 1872 Act was the product of Brahmo lobbying for a form of marriage that did not require Hindu rites. Its text required a declaration of religion and set minimum ages. In 1878 the Cooch Behar marriage became the occasion for a public dispute over whether Keshab had breached principles he promoted. Suniti Devi later explained that the wedding was treated as a Hindu marriage under the princely state's arrangements, not a marriage registered under Act III of 1872.

Attributed editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: The Act permitted marriage by civil registration for those who declared that they did not belong to the listed faiths, forbade polygamy for persons marrying under it, and set minimum ages for bride and groom. In 1878, Keshab's critics argued that the couple fell below the minimum-age standards he had championed, and that the wedding's ceremonies were inconsistent with Brahmo theology; Keshab's defenders argued that he had acted on a sincere sense of divine direction and that exceptional circumstances warranted flexibility.

Analysis: The documents show law functioning as a field in which reformers negotiated religious identity: Brahmos had to say what they were not (Hindu in the Act's sense) in order to marry freely. The dispute also shows the tension between charismatic authority and constitutional principle. The sources largely represent the educated men who led the factions; the perspectives of the young bride, of women Brahmos, and of rank-and-file members are far less visible. Students should consult the original Act and the periodicals rather than relying on summaries.

  2. Who wrote the principal accounts of the 1878 dispute, and for what likely purposes?
  3. What does the controversy teach historians about the relationship between reform principles and the conduct of reform leaders?

Section 74.3

The Prarthana Samaj and Religious-Social Reform in Western India

The Prarthana Samaj ("Prayer Society") was founded in Bombay in 1867, shortly after Keshab Chandra Sen's visits to western India had stimulated interest in theistic reform. Its early leaders included Dr. Atmaram Pandurang, and it soon drew in educated Maharashtrians and Gujaratis of distinction, among them Mahadev Govind Ranade (1842–1901), the jurist and economist; R. G. Bhandarkar (1837–1925), the pioneering Sanskrit scholar; and later figures such as K. T. Telang and N. G. Chandavarkar. Earlier reform networks in the region, including the Paramahansa Sabha of the late 1840s and campaigns for widow remarriage in the 1860s, formed part of its background.

The Prarthana Samaj resembled the Brahmo Samaj in its theism and its criticism of image worship, but its character differed. It was deliberately less confrontational, and it placed less emphasis on a new doctrinal system. Its members worshipped one God, but they looked to the devotional poet-saints of Maharashtra, such as Tukaram, Namdev, and Eknath, as a native tradition of ethical and loving worship, an argument that presented reform as a recovery of regional Hindu resources rather than as a rupture. The Samaj generally did not require members to leave their castes or Hindu social life, and it held that social reform should proceed through persuasion. Historians regard this "organic" strategy as characteristic of Ranade's thought.

The Samaj's practical program included support for widow remarriage, female education, the discouragement of child marriage, and a degree of caste reform, including interdining and attention to the situation of the "depressed" castes, though its record on the last was limited. It sponsored night schools, lectures, charitable work, and a weekly publication, the Subodh Patrika. Ranade also worked through related organizations, including the Pune Sarvajanik Sabha, founded in 1870 for public and political representation, and, with others, the National Social Conference, whose sessions from 1887 convened reformers from across the country to debate marriage, education, and caste.

Comparison with the Brahmo Samaj is instructive. Both movements were theistic, urban, and led by the English-educated, but the Prarthana Samaj experienced no comparable schism, relied heavily on a regional devotional heritage, and operated within a context in which Brahmin caste networks of western India (especially the Chitpavan Brahmins of Pune) were already politically and socially influential. Its social reach was also limited: it remained largely a body of professional and official elites, and its gradualism frustrated more radical critics, including Jyotirao Phule (Section 74.14), who regarded Brahmin-led reform as incapable of challenging Brahmin privilege.

The Prarthana Samaj demonstrates how reform organizations operated. They held regular meetings and lectures; they published in English and in Marathi and Gujarati; they founded schools and libraries; they petitioned government; and they drew on networks of lawyers, teachers, and officials. The organizational repertoire, as much as the doctrines, was the movement's lasting contribution, and it provided models adopted well beyond religious reform.

Section 74.4

Dayananda Saraswati and the Foundation of the Arya Samaj

Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883) was born Mool Shankar in Kathiawar in western India. He left home as a young man, became a renunciant, took the name Dayananda Saraswati, and studied Sanskrit grammar and the Vedas, notably under the blind teacher Swami Virajananda at Mathura in the early 1860s. His teacher is traditionally credited with persuading him to reject later Puranic and ritual accretions and to return to the Vedic texts. Dayananda became a public debater and preacher: he took part in religious debates at Banaras in 1869 with orthodox scholars, and he later engaged Christian and Muslim as well as Hindu opponents. In Calcutta in 1872–1873 he met Brahmo leaders, including Keshab Chandra Sen and Debendranath Tagore, and later tradition holds that these encounters encouraged him to preach in Hindi rather than Sanskrit.

His principal work, the Satyarth Prakash ("Light of Truth"), was first published in Hindi in 1875 and substantially revised by Dayananda in his final years (approximately 1882–83). Bibliographies differ on whether the revised edition is dated by revision, printing, or its completed posthumous circulation, sometimes given as 1884; the two versions must not be silently combined. The Arya Samaj was founded in Bombay on 10 April 1875, and a Lahore organization followed in 1877, together with a set of principles that became the movement's common platform. Dayananda held that the four Vedic Samhitas, understood through traditional grammatical and etymological analysis, were revealed truth, the source of all genuine knowledge, and that later texts, to the extent that they contradicted the Vedas, were corruptions. On this basis he rejected image worship, polytheism, the Puranic stories of avatars, pilgrimage to sacred sites as a means of salvation, ritual feeding of ancestors, child marriage, and the claim that caste status derived from birth. He argued that varna should reflect an individual's qualities and occupation, supported female education, and, with qualifications, widow remarriage.

It is essential to distinguish Dayananda's claims about an ideal Vedic past from the historian's reconstruction. As Chapters 5 through 7 showed, the Rigveda reflects a pastoral, ritual-centered society of the second millennium BCE, with many deities, sacrificial worship, and social hierarchies still taking shape; later Vedic and Brahmanical texts record transformations over centuries. Dayananda's reading, which found in the Vedas a pure monotheism, scientific knowledge, and a stable ideal social order, was an interpretation shaped by nineteenth-century debates, not a conclusion supported by historical and philological scholarship. This does not make his thought historically uninteresting; it makes it a statement of his time, not a window onto antiquity.

The Arya Samaj combined revivalism and reform in a way that sets it apart from the Brahmo Samaj. Brahmos criticized scriptural infallibility; Dayananda erected the Vedas into the supreme authority. His movement was therefore at once a critique of contemporary Hindu practice and a claim about the true Hindu past. It also adopted Hindi in the Devanagari script as the language of religious teaching, a choice with later consequences for language politics in northern India. Dayananda's polemical chapters on other religious traditions, including Christianity, Islam, and some Indian sects, gave the movement a combative public style that attracted admirers and enemies alike.

Dayananda died at Ajmer in 1883. He left an association with a distinctive doctrine, a growing network of branches, and unresolved questions about leadership and priorities, which his followers in the Punjab and the United Provinces would answer in different ways (Section 74.5).

Section 74.5

Arya Samaj Institutions: Education, Conversion, and Religious Boundaries

The Arya Samaj grew most strongly in the Punjab, where, following the Lahore organization of 1877, its appeal drew on urban trading and professional groups, particularly among Hindus of the Khatri and Arora communities, and on educated men in government service and the law. Its branches (samajes), weekly meetings, subscriptions, preachers, and printed tracts gave it an institutional density that few religious movements of the time possessed. Census returns, themselves instruments shaped by colonial classification as Chapter 73 showed, record the growth of Arya identification, though they must be read with care.

Education was its most successful institutional field. After Dayananda's death, Arya leaders in Lahore established the Dayananda Anglo-Vedic (DAV) school in 1886, which became a college, to combine English-language and modern scientific education with instruction in Sanskrit and Vedic teaching. Figures such as Lala Hansraj and Lala Lajpat Rai were prominent in it. The movement divided in 1893 over its direction: a "college party" favored the Anglo-Vedic model and tolerated meat-eating, while a "gurukul" or "culture" party, associated with Munshi Ram (later Swami Shraddhanand), favored a more strictly Vedic, Sanskrit-based curriculum and a vegetarian discipline. The latter group established a residential Gurukul, which relocated to Kangri near Haridwar in 1902. The Arya Samaj also founded girls' schools, notably at Jalandhar from 1890, orphanages (including for children orphaned by famine, in competition with Christian missions), and widow-support organizations.

Public debate (shastrarth) was integral to the movement's style, and Arya preachers engaged Christian missionaries, Muslim scholars, Sikh reformers, and orthodox "Sanatani" Hindus in print and in person. These debates could sharpen mutual awareness of difference and produce hostility; the murder in 1897 of the Arya preacher Pandit Lekh Ram, whose assailant was never identified, belongs to this charged atmosphere, although the circumstances remain contested. At the same time, the debates reflect a vigorous public culture in which religious claims were publicly argued in print and before audiences.

Two further issues require chronological precision. Conversion and re-entry into a religious community were already contested. The Arya Samaj developed ceremonies of purification (shuddhi) by which persons could be admitted or readmitted to Hindu status, and some such rites were performed in the later nineteenth century; but its best-known organized shuddhi campaigns and their political consequences belong mainly to the twentieth century and are reserved for later chapters. Cow protection, similarly, became an organized public cause in the 1880s and 1890s, with the Arya Samaj participating in the movement associated with Gaurakshini sabhas (Dayananda himself had written in favor of cow protection), and it contributed to tension with Muslim communities in some regions, including during the riots of 1893.

The Arya Samaj thus illustrates the dual possibility the chapter's central thesis describes. It pursued educational advancement, relief work, and criticism of caste by birth, child marriage, and ritual extravagance; and it also sharpened lines between communities through proselytizing, polemic, and the identification of Hinduism with Vedic and Hindi-speaking markers. Its relationship to the colonial state was equally complex, since its schools received grants and examined students within the colonial system even as it criticized missionary education. Neither role should be treated as the "real" Arya Samaj; the movement contained both tendencies, and different members emphasized different ones.

Historical Vocabulary
Shuddhi, Reconversion, and Religious Boundaries

Shuddhi (Sanskrit, "purification") refers in this context to rites by which a person could be admitted, or readmitted, to a Hindu community. Earlier Hindu practice had ritual means of restoring status for individuals; the Arya Samaj developed shuddhi as an organized public rite associated with reclaiming persons who had converted to other religions and with admitting new members.

The term matters because it illustrates how competition among missionary and reform organizations, together with colonial census categories that counted communities, made religious boundaries more explicit. Historians distinguish the limited late-nineteenth-century uses of shuddhi from the large and politically charged campaigns of the 1900s and 1920s. The word should not be read backward to describe all Arya Samaj activity.

Section 74.6

Ramakrishna, Devotional Experience, and Religious Pluralism

Ramakrishna (1836–1886), born Gadadhar Chattopadhyay in the village of Kamarpukur in Hooghly district, Bengal, was not a scholar, an organizer, or a writer. He became a priest at the Kali temple at Dakshineswar near Calcutta, consecrated in 1855 under the patronage of the wealthy Rani Rasmani, and spent decades there in devotional practice. Accounts preserved by his followers describe periods of intense devotion to the goddess Kali, practices associated with Tantric and Vaishnava traditions, instruction in Advaita Vedanta from a wandering renunciant, Totapuri, and shorter engagements with Islamic and Christian practice. He married Sarada Devi, who later became a revered figure in her own right, though their marriage is described in the tradition as spiritually, not conjugally, oriented.

His teaching, as his disciples recorded it, emphasized the direct experience of the divine, the sincerity of devotion, and the idea that different religious paths could lead to the same goal. This is better understood as an expression of older Bengali devotional pluralism than as a new religious system created by colonialism. Scholars have noted its roots in Shakta and Vaishnava bhakti and in the Advaita tradition, as well as its use of homely, vivid parables. Ramakrishna did not found an organization or issue a program of social reform.

An important source problem shapes everything historians can say about him. Most of what is known comes from followers: the Bengali Kathamrita by the disciple known as "M" (Mahendranath Gupta), whose volumes began to appear in the early twentieth century, and later biographies by monastic disciples. These works are valuable and detailed, but they are devotional and retrospective, shaped by the aims of the community that preserved them. Scholars have debated how far the texts reflect Ramakrishna's own words and how far they reflect the concerns of Calcutta's educated middle classes, a question raised, for example, in Sumit Sarkar's well-known study of Ramakrishna in his time.

Why did educated Bengalis find him significant? In an era dominated by rationalist religious argument, in which the Brahmo Samaj and missionary critics debated texts and doctrines, Ramakrishna offered a religious life grounded in experience and in a tradition of devotion that was recognizably Bengali. Keshab Chandra Sen and other Brahmos visited him from the mid-1870s, and younger students, including Narendranath Datta (the future Vivekananda), were drawn to him. His religious attitude differed from that of the Brahmo and Arya programs: it was not scriptural in the way of Dayananda, nor rationalist and congregational in the way of the Brahmos, and it did not demand institutional reform. Its significance for the history of reform lies largely in what his disciples made of it (Section 74.7).

Section 74.7

Swami Vivekananda, Practical Vedanta, and the Ramakrishna Mission

Narendranath Datta (1863–1902) was educated in Calcutta, studied Western philosophy, and had contact with Brahmo circles before he met Ramakrishna in the early 1880s. After Ramakrishna's death in 1886 he and a group of fellow disciples took monastic vows, and Narendranath, taking the name Swami Vivekananda, traveled widely across India in the late 1880s and early 1890s, observing poverty, caste disabilities, and the condition of religious life, and gaining supporters among rulers and educated people.

In September 1893 he addressed the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago, an event arranged as part of the World's Columbian Exposition. His speeches, which presented Vedanta as a universal philosophy of spiritual dignity and religious tolerance, drew attention in the United States and, through reports, in India. He lectured in the United States and Britain until 1896, attracted Western followers, including Margaret Noble (later Sister Nivedita), and published works such as Raja Yoga (1896). His encounter with Western audiences also meant that Vedanta was presented to them in a form shaped by comparison with Christianity and by the idea of a universal spiritual experience.

Returning to India in 1897, Vivekananda emphasized a "practical Vedanta": the idea that spiritual realization finds expression in service to human beings, especially the poor and suffering, and that religious teaching should be combined with education and material relief. He criticized ritualism, the preoccupation with ceremonial purity, and the neglect of the poor, and he challenged both Western missionary portrayals of India and the passivity he perceived in Indian society. On 1 May 1897 he established the Ramakrishna Mission Association in Calcutta, which undertook relief work during famine and plague and developed schools, hospitals, and a monastic order, with headquarters later established at Belur. Its formal legal registration came in 1909.

Historians have assessed Vivekananda's thought in several ways. Some scholars stress its continuity with the Advaita tradition and its creative reinterpretation as a modern social ethic; others, using terms such as "neo-Vedanta" or "neo-Hinduism," argue that its universalism and its engagement with Western categories distinguish it from earlier forms of Vedanta. His writings contain strong statements about service and national regeneration, and his influence on later nationalists, including Bengali revolutionaries of the early twentieth century, is well documented (Chapter 77). But it is important to distinguish his own religious and social thought from later political interpretations, and not to read him as a theorist of a particular modern political program. During his lifetime, the Mission's work was religious and humanitarian in design.

Compared with the Brahmo and Arya movements, the Ramakrishna movement combined two features: a devotional and experiential religion derived from Ramakrishna, and a monastic, service-oriented institution built by Vivekananda. It was, in organizational terms, more modest at the time of its founding than the Arya Samaj, but its model of a monastic order engaged in education and relief gave it a distinct place in the religious landscape and a durable institutional future.

Section 74.8

Sayyid Ahmad Khan and Muslim Reform after 1857

The rebellion of 1857 and its suppression (Chapters 69 and 70) left many Muslim communities in a precarious position. Delhi, the old Mughal capital, had been the rebellion's symbolic center, and colonial retribution was heavy there and in Awadh. Courts that had patronized poets, physicians, scholars, and calligraphers were gone or diminished; earlier shifts, such as the replacement of Persian by English and the vernaculars in official use during the 1830s, had already narrowed traditional avenues of employment. Some British officials suspected Muslims of having instigated the rebellion, and trials in the 1860s and 1870s of alleged "Wahhabi" conspirators, together with W. W. Hunter's widely read book of 1871 on whether Indian Muslims were bound in conscience to rebel, reflect that suspicion. Muslim responses varied: some withdrew into religious teaching and devotion, some sought employment and reconciliation with the new order, and some, like the scholars examined in Section 74.10, built new institutions of religious learning.

Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) belonged to a Delhi family with Mughal court connections and joined the Company's judicial service. During 1857 he was in Bijnor and used his position to protect British lives. Afterward he wrote Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind (1859), an analysis of the causes of the revolt, and other writings that addressed British suspicion of Muslims. He argued that misunderstanding between rulers and ruled, rather than a Muslim conspiracy, lay behind the rebellion, and that government needed Indian representation in its councils to understand its subjects. He also pursued an agenda of intellectual renewal, with the conviction that Muslims needed to master modern science and English learning without abandoning their faith.

In 1864 he helped found a Scientific Society (initially at Ghazipur, later at Aligarh) to translate European works on science and history into Urdu, and its journal, the Aligarh Institute Gazette, followed in 1866. After a visit to England in 1869–1870, where he observed British universities, he launched the periodical Tahzib al-Akhlaq ("Refinement of Morals") in 1870, which addressed social customs, education, religion, and ethics, and promoted self-criticism within the Muslim community. His religious writings, including Quranic commentary, argued that revelation and the findings of natural science could not ultimately conflict, and that scripture should be read in light of reason. This approach, a form of ijtihad (independent interpretation), provoked strong opposition from many ulama, who accused him of undermining orthodoxy.

Sayyid Ahmad's relationship to British authorities was cooperative but not uncritical. He sought support for education and a place for educated Muslims in government service, and he opposed what he regarded as destabilizing agitation. In the late 1880s he spoke publicly against Muslim participation in the Indian National Congress; those political arguments, and the later growth of Muslim political organization, belong to Chapters 76 and 77 and should not be allowed to stand for the whole of his career or for the views of all Indian Muslims.

It is equally important not to treat him as representative. Many Muslims, including scholars, Sufi leaders, artisans, peasants, and urban professionals in Bengal, Punjab, Madras, and Hyderabad, held different views of education, religion, and the colonial state. His audience was principally the north Indian Urdu-speaking Muslim gentry and the emerging educated middle class. His strength was institutional: he turned a program of ideas into organizations.

Section 74.9

The Aligarh Movement: Education, Social Leadership, and Religious Debate

The Aligarh movement grew from Sayyid Ahmad Khan's conviction that educational institutions, not political agitation, would improve the position of north Indian Muslims. In 1870 he helped form a committee to promote the education of Muslims, which collected funds and planned a school and college. The Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) School opened at Aligarh in May 1875, and the foundation stone of the college was laid in January 1877 by the Viceroy, Lord Lytton, so that the institution is commonly dated to 1875 as a school and to 1877 as a college. It later became the Aligarh Muslim University in the twentieth century, a development outside this chapter's scope.

The institution was modeled in part on the residential colleges of Oxford and Cambridge that Sayyid Ahmad had observed in England. It was residential, with a boarding system intended to cultivate discipline, communal life, and character; it offered English-language education, mathematics, and science alongside Urdu, Persian, Arabic, and religious instruction in Sunni and Shia theology; and it prepared students for the examinations of the colonial universities with which it became affiliated, first Calcutta and, from 16 November 1887, Allahabad. Its trustees and supporters included Muslim notables such as Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Hindu and Muslim donors, and Hindu students were admitted, so that the college's constituency was not exclusively Muslim, although its mission was to improve the educational standing of Muslims. British teachers and principals, among them Theodore Beck from 1883, played a significant role, and the institution depended on grants and on the goodwill of colonial officials.

The college's students came largely from landholding and service families of the north Indian Muslim elite and from the emerging professional classes. For such families, English education offered access to government employment and the professions in which Muslims, by the 1860s and 1870s, believed they were underrepresented. Student life included debating societies, sports, and a collective identity as "Aligarh men" that later became significant. The institution also sponsored the Muhammadan Educational Conference from 1886, a forum for annual discussion of educational issues among Muslims across the subcontinent.

Aligarh was controversial among Muslims themselves. Orthodox ulama condemned Sayyid Ahmad's rationalist theology and his willingness to cooperate with colonial education, and some issued legal opinions (fatwas) against him. Disputes arose over the place of religious instruction, the proportion of English and Islamic subjects, the authority to interpret scripture, and who should lead the community: gentry reformers, scholars, or Sufi leaders. Aligarh also entered the linguistic debates of northern India: the Hindi-Urdu controversy of the late 1860s and 1870s, in which Hindi activists sought official recognition for the Devanagari script, strengthened the association of Urdu with Muslim identity among many of its defenders.

The movement therefore sought new opportunities for a section of Muslim society while provoking disagreement on authority and leadership. Its significance lies in having created a durable educational institution, a network of alumni and supporters, and a model of Muslim educational organization. Its relationship to the later political history of Muslim organization, treated in Chapter 77, was real but must be traced through documents rather than assumed.

Section 74.10

Deoband: Islamic Scholarship, Religious Education, and Institutional Independence

Darul Uloom Deoband was established in 1866 in the small town of Deoband in Saharanpur district of the northwestern provinces. Its founders included Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi (1833–1880) and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi (1829–1905), scholars of Delhi and Saharanpur trained in the tradition of Islamic learning and associated with Sufi teachers, notably Haji Imdadullah. Later accounts connect some of them to the events of 1857 in the region, though the details are debated. The circumstances were clear: with no Muslim state to patronize religious scholarship, and with colonial institutions focused on other subjects, scholars needed new means to preserve and transmit Islamic knowledge.

Deoband's curriculum drew on the long-established dars-i nizami course but gave particular weight to the transmitted sciences: the Quran, the study of hadith (the sayings and practices of the Prophet), and jurisprudence in the Hanafi legal tradition. It also taught logic and some rational sciences, although with less emphasis than older madrasa syllabi. The aim was to train scholars able to guide Muslims in religious and moral life, and to cultivate personal discipline, piety, and adherence to law. Many Deobandi scholars were also affiliated with Sufi orders, particularly the Chishti, and Deoband's religious outlook combined legal rigor with Sufi-influenced moral formation.

Its institutional features were innovative. The school was organized as an association with an administrative council, regular examinations, salaried teachers, fixed terms, and annual reports. It was financed primarily by public contributions from Muslims of modest as well as substantial means, rather than by a single patron or by government grants, a principle that tradition associates with the founders and that gave the institution a measure of independence from the colonial state. Its model spread through affiliated madrasas across northern India. A department for issuing legal opinions (fatwas) became an important channel of guidance for Muslims on everyday questions, and the school published religious literature, notably in Urdu.

Deoband also took positions in the religious debates of the era. Its scholars criticized customs they considered unsanctioned innovations (bid'a), such as certain practices at saints' shrines and ceremonies, and they took part in controversy with Christian missionaries, the Arya Samaj, and, increasingly, with other Muslim groups, including the circle of Ahmad Riza Khan (Section 74.11). They did not, in this period, constitute a political movement; the later political activities associated with Deobandi scholars, including involvement in anti-colonial and Khilafat politics, belong to the twentieth century.

It would be a mistake to reduce the contrast between Aligarh and Deoband to modernity against tradition. Both used new organizational forms: associations, fundraising, examinations, printed periodicals, and reports. Both responded to the loss of Muslim political power. They differed in intellectual and educational priorities: Aligarh sought to integrate English and scientific education into Muslim learning and prepare students for government employment; Deoband sought to preserve and systematize classical Islamic scholarship and moral discipline in a world without Muslim rule. Barbara Metcalf's pioneering study of the Deobandi movement is the essential scholarly account of its character as a creative institutional response to new conditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Ulama, Ijtihad, and Taqlid

Ulama (Arabic, "those who know," singular alim) are scholars trained in Islamic learning, including law, hadith, and theology, who serve as teachers, judges, preachers, and legal advisers. Ijtihad means independent juristic reasoning to reach a ruling or interpretation from the Quran and hadith; taqlid means following the established rulings of a recognized legal school or earlier authority.

Nineteenth-century debate over these terms was not merely abstract. Sayyid Ahmad Khan's rationalist interpretation, the rejection of taqlid by the Ahl-i Hadith, and Deoband's adherence to the Hanafi school represent different positions on who may interpret scripture and by what method. Because "ulama" and "reformer" were not opposites, historians should ask which authority each figure claimed and how he justified it.

Section 74.11

Islamic Reform beyond Aligarh and Deoband: Diverse Religious Networks

The story of Muslim reform in nineteenth-century South Asia is not a two-movement story. Aligarh and Deoband were prominent, but they stood among many other currents, whose members differed on legal method, devotional practice, the role of Sufism, relations with the colonial state, and education.

The Ahl-i Hadith, active from the middle of the century, emphasized direct reliance on the Quran and the hadith and rejected binding adherence (taqlid) to the established legal schools, including the Hanafi school followed by most Indian Muslims. Their leaders, such as Nazir Husain of Delhi and Siddiq Hasan Khan of Bhopal, criticized many customary devotional practices as innovations. Colonial officials often called them "Wahhabis," a term associated with suspicion of political subversion, and the group sought to be known instead by its own name, Ahl-i Hadith, which it asked government to adopt in the 1880s. Their controversies with Hanafi scholars, including some Deobandis, were sharp, reminding us that reformist Muslim currents contested one another.

At the opposite end of devotional emphasis stood scholars who defended practices such as celebration of the Prophet's birthday, veneration of saints, and intercession at shrines, practices that critics such as the Ahl-i Hadith and many Deobandis regarded as innovations. The most influential was Ahmad Riza Khan (1856–1921) of Bareilly, an accomplished jurist and a member of the Qadiri Sufi order, whose fatwas and treatises defended these practices and criticized other groups. His followers were later called "Barelvis," a label that became fixed after the period of this chapter; it should not be assumed that the later sectarian identity existed fully formed in the nineteenth century. Sufi lineages, including the Chishti, Qadiri, and Naqshbandi orders, remained central to the religious life of many Muslims in towns and the countryside, and their shrines were important social institutions.

Other initiatives pursued reconciliation or novelty. The Nadwat al-Ulama, launched in the early 1890s (its first public conference was held at Kanpur in April 1894), aimed to bring scholars of different persuasions together and to reform madrasa curricula by combining classical learning with attention to modern conditions; its institution at Lucknow, and scholars such as Shibli Nomani, became important. Lahore associations such as the Anjuman-i Himayat-i Islam (1884) combined religious, educational, and charitable work. In eastern Bengal, earlier movements, such as the Faraizi movement associated with Haji Shariatullah and his son, had linked religious reform to the concerns of cultivators, and reformist currents continued there. In Qadian in the Punjab, Mirza Ghulam Ahmad began his movement in 1889, provoking intense controversy over his claims, a development with a long subsequent history. Shia communities in Awadh and elsewhere, as well as Ismaili groups such as the Khojas and Bohras, had their own religious scholars, institutions, and debates.

Printing helped all of these networks. Urdu newspapers, fatwa collections, pamphlets, and annual meetings multiplied. The press gave religious authority a new public dimension, as scholars debated one another in print and before readerships that older oral and manuscript scholarship had not reached. In this respect the diverse Muslim networks shared with Hindu and Sikh reformers a common repertoire of institutions, even where their doctrinal positions diverged. Students should note, too, that several of the identities now commonly labeled as sects or schools, including Deobandi, Barelvi, and Ahl-i Hadith, were still being formed in the late nineteenth century and became more firmly established later.

Section 74.12

The Singh Sabha Movement: Sikh Religious Renewal after Annexation

Chapter 65 described the end of the Sikh kingdom and the annexation of Punjab in 1849. The change affected Sikh elites, the army, the gurdwaras, and the sense of Sikh collective identity. In the following decades, Sikhs faced a new environment: colonial government that recruited Sikh soldiers but also restructured landholding and administration; an active Christian mission; and, from the 1870s, the preaching of the Arya Samaj and of Muslim reformers, all of whom competed for adherents and debated openly. A highly publicized episode in 1873, in which several Sikh students at an Amritsar mission school announced an intention to convert to Christianity, heightened anxiety. The conversion of the deposed Maharaja Duleep Singh to Christianity in 1853 had earlier become a symbol of loss.

Against this background, a group of Sikh notables, scholars, and landed gentry established the Singh Sabha at Amritsar in 1873. Its founders and supporters included members of the Sikh aristocracy, such as Thakur Singh Sandhawalia, and religious scholars such as Giani Gian Singh, together with representatives of hereditary leaders, such as Baba Khem Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak. The Sabha aimed to restore what its members regarded as pure Sikh teaching, to promote religious and general education, to publish Sikh literature, and to counter conversion. A second major organization, the Lahore Singh Sabha, was founded in 1879 and attracted a different constituency: educated professionals and middle-class reformers, with Gurmukh Singh and Jawahir Singh among its prominent figures. The Khalsa Diwan, formed at Amritsar in 1883, tried to coordinate the Sabhas, and later, in 1902, the Chief Khalsa Diwan sought a more unified structure.

The Singh Sabha movement was not ideologically uniform. The Amritsar Sabha, with leaders linked to the hereditary and aristocratic leadership of the Sikh community, was generally more accommodating of existing practices, including the veneration of hereditary Guru-descended families and customs with Hindu resonance. The Lahore Sabha and the reformers later called the Tat Khalsa ("pure Khalsa") emphasized a more sharply defined Sikh distinctiveness: the primacy of the Guru Granth Sahib, the rejection of rites they regarded as Brahmanical, the removal of images from gurdwaras, a Sikh marriage ceremony of its own (the Anand rite), and a clear separation from Hindu practice. The differences were practical and doctrinal, and they produced disputes, including over the practices at the Golden Temple at Amritsar.

Relations with other movements were complex. Early collaboration with Arya Samaj reformers, with whom Sikh reformers shared an opposition to idolatry and caste ritual, gave way in the 1880s to rivalry, since Arya Samaj literature criticized Sikh teachers and the Arya claim that Sikhism was part of Hinduism was contested. Sikh reformers reacted to Christian proselytism by establishing their own schools and tracts, and to Muslim and Hindu competitors by emphasizing distinctiveness. The colonial state, for its part, was both a patron (a number of British officials were sympathetic to Sikh institutions, partly because of Sikh military service) and a classifier, since the census counted Sikhs as a distinct category, and it also influenced which definitions of Sikhism received official recognition.

The Singh Sabha movement thus demonstrates the same pattern seen elsewhere in this chapter. It was a response to conversion pressure and to the loss of Sikh political power, an effort to organize and educate, and a debate within the community about authority and definition. It should not be read as the straightforward emergence of an already fixed identity, or as the project of a single unified group.

Section 74.13

Sikh Education, Print Culture, and the Definition of Religious Identity

Education and publishing were central to the Singh Sabha movement. Reformers established schools in Punjabi and Gurmukhi, and later in English, and a girls' school, the Sikh Kanya Mahavidyalaya at Ferozepur, was founded in 1892. The best-known institution was Khalsa College at Amritsar, established in 1892 after years of fundraising and negotiation among Sikh leaders, British officials, and princely patrons; its first collegiate classes began in 1897, so establishment should not be mistaken for the commencement of degree-level teaching. It offered higher education to Sikhs and others in the colonial university system, and it reflected both Sikh community aspirations and a considerable colonial role, since British officials sat on its governing bodies and shaped its development, a source of later disputes about control.

Gurmukhi printing grew rapidly. Newspapers such as the Khalsa Akhbar of Lahore (1886), tract societies, and presses brought religious literature before a wide readership. Bhai Vir Singh (1872–1957) of Amritsar, a writer and publisher, established the Wazir-i-Hind Press in 1892 and the Khalsa Tract Society in 1894, publishing devotional, historical, and fictional works that shaped Sikh literary culture. Reformers produced editions of scripture and commentary, histories, and guides to Sikh practice, and they sought to standardize rites, including the Anand marriage ceremony, and to reduce the diversity of local custom.

Debate over definitions of Sikhism took place largely through print. The scholar Kahn Singh Nabha (1861–1938), connected with the princely state of Nabha, published his pamphlet Ham Hindu Nahin ("We Are Not Hindus") in 1898, arguing that Sikhs constituted a distinct religious community, a position that was contested by Sikhs who emphasized continuity with Hindu traditions as well as by Hindu reformers. Such writings are best read as contributions to an ongoing argument within the community and with outsiders, not as a final settlement. The debate also reflects the tools available to the movement: a script, Gurmukhi, which Sikhs identified with their tradition; institutions for teaching it; and printed periodicals.

The relationship between Sikh institutions and the colonial state was ambivalent. Colonial officials valued Sikhs as soldiers and often favored an identity distinct from that of other Punjabis. Reform leaders accepted state recognition and patronage while worrying about outside control. The Chief Khalsa Diwan, set up in 1902, sought to unify the community's institutions but did not end disagreements, and the contested control of gurdwaras, which would culminate in the Gurdwara reform campaigns of the 1920s, is reserved for later chapters.

The Sikh case shows how print and education allowed a community to articulate collective identity with unusual explicitness. It also shows that the definition was contested: between Amritsar and Lahore, between hereditary leaders and middle-class reformers, and between those who emphasized Sikh separateness and those who stressed continuity with their neighbors. Ordinary Sikh villagers, many of whom practiced forms of devotion that reformers wished to change, are less visible in the sources than the reformers who wrote about them.

Section 74.14

Jyotirao Phule and the Satyashodhak Samaj

Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890) was born in Pune into a family of the Mali (gardener and horticulturist) community, ranked low in the Brahmanical hierarchy of western India. He was educated at a mission school, read widely in English, and came to see caste domination as the central social problem of his society. In 1848, with his wife Savitribai, he opened a school for girls in Pune, and over the following years he and his collaborators established further schools, including for children of groups excluded from formal education. In 1863 he opened a home intended to protect pregnant widows and their newborn children from the social and legal consequences of illegitimate births.

His distinctive contribution was an analysis of caste as a system of power. Phule argued that Brahmin priests and officials had built a structure of religious authority, ritual dependence, and educational monopoly that kept peasants and laboring castes, whom he called the Shudras and Ati-Shudras, in subjection and ignorance. He directed his criticism both at Brahmanical scripture and priestly mediation and at the failure of the colonial state to educate and protect cultivators. In his work Shetkaryaca Asud (The Cultivator's Whipcord, composed and presented in stages in 1882–83; its earliest printed circulation is not firmly dated), he described the peasant's exploitation by moneylenders, revenue officials, and Brahmin intermediaries, and in 1882 he submitted a memorandum on behalf of the cultivators to the government's Education Commission, arguing that public funds should reach the rural poor rather than concentrating on higher education for the privileged.

On 24 September 1873 Phule and his associates founded the Satyashodhak Samaj ("Truth-Seeking Society") in Pune. Its program rejected the necessity of Brahmin priests in ritual life and promoted the performance of marriages and other ceremonies without them, advocated education for the lower castes and for girls, and asserted the equality of all humans before one creator. Its membership came chiefly from the Mali, Kunbi, and related peasant and artisan groups; Brahmins were not excluded in principle, though they were few. The Samaj gathered members in meetings, supported a press (notably the weekly Dinbandhu, associated with Krishnarao Bhalekar), and developed its own ritual alternatives. Phule received the honorific title "Mahatma" in 1888, and he died in 1890. His book Sarvajanik Satya Dharma Pustak, setting out his religious views, appeared after his death.

His 1873 work Gulamgiri ("Slavery") is the most famous statement of his historical argument. Using a comparative and polemical vocabulary, Phule likened the position of the lower castes to that of the enslaved in the Americas, a comparison he made explicit by dedicating the book to the people of the United States for their abolition of slavery. He read Hindu myths, including the avatars of Vishnu, as symbolic accounts of the conquest of indigenous peoples by invading "Aryans," whom he identified with Brahmins, and he celebrated figures such as King Bali as heroes of the conquered. This historical claim reflects the racial-historical theories current in the nineteenth century, a debate that Chapter 5 treats critically; modern historians generally reject a simple model of an Aryan "invasion" as an adequate account of Vedic history. Phule's historical narrative should therefore be read as a powerful political and moral argument about caste and power, not as a reliable reconstruction of ancient events.

Phule's relationship to elite reformers was critical. He believed that Brahmin reformers such as those of the Prarthana Samaj were unwilling to challenge the privileges on which their status depended, and he looked instead to the cultivating castes and to colonial education as instruments of change, while criticizing the colonial regime when it failed to serve them. After his death the Satyashodhak Samaj continued, and its ideas fed into the non-Brahmin movements of the early twentieth century, discussed in Chapter 80.

Section 74.15

Anti-Caste Reform in Practice: Education, Community Organization, and Regional Movements

Anti-caste organizing was practical as well as intellectual. Its core tools were schools, associations, alternative ceremonies, and the assertion of access to public space and religious rites. Education was central because exclusion from literacy had been a means of maintaining hierarchy, and because schooling offered access to the employment and public recognition on which later political claims would depend.

Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) was among the first Indian women to work as a teacher in the modern educational system and a central figure in the Phules' schools. She taught, trained others, wrote poetry in Marathi, and later helped with relief work during famine and plague; she died in 1897 after contracting plague while caring for patients. Accounts of a colleague now widely identified as Fatima Sheikh, associated with the Phules' educational work in Pune, have received growing attention. The strongest contemporary trace is Savitribai's letter to Jyotirao dated 10 October 1856, which mentions “Fatima” but does not establish every detail of her later biography, surname, teaching appointments, or dates. Historians accordingly distinguish a credible association from modern reconstructions and popular anecdotes about her work and the hostility she encountered. A careful account acknowledges both the significance of these women's work and the limits of the sources.

Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj also sought to create alternatives to Brahmin-mediated ritual: it performed marriages without Brahmin priests, held meetings (jalsas) that included songs and plays, and sought to strengthen a sense of shared identity among the peasant and laboring castes. These practices were an attempt to build institutions of dignity at the village and community level.

The same period saw regionally distinctive initiatives. In Kerala, Narayana Guru (c. 1855–1928), a spiritual teacher from the Ezhava community, consecrated a Shiva image at Aruvippuram in 1888, an act that implicitly challenged the exclusive priestly rights of Brahmins and the restriction of temple worship, since Ezhavas were denied entry to many Hindu temples. The region was then largely under princely rule in Travancore and Cochin, with its own caste restrictions on public roads, education, and temple access. Narayana Guru's teaching emphasized the unity of humanity, spiritual self-development, and education, and its later formulations, associated with the idea of one caste, one religion, and one god for humankind, became widely known. In 1903 the Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Yogam was established, with associates such as Dr. P. Palpu and the poet Kumaran Asan, to organize Ezhava community advancement. Ayyankali, associated with the Pulaya community, challenged exclusion from public roads, notably in an episode commonly dated to 1893, and later organized for educational access; details of the road protest are preserved substantially through later histories and require appropriate qualification.

In Madras, the Tamil scholar Iyothee Thass (1845–1914) organized the Dravida Mahajana Sabha in 1891 and, in 1898, the Sakya Buddhist Society, arguing that those classed as "Paraiyar" and "untouchable" had been originally Buddhist, and he turned to Buddhism as a religious alternative. In Bengal, the Matua movement among the Namasudra community, associated with Harichand Thakur, stressed devotion, equality, and education. In Maharashtra, Gopal Baba Walangkar and others petitioned government in the 1880s on behalf of Mahar army veterans. Missionary Christianity also attracted converts from groups excluded by caste, as examined in Section 74.19.

Anti-caste organizing cannot be interpreted simply as an extension of reform led by the socially privileged. Its leaders often came from the communities they sought to mobilize or allied with them; its analysis centered on power and labor, not only on ritual purity or religious doctrine; and it frequently criticized elite reformers for confining reform to their own interests. At the same time, its strategies varied. Some groups sought entry into existing religious institutions; others created new ones; others looked to Christianity or Buddhism. Colonial rule created openings, through schools, courts, and the census, but also reinforced categories and offered uneven protection. The later development of these struggles, including the political leadership of B. R. Ambedkar, appears in Chapter 80.

Section 74.16

Women's Education, Female Institutions, and Social Opportunity

Female education expanded unevenly across the nineteenth century. Chapter 73 observed how colonial educational policy privileged particular groups; here the focus is on movements and individuals who sought to change the access of women and girls. Before mid-century, formal schooling for girls was rare outside missionary schools, although women of various social groups had learned to read through family teaching, convent-like settings, or religious instruction, and elite households in some regions maintained private tutoring.

In Bengal, the Hindu Female School of Calcutta, founded in 1849 by J. E. D. Bethune with support from Indian reformers including Vidyasagar, became a model, later developing into Bethune College (1879). Brahmo reformers promoted women's education, publishing journals such as Bamabodhini Patrika (from 1863) for female readers, and a small number of women entered higher education: in 1883 Kadambini Ganguly and Chandramukhi Basu became the first two women graduates of the University of Calcutta, and Kadambini later practiced medicine. In western India, Parsi and reform communities established girls' schools, Pandita Ramabai and others worked in Pune (Section 74.18), and in 1886 Anandibai Joshi of Pune, after study in the United States, qualified as a physician, though she died soon after returning. In the south, schools run by missionaries and by Indian reformers expanded in Madras presidency, and Telugu reformer Kandukuri Veeresalingam promoted girls' schooling alongside widow remarriage in the 1870s and 1880s.

Muslim education took particular forms. Home instruction by women and in religious settings was common. Some reformers wrote didactic works to guide women's conduct: the Urdu novel Mirat al-Uroos (1869) by the Aligarh-associated writer Nazir Ahmad, and, later, the religious handbook Bihishti Zewar by the Deobandi scholar Ashraf Ali Thanawi, were widely read. Such works endorsed female literacy, but within a vision of women's roles as moral managers of the household, and historians discuss whether they widened women's horizons or confined them. Prominent Muslim women, such as Nawab Faizunnissa Chaudhurani of Comilla in Bengal, patronized education. Organized girls' schooling for Muslims expanded mainly after this chapter's principal period. In the Punjab, Arya Samaj and Sikh institutions founded girls' schools in the 1890s (Sections 74.5 and 74.13).

Resistance and negotiation shaped these developments. Opponents feared that educated girls would neglect household duties, be exposed to improper influences, or be alienated from tradition, and concerns about seclusion (purdah and zenana) kept many girls from schools. In response, reformers and missionaries developed "zenana teaching," in which women teachers visited secluded women at home, and curricula that emphasized domestic skills, morality, and religion. The Lady Dufferin Fund (1885) promoted medical care for women by women. Families, in turn, negotiated: some taught daughters privately, others delayed marriage, and some resisted both reform and orthodoxy by choosing what suited their circumstances. The evidence for these choices often comes from reformers' reports and memoirs rather than from the women themselves.

The overall effect was real but limited. By the end of the century, small numbers of women had entered schools and a very few the professions; the great majority of South Asian women, rural, poor, and of lower castes, remained outside formal education. Women's education therefore illustrates both the reach and the social boundaries of reform: it opened opportunities, concentrated at first among urban and upper-caste groups, and it raised the questions of marriage, widowhood, and law considered in the next two sections.

Section 74.17

Widow Remarriage, Child Marriage, and the Age of Consent Debate

Marriage and widowhood were among the most contested reform issues because they touched family, property, caste, and law. Among many upper-caste Hindu communities, widows were expected not to remarry, and their lives were restricted by rules of dress, diet, and household status; child marriage was common in many regions and groups. The abolition of sati in 1829 (Chapter 67) had addressed the practice of widow-burning, but it left the condition of widows unresolved.

Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891), principal of the Sanskrit College in Calcutta, took up widow remarriage with particular energy. In the mid-1850s he published arguments, drawing on Sanskrit legal texts, notably a verse of the Parashara Smriti, to show that scripture permitted remarriage, and he organized petitions to the government, including one with a large number of signatures in 1855. Orthodox groups, led by figures such as Radhakanta Deb's circle, countered with their own petitions. The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (Act XV of 1856) legalized the remarriage of Hindu widows and declared the children of such marriages legitimate. However, the Act provided that a widow who remarried lost the rights she held in her late husband's property, a significant limitation. The first remarriage under the Act took place in December 1856, and Vidyasagar supported such marriages financially and socially, at considerable personal cost.

In practice, widow remarriage remained uncommon through the century, because social disapproval, loss of property, and community sanctions continued to deter it, even in reform circles. Campaigns continued in other regions: in Bombay presidency through reform associations and in the Telugu country under Veeresalingam, who arranged a widely noted widow marriage in 1881. These campaigns illustrate that legislative success did not automatically transform behavior and that law and social practice moved at different speeds.

The debate over marriage age and consent came to a head in the 1880s and 1890s. The Parsi reformer Behramji Malabari published notes on infant marriage and enforced widowhood in 1884 and campaigned for legal reform. The case of Rukhmabai, a Bombay woman who refused to live with the husband to whom she had been married as a child and was sued for restitution of conjugal rights, drew wide attention between 1884 and 1888 and raised questions about consent and the courts' role. In 1889 the death of Phulmoni Dasi, a girl of about eleven, after intercourse with her adult husband in Bengal, galvanized public concern. The Age of Consent Act of 1891 raised the age under the criminal law at which sexual intercourse with a girl was legally permissible from ten to twelve years, inside or outside marriage. It did not establish twelve as a general minimum age of marriage, a point often misunderstood.

The controversy was bitter. Reformers such as many members of the Prarthana and Brahmo Samajes supported the bill, arguing that law should protect girls. Opponents included orthodox leaders and some prominent nationalists, among them Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who did not necessarily defend child marriage but argued that reform should come from within society and that a foreign government should not legislate on matters of religion and family; critics of this position replied that appeals to community autonomy shielded real harm to children. Both sides appealed to scripture, to custom, and to ideas of national dignity, and women, whose lives were at stake, had little direct voice in the legislative debate. Their own writings, considered in Section 74.18, entered the debate from outside the legislative arena. The episode shows the entanglement of reform with colonial law and with the beginnings of political nationalism, a connection later chapters develop.

Section 74.18

Women's Voices: Pandita Ramabai, Tarabai Shinde, and Gender Critique

Women were not only subjects of reform; they were reformers, authors, and critics. Two works of the 1880s demonstrate the range of their intellectual contributions. They should be read as primary arguments by women about their own society, not merely as illustrations of programs conducted by men.

Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922) was born into a Maharashtrian Brahmin family whose father, a Sanskrit scholar, taught his wife and daughter. Orphaned by famine in the 1870s, she traveled with her brother, became known as a Sanskrit scholar and speaker, and received the title "Pandita" in Calcutta in 1878. She married a lawyer of a different caste in 1880 and was widowed in 1882. In Pune she founded the Arya Mahila Samaj and testified before the Education Commission of 1882 on women's education. She traveled to England in 1883, where she converted to Christianity, and then to the United States, where in 1887 she published The High-Caste Hindu Woman, an account of the lives of Hindu women from childhood through marriage and widowhood, written in English for a Western audience whose support she sought for an institution for widows. In 1889 she opened the Sharada Sadan, a home and school for high-caste widows, in Bombay, later moved to Pune; in 1896 she began the Mukti Mission at Kedgaon, giving shelter to famine-stricken women and children. Her conversion and the religious program of her institutions alienated many of her Indian supporters, including reformers who had admired her earlier work, and debates over her motives and methods continue; Uma Chakravarti's study of her life is a leading scholarly assessment.

Tarabai Shinde (1850–1910) lived in Buldhana, in Berar, in a Marathi-speaking family; she was educated at home and married. In 1882 she published in Pune Stri Purush Tulana ("A Comparison between Women and Men"). The immediate occasion was the trial in 1881 of Vijayalakshmi, a young Brahmin widow in Surat convicted of killing her newborn child and sentenced to transportation, a case that provoked newspaper commentary that, in Shinde's view, blamed women while excusing men. She wrote a forceful and often sarcastic critique of the double standards governing sexual morality, widowhood, and the allocation of blame, drawing on religious stories and everyday observation. The book provoked hostile replies in the Marathi press and then largely disappeared from public view for nearly a century, until it was rediscovered by historians in the twentieth century; Rosalind O'Hanlon's edition and study (1994) made it widely available. Little is known about Shinde's later life.

The two authors differed in religion, caste location, language, audience, and strategy, but both questioned practices within their own society while also challenging the assumptions of male reformers. Ramabai addressed Western readers and appealed for institutional support; Shinde wrote in Marathi, to a local readership, in an assertive polemical register. Ramabai's analysis of Hindu family law and widowhood was entangled with her turn to Christianity and with her dependence on Western donors, which shaped how she presented her subject; Shinde's argument drew on Hindu stories in order to turn them against male privilege. Neither fits a simple model of reform as a program devised by educated men for passive women.

Section 74.19

Reform, Revival, and New Religious Identities across South Asia

Not all nineteenth-century religious change fits the major Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh organizations. Other communities developed their own reform efforts, which both parallel and interact with those studied earlier.

The Parsi community, centered in Bombay and Gujarat, produced a notable reform movement. The Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha, established in 1851 by reformers including Naoroji Furdunji, Dadabhai Naoroji, S. S. Bengalee, and K. R. Cama, promoted education, a reinterpretation of Zoroastrian teaching, the removal of practices considered later accretions, and the improvement of women's position. Its journal, the Rast Goftar, gave it a public voice, and its work coincided with legislation on Parsi marriage and divorce in the 1860s. Orthodox priests and lay leaders resisted some of its changes, producing debates over ritual, purity, and authority within the community. The Parsis' small numbers and commercial prominence shaped a distinctive path.

Christian missionary institutions provided schools, hospitals, and presses, and many Indians participated as converts, teachers, clergy, and writers. Figures such as Krishna Mohan Banerjea in Bengal and Lal Behari Day were prominent Indian Christians, and in many regions, groups excluded by caste, such as communities in the Telugu and Tamil countries and Punjab, joined churches in large numbers, seeking dignity, education, or protection. Missionary objectives, which included conversion and often criticism of Hindu and Muslim practice, differed from those of Indian Christians, who developed their own leadership and questions about relation to Indian culture. Missionary activity also stimulated the defensive and imitative institutional efforts of other communities.

A Buddhist revival linked South Asia with Sri Lanka and wider Asia. In Sri Lanka, Buddhist monks and laity responded to missionary pressure with public debates, notably at Panadura in 1873, and Theosophists such as Henry Olcott, who arrived in 1880, helped found Buddhist schools. The Sinhalese Buddhist activist Anagarika Dharmapala (born Don David Hewavitarne) founded the Maha Bodhi Society in Colombo in 1891 with the aim of restoring Buddhist control over Bodh Gaya, the site of the Buddha's enlightenment, then held by a Hindu mahant, and moved its headquarters to Calcutta in 1892. Dharmapala, who also spoke at the 1893 Parliament of Religions, linked reform in Sri Lanka, pilgrimage to India, and international exchange, and Edwin Arnold's poem The Light of Asia (1879) had helped popularize interest in the Buddha among Western and Indian readers. The Bodh Gaya dispute shows how revival could involve conflict over sacred sites.

Comparing these movements, several patterns emerge. Most used associations, printing, schools, and public lectures. Most distinguished between "true" religion and later corruption. Most defined their communities more explicitly than before, in part because colonial enumeration and competition among communities rewarded clarity. Some strengthened religious boundaries, for example through conversion contests, polemic, and claims over shrines and institutions; others encouraged cooperation, as when Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi donors supported common educational ventures, or when reformers of different communities read, debated, and borrowed from one another. Changes in religious and social organization helped reshape collective identities, but they did not predetermine later political outcomes, which depended on further developments examined in subsequent chapters.

Cause and Effect

Why Religious and Social Reform Became Organized after 1858

Long-term causes

Older traditions of religious debate, devotion, and scholarship; the expansion of English-language education and a new middle class; missionary activity; the spread of printing; and structural inequalities of caste and gender.

Short-term causes

The aftermath of 1857, which prompted reassessment among Muslims and Hindus; the end of earlier patronage networks; conversion scandals; and legislative interventions such as the 1856 Act and the Age of Consent bill.

Immediate effects

Founding of associations, schools, and periodicals; public debates; schisms within movements; and new demands on colonial government.

Long-term consequences

New forms of religious authority and community identity; wider educational access, though unequal; sustained debates on caste and gender; and an institutional foundation for later social and political movements.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains organized reform. Colonial institutions shaped opportunities, but movements also arose from indigenous intellectual traditions and from the initiatives of people who were excluded by existing hierarchies.

Section 74.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined movements that differed profoundly in aims and methods. The Brahmo Samaj sought a reasoned, monotheistic Hinduism and divided over the pace and principles of social reform. The Prarthana Samaj pursued gradual change through regional devotional heritage. The Arya Samaj combined scriptural revivalism with educational institutions and polemical energy. Ramakrishna offered a devotional pluralism that his disciples turned into a monastic mission of service. Sayyid Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh movement sought to integrate modern education into Muslim life; Deoband built an independent institution for classical Islamic learning; and other Muslim networks pursued still other visions. The Singh Sabhas defined and organized Sikh religious life. Phule, Narayana Guru, and other anti-caste figures challenged the very structure of hierarchy. Women, notably Savitribai Phule, Pandita Ramabai, and Tarabai Shinde, contributed as educators, institution-builders, and critics.

Compared systematically, the movements show different sources of authority: the Vedas for the Arya Samaj, reason and conscience for the Brahmo Samaj, classical legal and hadith scholarship for Deoband, rational interpretation for Sayyid Ahmad Khan, the Guru Granth Sahib and Sikh tradition for the Singh Sabhas, and experience of oppression and a counter-history for Phule. They drew on different social bases: Bengali professionals, Punjabi urban groups, north Indian Muslim gentry and ulama, peasants and artisans in western India, and marginalized communities in the south. They used shared organizational methods but sometimes drew opposite conclusions.

Colonial institutions shaped the possibilities for reform. The press, schools, courts, railways, census, and legislation provided platforms and constraints, and colonial recognition could confer advantage in disputes. But South Asians were not merely responding to European ideas. They drew on Upanishadic, Vedic, devotional, legal, Persianate, Sikh, and Buddhist traditions, and on their own social experience, and reformers often criticized colonial policy and missionary arguments.

The chapter's central tension is that movements could contest some inequalities while preserving others, create opportunities while privileging particular groups, and promote both cooperation and sharper boundaries. Brahmo and Prarthana reformers advocated women's education while often retaining caste advantages; Arya reformers criticized birth-based caste while polemicizing against other communities; Aligarh opened doors for a section of the Muslim elite; anti-caste reformers demanded education for the excluded and criticized upper-caste reform; and women challenged male reformers' assumptions. The category of reform, therefore, must be used with care.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Older Traditions and Reform Institutions

What Changed

  • Voluntary associations with subscriptions, constitutions, elections, and annual reports became a normal form of religious and social organization.
  • Printing, newspapers, and public debate made religious argument a mass phenomenon.
  • Schools and colleges, including for some girls, became central to community aspirations.
  • Religious communities were defined more explicitly, in part through competition and enumeration.

What Stayed the Same

  • Devotional traditions, Sufi lineages, Vedantic ideas, and legal scholarship remained resources for reformers.
  • Caste, gender, and class hierarchies persisted, though contested.
  • Most South Asians, rural and poor, continued to live outside the reach of formal reform institutions.
  • Local custom, shrines, and household practice often continued despite reformers' criticism.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban, educated, and mostly upper-caste men; some women in reform households; and members of excluded groups who gained schooling. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural cultivators, laborers, and most women outside reform circles. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against treating the activities of articulate reformers as the experience of all South Asians.

Historical Debate

Religious Revival or Social Transformation? The Limits and Contradictions of Reform

The question: Should nineteenth-century movements be understood chiefly as religious revival and community definition, or as agents of social transformation, and what did their contradictions mean?

Interpretation A: reform as social transformation. Some historians emphasize that movements opened educational opportunity, challenged practices such as child marriage and untouchability, established new institutions, and created a public sphere of debate. On this view, reformers, including women and anti-caste activists, initiated lasting change in law, schooling, and social attitudes.
Interpretation B: reform as revival and boundary-making. Others, following work such as that of Kenneth W. Jones on Arya Samaj and related scholarship, emphasize that many movements defined religious communities more sharply, privileged particular groups, left deep hierarchies intact, and sometimes contributed to conflict among communities. On this view, reform is better seen as reorganization of religious life and community identity than as simple liberation.

Evidence: Both views draw on institutional records, publications, census data, legislation, and the writings of reformers, women, and anti-caste critics. Evidence for transformation includes the expansion of schools and legal change; evidence for limits includes the narrow social base of many movements, the persistence of caste and gender inequality, and records of polemic and conflict.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians connect nineteenth-century reform to later developments. Most scholars now argue that both processes occurred together and varied by movement and region. Political consequences must be traced through documented connections, not hindsight.

Student task: Choose one movement from this chapter and use specific evidence to assess whether it is better characterized by transformation, boundary-making, or both. Identify additional evidence that would help.

The chapter's themes lead forward in two directions. Chapter 75 turns to cities, industrial labor, migration, and the diaspora, the settings in which many of these institutions and the new middle classes lived and worked. Chapter 76 turns to organized nationalism, in which several reform leaders, institutions, and networks played a part, though their political roles require separate examination rather than assumption.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the religious and social reform movements through which South Asians, particularly between 1858 and about 1905, organized to renew, defend, reinterpret, or challenge their religious and social worlds. It has argued that these movements cannot be reduced to Westernization or to simple revival: they drew on older traditions, on new intellectual currents, and on colonial institutions, and they disagreed profoundly about what reform meant.

Among Hindu movements, the Brahmo Samaj, revived by Debendranath Tagore after Rammohun Roy, combined monotheism and reason with congregational worship, and then divided in 1866 and 1878 under the pressures of Keshab Chandra Sen's leadership and the Cooch Behar controversy. The Prarthana Samaj pursued gradual reform in western India, drawing on Maharashtrian devotional tradition. The Arya Samaj, founded by Dayananda Saraswati, grounded reform in the authority of the Vedas, built schools and associations, especially in the Punjab, and contributed to sharper religious boundaries through polemic and conversion disputes, though its best-known shuddhi campaigns belong to a later period.

Ramakrishna's devotional pluralism, preserved largely through disciples' accounts, was transformed by Vivekananda into a practical Vedanta of service and by the Ramakrishna Mission into an institution of education and relief. The chapter distinguished Ramakrishna's own teaching, the Mission's institutional program, and later political interpretations.

Muslim reform took several forms. Sayyid Ahmad Khan responded to the aftermath of 1857 with a program of scientific and English education, rationalist interpretation, and the Aligarh school and college. Deoband created an independent, donation-supported institution for classical Islamic learning. Aligarh and Deoband both used new organizational forms, and the contrast between them cannot be reduced to modernity versus tradition. Ahl-i Hadith, the circle of Ahmad Riza Khan, Sufi networks, the Nadwat al-Ulama, and other groups show that Muslim reform was diverse.

After the annexation of Punjab, the Singh Sabhas at Amritsar and Lahore organized Sikh religious renewal, education, and publishing, and debated Sikh distinctiveness, producing institutions such as Khalsa College and a rich Gurmukhi print culture. They were not ideologically uniform, and the later Gurdwara reform movement is reserved for later chapters.

Jyotirao Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj and his Gulamgiri analyzed caste as domination and mobilized peasant and laboring castes. Savitribai Phule and Fatima Sheikh contributed to education, and regional initiatives such as Narayana Guru's Aruvippuram consecration, Iyothee Thass's Buddhist turn, and Namasudra and other movements show that anti-caste organizing was not an extension of elite reform. Documentary limits, especially regarding Fatima Sheikh, must be acknowledged.

Women's education expanded unevenly, from the Bethune School and missionary and reform schools to the first women graduates and physicians, though most women remained outside formal education. Debates over widow remarriage, child marriage, and the Age of Consent Act of 1891, which raised the criminal-law age of consent from ten to twelve but did not set a general minimum marriage age, revealed the tangled relations among reform, orthodoxy, colonial law, and early nationalism. Pandita Ramabai and Tarabai Shinde offered original critiques of gender relations.

Parsi, Christian, and Buddhist reform efforts, including the Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha and the Maha Bodhi Society, widened the picture. Across the movements, shared methods, namely associations, printing, schools, and public debate, coexisted with conflicting authority claims. Reform strengthened some religious boundaries while encouraging debate and cooperation.

The chapter's central lesson is that reform contested some inequalities while preserving others, and that its political consequences must be established through documented connections rather than hindsight. Chapters 75 through 77 examine cities and labor, organized nationalism, and Muslim political organization, in which these institutions and networks played a part.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows South Asians as active authors of their own religious and social futures. Under colonial rule, reformers built associations, schools, presses, and institutions, and they argued, often fiercely, over authority, equality, and community. Their efforts transformed religious life and left a lasting legacy of organizations, debates, and educational institutions.

It also shows that reform was never a single project. The same decades produced scriptural revivalism and rationalist theism, devotional pluralism and legal scholarship, elite educational reform and anti-caste critique, male reformers' programs and women's own analysis. Their disagreements were not failures of a unified movement; they were the substance of the history.

The chapter also reminds us that boundaries were made historically. Associations, censuses, schools, and polemics helped define communities more explicitly, but later political outcomes were not contained in the nineteenth-century movements. Tracing real connections, rather than assuming them, is the historian's task as the narrative moves to cities and labor in Chapter 75 and organized nationalism in Chapter 76.

Finally, this chapter extends a theme found throughout the series: the long struggle over who may interpret tradition, who may learn, and who may speak. Questions raised by Phule, Ramabai, Shinde, Narayana Guru, and their contemporaries continued into the twentieth century, as later chapters, especially Chapters 80 and 107, will show.
