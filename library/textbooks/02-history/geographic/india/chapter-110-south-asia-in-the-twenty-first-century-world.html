South Asia in the Twenty-First-Century World

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

On October 29, 2026, the United Nations General Assembly adopted, without opposition from any member state, a decision postponing by three years the date on which Bangladesh and Nepal were to leave the category of "least developed countries." Both had been scheduled to graduate on November 24, 2026. Ambassadors from two of South Asia's poorer states, speaking through the Group of 77 developing countries, asked the world's assembled governments for time, and received it. The episode was small beside the great-power summits of that autumn, but it captures the chapter's subject: South Asian states had become participants in international institutions, markets, and rivalries, yet they negotiated their positions from very different strengths, and often in competition with one another as much as in concert.

This chapter concludes the chronological narrative of the textbook. Chapters 98 through 109 followed liberalization, party politics, the individual countries, regional security, economic and social change, and the environment. This chapter lifts the view above those national histories to ask how South Asia's place in the international system changed between 1991 and 2026. In 1991 India faced a balance-of-payments crisis and had lost its principal Cold War partner; Pakistan's long strategic relationship with Washington was fraying; Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, and Maldives were small, aid-dependent, and mostly absent from the calculations of the great powers. By November 2026 India hosted the BRICS summit in New Delhi, attended by the leaders of China and Russia; Pakistan had mediated talks between the United States and Iran; and Bangladesh and Nepal, after mass movements that changed their governments, were openly diversifying their foreign relations.

The chapter is organized around a question rather than a list of bilateral relationships: how did South Asian governments pursue security, economic opportunity, diplomatic influence, and national autonomy among competing powers? The answer cannot be reduced to India's rise. India is the region's largest economy, population, and military establishment, and it receives corresponding attention. But Pakistan's alignments with China, Saudi Arabia, and the United States, Bangladesh's balancing of exports and neighbors, Sri Lanka's debt and ports, Nepal's transit dependence, Bhutan's border diplomacy, and Maldives' ocean geography were each shaped by their own interests. South Asia was not a single foreign-policy actor, and the chapter does not treat it as one.

Three distinctions organize the analysis. The first separates economic weight, diplomatic influence, and military capability. A country can be large in one dimension and constrained in another; India's economy grew rapidly while its neighbors often resisted its regional leadership, and Pakistan's modest economy coexisted with a nuclear arsenal and an unexpectedly prominent diplomatic role in 2026. The second distinguishes agreements from implementation: summit declarations, memoranda of understanding, and announced investments are different historical facts from functioning ports, ratified treaties, and goods crossing borders. The third distinguishes forecasts from outcomes. Several developments at the cutoff, including the Iran war and its effects on shipping, the unfinished United States–India trade agreement, and growth forecasts, were unresolved; this chapter reports them as of November 20, 2026 and does not predict their conclusion.

Much of the evidence is documentary and statistical in ways earlier chapters were not: treaties and joint statements, trade agreements, World Bank and IMF reports, migration and remittance data, shipping and port records, and contemporary journalism. Each has limits. Government statements record intentions and presentations; development-finance forecasts are revised repeatedly; remittance figures depend on how transfers are measured; and news reporting from the last months before the cutoff has not been tested by later scholarship. The chapter therefore uses exact dates where they are verified, qualifies figures that are estimates, and marks claims that rest on early reporting.

Historical continuities run through the narrative. The Partition of 1947 and the rivalry between India and Pakistan (Chapters 86, 87, 92, 105) still prevented a functioning regional bloc in 2026. The Cold War alignments of the 1950s through the 1980s (Chapters 92 and 95) left residues in Russian arms supplies to India and in Pakistan's habit of seeking external patrons. The long Indian Ocean exchange systems examined in Volume I, and the colonial-era labor migrations of Chapter 75, were the historical foundations for twenty-first-century shipping lanes and Gulf labor markets. Chapter 111 then turns from these processes to the way South Asians remember and contest their past.

Historical Context

In 1991 the structures that had organized South Asian foreign policy for four decades were changing. The Soviet Union was dissolving, ending the arrangement under which India had received arms, diplomatic backing, and a rupee-trade partner. The United States no longer needed Pakistan as a conduit to the Afghan resistance, and sanctions on Pakistan's nuclear program (the Pressler Amendment, applied in 1990) reflected the shift. China had been reconciled with India since Rajiv Gandhi's 1988 visit but remained a strategic partner of Pakistan. In the smaller states, India's role was both essential and resented: Nepal and Bhutan relied on Indian territory for trade access, Sri Lanka had experienced the intervention of the Indian Peace Keeping Force (Chapters 96 and 103), and Bangladesh's relations with India were close but contested.

Liberalization (Chapter 98) altered India's external position more than any single diplomatic decision. A country that had restricted trade and investment began to seek both, reorienting its diplomacy toward commercial partners in East Asia, the Gulf, Europe, and the United States. Information-technology exports (Chapter 106) built relationships with American firms and with Indian professionals who migrated to the United States. The same decade produced the nuclear tests of 1998 (Chapter 97), which provoked sanctions but eventually led to a transformed relationship with Washington. At the same time, the Gulf economies that had expanded after the 1970s oil boom drew labor from across South Asia (Chapter 97), creating financial links that outlasted political changes.

Two features of the region constrain the narrative. First, geography: India surrounds Nepal, Bhutan, and Bangladesh on most sides, borders Pakistan and China across disputed frontiers, and sits at the center of the Indian Ocean basin, so its neighbors cannot easily avoid it, and often resist for that reason. Second, institutional weakness: SAARC (1985) never overcame the India–Pakistan rivalry, and no regional institution rivaled the European Union or ASEAN in shaping members' external relations. The chapter should be read against both facts.

The chapter's boundaries with its neighbors are deliberate. Detailed accounts of Kashmir, nuclear doctrine, and the border confrontations belong to Chapter 105; this chapter treats their international consequences. The domestic economic history of growth, cities, and technology belongs to Chapter 106; here the focus is external economic relationships. Climate science and environmental policy belong to Chapter 109, and cultural production by diasporas to Chapter 108. Because the final months before the research cutoff of November 20, 2026 are poorly documented by scholarship, the narrative relies on official documents, international organizations, and reporting, and flags what remains provisional.

Section 110.1

India's Emergence as a Major International Power

In mid-1991 India's foreign-exchange reserves had fallen to a level covering only a few weeks of imports, and the government pledged gold to raise emergency foreign currency. The crisis, examined in Chapter 98, was domestic in origin, but it forced a reconsideration of India's external relations. The Soviet Union, which had been India's principal arms supplier and a major trading partner, was collapsing. Policymakers who had defended import substitution and nonalignment as pillars of sovereignty now accepted that access to foreign capital, technology, and export markets was a condition of national strength. The diplomacy of the following decade therefore had an economic purpose that earlier foreign policy had often subordinated: a "Look East" policy from 1992 built relations with ASEAN, full diplomatic relations with Israel were established in January 1992, and relations with the United States, Europe, and the Gulf were reconsidered in commercial terms.

Growth supplied the material basis for a larger role. By 2025, most international estimates ranked India among the world's four or five largest economies at market exchange rates, and its population of roughly 1.4 billion was the largest in the world. Services, particularly software and business-process exports, connected Indian firms to American, European, and Gulf customers (Chapter 106). Military spending rose and domestic defense production was promoted, though India remained a leading importer of weapons. These indicators of scale require qualification. India's per-capita income remained far lower than that of the established powers; its manufacturing base and research expenditure were smaller than its economic ranking implied; and its prosperity was uneven across states and social groups (Chapters 100 and 107). Scale made India impossible for other governments to ignore, but scale was not equivalent to the ability to set agendas.

The tests of May 1998 marked a second turning point. Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee's government conducted nuclear tests on May 11 and 13, and Pakistan answered on May 28 and 30 (Chapter 97). The United States, Japan, and others imposed sanctions, and many governments condemned the tests. Yet within two years the diplomatic situation changed. A sustained dialogue between American and Indian officials began in 1998, and President Bill Clinton visited India in March 2000, the first presidential visit in more than two decades. The episode suggests a pattern in India's rise: tests that isolated India briefly were followed by a recognition among major powers that a nuclear-armed state with a large economy had to be engaged rather than excluded.

The reshaping of India’s external diplomacy can also be seen in its changing relationship with East Asia. The Look East initiative, initially concerned with recovering commercial access after the Cold War, developed into wider engagement with ASEAN, Japan, and South Korea; under the Act East formulation after 2014, transport links, maritime security, and infrastructure joined investment and trade. Japanese finance supported metropolitan transport and industrial corridors, while India's companies sought markets in Southeast Asia. These were not simply bilateral gestures. They altered the diplomatic geography of Indian development by linking domestic choices about roads, ports, standards, and industrial policy to international partnerships. At the same time, investment announcements did not always translate into completed projects, and commercial ties varied markedly across partner countries.

India's rise was also tested by the difference between visibility and institutional power. It became a regular participant in major summit diplomacy, conducted evacuations and disaster relief abroad, and cultivated defense-industrial relationships with several suppliers. Its public diplomacy emphasized civilizational connections and the large population of Indian origin living overseas. Yet an expanding diplomatic network did not supply a permanent United Nations Security Council seat, eliminate dependence on imported energy, or ensure that neighbors accepted Indian leadership. A useful historical assessment therefore asks which policies produced demonstrable change, such as trade flows, military agreements, working infrastructure, or negotiated settlements, and which chiefly produced declarations or symbolic recognition. By 2026 India's influence was unmistakably greater than in 1991, but it remained uneven across regions and issues.

India's emergence also depended on relationships built beyond the great powers: trade and investment ties with Japan, South Korea, and Southeast Asia; energy and diaspora links with the Gulf (110.9–110.10); and a growing network of trade agreements and negotiations in the 2020s, including the United Kingdom agreement signed in 2025 and the India–European Union free-trade negotiations concluded on January 27, 2026. Conclusion of those negotiations did not itself mean that the EU agreement had been signed, ratified, or brought into force. Yet regional acceptance lagged behind global recognition. India's neighbors frequently accused it of dominance, and the gap between its global standing and the reservations of its immediate region became one of the chapter's central problems.

Section 110.2

Strategic Autonomy, Multilateral Diplomacy, and India's Global Ambitions

Indian foreign policy had been framed since the 1950s as nonalignment, the refusal to join either Cold War bloc (Chapter 92). After the Soviet collapse that formula lost its original meaning, since there were no blocs to stay out of. Indian officials increasingly spoke instead of "strategic autonomy," the capacity to make decisions according to national interest without dependence on any single partner, and later of "multi-alignment," the cultivation of many partnerships at once. The change was one of emphasis as much as substance. The older language stressed distance from alliances; the newer language described engagement with all major powers while retaining freedom of action.

Historical Vocabulary
Strategic Autonomy, Nonalignment, Multipolarity, and the Global South

Nonalignment was a Cold War policy of declining to join military blocs. Strategic autonomy is a later phrase for retaining independent decision-making while cooperating with several powers; it does not mean neutrality, because a state may sign defense agreements and still claim autonomy. Multipolarity describes an international order with several centers of power rather than one or two; Indian, Chinese, and Russian leaders use the term approvingly, though they mean different things by it. The Global South is a loose political category, not a precise geographic one, referring to developing countries that often share concerns about trade, finance, and representation.

These terms are contested claims as much as analytical categories. Historians should ask who uses them, in what setting, and what policy they justify.

India participated in an unusually wide range of multilateral institutions. It was a founding member of the BRICS grouping (with Brazil, Russia, and China, joined by South Africa in 2010), a full member of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation from 2017 alongside Pakistan, a member of the G20 and a participant in the Quad (110.5). These forums include states whose interests conflict: BRICS and the SCO include China and Russia, while the Quad aligns India with the United States, Japan, and Australia. Indian diplomats described this as a strength, a means of preserving freedom of maneuver. Critics argued that it limited India's ability to join firm coalitions and made its positions hard to predict. Both observations describe real features of Indian practice.

India's 2023 presidency of the G20 was the most visible expression of its aspiration to represent developing countries. Before the New Delhi summit of September 9–10, 2023, India convened a "Voice of the Global South" summit in January 2023, and at the G20 it secured agreement on a leaders' declaration and the admission of the African Union as a permanent member. The agreement on the declaration was achieved despite disputes over the war in Ukraine; the wording on that war was a compromise, and Western and Russian officials read it differently. In 2026 India chaired BRICS. The eighteenth summit in New Delhi on September 12–13 was attended by leaders including Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, and the members adopted a New Delhi Declaration by consensus, though contemporary reports emphasized that the group avoided a direct condemnation of the US–Israeli war on Iran and that differences over Ukraine and the Middle East persisted. The 2026 summit therefore showed both India's convening power and the limits of consensus within the grouping.

India's campaign for a permanent seat on the United Nations Security Council is a long-standing test of aspiration against institutional achievement. India, with Brazil, Germany, and Japan (the "G4"), has argued that the Council's 1945 composition does not reflect twenty-first-century realities, and India served as a non-permanent member in 2021–2022. Reform requires approval by two-thirds of the General Assembly and the agreement of all five permanent members. Pakistan and other members of the "Uniting for Consensus" group oppose new permanent seats; China has expressed varying degrees of support for greater representation of developing countries without endorsing India's candidacy. No change to the Council's permanent membership had occurred by 2026. Historians must therefore distinguish India's diplomatic advocacy, which was sustained and visible, from institutional outcomes, which were minimal.

The multilateral record had a regional dimension. India's global agenda sometimes competed with its neighbors' preferences. Pakistan's opposition to Security Council expansion, Bangladesh's and Nepal's requests for extra time before leaving the least-developed-country category (110.18), and the smaller states' interest in diversifying their partners all suggested that "Global South" solidarity coexisted with regional rivalries. India could speak for developing countries in global forums more easily than it could persuade its own neighbors to accept its leadership.

Section 110.3

China and India: Border Rivalry, Economic Interdependence, and Strategic Competition

The relationship between India and China after the Cold War combined cooperation, economic exchange, and an unresolved boundary. The war of 1962 (Chapter 92) had left a legacy of mistrust and a frontier with no agreed line in several sectors. Rajiv Gandhi's visit to Beijing in December 1988 began a gradual normalization, and the Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity along the Line of Actual Control (1993) and the military confidence-building measures of 1996 committed both sides to maintaining the status quo and avoiding the use of force. Special Representatives were appointed in 2003 to seek a settlement, and political principles for a boundary settlement were agreed in 2005. These arrangements reduced the risk of conflict but did not resolve the dispute, and the Line of Actual Control itself remained undefined by agreement, so that each side patrolled according to its own perception.

Trade expanded while the border remained contested. China became one of India's largest trading partners, supplying machinery, electronics, pharmaceutical ingredients, and components for Indian manufacturing. Bilateral trade reached about US$127.7 billion in India's fiscal year 2024–25, according to reporting based on Indian data, with India's deficit close to US$100 billion. The imbalance became a political issue and a source of anxiety about dependence in strategic sectors, including electronics and, in 2025, rare-earth magnets, whose export restrictions by China affected Indian manufacturers. Economic interdependence did not create political trust; it created exposures.

Confrontations showed how the two dimensions interacted. The Doklam standoff of June–August 2017, in territory claimed by Bhutan and close to the strategic corridor linking India to its northeast, ended after about ten weeks without violence. The Galwan Valley clash of June 15, 2020 was more serious: twenty Indian soldiers were killed, and China later acknowledged a smaller number of its own casualties. The military history of these events belongs to Chapter 105; their international effects were broad. India restricted foreign direct investment from countries sharing land borders (the April 2020 rule known as Press Note 3), banned many Chinese mobile applications, and tightened scrutiny of Chinese firms. Flights were suspended, visas restricted, and high-level exchanges largely stopped. India's partnership with the United States, Japan, and Australia in the Quad was strengthened, and Indian officials described the border situation as a condition for the overall relationship.

A partial thaw followed. On October 21, 2024, the two governments announced an arrangement on patrolling in the Depsang and Demchok areas, and Prime Minister Modi and President Xi met at Kazan two days later. During 2025 and 2026, reports and official statements recorded the resumption of the Kailash-Mansarovar pilgrimage, tourist visas, and direct flights, and agreement to reopen border trade at three designated points, with Indian reporting indicating resumption from August 1, 2026. Wang Yi visited New Delhi in August 2025 for the twenty-fourth round of Special Representatives' talks; the twenty-fifth round took place in Beijing on August 25, 2026, where Chinese officials described cooperation as "gradually resuming." Xi Jinping then attended the BRICS summit in New Delhi in September 2026, reported as his first visit to India since the border clash. India raised concerns about a large dam on the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra) in Tibet, and China agreed to share some hydrological data.

Students should be careful about what these developments establish. They show a restoration of dialogue, flights, and some trade, and an agreement to continue boundary talks. They do not show a settlement of the boundary, a demilitarization of the frontier, or an end to strategic competition. Both countries maintained large deployments in the region; India continued to restrict Chinese investment while seeking components and capital; and both competed for influence in neighboring states (110.4). Indian and Chinese statements about the relationship differed in emphasis, with Beijing stressing that the boundary should not define the whole relationship and Delhi stressing that border peace was a prerequisite for normal ties.

The competition extended to infrastructure, technology, and alignments. India built roads, tunnels, and airstrips near the frontier; China built border villages and transport links on its side. Telecommunications and digital policy became areas of exclusion, with India limiting Chinese equipment suppliers. Beyond South Asia, India's strategic partnerships with the United States and its Indo-Pacific partners were influenced by its concern about China, while China's relationship with Pakistan (110.4) and its expanding presence in the Indian Ocean (110.11–110.12) formed the other half of what Indian officials called a two-front security problem. The relationship also demonstrates the limits of any simple concept of a Sino-Indian "rivalry": the two states cooperated in BRICS, held parallel interests in global trade and climate negotiations, and were each other's neighbor at the same time that each sought to constrain the other.

Section 110.4

China's Relationships with Pakistan and the Smaller South Asian States

China's relationship with Pakistan, built since the early 1960s, is the oldest and most strategic of its South Asian partnerships. In the twenty-first century it acquired an economic form in the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), announced during Xi Jinping's April 2015 visit and presented as a flagship of the Belt and Road Initiative. Initial announcements spoke of tens of billions of dollars in investment, with later totals cited as higher; actual delivery was lower and concentrated in energy projects, roads, and the port of Gwadar. Power plants built under CPEC eased Pakistan's electricity shortages, but they also created payment obligations to Chinese producers, which became a recurrent issue in Pakistan's negotiations with the International Monetary Fund. Projects in the north-south railway corridor (ML-1) progressed slowly. Attacks on Chinese nationals in Pakistan, including a March 2024 bombing that killed Chinese engineers, led Beijing to press for stronger security guarantees. Pakistan's military purchases from China, which supplied the majority of its major arms imports by the mid-2020s according to international arms-transfer data, deepened the relationship. China was also among Pakistan's principal creditors.

Beijing's influence in the other South Asian states followed different patterns. In Bangladesh, Chinese firms built infrastructure such as the Karnaphuli River tunnel, and China was the largest source of imports. Under Sheikh Hasina's government, which balanced Delhi and Beijing, Chinese financing was selective. After Hasina's fall in August 2024, the interim government and then the government of Tarique Rahman (from February 17, 2026) pursued closer relations. Rahman visited China from June 22 to 26, 2026, on his first major overseas tour, after Malaysia. Analysts at the Institute of South Asian Studies in Singapore reported that China pledged about US$300 million in grant assistance and that the two sides agreed to explore a foreign and defense ministers' dialogue; Bangladesh had also asked China to finance the Teesta River project. Dhaka's officials described the policy as "Bangladesh First," not an alignment with Beijing, but the agreements raised questions in Delhi.

Nepal had signed a Belt and Road framework in 2017 and a further framework for cooperation in December 2024; implementation was limited by Nepal's concerns about loans and by the difficulty of building in mountainous terrain. The government formed after the March 2026 election, led by Balendra Shah and including Foreign Minister Shishir Khanal, described a balanced policy of avoiding dependence on any neighbor and reviewing China-funded projects individually. Sri Lanka borrowed heavily from Chinese lenders for infrastructure, including the Hambantota port (leased to a Chinese company in 2017), and its 2022 default (110.16) made China's role in restructuring a major issue; President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, elected in 2024, visited both India (December 2024) and China (January 2025), reflecting a policy of avoiding exclusive alignment. Maldives developed a free-trade arrangement and infrastructure links with China, and President Mohamed Muizzu's government, elected in 2023 after a campaign critical of Indian military presence, moved toward China before re-engaging with India through financial support in 2024–2025 (110.8, 110.12).

Bhutan is a distinctive case. It has no formal diplomatic relations with China, has negotiated its northern and western boundaries with Beijing across many rounds since the 1980s, and has a special treaty relationship with India, revised in 2007 to remove a provision requiring India's guidance in foreign policy. The Doklam crisis of 2017 occurred in territory claimed by Bhutan. Thimphu's boundary diplomacy with China, including reported progress on a framework in the 2020s, has therefore been watched closely in Delhi, while Bhutan's hydropower exports and economic ties tie it closely to India.

The consequences of Chinese infrastructure finance have generated an argument that students should treat as an open historical question. One interpretation, often called the "debt-trap" thesis, holds that China uses loans to gain strategic assets. The Hambantota lease is its most cited example. Scholarly studies of Sri Lanka's debt, however, have found that Chinese lenders held a minority share of external debt and that most borrowing came from private bond markets and other creditors; the lease was concluded as part of a commercial arrangement to relieve debt payments on the port, not as seizure after default. Other analysts emphasize genuine risks: project loans on commercial terms, limited transparency, security guarantees, and the leverage that large creditors acquire during crises. The evidence supports caution toward both simple claims. What seems clear is that smaller states were not passive. They chose projects, bargained among lenders, canceled or delayed proposals, and used Chinese and Indian offers against each other.

Section 110.5

United States–India Relations: From Estrangement to Strategic Partnership

For most of the Cold War the United States and India were distant. Washington armed Pakistan and distrusted Indian nonalignment; India signed a treaty of peace and friendship with the Soviet Union in 1971 and bought Soviet weapons (Chapter 92). The end of the Cold War removed the main ideological barrier, but the nuclear tests of May 1998 brought sanctions and a new crisis. The response, unusually, was sustained negotiation. Strobe Talbott and Jaswant Singh held a long series of talks from 1998 to 2000, and President Clinton's visit in March 2000 signaled that Washington now regarded India as a major partner. The Bush administration, which took office in 2001, accelerated the change: it lifted most sanctions after September 2001 and, in January 2004, the two governments announced the "Next Steps in Strategic Partnership."

The civil nuclear initiative was the central achievement. India had been excluded from international nuclear commerce since 1974 because it possessed weapons and had not signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty. On July 18, 2005, Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and President George W. Bush issued a joint statement in which the United States agreed to work toward full civil nuclear cooperation, and India agreed to separate its civil and military facilities, place the civil ones under International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards, and continue its moratorium on nuclear testing. Congress passed the Henry J. Hyde United States–India Peaceful Atomic Energy Cooperation Act in December 2006. After the IAEA approved an India-specific safeguards agreement in August 2008, the Nuclear Suppliers Group agreed on September 6, 2008 to a waiver permitting trade with India, and the bilateral 123 Agreement was signed on October 10, 2008. The settlement was politically costly in India: the Left parties withdrew support from Singh's coalition in 2008 and critics argued that it compromised sovereignty. Commercial results were modest, since India's 2010 nuclear liability law deterred American suppliers, but the diplomatic effect was large.

Security cooperation followed. India was designated a "Major Defense Partner" in 2016 and signed foundational military agreements with the United States: on logistics support (2016), secure communications (2018), and geospatial data (2020). The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue, an informal consultation among India, the United States, Japan, and Australia first convened in 2007, was revived in 2017 and elevated to leaders' meetings in 2021. The concept of the "Indo-Pacific," linking the Indian and Pacific oceans, gave a shared strategic frame in which India's location was central. A new ten-year Framework for the U.S.–India Major Defense Partnership, signed on October 31, 2025, continued the trend; the signing itself did not mean that every planned cooperation measure had been implemented. Trade and technology ties also grew, and the Indian-American community became an important constituency (110.17).

Yet the partnership remained one of converging interests and recurring disputes, not alliance. India declined to join American-led operations, bought Russian weapons, and refused to condemn Russia's invasion of Ukraine (110.7). In 2025 the relationship deteriorated sharply over trade: the Trump administration imposed tariffs on Indian goods that reached 50 percent, including an additional penalty linked to India's purchases of Russian oil, and relations between the two governments cooled. On February 6, 2026, the two governments announced a framework for an interim trade agreement; the American fact sheet said tariffs on Indian goods would fall to 18 percent and described a commitment by India to stop buying Russian oil, while Indian statements emphasized market access and protection of sensitive sectors. On February 20 the US Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. By mid-November 2026 the agreement had not been formalized: American negotiators visited India in early November, and Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said on November 15 that the United States had agreed to give Indian goods preferential access relative to competitors. These are statements of negotiating positions; the final terms remained unknown at the cutoff.

Historical Debate

Can India Maintain Strategic Autonomy While Developing Closer Security Partnerships?

The question: As India deepened defense and technology ties with the United States, did it preserve independent decision-making, or did partnership gradually constrain its choices?

Interpretation A — Autonomy preserved: India accepted foundational military agreements without an alliance, kept buying Russian weapons and oil, joined BRICS and the SCO, and resisted pressure on Ukraine. The tariff dispute of 2025 showed that Delhi would bear costs rather than yield on matters it considered core. On this view the partnership is a hedge, one of several.
Interpretation B — Autonomy narrowed: India's dependence on American markets, technology, and diplomatic goodwill gave Washington leverage, which the 2025–2026 tariff negotiations demonstrated: the framework of February 2026 was reported by the American government to include limits on Russian oil purchases. Closer military ties and the China competition reduced India's practical alternatives.

Evidence: Both sides cite the same episodes: arms purchases and oil import figures, public statements by officials, and the content of agreements. Evidence is limited by the fact that negotiations were incomplete and that governments describe agreements differently.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians describe India's place in the world, as an independent pole, a partner of the United States, or a state whose independence is conditioned by circumstance.

Student task: Identify two additional kinds of evidence, beyond official statements, that would help decide between the interpretations.

The central historical problem is why two states could build a strategically important relationship without an alliance. Part of the answer lies in India's tradition of independence and its sensitivity to dependence; part lies in American willingness to accept partnership on those terms because of the China competition; and part in the structure of the relationship itself, which rests on commerce, technology, education, and people as well as security. The same structure explains its volatility, since disagreements over trade or Russia could be sharp without destroying the underlying ties.

Section 110.6

United States Relations with Pakistan, Bangladesh, and the Wider Region

Pakistan's relationship with the United States was historically closer than India's: Pakistan joined the Cold War alliances SEATO and CENTO and was the conduit for American support to the Afghan resistance in the 1980s (Chapter 95). In 1990 the United States cut assistance because of Pakistan's nuclear program, and relations remained poor until September 11, 2001. President Pervez Musharraf then joined the American-led campaign against the Taliban, Pakistan received substantial military and economic aid, and it was designated a major non-NATO ally in 2004. The cooperation was always uneasy. Washington accused Pakistan's intelligence services of tolerating the Afghan Taliban; Pakistan accused the United States of ignoring its casualties and sovereignty. Drone strikes in Pakistan's tribal areas, the American raid that killed Osama bin Laden at Abbottabad in May 2011, and the NATO strike at Salala in November 2011 produced crises, and the Trump administration suspended security assistance in 2018. After the American withdrawal from Afghanistan in August 2021, relations had little shared agenda.

They revived in different forms from 2025. Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir, was received in Washington in June 2025, and President Trump publicly praised Pakistan's role following the India–Pakistan conflict of May 2025 (Chapter 105), a claim India rejected insofar as it implied American mediation. Pakistan reached a trade arrangement with the United States and discussed cooperation on minerals and technology. Its most visible role came after the US–Israeli attack on Iran began on February 28, 2026. Pakistan, which borders Iran, declared neutrality, condemned the strikes, and offered to host talks; it brokered a two-week ceasefire announced on April 7–8 and hosted direct United States–Iran negotiations in Islamabad on April 11–12. On June 18, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced an electronically signed Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding between the United States and Iran, with Pakistan acting as mediator; the document and announcement are established, but hostilities and disruption of Hormuz shipping resumed in later months, and by the cutoff the conflict's outcome was not settled. Pakistan's mediation raised its diplomatic profile, and Indian commentary described it as a setback for Delhi. The distinction between prominence and enduring influence should be maintained: Pakistan remained dependent on IMF support, Gulf finance, and Chinese backing (110.16).

For Bangladesh, the United States was the largest single export market for ready-made garments, though it did not grant duty-free access for them. American policy emphasized democratic governance and labor standards: Washington sanctioned the Rapid Action Battalion in December 2021 and imposed visa restrictions linked to elections in 2023, which Sheikh Hasina's government resented. After Hasina's fall in 2024 the relationship was rebuilt with the interim government, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio congratulated Tarique Rahman's government after the February 2026 election; observers described American interest as motivated in part by competition with China. Tariffs imposed in 2025 affected Bangladeshi exporters, and trade negotiations were part of the new government's diplomacy.

In the smaller states, American engagement was shaped by development assistance, security cooperation, and competition. Nepal approved a Millennium Challenge Corporation grant compact worth about US$500 million in 2022 after a bitter domestic debate over sovereignty; Sri Lanka had earlier declined such a compact. The United States assisted Sri Lanka and Maldives with maritime security and humanitarian response, while Bhutan has no formal diplomatic relations with Washington. These governments evaluated American offers by their own priorities: Nepal's debates concerned sovereignty, Sri Lanka's concerned neutrality, and Maldives' focused on security and finance.

Comparison shows that American policy treated South Asian states unevenly, with India as the principal partner, Pakistan as an intermittent partner on security and mediation, and the others as recipients of aid and diplomacy. The states in turn used Washington selectively. Pakistan sought American markets and diplomatic recognition without surrendering its relationship with China; Bangladesh sought exports and investment; and the smaller states sought assistance without commitments. American tariffs and changing priorities in 2025–2026 also reminded governments that a patron's attention could shift.

Section 110.7

Russia and South Asia: Historical Strategic Ties in a Changing World

The Indo-Soviet relationship was one of the pillars of India's Cold War foreign policy. The 1971 Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation, concluded on the eve of the Bangladesh War (Chapter 92), provided diplomatic backing; the Soviet Union supplied aircraft, tanks, and submarines on favorable terms; and projects such as steel plants and power stations built Indian industrial capacity. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 disrupted this system, ending rupee-ruble trade and creating disputes over debt and spare parts. The relationship was nevertheless rebuilt with the Russian Federation: a treaty in 1993, a strategic partnership declared in 2000, and an upgrade to a "Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership" in 2010. Russia remained a leading supplier of Indian military equipment, though its share declined as India diversified to France, Israel, and the United States.

Several enduring projects illustrate the relationship. The BrahMos cruise missile was developed through a joint venture. The Kudankulam nuclear power station in Tamil Nadu was built with Russian technology. India agreed to buy S-400 air-defense systems in 2018, with deliveries beginning in late 2021, in the face of American warnings of sanctions. These projects reflected mutual advantages: Indian access to technology without political conditions, and Russian need for markets. They also created dependence on Russian spare parts and servicing for Indian forces, which made rapid diversification difficult.

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022 forced a choice that India handled by avoiding one. Delhi abstained on several United Nations votes, called for dialogue and an end to violence, and declined to join sanctions. It also greatly increased purchases of discounted Russian crude oil, which had formed a very small share of its imports before the war and a large share, at its peak roughly a third or more, afterwards, according to trade data. Indian officials justified the purchases as protecting consumers from high prices and noted that European countries also imported Russian energy. Western governments criticized the purchases as helping finance the war, though American officials also acknowledged that Indian purchases helped keep global prices stable. Indian refiners resold refined products to third countries, and the trade made Russia one of India's largest suppliers. Sanctions imposed in October 2025 on major Russian oil companies led Indian refiners to reduce purchases; the American government asserted in February 2026 that India had committed to halt them as part of the trade framework (110.5). Indian officials' description was more cautious, and by the cutoff the actual volume of purchases was a matter on which reporting and official statements differed.

President Putin visited New Delhi in December 2025, and he attended the BRICS summit there in September 2026, holding a bilateral meeting with Prime Minister Modi. Such events demonstrate that India maintained its relationship with Russia while negotiating with Washington. They do not show an endorsement of Russian policy in Ukraine; India's public position called for peace talks and did not endorse the invasion. The relationship also had limits: Russia's growing dependence on China and its provision of military assistance to Pakistan in earlier decades affected Indian assessments, and trade other than oil and arms was modest.

Russia's relations with Pakistan changed in a more limited way. Pakistan and Russia had been estranged during the Afghan war but developed defense and energy discussions in the twenty-first century, including joint exercises and some trade after 2022. These ties did not displace India's established relationship, and Pakistan's principal external partners remained China, Saudi Arabia, and the United States. Russia, for its part, sought to maintain relations with both countries. The broader point is that Russia's role in South Asia declined from the Soviet period but remained significant for India's military equipment, nuclear energy, and diplomacy.

Section 110.8

Strategic Autonomy and the Foreign Policies of South Asia's Smaller States

The vocabulary used for India's foreign policy does not translate easily to the other states. Several concepts are worth distinguishing. Nonalignment refers to refusing alliances; neutrality is a legal status in war; balancing means allying with one power against another; hedging or diversification means keeping options open with several; and strategic autonomy describes the capacity for independent decision. South Asian states practiced different mixtures of these, according to size, geography, and domestic politics.

Pakistan pursued security through a close alliance with China, a defense arrangement with Saudi Arabia, and selective relations with the United States and Russia. On September 17, 2025, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman signed a Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement in Riyadh, providing that aggression against one would be treated as aggression against both; Pakistani and Saudi officials were still describing cooperation under it in 2026. The agreement matched Pakistan's long dependence on Gulf finance and labor markets (110.9–110.10) and added a security dimension. Its precise operational meaning, including any nuclear dimension, was not specified publicly and should not be asserted. Bangladesh under Tarique Rahman declared a "Bangladesh First" policy. His government expanded relations with China, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, and Türkiye, maintained contact with Washington, and described India as an essential neighbor; analysts at the Institute of South Asian Studies debated in September 2026 whether this was diversification or movement toward a new alignment, and uncertainty over his attendance at the New Delhi BRICS summit reflected the tension with Delhi over the extradition of Sheikh Hasina, who was in India.

Sri Lanka was both a recipient of Chinese infrastructure finance and a country that depended on India in its 2022 crisis, when Indian credit lines helped provide fuel and food. President Dissanayake's government, elected in 2024 from a party historically critical of India, visited both capitals and concluded security understandings with India in 2025 while stating that Sri Lankan territory would not be used against Indian interests. Nepal, landlocked and reliant on Indian transit, sought to balance India and China; the government of Prime Minister Balendra Shah, which took office in March 2026 after a youth-led political upheaval, promised a "bridge" role between the neighbors, and it objected to India–China arrangements over the Lipulekh pass, which Nepal claims. Bhutan combined deep economic and security ties with India and cautious boundary talks with China. Maldives, with an exposed island economy, alternated between Indian and Chinese partners: Muizzu's government demanded the withdrawal of Indian military personnel, then accepted Indian currency and credit support as its debt pressures grew, and hosted Prime Minister Modi in 2025.

Why would small states diversify even when geography imposed dependence? The reasons were domestic as well as strategic. Nationalism in Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Maldives often defined itself against Indian dominance, and governments faced electorates that rewarded assertions of independence. External partners offered alternative finance and markets, and competition among them increased the smaller states' bargaining power. Dependence nevertheless constrained them: Nepal's trade passed through Indian ports, Bhutan's hydropower was sold to India, Bangladesh's land borders were almost entirely with India, and Sri Lanka and Maldives needed Indian assistance in crises. Diversification was therefore a strategy for increasing leverage, not for escaping geography.

A comparative view also corrects the picture of South Asian politics as a Sino-Indian contest. Youth-led movements and electoral changes in Bangladesh and Nepal in 2024–2026 did not simply reorient them toward Beijing; they produced governments that publicly sought relations with India, China, the United States, and Gulf and Southeast Asian states. Analysts disagreed about the long-term direction, and the evidence by November 2026 was limited to a few months of government practice. What can be said is that each state's foreign policy responded to its own domestic politics and economic needs, and that the principal powers competed for attention more than they controlled outcomes.

Section 110.9

Gulf States, Energy Security, and South Asia's Western Connections

South Asia's relationship with the Gulf, the six Arab states of the Gulf Cooperation Council (Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, and Bahrain), rests on exchange across the Arabian Sea that is thousands of years old (Chapters 25, 38, and 49). Its modern form took shape after the 1970s oil boom, when Gulf states recruited South Asian workers on a vast scale (Chapter 97). Since 1991 the relationship has widened from labor and oil to finance, ports, logistics, and diplomacy. The Gulf became the main source of imported energy for South Asia, a major source of remittances, and a rising source of investment and financial support.

Energy dependence is the most visible link. India imports most of the crude oil it consumes, and a large share has come from Gulf producers such as Saudi Arabia, Iraq, and the UAE; Qatar has been the principal supplier of its liquefied natural gas. Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka also import oil, and Pakistan and Bangladesh depend on imported LNG. Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives obtain fuel largely through India or by sea. Fuel prices therefore affect trade balances, subsidies, inflation, and power supplies across the region. Governments responded with strategic reserves, long-term supply contracts, and diversification toward coal, hydropower, and solar power, but the dependence remained.

Economic partnership went beyond oil. India signed a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement with the UAE in 2022, and Emirati funds invested in Indian infrastructure and companies; Gulf port operators such as DP World developed terminals in India, and Abu Dhabi interests entered Pakistan's port sector. Saudi Arabia and the UAE repeatedly provided Pakistan with deposits, deferred-payment oil, and rollovers that helped it avoid default, sometimes alongside IMF programs, and in September 2025 Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a mutual defense agreement (110.8). Qatar, Oman, and Saudi Arabia also served as diplomatic intermediaries in West Asian conflicts, and in 2023 the proposed India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor was announced at the G20 summit in New Delhi, though its progress was slowed by conflict. Gulf governments cultivated India as an economic and political partner while also maintaining deep ties with Pakistan, an example of the diversification that South Asian governments themselves pursued.

The war that began on February 28, 2026, with American and Israeli strikes on Iran, demonstrated the region's exposure. Iran disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and attacked targets in Gulf states; the result was a global fuel shock and fuel shortages in parts of Asia. Pakistan raised administered energy prices, Nepal and Bangladesh faced shortages, and India, according to reporting, supplied diesel to Bangladesh in April at its request. The World Bank's April 2026 South Asia Economic Update lowered its forecast for regional growth in 2026 to 6.3 percent, from 7.0 percent in 2025, citing Middle East conflict and energy volatility, and a May 2026 World Bank analysis highlighted the combined exposure of South Asian economies to oil prices and to remittances from GCC countries. Ceasefires in April and a memorandum of understanding in June did not produce lasting normalization; the strait remained a source of risk in autumn, and in early November an Iranian parliamentarian asserted that ships linked to India and several other countries enjoyed an exemption, without independent confirmation of its precise scope or uniform implementation. The October 2026 World Bank update then raised its 2026 regional forecast to 6.9 percent, attributing resilience to domestic demand and remittances while warning about energy prices. The sequence illustrates an important distinction between an acute shock, such as a closed strait, and structural dependence, which remains after prices fall.

The Gulf crisis also affected relationships among South Asian states. Pakistan's mediation gave it new importance with Washington and Tehran, while India, which also maintained relations with Iran and Israel and the Gulf monarchies, avoided taking sides and supplied humanitarian medicine to Iran. Neither state controlled the underlying conflict, and both were affected by it. For historians the episode is most useful as evidence that the economic integration of South Asia with the Gulf was deeper than any political alignment, and that this integration created vulnerabilities as well as opportunities.

Section 110.10

South Asian Migration to the Gulf and the Global Labor Economy

Migration from South Asia to the Gulf is one of the largest temporary labor flows in the modern world. India's government told its parliament in December 2025 that more than nine million Indian migrant workers lived in Gulf countries, and Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal, and Sri Lanka also sent large numbers. Workers are employed in construction, domestic service, retail, transport, and health care. This section treats the institutional, diplomatic, and labor-governance aspects; the macroeconomic and demographic side belongs to Chapter 106.

The legal basis of this migration is temporary contract employment. Most Gulf states have not offered citizenship or permanent residence to the great majority of foreign workers, whose residence depends on employer sponsorship, known generally as the kafala system. Under it employers had wide control over workers' ability to change jobs or leave the country; recruitment agencies in South Asia and the Gulf charged fees that left many workers indebted; and contract substitution, wage withholding, and unsafe conditions were documented by international organizations and journalists. Reforms occurred unevenly. Qatar abolished exit permits and employer consent requirements for changing jobs and introduced a minimum wage in 2020 after international criticism, including controversy over deaths of workers connected with the 2022 World Cup, whose scale is disputed. Saudi Arabia introduced a labor reform initiative in 2021 that eased some restrictions, though enforcement and coverage varied. Observers disagree about how much practice changed, and the evidence is stronger for legal reforms than for their enforcement.

South Asian governments handled migration as both an economic resource and a diplomatic problem. India's Ministry of External Affairs regulated recruiters through the Protector of Emigrants system and an online portal, and the Indian government signed labor agreements with Gulf states. Nepal required permits and banned or restricted recruitment to certain countries and for certain categories; Bangladesh and Pakistan operated state recruitment bodies; Sri Lanka regulated domestic worker migration. Governments faced a dilemma: restricting departures could reduce abuse but also cut remittances and push workers into irregular channels, while heavy dependence on the Gulf reduced their leverage when workers were mistreated. Embassies evacuated nationals in crises, as India did from Iraq and Kuwait in 1990–91 and from conflict zones later, and the 2026 Iran war again placed consular protection at the center of diplomacy.

Everyday Life and Social History

Overseas Employment, Family Remittances, and the Effects of Diplomacy

A worker from Kerala, Uttar Pradesh, Sylhet, Punjab, or Nepal's hill districts often began by paying a recruiter, sometimes borrowing from relatives or moneylenders to do so. After arriving, the worker typically lived in employer-provided accommodation, worked long hours in a hot climate, and sent a large share of earnings home through bank transfers or money-transfer firms. For the family, remittances paid for food, school fees, medical care, debt repayment, housing, and sometimes the next migrant's recruitment fee. Villages with many migrants show new houses, changed marriage patterns, and households managed for years by women while men worked abroad.

Diplomacy affected these lives indirectly but sharply. A change in visa rules, a bilateral labor agreement, an evacuation, a conflict closing airspace, or a fall in Gulf construction could suddenly change whether a worker was paid or whether a family received money. During the 2026 war, concern about remittances and the safety of workers was widely discussed in South Asian capitals, and the World Bank noted that Nepal's reliance on remittances, about a quarter of its economy, made it especially exposed.

  2. Why might a government prefer not to restrict labor migration even when abuses occur?

Three terms must be distinguished. Temporary contractual employment describes the Gulf pattern, in which workers have no path to settlement. Permanent migration describes movement to countries such as the United States, Canada, Britain, or Australia, which offer residence and eventually citizenship, and attracts professionals and students (110.17). Citizenship is a legal membership that Gulf states rarely grant. The contrast explains differences in remittance behavior: temporary workers return or maintain households at home, while settled migrants may remit less regularly but invest, form organizations, and lobby. The World Bank estimated India as the world's largest recipient of remittances, at roughly US$137.7 billion in 2024, with the Gulf's share declining as flows from the United States, Britain, and other advanced economies increased; for Nepal, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, the Gulf remained the dominant source.

Gulf labor policies were also changing. Saudi Arabia and other states pursued "nationalization" of employment, seeking to replace foreign workers in some sectors with citizens, and Gulf governments diversified economies toward tourism, finance, and technology, which changed demand for skills. These developments, together with the war's disruption of Gulf economies in 2026, which the World Bank projected would reduce Gulf growth sharply, created uncertainty about the long-term future of South Asian labor demand. As of November 2026 the scale of any permanent change in migrant flows was not yet documented.

Section 110.11

The Indian Ocean: Trade Routes, Energy Corridors, and Geopolitical Geography

The Indian Ocean was for most of recorded history a zone of exchange. The monsoon winds that carried ships between the Red Sea, the Arabian coast, western India, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia (Chapter 25) enabled early commerce in cotton textiles, spices, pearls, and ivory; the Chola maritime expeditions (Chapter 33), Arab and Gujarati merchants (Chapters 38 and 49), and the Portuguese arrival of 1498 all belong to its history. Early modern European companies (Chapter 61) turned that commerce into territorial power, and the British Empire later treated the ocean as an imperial lake. The twenty-first century's traffic of container ships and tankers is a successor to these routes, although its scale and technology differ.

  1. Why does Sri Lanka's position make Colombo important to east–west shipping?
  2. Which South Asian states depend on the Strait of Hormuz, and which depend on the Strait of Malacca?
  3. How does geography explain Nepal's and Bhutan's dependence on India?
  4. Why might ports on the Bay of Bengal matter to Bangladesh's trade and to Indian northeastern connectivity?

Four maritime chokepoints dominate contemporary analysis. The Strait of Hormuz links the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea and carries a very large share of the world's oil and liquefied gas, including most of South Asia's Gulf imports. Bab el-Mandeb, at the southern end of the Red Sea, funnels traffic bound for the Suez Canal; attacks on shipping by Yemen's Houthi movement beginning in late 2023 caused many vessels to divert around Africa. The Strait of Malacca connects the Indian Ocean to the Pacific and carries energy and trade bound for East Asia, including much of China's oil. India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands lie near its western approach, which gives India a strategic position there. The Cape of Good Hope route is the alternative when Red Sea traffic is disrupted. For South Asian states, these straits are far more than lines on a map: closures raise insurance and freight costs, delay deliveries, and spread inflation.

Geography shapes the states differently. India has a long coastline and island territories, major ports, and growing transshipment ambitions; one example is Vizhinjam in Kerala, opened in 2025 as a deep-water container port intended to reduce Indian cargo's reliance on Colombo and foreign ports. Pakistan's coast is narrower and the country relies on Karachi and Port Qasim, with Gwadar a newer port. Bangladesh's trade passes through Chittagong, and its economy is bound to the Bay of Bengal. Sri Lanka sits along a major east–west route, and its Colombo terminal handles a large share of South Asian transshipment. Maldives, with a small population and large ocean territory, depends on tourism and on the security of surrounding waters. Nepal and Bhutan, landlocked, rely on India's ports and roads and on bilateral transit treaties.

The modern shipping system depended on institutions that were not visible on most strategic maps. Flag registration, port-state inspections, marine insurance, vessel classification, customs processing, and international conventions governed the movement of ships more continuously than dramatic naval crises did. A container leaving Bangladesh or western India might be carried by a ship registered in a third country, insured in another, and transshipped at a port outside South Asia before reaching its market. Consequently, control over a port or coastline was not equivalent to control over the shipping network. Reliability, turnaround times, deep-water berths, warehousing, and the availability of shipping services all influenced whether a port became an international hub. Political ambition alone could not create the regular traffic that made a shipping center commercially valuable.

The law of the sea supplied another layer of international order. Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, coastal states exercise different kinds of authority in territorial seas and exclusive economic zones, while merchant vessels retain internationally recognized navigation rights. India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Maldives had differing interests in delimitation, fishing, seabed resources, and maritime surveillance. Bangladesh's maritime boundary settlements with Myanmar in 2012 and India in 2014 illustrate how adjudication could clarify economic rights without removing every dispute over fisheries or enforcement. Such cases challenge the assumption that regional maritime relations consisted solely of naval competition. Legal bargaining, hydrographic surveys, commercial access, and search-and-rescue responsibilities also shaped the region's oceanic presence.

The 2026 disruption of Gulf shipping demonstrated how shocks propagated through these systems. A delayed tanker could affect fuel deliveries, domestic transport costs, electricity generation, and government subsidy bills far from the Strait of Hormuz. Shipping companies and insurers responded to risk assessments, while importers weighed alternative routes, sources, and inventories. Those choices differed across countries: energy import dependence was intense in some economies, while others were more exposed through employment and remittances from Gulf labor markets. An Iranian parliamentarian's reported assertion in early November that certain Indian-linked ships were exempt from restrictions illustrated the problem of evidence: neither the definition of an eligible vessel nor the uniform implementation of the claimed exemption was established by that statement alone. The maritime historian must therefore distinguish official claims, observed vessel movements, and the practical economic effects of disruption.

Students should separate three things that discussion of Indian Ocean "geopolitics" often combines. First, there are commercial shipping routes, in which private shipping lines carry most trade, and which are open to all flagged vessels under international law of the sea. Second, there are military responsibilities, in which navies patrol, deter piracy, and may seek access to ports. Third, there are territorial claims, which in the Indian Ocean mostly concern maritime boundaries and island sovereignty, and which South Asian states resolved in several cases by arbitration, such as Bangladesh's boundaries with Myanmar (2012) and India (2014). The ocean is not an arena of simple great-power seizure. It is a shared commercial space in which different states hold overlapping interests, and the next section considers how security and ports interact.

Section 110.12

Maritime Security, Ports, and the Strategic Role of Island States

Historical Vocabulary
Sea Lanes, Chokepoints, Strategic Ports, and Maritime Security

Sea lanes are the customary routes followed by merchant shipping. Chokepoints are narrow passages through which trade must pass. A strategic port is a harbor of commercial or military significance, but the term is often used loosely: a port may be commercially operated while analysts speculate about possible military use. Maritime security includes protection against piracy, smuggling, terrorism, and illegal fishing, as well as naval deterrence and disaster response.

Students should require evidence before accepting that a commercial port has a military function. Ownership, lease terms, actual traffic, and documented naval access are different matters.

South Asian navies grew and acquired wider missions after 1991. The Indian Navy became the region's largest, commissioning the carrier INS Vikrant, built in India, in 2022 and deploying ships for anti-piracy patrols in the Gulf of Aden from 2008, for protection of merchant shipping in the Red Sea and Arabian Sea after the Houthi attacks, and for humanitarian operations after cyclones and earthquakes. India established information-sharing arrangements, developed bases in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and used the term "SAGAR" (and later "MAHASAGAR") to describe a regional maritime partnership. Pakistan's navy pursued a more limited coastal and submarine strategy with Chinese-supplied vessels and held multinational exercises. Bangladesh, following its boundary settlements, developed interests in the Bay of Bengal's resources and in a "blue economy." Sri Lanka and Maldives lacked large navies but sat on key routes; they participated in the Colombo Security Conclave with India, and later with other Indian Ocean states, which addressed maritime security, terrorism, and trafficking.

Ports have been the subject of the sharpest claims. Hambantota in southern Sri Lanka was built largely with Chinese loans and underused after opening in 2010; in December 2017 a Chinese state-owned company took a 99-year lease on its operations for about US$1.1 billion, with proceeds used to service debt. Critics saw a strategic seizure; the Sri Lankan government retained sovereignty, and the port's main activity was commercial shipping and vehicle handling. Sri Lanka's government in 2022 allowed a Chinese research ship to dock there, over Indian objections, and later imposed a moratorium on such visits, showing the sensitivity of the issue. Colombo hosts terminals operated by Chinese, Indian (Adani), and local interests, and the Colombo Port City development is built on reclaimed land with Chinese investment. Gwadar in Balochistan is the centerpiece of CPEC; its traffic remained small and its operations were constrained by insecurity, though a new international airport opened. Statements that China would turn Gwadar or Hambantota into naval bases have been made repeatedly by analysts, but as of November 2026 no verified permanent Chinese military base existed in either port, and such claims should be treated as speculation about possible futures.

Island states exercised influence disproportionate to their size by choosing partners. Maldives' 2023 election turned partly on whether Indian military personnel, who operated helicopters and aircraft for medical evacuation, should remain; Muizzu's government replaced them with civilians by May 2024, accepted a Chinese research vessel's visit in 2024, and then, facing debt pressures, secured Indian financial assistance, including a currency swap and treasury-bill rollovers, and invited Modi in July 2025 for Independence Day celebrations and a new Indian line of credit. Sri Lanka, similarly, sought Indian support in 2022 and Chinese debt restructuring, while the Dissanayake government made security undertakings to India. Such episodes show that small states bargained rather than submitted, though their bargaining power diminished when they were financially distressed.

Port financing also requires distinctions often lost in the language of strategic rivalry. Ownership of operating equipment, a concession to manage a terminal, a long-term lease, and sovereign control of surrounding territory are legally and operationally different. A state may grant a commercial concession without surrendering the power to regulate customs, navigation, policing, or military entry. Conversely, debt burdens or dependence on a single commercial operator can influence future negotiations even without a naval installation. The evidence relevant to a disputed project thus includes the concession contract, port throughput, vessel-call records, debt terms, host-country legislation, and any documented military access. These sources offer a more reliable test than the simple description of every Chinese-financed port as a military base or every Indian-sponsored facility as an instrument of political control.

Cooperation also existed. Navies participated in joint exercises with several powers, rescued mariners, and coordinated disaster response after cyclones and the 2004 tsunami (Chapter 97). Such cooperation did not remove rivalry, but it illustrated that maritime space was shared. Historians should therefore treat the Indian Ocean as a region where commerce, security, and finance interacted, with South Asian states acting as participants whose choices mattered, not as passive stages for great-power competition.

Section 110.13

South Asia in Global Trade, Investment, and Supply Chains

After 1991 South Asian economies became more connected to global production and finance, but they specialized in different ways. Domestic growth is the subject of Chapter 106; here the question is how each economy was tied to external markets and what bargaining power that gave it. India's most visible link was services: software, back-office work, engineering, and, later, global capability centers established by multinational companies. These activities depended on access to American and European clients and on the mobility of Indian professionals. India also became a major exporter of pharmaceuticals and, from the 2020s, of smartphones, as firms diversified assembly outside China, though components often still came from China, a dependence discussed in 110.3. It remained outside the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership in 2019, after concerns about Chinese imports, and instead negotiated bilateral and small-group agreements: with the UAE and Australia (2022), the European Free Trade Association states, the United Kingdom (signed in 2025), New Zealand, and the European Union, for which negotiations were formally concluded on January 27, 2026, although signature, domestic approval, and entry into force remained separate steps at the research cutoff.

Bangladesh's integration came through garments. Ready-made garments made up the large majority of its exports, and the country became one of the world's largest apparel exporters, employing millions of workers, most of them women (Chapter 102). Exporters depended on preferential access to the European Union and other markets that granted duty-free entry to least developed countries, which is why the deferral of its graduation from that category, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on October 29, 2026, mattered: the new date is November 24, 2029. Business groups had warned that immediate graduation could reduce exports by a significant percentage, and the government used the request for time as a form of trade diplomacy. Pakistan's exports were dominated by textiles and rice; it benefited from the European Union's GSP+ scheme and relied on Chinese, American, and Gulf markets, but its low export share relative to its economy was a long-standing weakness. Sri Lanka depended on apparel, tea, and tourism and, through Colombo, on port services. Nepal and Bhutan exported electricity and few other goods; Maldives depended on tourism for most of its foreign earnings.

External shocks repeatedly changed these positions. The global financial crisis of 2008 reduced demand; COVID-19 in 2020 disrupted garment orders, remittances, and supply chains, and revealed dependence on Chinese inputs; the Ukraine war and the 2026 Gulf war raised fuel and fertilizer prices. Tariff policy in the United States after 2025 produced the greatest uncertainty for exporters. Washington imposed tariffs on many South Asian goods, with India facing the highest rate at one stage, and in 2026 the legal basis for some tariffs changed when the Supreme Court ruled on February 20 against those imposed under emergency powers; reports in autumn noted that the administration was pursuing other legal authorities and that several trade probes complicated negotiations. For South Asian exporters the lesson was that market access rested on policy instruments that could change with little notice.

Investment relationships were similarly shifting. Foreign direct investment in India rose from small amounts in the early 1990s to large annual flows, though restrictions on investors from neighboring countries and concern about regulatory predictability limited some inflows. The Gulf funds, Japanese and Singaporean investors, and American technology firms all expanded their presence. In smaller economies, investment was often tied to infrastructure and was strategically sensitive: Chinese, Indian, Japanese, and multilateral lenders competed to finance ports, power plants, and bridges. Throughout, bargaining power depended on the size of the domestic market, the availability of alternatives, and the state of public finances. India's large market provided leverage in negotiations that Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, or Nepal could not match, which is one reason smaller states sought multilateral protections such as preferential access.

The conclusion of trade negotiations had to be distinguished from legally enforceable market access. The India–EU agreement announced on January 27, 2026 followed years of talks over tariff schedules, technical standards, intellectual property, and market access; at the November cutoff its implementation was not equivalent to that of a treaty already in force. The same distinction applied to the February 2026 United States–India framework, which announced intentions but left important provisions under negotiation. Exporters therefore faced several overlapping realities: current tariffs under existing law, preferential treatment already implemented, and proposed future preferences dependent on additional approvals. A historical comparison should not count all three categories as completed liberalization.

A comparison therefore shows both integration and asymmetry. South Asian economies were deeply embedded in the global economy and highly exposed to it. The World Bank's October 2026 update noted that the region had limited involvement in the fast-growing supply chains for artificial-intelligence-related goods, even while its services and consumer markets grew. Participation in global value chains was thus uneven, concentrated in a few products, and dependent on external partners whose policies the region's governments could not control.

Section 110.14

SAARC, SAFTA, BIMSTEC, and the Limits of South Asian Regional Integration

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation was established at a summit in Dhaka in December 1985, at the initiative of Bangladesh's president Ziaur Rahman, with seven members (Afghanistan joined in 2007). Its charter excluded bilateral and contentious issues from its agenda, a provision that reflected the founders' awareness of India–Pakistan hostility and also guaranteed that the organization could not address the main obstacle to cooperation. SAARC created centers, conventions on terrorism and narcotics, and a university, but its economic ambitions were modest. A preferential trading arrangement (SAPTA) came into effect in 1995, and the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA), signed at the Islamabad summit on January 6, 2004, entered into force on January 1, 2006.

Historical Vocabulary
Regional Integration, Free Trade, Preferential Tariffs, and Non-Tariff Barriers

Regional integration is the process by which neighboring states reduce barriers to the movement of goods, services, capital, and people. A free-trade area removes tariffs among members while each retains its own external tariffs. Preferential tariffs are lower rates given to partners. Sensitive lists exempt products from liberalization. Non-tariff barriers, such as testing requirements, licensing, customs delays, and restrictions at land crossings, can be as important as tariffs.

A treaty can establish an institution without producing integration; students should look for implemented tariff reductions, trade volumes, and functioning border facilities.

The results were limited. The World Bank has described South Asia as among the world's least integrated regions, with trade among its countries a small fraction of their total trade, compared with a much higher proportion in East Asia. Sensitive lists, non-tariff barriers, and poor transport links reduced the effect of tariff cuts, and informal trade across borders was substantial but unrecorded. Political relations were decisive. After a terrorist attack at Uri in September 2016, India and other members withdrew from the planned Islamabad SAARC summit, which was cancelled; no leaders' summit has been held since 2014. Trade between India and Pakistan, which India had granted most-favored-nation status in 1996 without reciprocity, was curtailed: after the Pulwama attack in February 2019 India withdrew that status and imposed a prohibitive duty, and Pakistan suspended trade in August 2019 after India's constitutional changes in Kashmir (Chapter 105). Further closures in 2025 left official trade negligible.

The origins of SAARC show that its weakness was not inevitable from the outset. Bangladesh's initiative gave smaller states a forum in which they could meet India and Pakistan on a formally equal footing and cooperate on education, agriculture, health, and disaster management. Summits, ministerial meetings, technical committees, and a secretariat in Kathmandu offered a durable institutional architecture. Nevertheless, the organization was deliberately cautious about bilateral disputes, and its rules depended heavily on consensus. That design made agreement possible where interests overlapped but left members with limited mechanisms for overcoming a determined objection. The contrast with regional bodies possessing stronger commercial or adjudicatory institutions helps explain why a forum can survive formally while its most visible political processes become intermittent.

SAFTA's tariff commitments were only part of the problem. Governments retained sensitive lists excluding large numbers of products, while rules of origin, product standards, licensing procedures, testing requirements, banking restrictions, and land-border congestion affected trade that nominally qualified for lower tariffs. An exporter could therefore face a favorable published tariff and still find it costly or impracticable to deliver goods across the nearest frontier. Informal commerce and third-country routing sometimes developed precisely because direct trade channels were obstructed. The World Bank's widely cited estimate that intraregional trade represented only about five percent of South Asia's total trade is a useful indicator of the structural problem, but it should not be mistaken for a precise constant applying equally to every year and trading partner.

The suspension of the SAARC summit process illustrated the dependence of regional institutions on wider politics. The proposed Islamabad summit of 2016 was postponed after India and several other members declined to attend amid severe political tension, and no subsequent leaders' summit had restored a regular rhythm by 2026. That paralysis did not mean every SAARC-associated activity ended: technical exchanges and affiliated organizations could continue at varying levels, but the organization lacked the political momentum to negotiate an ambitious common market. Nor was rivalry the only constraint. Relative economic size, competing domestic industries, weak transport links, and concerns about security or transit rights also limited the willingness of governments to remove barriers.

BIMSTEC and smaller corridor agreements offered alternative strategies. Because BIMSTEC links the Bay of Bengal with parts of Southeast Asia and does not include Pakistan, its members could pursue cooperation on energy, connectivity, and security without the same bilateral veto point. Yet broader membership and ambitious charters were not substitutes for functioning customs agreements, funded transport projects, or predictable dispute resolution. The growth of overlapping forums is best understood as institutional experimentation: governments assembled narrower combinations of states around particular tasks, while preserving diplomatic room to withdraw when domestic objections intensified. By 2026 the central lesson was not that South Asians had rejected regional cooperation, but that practical cooperation often advanced in smaller arrangements rather than through a single comprehensive bloc.

Alternatives proliferated. The Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC), founded in 1997 and joined by Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Thailand, excludes Pakistan and links South Asia with Southeast Asia. It adopted a charter in 2022 and held a summit in Bangkok in April 2025, but a free-trade agreement under negotiation since 2004 remained unfinished, and its secretariat was small. Other arrangements included the Bangladesh–Bhutan–India–Nepal (BBIN) initiative on connectivity (110.15), the Colombo Security Conclave, and the Indian Ocean Rim Association. India also negotiated bilateral agreements, such as its free-trade agreement with Sri Lanka (2000) and arrangements with Nepal and Bhutan. These overlapping groupings suggest that governments preferred flexible coalitions of the willing to a single organization in which Pakistan or India could veto action.

Historical Debate

Does Economic Integration Require Political Reconciliation?

The question: Can South Asia integrate economically while India–Pakistan and other political disputes persist, or must political reconciliation come first?

Interpretation A — Politics first: SAARC's failure and the collapse of India–Pakistan trade show that security rivalry blocks integration. Trade requires predictability, and governments that fear one another will not allow transit or open markets. Reconciliation must precede deep economic cooperation.
Interpretation B — Economics can lead: Subregional projects among willing states, such as BBIN and BIMSTEC, and bilateral transit and electricity trade, show that cooperation can advance without Pakistan, and that economic interests may in time alter political incentives. Europe's early integration and ASEAN's growth despite disputes are cited as precedents.

Evidence: Trade statistics, the history of cancelled summits, and the results of subregional arrangements, which have progressed but unevenly, are cited by both sides. The analogy with other regions is contested because South Asia's geography and power asymmetry differ.

Why it matters: The answer determines whether regional institutions should be understood as failures of design or products of political conditions, and what kinds of reform could be effective.

Student task: Choose one subregional initiative and assess whether its progress supports A or B.

Section 110.15

Regional Connectivity, Transport Corridors, and Economic Cooperation

Geographic proximity has not automatically produced extensive regional commerce, and the reason lies partly in the way infrastructure, procedures, and politics interact. Many of South Asia's land borders were drawn in 1947 across integrated economies (Chapter 86): rail lines were cut, river routes interrupted, and the movement of goods became subject to customs, permits, and security checks. Seventy years later, goods crossing even the busiest borders faced slow processing, limited warehousing, and restrictions on vehicles. Trade between neighbors often cost more than trade with distant partners.

The most successful connections have been bilateral. India and Bangladesh restored rail links closed since 1965, opened new border crossings, and signed agreements allowing the use of Bangladeshi ports for Indian cargo; the Akhaura–Agartala rail link opened in 2023. Electricity trade grew: Bhutan's hydropower exports to India became its main source of revenue, and Nepal signed a long-term agreement in 2024 to export up to 10,000 megawatts to India over a decade, with a small quantity sent through India to Bangladesh from November 2024. Nepal's access to the sea relied on transit through Kolkata-Haldia and Visakhapatnam, and railways to Kathmandu remained under planning. These arrangements show that cooperation is possible when interests align, and also that it depends on Indian territory, so that the neighbors' bargaining over tariffs, prices, and transit rights is shaped by asymmetry.

Subregional frameworks sought to multiply these links. The BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement, signed in 2015, was intended to allow vehicles to cross borders among the four countries, but Bhutan's parliament did not approve it, and implementation proceeded through protocols between smaller groups. The South Asia Subregional Economic Cooperation program supported by the Asian Development Bank promoted roads, energy, and trade facilitation. India's "Neighbourhood First" policy and its development credit lines financed railways, roads, ports, and power links across the region. Chinese and Japanese financing also supported projects, and competition among lenders sometimes accelerated them and sometimes made them subjects of political dispute. In Pakistan, connections with India were largely closed; with Afghanistan, transit trade and border crossings were repeatedly disrupted, including by closures after clashes in late 2025; and Pakistan's own corridors with China and Central Asia depended on security conditions. The India–Middle East–Europe corridor and India's Chabahar project in Iran, intended to open a route to Afghanistan and Central Asia, were slowed by war and sanctions in West Asia.

Cause and Effect

Why Has Proximity Not Produced Regional Economic Integration?

Long-term causes

Partition cut transport networks and markets; mistrust between India and Pakistan; asymmetric size, with India bordering most other states; import-substitution policies that discouraged trade before the 1990s.

Short-term causes

Terrorist attacks and military crises that led to cancelled summits and closed crossings; non-tariff barriers; limited border infrastructure; periodic closures.

Immediate and long-term effects

Low intraregional trade; reliance on distant markets; informal trade; smaller states' diversification toward China, the Gulf, and Southeast Asia; fragmented subregional projects.

Historical complexity

Where interests aligned, as in hydropower, rail restoration, and some road links, cooperation advanced. Barriers were political, procedural, and physical, and removing one did not remove the others.

Student reflection: Which of these causes could be changed by policy within a decade, and which are structural?

Landlocked Nepal and Bhutan illustrate the political economy of transit particularly clearly. Their importers relied on overland access to Indian transport networks and seaports, so customs processing, storage charges, rail access, road reliability, and the maintenance of political confidence affected the actual price of international commerce. A legal transit entitlement was less useful if the route was interrupted or unpredictable. Bangladesh's links to India's northeastern states offered a different bargain: shorter routes might benefit traders on both sides, yet governments still had to agree on fees, standards, vehicle permits, and security procedures. These cases demonstrate that cross-border infrastructure produces benefits only when accompanied by institutions capable of making routine movement reliable.

Energy connectivity created further opportunities and tensions. Cross-border electricity exchanges could connect hydropower producers with larger markets, smooth shortages, and provide export revenue; they also required transmission investment, long-term purchase contracts, and agreement on price and dispatch. Regional rail and road initiatives faced parallel questions about maintenance responsibility and distribution of gains. A corridor advertised as a regional public good might reduce costs for one territory while concentrating employment, land acquisition burdens, or political risk in another. Assessing it therefore requires evidence of operating capacity and actual traffic, not only a route map or the value of announced construction contracts.

Disputes over transit rights and finance reflected deeper concerns. Landlocked states wanted guaranteed access; India worried about security through its narrow northeastern corridor; Bangladesh weighed the benefits of transit fees against sovereignty concerns; and the cost of infrastructure raised questions about who would repay and who would control. The relationship between commercial projects and geopolitical concerns was especially evident in ports, pipelines, and power lines, where every project was interpreted as favoring one external power. The historian's task is to separate the commercial viability of a project from the political interpretations placed upon it, and to observe how often states accepted projects when they reduced costs and delayed them when they appeared to reduce autonomy.

Section 110.16

International Finance, Development Institutions, and Economic Vulnerability

South Asian governments relied on international financial institutions at moments of crisis and in long-term development. India turned to the International Monetary Fund in 1991 and drew on its resources until 1993; it has not borrowed from the Fund since, and it later became a significant shareholder and borrower in development banks. Pakistan, by contrast, entered a long series of IMF programs, with about two dozen arrangements since 1958; its most recent, a US$7 billion Extended Fund Facility approved in September 2024, was accompanied by a climate-resilience facility and reviews during 2026 that raised questions about tax revenue and power-sector tariffs. Bangladesh agreed to an IMF program in early 2023, during a balance-of-payments squeeze; Nepal and others had programs with the Fund. These agreements carried conditionality: commitments to raise revenue, adjust prices, reform state enterprises, and maintain exchange-rate flexibility. Their consequences were disputed, with supporters seeing stabilization and critics seeing austerity that burdened the poor.

Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis is the clearest example of financial vulnerability. Reserves had been depleted after years of borrowing in international bond markets, tax cuts in 2019, the pandemic's loss of tourism revenue, and fertilizer and exchange-rate policies that reduced agricultural output. The government suspended payment on external debt in April 2022, shortages of fuel, food, and medicine followed, and mass protests forced President Gotabaya Rajapaksa to resign in July (Chapter 103). India provided credit lines and assistance of several billion dollars, an important contribution to short-term relief. In March 2023 the IMF approved an arrangement of about US$2.9 billion after Sri Lanka obtained financing assurances from India, China, and other creditors. Restructuring negotiations with official creditors and bondholders took years and were concluded in 2024. The World Bank's October 2026 reporting described Sri Lanka's recovery as uneven, with continuing household hardship, so that macroeconomic stabilization did not translate quickly into social recovery.

The development banks played different roles. The World Bank financed social programs and infrastructure and, through reports such as its biannual South Asia Economic Update, shaped policy debate. The Asian Development Bank, founded in 1966, was a major lender for roads, energy, and regional projects. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, established in 2016 on China's initiative with India as a prominent shareholder and borrower, showed that Indian officials could cooperate with Chinese-led institutions while competing with Chinese bilateral policy. Bilateral lenders, including Japan, India, China, and Gulf governments, financed ports, power, and rail, and in times of balance-of-payments stress provided deposits and credit lines. Smaller states often combined sources: Bangladesh with Japan, China, India, and the multilateral banks; Nepal with the Asian Development Bank, the World Bank, India, and China; Maldives with India, China, and Gulf lenders.

Distinctions among types of finance matter. Emergency assistance addresses a crisis of payments and is brief; long-term development lending finances projects and reforms; infrastructure finance may be commercial or concessional and may carry political significance; and private foreign investment brings capital but can reverse quickly. Debt sustainability depends on interest rates, exchange rates, export earnings, and revenue, not only on the quantity of debt. The 2022 interest-rate increases in advanced economies and the 2026 energy shock raised the cost of servicing external obligations, which is why a region that was among the world's fastest growing nonetheless contained economies in periodic distress. Maldives faced heavy foreign-currency obligations in the mid-2020s, Pakistan remained dependent on rollovers, and Bangladesh's 2026 outlook was affected by weak banking-sector conditions and rising poverty, according to World Bank reporting.

The chronology of Sri Lanka's restructuring shows the importance of classifying institutions correctly. The G20 Common Framework was designed for particular eligible low-income debtor countries and did not apply to Sri Lanka, a middle-income country. After its 2022 default, Sri Lanka instead engaged official creditors through a specially coordinated process and negotiated separately with other bilateral lenders and international bondholders. An IMF working paper published in 2025 describes the resulting coordination challenges. This distinction matters because the existence of a global debt forum does not guarantee a uniform legal pathway for every distressed borrower. Creditors differed in the claims they held, the relief they could offer, and their willingness to coordinate, while domestic fiscal reforms imposed costs on households before the full benefits of stabilization could emerge.

Growth did not eliminate vulnerability. High regional growth projections, 6.9 percent for 2026 in the October update, coexisted with energy and food-price exposure, dependence on remittances, small tax bases, and limited fiscal space. For historians the key point is that financial institutions were arenas of bargaining as well as sources of money. Governments negotiated terms, appealed to creditors, played lenders against one another, and in some cases defaulted, and each choice changed their relationships with external powers.

Section 110.17

Diaspora Networks, International Migration, and Transnational Influence

South Asians abroad form one of the largest diasporas in the world, and their role is institutional as well as demographic: they connect labor markets, universities, firms, and political systems. Students should first distinguish layers. The oldest diasporic communities were created by colonial migration (Chapter 75): indentured laborers who went to Mauritius, Fiji, Guyana, Trinidad, and South Africa; merchants in East Africa and Southeast Asia; and soldiers and clerks across the empire. Postwar migration brought South Asians to Britain, and after 1965 changes in American immigration law brought professionals to the United States. A third layer, the Gulf labor migration discussed in 110.10, is mostly temporary. A fourth, newer layer consists of students and professionals moving to Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Germany, and the United States. By the early 2020s the United Nations counted India as the origin of the world's largest number of international migrants, around eighteen million people, with Pakistan, Bangladesh, and others also among major sources.

Diasporas contribute in several ways. Entrepreneurs and executives of South Asian origin lead major technology and financial firms, and Indian-American households have among the highest median incomes of any ethnic group in the United States. Students pay fees and, after graduation, either remain in labor markets abroad or return with skills and networks. Scientists and physicians maintain ties to home institutions. Hometown associations, religious organizations, and charities direct philanthropy to villages and disaster relief. Remittances, whose scale was noted in 110.10, are the most measurable contribution, though they come from varied sources and occupations. These networks help explain why successive Indian governments, from the creation of Pravasi Bharatiya Divas in 2003 and the Overseas Citizenship of India card in 2005 onward, treated the diaspora as a resource in foreign policy.

Analysis: The data show scale and distribution. They cannot show who the migrants are, how they are treated, how families spend funds, or what political relationships result. They also record formal flows only, and the figures for 2026 were preliminary at the cutoff.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the dataset measure, and what does it omit?
  2. Who produced the data, and for what purposes might governments and institutions use it?
  3. What can historians infer about relationships between countries from remittance patterns?
  4. What can they not establish without other kinds of sources?

Diaspora politics has diplomatic consequences. Indian-American support was important in the campaign for the nuclear agreement of 2008 and in subsequent cooperation, and political leaders of South Asian origin in the United Kingdom, Canada, and other countries have shaped public debate. Governments in South Asia and their critics have also mobilized overseas communities. Tamil diaspora organizations in Canada, Britain, and Australia raised funds and later campaigned for accountability after the Sri Lankan civil war (Chapter 103). Sikh activists abroad, including advocates of a separate Khalistan, became the subject of a serious diplomatic dispute between India and Canada after 2023, when Canadian authorities alleged Indian involvement in the killing of a Sikh activist, which India denied; the disagreement led to expulsions of diplomats and illustrates how migrant politics can become inter-state politics. Pakistani, Kashmiri, and Bangladeshi communities in Britain likewise engaged in homeland politics.

Transnational networks were not all organized in the same way. Long-established communities in East Africa, the Caribbean, Britain, and Southeast Asia inherited institutions formed through colonial labor, commerce, and education. Newer communities of engineers, physicians, academics, students, and entrepreneurs in North America and Europe often built ties through professional associations, alumni networks, investment groups, and specialized technology firms. Temporary workers in the Gulf generally occupied a different legal position and often had fewer opportunities for settlement or political participation. Yet even a temporary migrant could become a durable link between households, recruiting agencies, banks, employers, and two governments. The word diaspora thus covers multiple histories, and the effects of migration depend on legal status, generation, occupational position, and ties to the country of origin.

Remittances are only one of the ways such networks shape international relationships. Migrant professionals can transfer technical expertise, connect suppliers and customers, establish research collaborations, and help firms recruit skilled workers; charitable associations may fund schools or relief activities after disasters. These connections can aid development, but they may be distributed unequally among regions and social groups. Governments also compete to attract investment and expertise from their overseas populations through bonds, preferential investment schemes, and cultural or consular programs. Success cannot be inferred merely from the size of an overseas population: the strength of professional associations, business opportunities, reliable regulation, and ease of travel all affect whether the relationship generates tangible exchange.

Diasporic political influence is similarly variable. A community may mobilize around elections, minority protections, conflict in a homeland, or immigration reform in a destination state, but it rarely speaks with one voice. Different generations can disagree about national identity, and organizations formed abroad may promote interpretations of events at home that are contested by residents. During crises, consulates and embassies must also act as practical institutions responsible for evacuation assistance, documentation, welfare, and communication with host governments. These everyday functions reveal the limits of celebrating migration only as soft power. The same global networks that create diplomatic opportunity expose individuals to abrupt changes in border rules, employment policies, discrimination, and conflict.

Tensions about citizenship, loyalty, and restriction persist. India does not allow dual citizenship but offers overseas citizenship with limited rights; the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia have repeatedly tightened visa and student rules, and in September 2025 the American government announced a US$100,000 payment requirement for specified new H-1B petitions involving workers outside the United States, subject to exceptions. The restriction did not automatically apply to existing H-1B holders or every extension or change-of-status petition, and a September 2026 proclamation extended the relevant policy. Anti-immigrant politics and incidents of discrimination affected South Asians abroad. In Gulf states, workers had no path to citizenship. These restrictions show that diaspora connections depend on the policies of host states and that the diaspora's influence rests on conditions its members do not control.

Section 110.18

South Asia in Global Governance, Development, and International Cooperation

South Asian states have participated in the United Nations and other international organizations since their independence, but the character of participation changed. Their most distinctive contribution has been peacekeeping: Bangladesh, India, Nepal, and Pakistan have for many years been among the largest contributors of uniformed personnel to United Nations operations, deploying troops and police to Africa, the Middle East, and elsewhere, a role that provided income and prestige for militaries and links to the international system. Pakistan, a non-permanent member of the Security Council in 2025–2026, used its seat to speak on Kashmir, Afghanistan, and Gaza and held the presidency in July 2025; India's own campaign for permanent membership was noted in 110.2. These roles show governments using the same institutions for different purposes.

Debates over development and economic governance brought regional states together, though not always. India and other developing countries argued in the World Trade Organization for protection of agricultural subsidies and food-security stockpiling, and India, with South Africa, proposed a temporary waiver of intellectual-property rules during the COVID-19 pandemic. India's "Vaccine Maitri" donations in 2021 supplied vaccines to Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Maldives, and others, and its digital public infrastructure, including the Unified Payments Interface, was extended to partners including Bhutan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and the UAE. India hosted an AI Impact Summit in New Delhi in February 2026, attended by Sri Lanka's president among others. Such initiatives presented India as a provider of development models, a claim greeted with interest by some neighbors and suspicion by others who worried about influence.

Climate diplomacy provides a related example, treated here only as part of international cooperation; its environmental analysis belongs to Chapter 109. India launched the International Solar Alliance with France in 2015, pledged at the Glasgow conference of 2021 to reach net-zero emissions by 2070, and insisted that wealthy countries provide climate finance. Pakistan, after catastrophic floods in 2022, helped press for a fund for loss and damage; Maldives and Bangladesh spoke as especially vulnerable states; Bhutan promoted its carbon-negative status; and Nepal emphasized glacier and mountain concerns. Countries did not always agree: India's objections to the climate-finance goal agreed at the 2024 conference in Baku, which it said was too small, differed from the priorities of the most vulnerable states, which prioritized adaptation finance. Climate negotiations therefore showed both common interests and variation in emphasis.

Humanitarian and development cooperation took many forms. India sent relief to Nepal after the 2015 earthquake, to Sri Lanka in 2022, and to neighbors after cyclones; Pakistan received international assistance after the 2022 floods; and Bangladesh, which hosts about a million Rohingya refugees (Chapter 102), appealed to donors for sustained support. Responses to crises were also matters of influence, since assistance could build goodwill or provoke resentment. Debt-relief efforts involved both the G20 Common Framework, available only to eligible lower-income countries, and other creditor negotiations. Sri Lanka was not eligible for the Common Framework: after its 2022 default it negotiated through separate official-creditor coordination, bilateral engagements, and private-bondholder restructuring. Its experience shows why the formal institutional route matters as much as the headline amount of relief.

Historians should avoid treating "South Asia" as a unitary voice. The region does not caucus in the United Nations as a bloc. India speaks for developing countries in some settings and for itself in others; Pakistan often aligns with Islamic-world and Chinese positions; Bangladesh and Nepal, as least developed countries, advanced concerns about trade preferences; and Maldives and Bhutan, with tiny populations, used moral and technical arguments. The outcome is a landscape in which South Asian states are active participants in global governance but without a shared agenda, a pattern that echoes the limits of regional integration traced in Division IV.

Section 110.19

South Asia's Global Position in 2026: Power, Opportunity, and Constraint

As of November 20, 2026, South Asia's international position can be described country by country and as a region. India was the dominant economy and a recognized major power: it chaired BRICS in 2026 and hosted its summit, hosted an AI summit, was negotiating or concluding trade agreements with the European Union, the United States, and others, and maintained partnerships with Washington, Moscow, Tokyo, and the Gulf. Its constraints were an incomplete trade agreement with the United States, a border relationship with China that had stabilized without resolution, a difficult neighborhood, and dependence on imported energy and some imported components. Pakistan held an unusual combination of difficulty and prominence: a nuclear-armed state dependent on IMF programs and Gulf and Chinese support, with a defense pact with Saudi Arabia, renewed engagement with Washington, a mediating role in the Iran war, and persistent security challenges on its western borders and from militancy (Chapter 101).

Bangladesh was both an economically significant exporter and a state in political transition: its new government under Tarique Rahman pursued diversified relations, secured a three-year postponement of LDC graduation, and faced worsening poverty and banking stress according to World Bank reporting. Sri Lanka was stabilizing after default but with uneven social recovery, and maintained balanced relations with India and China. Nepal, under a government elected in March 2026, depended heavily on remittances and on transit through India while promising balanced diplomacy. Bhutan maintained its close partnership with India and unresolved boundary talks with China, alongside development initiatives. Maldives remained strategically placed and financially vulnerable, with relations with both India and China. Afghanistan, as a historically connected neighbor, remained outside regional institutions in most respects.

The external powers occupied different positions. China was the largest trading partner and infrastructure financier for several states and a security partner of Pakistan, but it faced local suspicion and had had to stabilize relations with India. The United States was a leading market and technology partner but an unpredictable one, whose tariffs and priorities changed. Russia was an arms and energy partner chiefly of India. The Gulf states were indispensable sources of energy, jobs, and finance, and their security was threatened by the Iran war. International financial institutions remained arbiters in several crises. The World Bank's October 2026 regional outlook projected growth of 6.9 percent for the region in 2026 and 6.7 percent in 2027, with strong domestic demand and remittances cited as supports and energy prices, climate shocks, and external finance as risks. These are forecasts available at the cutoff, not final results.

Why the comparison matters: The perspectives are not equal in power or resources. A large economy and a small island state negotiate from different positions; the exercise shows why generalizations about "South Asia" conceal contrasting interests.

A second way to evaluate the regional position is to compare kinds of external dependence rather than rank countries by size alone. India had broader access to capital markets, more diversified export activities, and a comparatively extensive diplomatic network, yet still relied on imported energy and certain technology inputs. Bangladesh's export earnings were concentrated in garments and therefore vulnerable to consumer demand and changes in trade preferences. Pakistan's need for external financing and energy imports constrained its room for economic maneuver even when security relationships gave it diplomatic leverage. Sri Lanka's tourism and shipping services generated foreign exchange but left it sensitive to debt service and international transport conditions. Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives faced particularly pronounced constraints arising from geography, market size, or concentrated export activities.

Diplomatic agency nevertheless survived these constraints. Nepal and Bhutan could negotiate transit, power sales, and border matters according to different priorities; Bangladesh could court export markets and infrastructure finance without simply joining one regional alignment; and Maldives and Sri Lanka could use their maritime positions to negotiate with several interested governments. The results were not always equally favorable. Infrastructure could generate employment and revenue or leave costly obligations, security agreements could provide equipment and training or provoke domestic controversy, and foreign investment could concentrate influence where contracts were opaque. The historian's task is to compare the choices governments actually made and the measurable consequences, rather than presume that all smaller-state decisions were dictated by India, China, or another external power.

The events of 2026 sharpened the difference between diplomatic visibility and durable institutional capacity. India's BRICS chairmanship demonstrated convening power, while the region's unfinished trade negotiations showed the friction between declarations and legal implementation. Pakistan's role in US–Iran diplomacy attracted international attention but did not eliminate its domestic economic and security problems. The UN decision to postpone Bangladesh's and Nepal's graduation from least-developed-country status demonstrated that multilateral diplomacy could deliver a concrete policy result for smaller countries. Across these examples, success was issue-specific. Global influence did not amount to regional unity, and a favorable summit communiqué or a successful mediation initiative did not by itself settle long-standing structural constraints.

The central tension at the cutoff was that South Asia's global importance grew while its regional institutions and internal trade stayed weak. Demographic scale, a large young workforce, large consumer markets, and growing services and manufacturing made the region important to others. Yet political divisions, uneven state capacity, and dependence on external energy, finance, and markets limited coordination. A region of nearly two billion people did not have a functioning summit process for its principal association, and its governments were more likely to negotiate separately with outside powers than with each other.

Section 110.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The history of South Asia's international transformation between 1991 and 2026 can be summarized in four processes. The first is economic integration with the world: liberalization, services and garment exports, remittances, and external finance linked the region to global markets, Gulf labor and energy systems, and international institutions. The second is strategic diversification: India's partnerships widened from the Soviet Union to the United States, Russia, Japan, the Gulf, and Europe; Pakistan layered Chinese, Saudi, American, and mediation roles; and the smaller states increased their bargaining by courting several partners. The third is the redefinition of the Indian Ocean as an arena of commerce and security, in which ports, navies, and chokepoints became central. The fourth is migration and diaspora networks, which tied South Asian societies to labor markets and political systems abroad.

These changes expanded South Asia's global influence considerably, but they did not make the region more politically unified. This is the chapter's central historical conclusion, and it explains why India can be increasingly influential while the region remains weakly integrated. India's rise raised neighbors' concerns as well as opportunities; Pakistan's rivalry prevented SAARC from functioning; the smaller states used external partners to offset Indian weight; and external powers competed for access. Influence was often exercised bilaterally, and sometimes by default, when states found that the quickest way to secure finance, markets, or security was through a partner outside the region. Distinctions among economic weight, diplomatic influence, and military capability remain essential: India led in the first, achieved mixed results in the second, and had substantial capacity in the third; Pakistan was weaker economically but diplomatically prominent in 2026; Bangladesh's export strength did not translate into regional leadership; and the smaller states exercised agency out of proportion to their size.

The relationship between India's emergence and the independence of the smaller states was therefore complicated. India's neighbors depended on India for trade, transit, energy, and crisis assistance, but they also asserted independence through elections, alternative partnerships, and their own diplomacy, and the changes in Bangladesh and Nepal in 2024–2026 showed that dominance could not be taken for granted. Smaller states were neither satellites nor equals; they were actors constrained by geography and finance. India's own interests were served by cooperation with them, and its policy toward neighbors sometimes succeeded through assistance and sometimes provoked resentment.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Foreign-Policy Independence, Strategic Alignment, and International Influence, 1947–2026

What Changed

  • India moved from state-led autarky and Cold War nonalignment to market-linked multi-alignment and a role in G20, BRICS, and the Quad
  • Pakistan moved from Cold War alliance with the United States toward a layered strategy including China, Saudi Arabia, and mediation
  • Smaller states moved from dependence on India or Britain-era arrangements toward deliberate diversification
  • Gulf labor markets, remittances, and energy trade became central to regional economies
  • The Indian Ocean became the focus of naval and port competition

What Stayed the Same

  • The India–Pakistan rivalry inherited from Partition continued to prevent regional integration
  • States valued independence of decision and distrusted dominance by any single power
  • Geography, including India's central position, mountain frontiers, and maritime location, continued to shape options
  • Russian arms, Chinese support for Pakistan, and Gulf labor ties had roots in earlier decades

Who experienced change most directly? Governments and exporters felt shifts in alignment and tariffs; migrant workers and their families experienced the Gulf's economic and security shocks. Who experienced greater continuity? Border communities, and populations affected by India–Pakistan and India–China tensions, lived with similar constraints across decades. Why it matters: The pattern shows that new power did not erase old divisions.

The longer historical perspective reinforces this distinction. The region inherited from colonial rule a common infrastructure of rail, ports, law, and commercial networks, but Partition broke many of the connections that had once crossed the subcontinent. Independent states made divergent development choices, built their own diplomatic institutions, and developed different relationships with Cold War powers. The liberalization era widened access to the world economy without resolving that earlier political division. International migration, technological exchange, and maritime commerce created new forms of connection, yet the most important routes of influence frequently connected an individual South Asian country to a distant partner rather than to a neighboring state. The region's history after 1991 is thus neither a simple ascent toward unity nor a story of uninterrupted fragmentation; it is the simultaneous growth of global connections and the persistence of regional limits.

This synthesis also requires restraint about the word power. Economic scale describes production and purchasing capacity, diplomatic capacity involves the ability to assemble cooperation and influence decisions, and military capability concerns the resources available for deterrence or force. Each can strengthen or constrain the others, but none guarantees political legitimacy, social development, or acceptance by neighbors. The continuing relevance of geography, sovereignty, historical memory, and economic inequality explains why South Asia's countries often chose different alignments when confronted with the same international events. The chronological account closes with those tensions unresolved rather than supplying predictions about how they must end.

The chronological narrative of this textbook ends here, at a moment when many outcomes remained open: the trade agreement between India and the United States, the future of shipping through Hormuz, the evolution of India–China relations, and the foreign policies of the governments elected in Bangladesh and Nepal. This is not a failure of the account but a feature of contemporary history, which is written before its consequences are known. Chapter 111 turns from events to interpretation: how South Asians remember, dispute, and use the past that Chapters 1 through 110 have described.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined South Asia's changing position in the international system between 1991 and 2026. Its central argument is that the region's global importance grew substantially while its political unity and economic integration did not, and that both facts must be explained together.

India's emergence began with the 1991 crisis and liberalization, was marked by the 1998 nuclear tests, and was sustained by growth, technology exports, and a widening network of partnerships. Its diplomatic vocabulary shifted from nonalignment to strategic autonomy and multi-alignment, and it presented itself as a voice for the Global South through the 2023 G20 presidency and the 2026 BRICS chairship, though its campaign for a permanent Security Council seat had no institutional result.

Relations with China combined economic interdependence and strategic rivalry. After the border agreements of 1993 and 1996 and rapid trade growth, Doklam (2017) and Galwan (2020) produced a deep rupture, followed by a partial normalization from October 2024 that included flights, pilgrimages, border trade, and Xi Jinping's attendance at the New Delhi BRICS summit, without a boundary settlement.

China's relations with the smaller states varied. CPEC made China central to Pakistan's infrastructure and security; Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Maldives pursued Chinese finance selectively; Bhutan maintained boundary talks without diplomatic relations. The debt-trap thesis was shown to oversimplify, and smaller states were found to bargain actively.

The United States and India built a partnership through the civil nuclear agreement of 2005–2008, defense agreements, and the Quad, without an alliance. Tariff disputes in 2025 and an unfinished interim trade agreement at the cutoff showed its volatility. American relations with Pakistan oscillated between cooperation and estrangement and revived in new forms in 2025–2026, while Bangladesh and the smaller states used Washington selectively.

Russia remained an important partner of India for arms, nuclear energy, and diplomacy, and the Ukraine war forced India to defend purchases of Russian oil while negotiating with Washington. Across the region, governments practiced different forms of balancing, hedging, and autonomy, shaped by size, geography, and domestic politics, including the changed governments of Bangladesh and Nepal in 2026.

The Gulf connected South Asia through energy, finance, and migration. Millions of workers under temporary contracts sent remittances that sustained households and national budgets, while the 2026 Iran war showed how exposed the region was to disruption in Hormuz and Gulf labor markets. The Indian Ocean, historically a space of exchange, became a field of naval and port activity, in which claims about strategic ports required careful evidence.

Regional integration remained weak. SAARC, SAFTA, and BIMSTEC produced institutions and agreements but limited trade, because of India–Pakistan hostility, non-tariff barriers, and poor connectivity; subregional links in energy and transport advanced where interests aligned. Financial institutions and creditors shaped policy in crises such as Sri Lanka's 2022 default and Pakistan's recurring programs.

Diaspora networks, multilateral participation, and international cooperation extended South Asian influence, but governments spoke for themselves, not for a region. At the cutoff, the World Bank forecast growth of 6.9 percent for 2026, a projection and not a result, and several major questions remained open. These unresolved questions lead to Chapter 111's examination of history and memory.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a region of the postcolonial world became part of a multipolar international system without becoming a bloc. The processes it traces, liberalization, nuclear status, migration, maritime exchange, and financial dependence, grew from the histories of the preceding chapters, and they will shape what follows.

It also shows that influence and unity are different things. India's rise, Pakistan's strategic role, Bangladesh's exports, and the small states' bargaining each enlarged South Asia's presence in world affairs, yet the Partition-era rivalry and asymmetries of size continued to restrict cooperation among neighbors.

The chapter connects regions: the Gulf to Kerala and Sylhet, the Himalaya to the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea to ancient trade routes. It also connects the series' volumes, because the maritime networks of Volume I, the empires and colonial states of Volumes II through IV, and the nationalism and Partition of Volume V all lie beneath these contemporary relationships.

Because the chapter ends at a cutoff and not at a conclusion, it asks students to practice a particular historical discipline: describing the present with care, naming uncertainty, and resisting prediction. Chapter 111 now turns to the way South Asian peoples interpret and argue about the entire history they have inherited.
