Part VI — The Gupta Age and Classical Transformations

Religion and Philosophy in the Classical Age

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

At Udayagiri, in present-day Madhya Pradesh, a stone relief of Vishnu in his boar incarnation — his tusk supporting a rescued earth goddess — was carved directly into a hillside under the patronage associated with Chandragupta II, one of the great devotional monuments of the early fifth century CE. Not far away, at Eran, a colossal freestanding stone boar sculpture, its body covered in rows of carved miniature figures representing sages and personified rivers, was raised somewhat later, in the first regnal year of the ruler Toramana, at the initiative of a donor named Dhanyavishnu. A visitor to Udayagiri or Eran in this broader period would also have found, within reach, Shaiva shrines, Buddhist monastic establishments, and Jain communities of merchants and ascetics, all drawing on patronage from many of the same regional elites. No single image, temple, or inscription can summarize what was happening to religious life across South Asia during these centuries. But together, sites like these reveal a pattern this chapter will trace in detail: older traditions of sacrifice, textual study, and ascetic renunciation were not abandoned, but they were joined, reorganized, and in some respects transformed by newer forms of image worship, temple building, devotional narrative, and philosophical system-building.

Chapter 27 examined the institutional and economic foundations of Gupta-era society — land grants, guilds, administration, social hierarchy, and the material resources that made large-scale religious patronage possible. This chapter turns from those foundations to the religious and philosophical transformations they helped support, asking not how temples, monasteries, and religious endowments were funded, but what worshippers, monks, ascetics, philosophers, and ordinary householders believed, practiced, argued about, and built during the fourth through sixth centuries CE.

It would be a serious historical error to describe this period as the moment when a single, finished religion called "Hinduism" sprang into being, sponsored from above by Gupta emperors. It would be an equally serious error to imagine that older Vedic and Brahmanical traditions, examined in earlier chapters, simply vanished and were replaced. The evidence instead shows something more interesting and more historically accurate: a long, uneven, regionally varied process in which Vedic ritual specialists, Puranic storytellers, devotional communities oriented toward Vishnu, Shiva, and powerful goddesses, Buddhist monks and lay patrons, Jain ascetics and merchant communities, and philosophers debating the nature of knowledge and reality all operated within a shared, connected, and economically changing world, borrowing from one another, competing for royal and popular support, while permanent Brahmanical temple construction and deity-image worship became increasingly prominent and durable.

This chapter follows the Authoritative Chapter Outline's fixed sequence for Chapter 28: Puranic traditions, Vaishnavism, Shaivism, goddess traditions, temple worship, Mahayana Buddhism, Jain communities, schools of Hindu philosophy, religious pluralism and patronage, and a concluding synthesis. That sequence is not accidental. It moves from the broad umbrella of Puranic religious culture, through its major deity-centered traditions, to the institution — the temple — that increasingly housed and expressed those traditions, and then outward to the other major religious communities of the period, before turning to philosophical argument and, finally, to the question of how all these traditions coexisted, competed, and shared a single historical world.

Readers should keep several cautions in mind throughout. First, this chapter uses terms such as Vaishnavism, Shaivism, and Puranic Hindu traditions rather than a single undifferentiated "Hinduism" wherever greater precision is possible, following this textbook's consistent practice of naming specific traditions rather than projecting a later, more unified religious category backward onto an earlier and more plural reality. Second, Buddhist and Jain history in this period is a story of adaptation and continued vitality, not decline; describing this era as beginning Buddhism's "disappearance" from India would seriously misrepresent evidence examined later in this textbook, which shows Buddhist institutions remaining significant for many centuries after the Gupta period. Third, the chapter treats the Puranas, sectarian devotional literature, and philosophical treatises as historical sources requiring the same critical handling that Chapter 1 established for all South Asian textual evidence: composed and compiled in layers over long periods, reflecting the concerns of the communities that transmitted them, and not simply transparent windows onto belief or practice.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the fourth through sixth centuries not as the birth of a finished classical religious order but as an especially well-documented and consequential stage within much longer processes of religious change — processes that had begun well before the Gupta dynasty and that would continue, in different forms, for many centuries afterward, as later chapters in this textbook will show.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the transformation from Vedic and Brahmanical religious practice toward Puranic devotional traditions, distinguishing continuity from genuine change.
  2. Describe the layered, composite character of the Puranas as textual sources and explain why they should not be read as contemporary chronicles.
  3. Trace the consolidation of Vishnu-centered devotional traditions, including the integration of Vasudeva-Krishna traditions and the concept of avatara.
  4. Trace the development of Shiva-centered devotional traditions, including the significance of the linga and Shaiva ascetic and householder practice.
  5. Analyze the growing prominence of goddess traditions and explain the process by which local and regional cults were connected to wider Sanskritic frameworks without losing their distinct identities.
  6. Explain why permanent stone temples and image worship became increasingly important religious institutions during this period, and connect this development to the economic foundations examined in Chapter 27.
  7. Distinguish Mahayana Buddhist developments from earlier and continuing Buddhist traditions, avoiding a simple narrative of Buddhist replacement or decline.
  8. Describe Jain monastic and lay community life in this period and assess evidence for Jain participation in urban, mercantile, and courtly life.
  9. Identify the major schools of classical Hindu philosophy (darshanas) and explain their central questions and long, uneven process of systematization.
  10. Evaluate the character of religious pluralism and royal, merchant, and guild patronage in the classical age, avoiding both an overstated "golden age of tolerance" narrative and a simplistic "state religion" narrative.
  11. Assess the limitations of inscriptional, textual, and art-historical evidence for reconstructing religious belief and practice in this period.
  12. Explain why historians debate the appropriateness of the term "Hinduism" for describing fourth- through sixth-century religious life.

Historical Context

Chapters 26 and 27 traced the political formation of the Gupta Empire and the economic and social structures — land grants, guilds, agrarian expansion, and a graded social hierarchy — that sustained it. Those chapters established that Gupta rule was neither as centralized nor as uniform as an older historiography once assumed, and that much of the empire's stability rested on relationships between the imperial center and regional elites, local landholders, guilds, and religious institutions. This chapter builds directly on that foundation, but it asks a different kind of question: not how resources were generated and distributed, but how those resources, together with much longer traditions of Vedic ritual, Upanishadic philosophy, Buddhist and Jain monasticism, and regional devotional practice examined in earlier chapters, combined to produce the religious world of the classical age.

By the fourth century CE, South Asia's religious landscape already had a long and varied history. Chapters 5 through 8 traced the Vedic tradition from its early pastoral and ritual origins through its later, more elaborate sacrificial and social system. Chapters 11 through 13 examined the rise of Buddhism, Jainism, and Upanishadic philosophical speculation as responses to, and transformations of, Brahmanical religious authority. Chapter 28 does not repeat that history. It begins from the recognition that, by the Gupta centuries, all of these traditions — Vedic ritualism, Brahmanical social and legal thought, Upanishadic philosophy, Buddhist monasticism, and Jain asceticism — had continued to develop for many centuries, alongside a wide range of regional, local, and popular religious practices that earlier textual evidence documents only unevenly.

What changed during the fourth through sixth centuries, this chapter argues, was not the sudden appearance of new religions but the increasing prominence of several developments that had begun earlier and now became more visible, more widely patronized, and more architecturally and textually documented: devotional worship (commonly discussed using the Sanskrit term bhakti) directed toward personal deities, especially Vishnu and Shiva, and toward powerful goddesses; the compilation and continued elaboration of Puranic literature, weaving cosmology, mythology, genealogy, and religious instruction into an increasingly accessible narrative form; the construction of permanent stone and brick temples housing images of deities, replacing or supplementing earlier, more modest or perishable shrines; continued Buddhist monastic scholarship and lay devotion, increasingly organized around Mahayana teachings and their distinctive practices; and continued Jain monastic and lay community life, closely connected to trade and urban patronage.

These developments did not occur in isolation from the economic and institutional history examined in Chapter 27. Land grants to religious institutions (agrahara grants to Brahmins and endowments to temples and monasteries), guild patronage of religious construction, and the wealth generated by agrarian expansion and trade all provided the material means for the temple-building and institutional expansion this chapter examines. But material means alone do not explain religious change. This chapter asks what worshippers, monks, philosophers, and ordinary people were actually doing, believing, and arguing about — a question that inscriptions, surviving temples and images, and sectarian and philosophical texts can help answer, with appropriate historical caution about whose experience such evidence best documents.

Section 28.1

Puranic Hindu Traditions

Chapters 6, 7, and 13 traced the long development of Vedic religion — its early sacrificial practice, its expanding ritual and social system, and its later philosophical reinterpretation in the Upanishads. By the early centuries of the common era, alongside this Brahmanical tradition of Vedic study and ritual, a different but related body of literature was taking increasingly organized textual form: the Puranas, a Sanskrit word meaning roughly "ancient" or "old," referring to a genre of texts combining cosmology, cosmogony (accounts of the origin of the universe), genealogies of gods, sages, and royal dynasties, mythological narrative, ritual and pilgrimage instruction, and, in many cases, sectarian devotional material oriented toward a particular deity such as Vishnu or Shiva.

Historians must handle the Puranas with the same critical caution this textbook has applied to every other South Asian textual tradition. The major Puranas were not composed at a single moment by a single author. They developed over many centuries through processes of oral transmission, compilation, expansion, and revision, with different layers of a single text sometimes reflecting different historical periods, regions, and sectarian concerns. Assigning a single composition date to "the Puranas" as a whole is not historically meaningful; individual passages, sections, or recensions must be evaluated on their own evidentiary terms, and many scholars date the core material of several major Puranas to approximately the Gupta period while recognizing that later material continued to be added for centuries afterward. This textbook treats the Puranas as invaluable evidence for the religious imagination, cosmology, and devotional concerns of the communities that composed, transmitted, and used them, while cautioning against treating any given Puranic passage as a precise, datable historical record.

What is historically significant about the Puranic tradition, for the purposes of this chapter, is not any single narrative it contains but the broader religious orientation it represents and helped to spread. Puranic religion did not reject Vedic ritual or Brahmanical authority; Brahmins continued to perform sacrifices, continued to be honored as ritual specialists, and continued to receive land grants and patronage, as Chapter 27 examined. But Puranic literature increasingly organized religious life around personal deities — chiefly Vishnu and Shiva, along with powerful goddesses examined in Section 28.4 — who could be approached through devotion, narrative, pilgrimage, vow, gift, and image worship, in addition to or instead of Vedic sacrifice. This orientation is often described using the Sanskrit term bhakti, meaning devotion or loving attachment to a personal deity, discussed further in the Historical Vocabulary feature below.

The Puranic corpus also absorbed and reorganized a very wide range of material: genealogies connecting ruling dynasties to divine or legendary ancestors, providing political legitimacy; accounts of sacred geography, associating particular rivers, mountains, and shrines with divine presence and encouraging pilgrimage; ethical and legal instruction, overlapping with material found in Dharmashastra texts; and cosmological schemes describing cycles of creation and destruction across immense timescales. This breadth means that Puranic literature functioned not merely as scripture in a narrow sense but as an encyclopedic vehicle for transmitting religious, historical, and cultural knowledge to audiences well beyond the small circle of Sanskrit-literate ritual specialists, since Puranic material was recited, performed, and retold in ways that could reach audiences who could not themselves read Sanskrit.

Historians describe this broader religious world using terms such as Puranic Hinduism or Puranic Brahmanism rather than treating it as identical to earlier Vedic religion or later, more institutionally organized forms of Hindu tradition. This chapter follows that practice, reserving the terms Vaishnavism and Shaivism, examined in the next two sections, for the specific deity-centered traditions that developed within this broader Puranic framework, and returning in Section 28.9 to the question of how this framework related to the Buddhist and Jain traditions examined in Sections 28.6 and 28.7.

Section 28.2

Vishnu and Vaishnavism

Vaishnavism — devotional and theological traditions centered on the god Vishnu — did not originate in the Gupta period. Inscriptional evidence, including the second-century BCE Heliodorus pillar at Besnagar recording a Greek ambassador's devotion to Vasudeva, demonstrates that Vaishnava devotion, associated in particular with the deity Vasudeva-Krishna, had already attracted committed followers centuries before the Gupta dynasty. What the fourth through sixth centuries witnessed was not the invention of Vaishnava devotion but its further consolidation, textual elaboration, and prominent royal and popular patronage.

Central to Vaishnavism of this broader period was the developing concept of avatara ("descent"), the idea that Vishnu periodically takes form in the world to restore cosmic and moral order (dharma) in times of crisis. Puranic literature of this period increasingly systematized lists of Vishnu's avataras, among which the boar (Varaha), who rescues the earth goddess from cosmic waters, received especially prominent artistic representation, as the rock-cut relief carved into the hillside at Udayagiri, closely associated with Gupta imperial patronage under Chandragupta II, and the colossal freestanding stone boar sculpture at Eran both demonstrate. The Eran sculpture belongs to a somewhat later and politically distinct moment: it carries an inscription dated to the first regnal year of the ruler Toramana, associated with the Huna challenge to Gupta authority examined in Chapter 30, and records the donor Dhanyavishnu as responsible for causing the stone religious structure to be made. The exact absolute chronology of Toramana's first regnal year remains debated among specialists, and this textbook does not assign it a fixed calendar year. Discussing Udayagiri and Eran together in this section is not an error of chronology; it deliberately illustrates that Vaishnava devotional patronage, and its distinctive visual language, continued across a significant political transition, a point taken up further in the Primary Evidence Window below. Other avataras, including forms later organized into standard lists such as the man-lion (Narasimha) and, increasingly significant in later centuries, Krishna and Rama, were also venerated, though the precise, fixed list of "ten avataras" (dashavatara) that later Vaishnava tradition would systematize was still in the process of formation during this period rather than fully fixed.

The integration of the Vasudeva-Krishna devotional tradition, associated with the Bhagavata community and with theological material found in texts such as the Bhagavad Gita — itself the product of a longer compositional history extending back well before the Gupta period, as discussed in earlier chapters — into a broader Vaishnava framework centered on Vishnu is an important development of this era. Krishna's identification as an avatara of Vishnu connected a beloved narrative and devotional tradition, rich in stories of Krishna's youth, heroism, and teaching, to the wider cosmological and philosophical framework of Vishnu worship, without erasing the distinct character of Krishna devotion itself.

Gupta rulers, beginning prominently with Chandragupta II, associated themselves with Vaishnava imagery and patronage, adopting titles and sponsoring monuments connected to Vishnu, as the Udayagiri Varaha relief and its associated inscriptions illustrate. This royal association mattered politically as well as religiously: identifying royal authority with Vishnu's role as restorer and protector of cosmic order provided a potent language of legitimate kingship, discussed further in Section 28.9. However, students should resist the temptation to conclude that "the Guptas made India Vaishnava." Gupta inscriptions and monuments also patronized Shaiva and other traditions, discussed in the following sections, and Vaishnava devotion flourished across many regions and social groups independently of direct royal sponsorship, through temple construction, pilgrimage, storytelling, and household devotion that historians can trace only partially through surviving physical and textual evidence.

Vaishnava devotional practice during this period combined several elements: narrative recitation and retelling of Puranic and epic material concerning Vishnu and his avataras; temple worship involving image consecration, ritual offering, and darshan (the reciprocal, religiously significant act of seeing and being seen by the deity's image, examined further in Section 28.5); pilgrimage to sites associated with Vishnu's mythology or manifestation; and, for some communities, more intensely personal and emotional devotional practices whose fuller textual and poetic elaboration belongs to later centuries and later chapters of this textbook, since the elaborately developed bhakti movements of the medieval period, examined in Chapter 48, built upon but considerably extended the devotional practices visible in this earlier period.

Section 28.3

Shiva and Shaivism

Shaivism — devotional and theological traditions centered on the god Shiva — developed alongside Vaishnavism during this period with comparable depth and should not be treated as a secondary or lesser tradition. Shiva's religious character combined several distinct dimensions that Puranic and Shaiva literature worked to integrate: an ascetic, world-renouncing dimension, associated with Shiva as a yogi meditating in remote, wild places; a householder and family dimension, associated with Shiva's marriage to the goddess (discussed further in Section 28.4) and his role as a devoted spouse and father; a destructive and transformative cosmic dimension, associated with Shiva's role in the periodic dissolution of the universe within Puranic cosmological schemes; and a benevolent, protective dimension, associated with Shiva's responsiveness to devoted worshippers.

The linga, an aniconic (non-figural) cylindrical form, became during this period an increasingly widespread and important object of Shiva worship, installed in shrines and temples and treated as a representation of Shiva's presence and generative cosmic power. Alongside the linga, figural representations of Shiva — as an ascetic, as Nataraja (lord of dance, though the fully developed bronze Nataraja iconography familiar from later South Indian art belongs to a later period examined in subsequent chapters), and in family scenes with the goddess and their sons — also developed during this era. Historians caution against reducing Shaiva worship to the linga alone; both aniconic and figural representations coexisted and served different devotional and ritual purposes.

Shaiva communities during this period included both householder devotees, who worshipped at shrines and temples, participated in pilgrimage, and observed vows and festivals connected to Shiva, and ascetic practitioners, who pursued yogic and renunciatory disciplines associated with Shiva's own mythological example. The relationship between these householder and ascetic dimensions of Shaivism — a tradition that simultaneously celebrated a married household deity and an ascetic renouncer — is one of the more historically interesting features of Shaiva religious culture, and Puranic and later Shaiva literature devoted considerable narrative and theological attention to reconciling or holding together these apparently opposed ideals.

As with Vaishnavism, Shaiva traditions received patronage from Gupta and allied regional rulers, though not to the exclusion of Vaishnava or other patronage. Inscriptions record grants and dedications to Shaiva establishments alongside Vaishnava ones, and it would misrepresent the evidence to describe the Gupta court as consistently or exclusively aligned with one tradition over the other across its full duration. Students should understand Vaishnavism and Shaivism during this period not as rigid, mutually exclusive confessional identities comparable to some later or non-South Asian religious categories, but as distinguishable devotional and theological traditions that individuals, families, dynasties, and local communities could and did patronize, honor, and move between, sometimes simultaneously, a point developed further in Section 28.9.

Section 28.4

Goddess Traditions

A history of classical-age religion organized only around Vishnu and Shiva would seriously misrepresent the period's religious landscape. Goddess-centered traditions, referred to collectively using terms such as Devi (the Goddess) or, in later and more systematized theological contexts, Shakti (divine feminine power), were widespread, significant, and, during the fourth through sixth centuries, increasingly connected to broader Puranic and Sanskritic religious frameworks, without losing their often highly localized and regionally specific character.

Goddess worship in South Asia has roots considerably older than the Gupta period, including evidence, discussed in earlier chapters, for female figurines and possible goddess-associated imagery from the Indus Civilization and subsequent periods, though historians caution strongly against assuming direct, unbroken continuity between such early evidence and later, textually documented goddess traditions, since the interpretive gap between an unprovenanced figurine and a fully articulated later theological tradition is considerable and cannot be bridged by assumption alone. What can be said with more confidence is that, by the classical period, goddess traditions encompassed a very wide range of figures and practices: goddesses associated with fertility, prosperity, and well-being, such as Lakshmi, closely associated with Vishnu as his consort and with royal and household prosperity; fierce, powerful goddesses associated with protection and the destruction of demonic threats to cosmic order, such as Durga, whose mythology, increasingly elaborated in Puranic literature of this period and shortly after, presents her as a goddess created from the combined power (shakti) of the male gods to defeat a buffalo-demon that they themselves could not overcome; and an enormous number of local, village, and regional goddesses, often associated with particular places, lineages, or communities, whose worship continued largely outside, or only loosely connected to, Sanskritic textual traditions.

The historically significant process this section highlights is one of integration without erasure. Puranic and Shaiva literature of this period increasingly connected powerful goddesses to the wider pantheon — as consorts of Vishnu or Shiva, as manifestations of a single underlying goddess principle, or as independent figures granted cosmological significance in their own right — while local and regional goddess cults, with their own distinct names, myths, and ritual practices, continued to flourish, sometimes only loosely or partially absorbed into these wider frameworks. Historians should avoid projecting backward the fully systematized theological category of Shakti or the elaborate Tantric goddess traditions that developed considerably more fully in later centuries, examined in subsequent volumes of this textbook; the Gupta-period evidence shows an important and consequential early stage of this integration, not its completed form.

This pattern of integration without erasure recurs throughout this chapter and reflects a broader feature of Puranic Hindu religious culture during this period: its capacity to incorporate diverse regional and local traditions into an expanding, but never fully uniform, religious framework. This capacity helps explain both the geographic reach of Puranic Hindu traditions across many different regions of South Asia and the immense internal diversity that this textbook consistently emphasizes rather than obscures.

Section 28.5

Temple Worship

If any single institutional development best captures the religious transformations of the fourth through sixth centuries, it is the increasing prominence of the permanent stone or brick temple (mandira) as a site of worship. Earlier periods of South Asian religious history, examined in previous chapters, knew shrines, sacred trees and groves, sacrificial enclosures, and a range of other sacred spaces, but the Gupta period saw a marked expansion in the construction of durable, architecturally developed temple structures housing a consecrated image (murti) of a deity.

Early examples of this architectural development include the relatively modest flat-roofed shrine known as Temple 17 at Sanchi, whose simple form — a small square sanctum fronted by a columned porch — represents an important early stage in the development of the freestanding Hindu temple, and the more elaborately carved Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh, whose surviving relief panels depicting Vishnu's avataras and other mythological scenes illustrate the increasing integration of narrative sculpture into temple architecture. These structures, along with rock-cut and relief monuments such as those at Udayagiri, represent only a portion of what was certainly a much larger number of temples built during this period, many now lost, altered by later reconstruction, or represented only by inscriptional evidence recording their endowment.

Why did permanent temple construction expand so significantly during this period? This chapter's Cause and Effect feature, below, addresses this question directly, but several factors deserve introduction here. Economically, as Chapter 27 examined, agrarian expansion, guild wealth, and land-grant practices made substantial resources available for large-scale religious construction and endowment. Religiously, the increasing centrality of image worship and personal devotion to particular deities created growing demand for permanent, dignified spaces in which a deity's image could be consecrated, cared for, and approached by worshippers. Politically, temple patronage offered rulers and regional elites a visible, lasting means of expressing piety, legitimacy, and generosity, connecting royal or elite identity to divine favor in ways that could be witnessed by wide audiences.

Temple worship during this period centered on the ritually significant act of darshan — a reciprocal act of seeing and being seen by the deity's consecrated image, believed to convey the deity's presence, blessing, and attention to the worshipper. Temple ritual also involved regular offerings of food, flowers, light, and other substances to the image, following elaborated liturgical procedures that priestly specialists, often but not always Brahmins, administered. Temples frequently received endowments of land, produce, or other income specifically dedicated to funding these ongoing rituals, connecting temple religious life directly to the agrarian and economic structures examined in Chapter 27.

It is essential to emphasize that temple worship did not replace earlier forms of religious practice. Vedic domestic and public sacrifice continued; ascetic and yogic practice continued; pilgrimage to sacred rivers, mountains, and natural sites continued; and Buddhist and Jain monastic and devotional practice, examined in the following two sections, continued and in some respects flourished alongside, rather than in competition eliminating, the expansion of temple-based Hindu worship. The classical-age temple should be understood as one increasingly important addition to an already rich and varied religious landscape, not as a wholesale replacement of what came before.

Section 28.6

Mahayana Buddhism

Buddhism's history during the Gupta centuries is best understood as a story of continued adaptation, institutional development, and doctrinal elaboration, not decline. Chapters 11, 16, and 21 traced Buddhism's origins, its patronage under Ashoka, and its flourishing under Kushan rule, including the development of Gandharan and Mathura artistic traditions. By the fourth century CE, Buddhism had already developed a complex and internally diverse institutional and doctrinal landscape, including a range of traditions later grouped, not always precisely, under headings such as "Mahayana" and "non-Mahayana" (a category historians prefer to the older, doctrinally loaded term "Hinayana," which originated as a polemical label used by Mahayana proponents rather than a neutral historical description).

Mahayana ("Great Vehicle") Buddhist traditions, whose textual and doctrinal roots extend back several centuries before the Gupta period, continued to develop significantly during the fourth through sixth centuries. Central to Mahayana teaching is the ideal of the bodhisattva — a being who undertakes the path toward full buddhahood for the benefit and liberation of all sentient beings, motivated by the aspiration for awakening on behalf of others (bodhicitta) rather than by a narrower pursuit of individual liberation alone. Mahayana literature of this period elaborated an expanding pantheon of bodhisattvas and celestial buddhas, alongside sophisticated philosophical schools addressing the nature of reality, emptiness (shunyata), and consciousness, whose fuller philosophical elaboration involved influential thinkers and textual traditions whose detailed study belongs more properly to specialized courses in Buddhist philosophy but whose historical significance for this chapter lies in their evidence of vigorous, continuing intellectual activity within Buddhist institutions during the classical age.

Buddhist monastic institutions (viharas) continued to receive substantial patronage during this period from royal, merchant, and lay donors, functioning as centers of scholarship, textual transmission, and religious instruction alongside their role in supporting resident monastic communities. Major Buddhist institutional and scholarly centers, including those that would develop into the great monastic university complexes examined in later chapters, had roots extending into or before this period. The stupa, an earlier architectural form associated with the veneration of relics, continued to be built, expanded, and venerated, often alongside newer temple and monastic structures, reflecting continuity in Buddhist architectural and devotional practice even as newer forms developed.

One of the most valuable sources for Buddhist institutional life during this period comes from outside South Asia: the Chinese Buddhist monk and pilgrim Faxian, who traveled through Central Asia and South Asia between 399 and 412 CE in search of Buddhist texts and teachings, and who recorded his observations of monasteries, monastic discipline, popular devotional practice, and the general condition of Buddhist institutions in the regions he visited. Chapter 27 drew on Faxian's account chiefly for economic and social observations; this chapter returns to his testimony for what it reveals about Buddhist institutional and devotional life specifically. Historians must use Faxian's account with appropriate caution: he was a foreign visitor, deeply invested in Buddhism's flourishing, whose observations reflect his own religious commitments and the limits of what a traveling pilgrim could observe and accurately record, and his account should not be treated as an objective statistical survey of religious conditions across the whole subcontinent.

Students should resist a common but historically inaccurate narrative in which Buddhism simply and steadily declined from the Gupta period onward, disappearing from India by some early date. The evidence examined here, together with evidence in subsequent chapters covering the centuries that followed, shows Buddhist monastic, scholarly, and devotional institutions remaining significant across large parts of South Asia for many centuries after the Gupta period, with more complex and regionally varied processes of institutional transformation and, eventually, substantial decline in most of the subcontinent occurring considerably later and for a combination of causes examined more fully in later chapters, rather than as a straightforward consequence of Gupta-era Puranic Hindu revival.

Section 28.7

Jain Communities

Jain religious communities, whose origins and early development Chapter 12 examined, continued to flourish during the classical age as an active, organized, and historically significant tradition, not as a marginal survival overshadowed by Puranic Hindu and Buddhist developments. Jain teaching, centered on the path to liberation taught by a lineage of enlightened teachers known as Jinas or Tirthankaras, and organized around the ethical discipline of ahimsa (non-violence) alongside truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity or marital fidelity, and non-attachment, continued to guide both monastic and lay communities across this period.

Jain monastic communities during the classical age maintained rigorous ascetic discipline, including, for fully ordained monks, strict practices of non-violence extending to careful attention to avoiding harm to even the smallest forms of life, a discipline that shaped distinctive Jain practices around diet, movement, and daily conduct. Jain monastic organization during this period included both major lineages that would later be more formally distinguished, in later centuries, as Digambara ("sky-clad") and Shvetambara ("white-clad") traditions, differing in matters including monastic dress and certain points of scripture and interpretation; historians caution against treating this sectarian distinction as fully and formally crystallized at every point during the classical period in the same way it would become in later centuries, since the process of sectarian differentiation, like the processes examined for Puranic and Buddhist traditions in this chapter, unfolded gradually.

Lay Jain communities during this period included notable strength among merchant and trading communities in western and central India, including regions examined in Chapter 27 for their guild-based commercial activity, though this association should not be generalized into a uniform social profile for Gupta-period Jainism as a whole, since Jain communities also included agriculturalists, artisans, and patrons from other social backgrounds whose presence is documented less fully in surviving inscriptions. Jain merchants and lay patrons supported monastic communities, funded the construction and maintenance of Jain temples and images, and commissioned inscriptions recording their donations and religious commitments, evidence that allows historians to reconstruct, with reasonable confidence, patterns of Jain patronage and community organization even where continuous literary narrative is less abundant than for some other traditions.

Jain temple architecture and image production also developed during this period, with images of the Jinas, characterized by their distinctive meditative posture and iconographic markers, becoming increasingly widespread objects of veneration in Jain temples, paralleling in general form, though not in theological content, the temple and image-based worship examined for Vaishnava and Shaiva traditions in Sections 28.2, 28.3, and 28.5. As with Buddhism, students should understand Jain history during this period as one of continued institutional vitality, regional variation, and adaptation to a changing religious and economic landscape, rather than as a story of marginalization by an ascendant Puranic Hindu majority. Jain communities would continue to play a significant role in the commercial, intellectual, and religious life of western and central India for many centuries after the Gupta period, as later chapters of this textbook will show.

Section 28.8

Schools of Hindu Philosophy

Religious life in the classical age was not confined to worship, image, and narrative. It also included rigorous, technically sophisticated philosophical argument, conducted by scholars trained in careful reasoning, textual analysis, and formal debate. This section examines the classical systematization of philosophical traditions conventionally grouped, in later scholarly and traditional Indian classification, as six darshanas ("viewpoints" or "systems of vision"): Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta.

Historians must approach this six-school classification with the same chronological caution this chapter has applied throughout. These traditions did not emerge simultaneously, nor were they organized into a fixed, mutually exclusive system of six schools from their beginnings. Each tradition developed over a long period, often beginning with earlier textual seeds (some extending back, in seed form, well before the Gupta period, as earlier chapters on Vedic and Upanishadic thought have shown) and reaching more systematic, technically elaborated expression through foundational texts (commonly aphoristic sutra texts and their accompanying commentaries) whose composition or significant compilation is often associated with approximately the Gupta period or shortly before and after, though precise dating for many of these texts remains genuinely debated among specialists.

Nyaya, concerned chiefly with logic, epistemology (the theory of knowledge and valid means of knowing), and rigorous methods of debate, and Vaisheshika, concerned with a systematic categorization of the basic constituents of reality (substances, qualities, actions, and other categories), developed as closely related traditions addressing how knowledge is validated and what fundamentally exists. Samkhya, one of the oldest of these systematic traditions in its conceptual roots, offered a dualistic account of reality distinguishing consciousness (purusha) from material nature (prakriti), while Yoga, closely associated with Samkhya's metaphysics, systematized disciplined practices of meditation, ethical conduct, and bodily and mental training aimed at liberating consciousness from its entanglement with material nature — practices whose foundational systematic text, traditionally associated with the sage Patanjali, is generally placed at approximately this broad period, though, again, with genuine scholarly debate about precise dating. Mimamsa concerned itself chiefly with the correct interpretation and performance of Vedic ritual injunction, defending the authority and eternal validity of Vedic scripture and ritual obligation. Vedanta ("end of the Vedas"), building directly on Upanishadic philosophical material examined in Chapter 13, addressed the nature of ultimate reality (Brahman), the self (atman), and the relationship between them, laying groundwork for far more extensive and influential Vedantic philosophical elaboration in later centuries, examined in subsequent chapters of this textbook.

These six traditions did not operate in isolation from one another or from the devotional and Puranic developments examined earlier in this chapter. Philosophers trained in Nyaya methods of argument might defend Vaishnava or Shaiva theological positions; Mimamsa's defense of Vedic ritual authority connected directly to the continuing importance of Vedic sacrifice within Brahmanical religious life; and Vedanta's philosophical treatment of ultimate reality would, in later centuries, become closely intertwined with devotional Vaishnava and Shaiva theology in ways this textbook examines in later chapters. These philosophical schools also engaged, sometimes through direct debate and mutual critique, with Buddhist and Jain philosophical traditions, which developed their own sophisticated logical, epistemological, and metaphysical systems during the same broad period, examined briefly in Section 28.6 and more fully in specialized treatments beyond this textbook's scope.

The historical significance of this philosophical activity, for the purposes of this chapter, lies in what it demonstrates about the character of classical-age religious life: it was not solely a matter of temple ritual, image worship, and devotional narrative, however important those developments were, but also a matter of sustained, technically rigorous intellectual argument about the nature of knowledge, reality, causation, ritual obligation, and liberation, conducted within courtly, monastic, temple, and other scholarly settings discussed further in Chapter 29's treatment of Sanskrit cosmopolitan culture and scholarship.

Section 28.9

Religious Pluralism and Patronage

Having examined Puranic Hindu traditions, Vaishnavism, Shaivism, goddess worship, temple institutions, Mahayana Buddhism, Jain communities, and classical philosophy as largely distinct subjects, this section asks how all of these traditions coexisted within a single historical world, sharing courts, cities, trade routes, and often the same patrons.

Two equally misleading narratives should be avoided. The first narrative describes Gupta India as a uniquely and uncomplicatedly "tolerant" golden age in which all religious traditions coexisted in perfect, conflict-free harmony; this narrative, while capturing something real about the period's genuine pluralism, tends to flatten real tensions, competition for patronage and prestige, and occasional evidence of sectarian rivalry, and it risks projecting a modern ideal of religious tolerance onto a period whose own categories and concerns were considerably different. The second narrative describes the Gupta state as sponsoring a unified "Hindu" state religion that marginalized or suppressed Buddhist and Jain traditions; this narrative, examined more fully in the Historical Debate feature below, is not well supported by the inscriptional evidence, which shows Gupta and allied rulers, officials, and elites patronizing Vaishnava, Shaiva, Buddhist, and Jain institutions across the period, sometimes within the same reign, family, or even individual biography.

What the evidence does support is a picture of plural, strategic, and overlapping patronage. Rulers, provincial governors, guild leaders, merchants, and landholders directed religious patronage — land grants, temple and monastic endowments, image commissions, and inscriptional dedications — according to a combination of personal devotional commitment, political calculation, family tradition, regional custom, and the practical availability of religious institutions in a given locality. A single dynasty might include rulers personally devoted to Vishnu alongside others associated with Shiva; a single royal family might endow a Vaishnava temple while also supporting a Buddhist monastery within its territory; and merchant guilds, as Chapter 27 examined, patronized a range of religious traditions in different contexts as an expression of prosperity, social standing, and religious merit-making, though the evidence more securely supports this variety across the guild landscape as a whole than any single guild's routine, simultaneous patronage of multiple traditions at once.

This plural patronage existed alongside genuine competition. Religious traditions competed for royal favor, for control of or access to pilgrimage sites and sacred geography, for intellectual prestige in philosophical debate, and for the loyalty and material support of lay populations. Puranic and Shaiva literature of this period sometimes incorporated polemical material asserting the superiority of one deity or tradition over others; Buddhist and Jain texts likewise sometimes engaged critically with Brahmanical philosophical and ritual claims. Coexistence and competition, in other words, were not mutually exclusive conditions but two aspects of the same historically real, dynamic religious environment.

Royal association with particular religious traditions, examined earlier in this chapter for Vaishnava imagery under Chandragupta II, also served identifiable political functions connected to the themes of imperial kingship and legitimacy examined in Chapter 26. Portraying a ruler as favored by, or symbolically aligned with, a powerful and cosmically significant deity such as Vishnu or Shiva provided a language of legitimate authority that could resonate widely across a diverse and only loosely centralized empire, without requiring uniform religious conformity among the empire's subjects or even among the ruling family's own members across generations. This political dimension of religious patronage does not mean that royal devotion was purely cynical or insincere; historians generally treat royal religious patronage as reflecting some combination of genuine devotional commitment and political calculation, in proportions that are rarely fully recoverable from surviving evidence.

Section 28.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined the religious and philosophical transformations of the classical age not as the sudden creation of a single finished religious system but as a stage — an especially well-documented and historically consequential stage — within long, uneven, and regionally varied processes of religious change that had begun well before the Gupta period and continued, in different forms, for centuries afterward.

The Puranic argument of Section 28.1 established the broad devotional and textual framework — layered, continuously developing, and encyclopedic in scope — within which the more specific deity-centered traditions examined in Sections 28.2 through 28.4 operated. Vaishnavism and Shaivism, examined as substantial, independently significant traditions with roots predating the Gupta period, developed increasingly elaborated theology, iconography, and royal and popular patronage during this era, while goddess traditions demonstrated a parallel and equally important process of integration into wider Sanskritic frameworks without losing their often highly localized distinctiveness. None of these traditions displaced continuing Vedic ritual practice or Brahmanical social and legal authority; they developed alongside and in relationship with those older structures.

The institutional argument of Section 28.5 identified the permanent stone or brick temple as the era's most historically significant new religious institution, connecting directly to the economic and social structures examined in Chapter 27 while also representing a genuine development in religious practice centered on image consecration, ritual offering, and darshan. This institutional development did not replace older forms of sacred space and practice but added to and increasingly organized around itself a growing share of devotional religious life.

The comparative argument of Sections 28.6 and 28.7 established that Buddhist and Jain communities during this period should be understood through a framework of continued adaptation and institutional vitality rather than decline. Mahayana Buddhist doctrinal and devotional developments, continuing monastic scholarship, and evidence such as Faxian's pilgrim account demonstrate a flourishing, internally diverse Buddhist institutional world; Jain monastic discipline and, especially, Jain lay patronage among merchant communities demonstrate comparable continuity and adaptation. Both traditions would remain historically significant for many centuries beyond the scope of this chapter, as later volumes of this textbook will show.

The intellectual argument of Section 28.8 demonstrated that classical-age religious life included sustained philosophical argument, not only devotional practice, through the gradual systematization of the six darshanas addressing questions of knowledge, reality, causation, ritual, and liberation — questions that connected directly to, and sometimes directly informed, the devotional and doctrinal developments examined earlier in the chapter.

The synthesizing argument of Section 28.9 established this chapter's central interpretive framework: classical-age religious life was neither a uniform, conflict-free pluralistic idyll nor a unified state-sponsored religious order marginalizing rival traditions, but a genuinely plural, competitive, and interconnected environment in which royal courts, merchant guilds, and individual patrons supported a range of religious traditions across different contexts, for reasons combining devotional commitment, political calculation, family and regional custom, and the practical religious landscape available to them.

Chapter 29 now turns to the broader Sanskrit cosmopolitan culture, literary achievement, scientific and mathematical developments, and visual and artistic traditions of the classical age — developments closely related to, but distinct from, the specifically religious and philosophical transformations examined in this chapter, and developments that this chapter has deliberately left for Chapter 29's fuller treatment. Students should carry forward from this chapter a central lesson applicable to every subsequent period this textbook examines: religious traditions are never static, uniform, or created whole at a single moment by a single ruling authority; they are continuously reorganized, reinterpreted, and renegotiated by the many communities, patrons, and thinkers who sustain them.

Primary Evidence Window

The Toramana Inscription on the Eran Stone Boar

Evidence type: Monumental freestanding stone sculpture and dedicatory inscription · Site: Eran, present-day Madhya Pradesh · Date: the first regnal year of the ruler Toramana, around the turn of the sixth century CE; the exact absolute chronology remains debated among specialists · Patron named in the inscription: Dhanyavishnu, identified as the younger brother of the deceased Matrivishnu · Language: Sanskrit

Context: The colossal freestanding stone sculpture of Vishnu in his boar (Varaha) incarnation at Eran, standing several meters in height and covered with rows of carved figures representing sages and personified rivers along its body, carries an inscription dated to Toramana's first regnal year. Toramana was a ruler associated with the Huna challenge to Gupta imperial authority, a political history reserved for Chapter 30; this chapter is concerned only with what the inscription itself records about religious patronage. The text names Dhanyavishnu, rather than Toramana, as the person responsible for causing the stone religious structure associated with Narayana (a name of Vishnu) in his boar form to be made.

Description of the evidence: The monument depicts Vishnu's boar avatara rescuing the earth goddess from cosmic waters, a Puranic narrative examined in Section 28.2. The inscription dates the dedication to Toramana's first regnal year and identifies Dhanyavishnu as the donor who commissioned the religious foundation in memory of his elder brother, Matrivishnu.

Analysis: This monument allows historians to connect textual evidence for the developing avatara concept, examined in Section 28.2, to a securely inscribed material object, demonstrating how Puranic mythology was translated into monumental public art. It also shows that Vaishnava devotional patronage by regional elite donors continued across a significant political transition: the inscription names a reigning Huna-associated ruler for dating purposes while crediting an evidently local or regional donor, not the ruler himself, with the religious act. This is more historically informative than treating the monument as evidence of direct royal sponsorship, and it cautions against assuming that every prominent religious monument of this period reflects a reigning dynasty's personal devotional program. At the same time, the inscription reveals only the perspective and intentions of Dhanyavishnu and those who commissioned the record; it cannot tell historians how ordinary worshippers who visited the site experienced or understood the monument, a limitation this chapter's Everyday Life feature addresses further.

Historical Debate

Is "Hinduism" the Right Word for Classical-Age Religion?

The question: Should historians describe the Puranic, Vaishnava, Shaiva, and goddess-centered traditions examined in this chapter using the single term "Hinduism," or does that term misrepresent a more plural, less unified historical reality?

Interpretation A — "Hinduism" is a useful, if imperfect, umbrella term: Some historians argue that despite its later coinage and its association with later, more institutionally self-conscious religious identity, "Hinduism" remains a defensible term for describing the shared Sanskritic, Puranic, Vedic-derived religious world examined in this chapter, provided historians remain clear that it names a broad and internally diverse family of traditions rather than a single organized religion with fixed doctrine, clergy, or membership comparable to some other world religious traditions.
Interpretation B — "Hinduism" risks anachronism and false unity: Other historians argue that the term "Hinduism," which entered wide use considerably later and often in colonial-era contexts that sought to classify South Asian religion using categories borrowed from the comparative study of other world religions, risks imposing a false sense of doctrinal and institutional unity onto a classical-age reality that this chapter has shown to be genuinely plural: Vaishnava, Shaiva, and goddess traditions each possessed distinct textual, theological, and ritual identities, and many worshippers likely felt no need to subsume their specific devotional commitments under a single overarching religious label.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same body of inscriptional, textual, and art-historical evidence examined throughout this chapter; they differ chiefly in how much unifying weight they place on shared Sanskritic language, shared Puranic cosmology, and shared participation in overlapping ritual and pilgrimage practices, as against the evidence for distinct sectarian identity, competition, and regional variation.

Why it matters: This textbook uses more specific terms — Puranic Hindu traditions, Vaishnavism, Shaivism, goddess traditions — wherever precision allows, reserving "Hindu" and "Hinduism" as broader descriptive umbrella terms rather than as labels implying a single, unified religious institution, consistent with the Master Instructions' guidance on this point.

Historical Vocabulary
Purana and "Puranic"

Purana, literally "ancient" or "old," designates a genre of Sanskrit texts combining cosmology, genealogy, mythology, ritual instruction, and sectarian devotional material, compiled and expanded in layers over many centuries. "Puranic" is used by historians as an adjective describing the broader devotional, cosmological, and narrative religious culture associated with this literature, as distinct from earlier Vedic ritual religion and from the more specific deity-centered traditions (Vaishnavism, Shaivism) that developed within it.

Historians avoid treating any individual Purana as a single-authored, single-dated text, since different sections and recensions of the same Purana often reflect different historical periods and regional or sectarian interests. This chapter uses "Puranic" descriptively, to indicate the broad religious and literary world this literature represents, rather than implying uniform authorship or a single date of composition.

Historical Vocabulary
Bhakti and Darshana

Bhakti refers to devotion or loving, personal attachment directed toward a deity, expressed through worship, narrative, pilgrimage, vow, and image veneration. This chapter uses bhakti to describe devotional religious practice of the classical age while noting that the more intensely emotional, vernacular, and socially expansive bhakti movements examined in Chapter 48 developed considerably further in later centuries. Darshana carries two distinct but related meanings important to this chapter: in religious ritual, it refers to the reciprocal act of seeing and being seen by a deity's consecrated image, discussed in Section 28.5; in philosophy, it refers to a "viewpoint" or systematic school of thought, as in the six classical darshanas examined in Section 28.8. Students should distinguish these two related uses by context.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Vedic Sacrifice to Puranic Devotion

What Changed

  • Religious practice increasingly organized around personal, approachable deities (Vishnu, Shiva, goddesses) rather than around Vedic sacrificial ritual alone
  • Permanent stone and brick temples housing consecrated images became increasingly important sites of worship
  • Puranic narrative and cosmology became an increasingly accessible vehicle for religious instruction, reaching audiences beyond Sanskrit-literate ritual specialists
  • Philosophical traditions moved toward more systematic textual codification in foundational sutra texts and commentaries

What Stayed the Same

  • Vedic domestic and public sacrifice continued to be performed and valued
  • Brahmins retained ritual authority and continued to receive land-grant patronage
  • Buddhist and Jain monastic and lay traditions continued their own long-developing institutional lives
  • Religious life remained regionally and socially diverse rather than becoming uniform across the subcontinent

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents historians from describing the classical age as either a total religious rupture or an unchanged continuation of earlier Vedic practice, while still appreciating the genuinely significant long-term religious developments this chapter has traced.

Cause and Effect

Why Permanent Temples Multiplied

Long-term causes

Centuries-long development of devotional (bhakti) religious orientation and Puranic narrative and cosmology created sustained demand for spaces of image-centered worship, building on ritual and architectural traditions traceable to earlier periods.

Short-term causes

Gupta-era agrarian expansion, guild wealth, and land-grant practice, examined in Chapter 27, made substantial resources available for large-scale religious construction, while royal and elite patrons sought visible means of expressing piety and legitimacy.

Effect

Permanent stone and brick temples, such as those at Sanchi and Deogarh, and monumental images, such as the Udayagiri relief and the freestanding Eran sculpture, proliferated across northern and central India, increasingly organizing devotional religious practice.

This textbook's response

This development, traced through Section 28.5, is treated as one especially significant expression of the broader devotional and institutional transformations examined throughout this chapter, not as an isolated architectural trend.

Historical complexity: Temple construction did not proceed at a uniform pace or scale across every region, and many temples from this period have been lost, altered, or are known only through inscriptional reference rather than surviving structure, meaning the visible archaeological record likely understates the full extent of classical-age temple building.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religious Practice beyond Courts and Councils

Much of the evidence examined in this chapter — royal inscriptions, monumental sculpture, and philosophical treatises — documents elite religious life more fully than the religious lives of ordinary farmers, artisans, and laborers. Historians must therefore read this evidence with awareness of whose experience it best represents and reconstruct, more cautiously, what can be inferred about broader social participation in the developments this chapter examines.

Village and household religious practice likely combined elements examined throughout this chapter — devotion to Vishnu, Shiva, or a local goddess; participation in temple worship where temples were accessible; continued veneration of local shrines, sacred trees, and natural sites not necessarily connected to elite Sanskritic textual traditions; and participation in festivals and pilgrimage when resources and circumstances allowed. Artisans and craftspeople, some of whose skilled labor produced the very temples and images this chapter has examined, participated in this religious world both as workers commissioned by elite patrons and, presumably, as worshippers and devotees in their own right, though direct evidence for their personal religious experience is considerably harder to recover than evidence for the patrons who commissioned their work. Approaching this imbalance in the evidence honestly, rather than assuming elite religious life was simply representative of religious life generally, is an important historical skill this chapter asks students to practice.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transformation of Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and philosophical traditions during the fourth through sixth centuries CE, arguing throughout that this period should be understood as an especially consequential stage within long, uneven religious processes rather than as the sudden creation of a finished classical religious order.

The chapter began with Puranic Hindu traditions, examining the layered, centuries-long compilation of Puranic literature and its role in organizing an increasingly devotional, narrative-centered religious culture alongside continuing Vedic and Brahmanical practice. It then examined Vaishnavism and Shaivism as substantial, independently significant devotional and theological traditions, each with roots predating the Gupta period and each developing increasingly elaborated iconography, narrative, and royal and popular patronage during this era, including the developing concept of avatara and the diverse dimensions of Shiva's ascetic, householder, and cosmic character.

The chapter gave sustained attention to goddess traditions, showing how powerful local and regional goddesses were increasingly connected to wider Puranic and Sanskritic frameworks without losing their distinct regional identities, a pattern of integration without erasure that recurs throughout classical-age religious history.

The chapter identified the permanent stone or brick temple as the period's most historically significant new religious institution, connecting temple construction to the economic and social foundations examined in Chapter 27 while emphasizing the specifically religious significance of image consecration, ritual offering, and darshan, and insisting that temple worship supplemented rather than replaced older forms of religious practice.

Turning to Buddhist and Jain communities, the chapter argued firmly against narratives of Buddhist decline during this period, documenting continued Mahayana doctrinal elaboration, monastic scholarship, and lay devotion, supported by evidence including Faxian's pilgrim account. It found comparable institutional vitality among Jain monastic and, especially, merchant lay communities in western and central India, whose patronage patterns are documented through inscriptional evidence.

The chapter then examined the classical systematization of the six darshanas — Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta — as evidence that classical-age religious life included sustained, technically rigorous philosophical argument about knowledge, reality, ritual, and liberation, developing gradually over centuries rather than emerging as a fixed system at a single moment.

Finally, the chapter examined religious pluralism and patronage, rejecting both an idealized narrative of uncomplicated tolerance and a simplistic narrative of a unified state-sponsored Hindu religion, and instead describing a genuinely plural, competitive, and interconnected religious environment in which rulers, guilds, and individual patrons supported a range of religious traditions across different contexts, for a combination of devotional, political, and social reasons.

Throughout, this chapter has used specific terminology — Puranic Hindu traditions, Vaishnavism, Shaivism, goddess traditions — in preference to an undifferentiated "Hinduism" wherever greater precision serves historical understanding, while acknowledging in the Historical Debate feature that historians themselves continue to discuss the appropriate use of that broader umbrella term.

Chapter 29 now turns to the broader cultural achievements of the classical age — Sanskrit cosmopolitan literary culture, mathematics and astronomy, medicine, and visual art including the paintings of Ajanta — building on, but analytically distinct from, the specifically religious and philosophical developments this chapter has traced.

Key Terms

Purana
A genre of Sanskrit texts, compiled in layers over many centuries, combining cosmology, genealogy, mythology, and sectarian devotional material.
Bhakti
Personal, loving devotion directed toward a deity, expressed through worship, narrative, pilgrimage, and image veneration.
Avatara
The Vaishnava concept of Vishnu's periodic "descent" into the world in various forms to restore cosmic and moral order.
Vaishnavism
Devotional and theological traditions centered on the god Vishnu and his associated forms and avataras.
Shaivism
Devotional and theological traditions centered on the god Shiva, encompassing ascetic, householder, and cosmic dimensions.
Linga
An aniconic cylindrical form representing Shiva's presence and generative cosmic power, widely installed in shrines and temples.
Devi / Shakti
Terms referring to the Goddess and to divine feminine power, encompassing both pan-regional figures such as Durga and Lakshmi and numerous local goddess traditions.
Murti
A consecrated image of a deity, serving as the focus of temple ritual and darshan.
Mandira (Temple)
A permanent structure, typically of stone or brick, housing a consecrated deity image and serving as a center of organized worship.
Darshan
The ritually significant, reciprocal act of seeing and being seen by a deity's image.
Mahayana
A broad set of Buddhist doctrinal and devotional traditions emphasizing the bodhisattva ideal, developing alongside continuing non-Mahayana Buddhist traditions.
Bodhisattva
In Mahayana Buddhism, a being who seeks awakening out of compassionate commitment to the liberation of all sentient beings.
Vihara
A Buddhist monastery, serving as a center of monastic residence, scholarship, and textual transmission.
Stupa
A domed Buddhist architectural structure associated with the veneration of relics.
Jina / Tirthankara
An enlightened teacher in the Jain tradition whose teaching establishes the path to liberation.
Ahimsa
The principle of non-violence, central to Jain ethical discipline and also significant in Buddhist and some Hindu traditions.
Darshana (philosophical)
A systematic school or "viewpoint" of classical Indian philosophy, as in the six darshanas examined in this chapter.
Nyaya
A classical philosophical school concerned chiefly with logic, epistemology, and methods of valid reasoning and debate.
Vaisheshika
A classical philosophical school offering a systematic categorization of the basic constituents of reality.
Samkhya
A classical philosophical school offering a dualistic account of reality distinguishing consciousness from material nature.
Yoga (philosophical school)
A classical philosophical and practical tradition systematizing disciplined meditative and ethical practice, closely associated with Samkhya metaphysics.
Mimamsa
A classical philosophical school concerned with the correct interpretation and performance of Vedic ritual injunction.
Vedanta
A classical philosophical tradition, building on Upanishadic thought, addressing the nature of ultimate reality, the self, and their relationship.
Agrahara
A land grant made to Brahmins, often exempting the recipient from certain taxes, examined economically in Chapter 27 and as a foundation of religious patronage here.
Faxian
A Chinese Buddhist monk and pilgrim whose 399–412 CE journey through South Asia provides valuable, though outsider, evidence for Buddhist institutional life.
Varaha
The boar avatara of Vishnu, who rescues the earth goddess from cosmic waters, prominently depicted in the Gupta-period rock-cut relief at Udayagiri and the later freestanding sculpture at Eran, inscribed under Toramana.
Toramana
A ruler associated with the Huna challenge to Gupta authority (examined in Chapter 30) whose first regnal year dates the inscription on the Eran stone boar, though the religious dedication itself is credited to the donor Dhanyavishnu.
Dhanyavishnu
The donor named in the Eran boar inscription, identified as the younger brother of the deceased Matrivishnu, credited with causing the stone Varaha monument to be made.
Vasudeva-Krishna
An early devotional tradition centered on the deity Vasudeva-Krishna, later integrated into the wider Vaishnava framework as an avatara of Vishnu.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold together two ideas that can seem, at first, to be in tension: that the classical age witnessed genuinely significant religious transformation, and that this transformation did not create a single, finished, uniform religious order. Learning to see Puranic devotion, temple worship, Mahayana Buddhism, Jain community life, and classical philosophy as parts of one connected, competitive, and plural religious world — rather than as separate boxes to be checked off — is a skill this textbook will continue to ask students to practice whenever later chapters examine religious change in other periods.

The developments traced in this chapter — the expansion of Vaishnava and Shaiva devotion, the increasing integration of goddess traditions, the rise of the permanent temple, the continued vitality of Buddhist and Jain institutions, and the systematization of classical philosophy — did not occur because a single ruler or dynasty decreed them. They occurred because generations of worshippers, patrons, monks, philosophers, and craftspeople took up, reshaped, and passed on inherited traditions in response to the resources, opportunities, and questions available to them.

Students who understand that classical-age religion was plural rather than monolithic, that Buddhist and Jain communities continued to flourish rather than simply declining, and that devotional worship and rigorous philosophical argument developed side by side rather than in separate worlds, are well prepared to encounter the literary, scientific, and artistic achievements of Chapter 29 not as an isolated cultural flowering but as part of the same richly connected classical-age world this chapter has examined.

Chapter 29 now turns to that wider cultural world directly: Sanskrit cosmopolitan literary culture, the achievements of Kalidasa and other writers, developments in mathematics and astronomy associated with figures such as Aryabhata, medicine, sculpture and painting, and the paintings of Ajanta — achievements that grew from, and often expressed, the very religious and philosophical transformations this chapter has traced.
