Resistance before 1857

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 10 July 1806 at Vellore and in June 1855 at Bhognadih, different communities challenged East India Company power. Between these episodes lay decades of struggles over land, military service, religion, regional sovereignty, and the meanings of justice.

On 10 July 1806 Indian sepoys in the fortress of Vellore attacked European members of the garrison, in a confrontation shaped by military regulations, fears about religious practice, and the political memory of Mysore. Nearly half a century later, Santhal cultivators assembled at Bhognadih to challenge an order they associated with moneylenders, police, and unjust government. Between these episodes came resistance in almost every kind of landscape: fortified towns in the south, rice-growing districts in eastern Bengal, the villages of Odisha, western Indian hill passes, the Khasi Hills, and the agrarian settlements of the Rajmahal region. Their histories were not one continuous campaign; that diversity is the chapter’s subject.

Chapters 62–65 traced the Company’s political conquests and territorial expansion. Chapter 66 examined revenue settlements, credit, and economic change, and Chapter 67 investigated knowledge, law, education, and reform. Chapter 68 turns the inquiry around. It asks what colonized soldiers, chiefs, cultivators, religious communities, and peoples living in forest and hill regions did when they rejected particular claims of Company government. The answer includes armed uprisings, but also petitions, negotiation, refusal, migration, and efforts to preserve customary authority.

A movement’s social composition matters as much as the name later given to it. A military regiment’s grievance about service allowances was unlike a princely court’s defense of succession. A forest community struggling to retain control of routes and resources was not equivalent to a tenant confronting an eviction order. Those interests could overlap, and alliances sometimes brought different groups together, but historical comparison must begin with the institutions and objectives of the participants themselves.

Many colonial documents described insurgents as dacoits, fanatics, or disturbers of public order. Such language revealed official expectations and anxieties, not an impartial summary of the people being governed. The Company wrote investigations after military disasters, memoranda to defend repression, and reports to justify reorganizations. Its records are often detailed about movements of troops and seizures of property, yet much less reliable when they announce the motives of an entire community.

Other forms of evidence survive unevenly. Proclamations and petitions sometimes provide the stated demands of leaders. Recorded examinations, such as the testimony attributed to Kanhu Murmu, preserve parts of the insurgents’ political language through the filter of colonial questions and translation. Local literature, oral traditions, songs, and later public commemorations preserve memories that official archives marginalized. Each source requires criticism; no genre has an automatic monopoly on historical truth.

Geography shaped the course of conflict. Forts concentrated defending forces but exposed them to siege. Forests and hills aided mobile resistance while complicating the Company’s efforts to provide continuous policing and revenue collection. Roads that promised commerce could threaten the autonomy of the peoples through whose territory they passed. Rivers, markets, seasonal migration, and religious journeys helped news move among communities without a permanent central headquarters.

The chapter also pays attention to people who did not command armies. Households supplied food and refuge, women could govern, organize, teach, and sustain social networks, and local communities decided whether to support, evade, or assist armed parties. Collaboration and resistance were not permanently fixed identities. Decisions changed in response to losses, rivalries, promised concessions, and the danger of reprisals. That complexity is fundamental to understanding colonial power in practice.

The narrative ends in 1856, after the suppression of the Santhal Hul and the annexation of Awadh. Its purpose is to establish the varied experiences and recurrent tensions of Company rule, not to narrate the rebellion beginning in 1857. Chapter 69 opens Part XIII and will examine that uprising on its own terms. Earlier resistance helps explain the world in which it occurred without making the later crisis inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish military mutiny, princely resistance, agrarian protest, Adivasi uprising, and religious mobilization as different forms of political action.
  2. Explain the causes and consequences of the Vellore uprising of 1806 and the Barrackpore mutiny of 1824.
  3. Assess Sannyasi–Fakir resistance through religious, commercial, and revenue relationships in eighteenth-century Bengal.
  4. Reconstruct the political structures threatened by the Polygar Wars and the Paika Rebellion.
  5. Analyze Kittur, Bhil resistance, and the Jungle Mahals without imposing later colonial or nationalist categories.
  6. Explain the variety of land, forest, and political institutions among named Adivasi communities.
  7. Compare the Kol uprising, Khasi resistance, and Santhal Hul in causes, organization, and meanings of justice.
  8. Explain how cultivators used petitions, collective refusal, religious networks, and sometimes armed resistance.
  9. Examine the intersection of religious belief and agrarian conflict in Faraizi and Mappila movements.
  10. Evaluate the roles of women, households, local allies, and geographic networks in sustaining resistance.
  11. Critically assess Company inquiries, parliamentary debates, translated testimony, oral history, and later scholarship.
  12. Identify the antecedents of 1857 while preserving the chronological and political distinction of Chapter 69.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did the different communities resisting Company rule seek to recover, preserve, or transform?
  2. Why did a government dependent on Indian soldiers encounter opposition within its own regiments?
  3. How did Company definitions of law and property alter older relationships among chiefs, cultivators, and religious institutions?
  4. When is anticolonial a useful historical term, and when does it hide participants’ specific objectives?
  5. How did geography determine the strategic possibilities of forts, hills, forests, roads, and settlements?
  6. Why can economic grievances and religious conceptions of justice not always be separated?
  7. What differentiated the Kol, Khasi, and Santhal conflicts despite broad similarities in colonial pressure?
  8. How did women, kinship networks, household labor, and local allies shape resistance and its suppression?
  9. What do colonial archives reveal and conceal about the people they called rebels or dacoits?
  10. How did earlier resistance form part of the history before 1857 without making that rebellion inevitable?

Historical Context

Between the later eighteenth century and 1856 the East India Company acquired territories and dependencies through wars, alliances, successions, revenue arrangements, and treaties. Its capacity to collect revenue financed an army of largely Indian recruits, courts, police, and a growing bureaucracy. Yet sovereignty worked differently in established presidencies, newly annexed regions, princely territories, and hill societies. A military map can show a conquered district while concealing the negotiations required for its government to operate.

The chapter follows conflicts in Bengal, the Tamil south, Odisha, western India, Karnataka, Chotanagpur, the northeastern hills, Malabar, and the Rajmahal region. These areas did not share the same land tenure or institutions. The labels Adivasi, tribal, mutiny, and peasant rebellion come from different historical and historiographical traditions and must be used carefully. In some cases, modern collective names have acquired political meanings that nineteenth-century participants did not employ.

The primary evidence is uneven. British India Office records are especially rich for military events; local statements may survive only when copied into a colonial prosecution. Parliamentary debates expose disagreements within Britain over repression, but are often themselves partisan. Later commemorations preserve important historical memory without always resolving contested dates and detailed actions. Students should compare the creation, purpose, language, and transmission of sources.

The chronology ends before 1857. Chapter 68 analyzes the longer experience of resistance, suppression, and adaptation, while Chapter 69 explains the distinct military and political crisis of the following year. In keeping with the series’ approach, comparisons should illuminate mechanisms and differences, not replace regional histories with a single story of inevitable imperial conquest or inevitable national revolution.

Section 68.1

Resistance to the Company State: Context, Categories, and Sources

The label resistance names a relationship to power, not a single method or social class. Soldiers could refuse orders, desert, petition their officers, or take up arms. Peasants could withhold dues, appeal to a local chief, migrate, disrupt a moneylender's operations, or assemble in revolt. Fortified leaders defending a hereditary position were not socially equivalent to agricultural workers protesting eviction. Adivasi communities defended varying forms of control over fields, forests, mobility, and ritual life. Religious movements might organize adherents against particular landlords while accepting other forms of authority. Movements could change over time: negotiation became armed fighting when compromise failed, while insurgent leaders might later accept settlement terms. These distinctions are indispensable if comparative history is to explain rather than flatten experience.

The history cannot simply be divided between loyal people and rebellious people. Local rulers cooperated with Company armies in one campaign and opposed them in another; chiefs offered intelligence while retaining sympathy with dispossessed neighbors; villagers supplied food to one side under pressure and later assisted its rival. Alliances were often practical responses to overwhelming force, economic necessity, older rivalries, and the possibility of protecting some local autonomy. The Company's own Indian soldiers made the conquest of other Indian territories possible, yet armed resistance also emerged within its ranks. Military service, landholding, religion, and local politics continually crossed the supposed boundary between colonizer and colonized.

The sources are shaped by that unequal relationship. British and Company officials created reports to justify expenditure, explain their inability to anticipate disturbances, assign blame, or defend controversial punishment. They routinely used labels such as dacoit, marauder, fanatic, outrage, and disturbance for events they wished to suppress. Such words are historical evidence about officials' understandings but are not neutral categories for historians to adopt. Different authors in the same colonial government could disagree: a revenue officer might identify oppressive land demands while a military commander attributed events to criminal leadership. Petitions, proclamations, captured correspondence, and recorded testimonies can reveal different political languages, although they too may reach us only through translation and selective preservation.

Later historical writing requires comparable care. Nationalist accounts often honored regional leaders as precursors of independence, recovering lives marginalized by colonial records but sometimes assigning a common modern nationalism to movements whose explicit goals were local or dynastic. Subaltern and regional histories have reconstructed village participation, autonomous political reasoning, and the connections between everyday exploitation and collective mobilization. Community memory, including songs, ritual performance, commemorations, and family narratives, adds further dimensions but cannot always establish the precise wording or date of an event. Historians should compare forms of evidence rather than rank every official document as factual and every oral account as legend.

The chapter begins in the later eighteenth century and ends in 1856. It moves from early military and regional opposition to Adivasi insurgencies, peasant and religious mobilization, and the changing methods of colonial repression. The rebellion beginning in 1857 receives its own chapter because it was not merely an enlargement of one earlier movement. What the preceding decades reveal is a repeated difficulty in translating military conquest into accepted government. Resistance exposed the limits of colonial intelligence, the fragility of local alliances, and the fact that law and revenue systems remained contested political arrangements.

The comparison must also distinguish the chronological reach of Company power. A district acquired in the 1760s had decades of administrative experiments behind it by the time the Company intervened in a hill polity after 1826. Military technology, recruitment, mapping, transport, and the availability of court officials changed in the intervening years. Resistance therefore occurred not against one static institution but against an expanding and changing state. Even the term rebellion is relational: it presupposes an authority against which participants allegedly rebelled, though some communities disputed that the Company possessed legitimate sovereignty over them at all. Establishing that contested premise is the first task of historical analysis.

Historical Vocabulary

Mutiny, Rebellion, and Insurgency

Mutiny is a refusal of command within a military institution; rebellion can refer more broadly to collective opposition to an asserted government; insurgency emphasizes sustained challenges to political authority. These terms are analytical tools and also words used by rulers who regarded opponents as illegitimate. Students should ask what authority participants recognized and how they described their own actions.

Section 68.2

Early Military Mutinies: Vellore, 1806, and Barrackpore, 1824

At Vellore in the Madras Presidency, regulations introduced in 1805–1806 concerned a new form of headgear, facial hair, and the use of caste or religious marks on parade. Soldiers interpreted these changes amid fears that their rulers intended to compel religious conversion. Such fears were not demonstrably the government's purpose, but the distinction between official intentions and soldiers' perception is historically crucial. The fort also housed members of Tipu Sultan's family following the defeat of Mysore in 1799, and Company investigations suspected political connections between the former ruling household and disaffected troops. The presence of those princes shaped the revolt's symbolism without proving that they organized every stage of it.

Before dawn on 10 July 1806, Indian soldiers at Vellore attacked European members of the garrison and briefly gained control of important positions. Survivors and messengers brought news to Arcot; a relieving force led by Robert Rollo Gillespie entered the fort and brutally suppressed the rising. European casualties were substantial, as were the deaths of sepoys during the assault and reprisals, but figures vary across accounts and should not be turned into falsely precise totals. Courts of inquiry and military correspondence followed. The disputed dress rules were rescinded, senior officers faced political repercussions, and the Company scrutinized the connections it believed existed among the Mysore princes, the troops, and local agents.

The official record is especially revealing because its explanations were not uniform. Some officials emphasized religious injury; others highlighted political intrigue, command failures, rumors, or particular acts of indiscipline. The British Library's IOR/H/507 records contemporary July–August correspondence and inquiries, while IOR/H/510 includes extracts from Madras proceedings and later defenses of conduct. Their existence is verifiable from archival catalogues, but a catalogue summary is not an examination of the original pages. A critical historian asks which witnesses were questioned, who translated testimony, whether suspects spoke under duress, and how officers explained their own actions after a disaster that endangered their careers.

In 1824 a different crisis erupted at Barrackpore in Bengal. The 47th Bengal Native Infantry was ordered toward the Burmese war, and soldiers complained about transport, the practical burden of carrying equipment, and the terms under which they would be expected to serve. Anxiety about travel and established allowance arrangements interacted with shortages of suitable transport animals and perceptions that official instructions disregarded military custom. The dispute escalated into collective refusal and confrontation in November. Company forces employed artillery and other armed coercion against the regiment. Subsequent punishment and disbandment made the episode a warning to soldiers elsewhere, but it did not resolve the enduring issue of whether military demands respected the conditions on which Indian recruits believed they served.

Vellore and Barrackpore are linked by the structural vulnerability of a colonial army composed largely of Indian recruits, but their causes should not be compressed into a single religious grievance. Vellore joined dress regulations, religious apprehension, and the political memory of Mysore; Barrackpore concentrated on service obligations and the immediate requirements of a distant campaign. Both show why coercive discipline could restore order while damaging legitimacy. They also demonstrate a methodological point: calling an event a mutiny defines it from the standpoint of military command. Understanding why soldiers acted requires attention to their own claims, and those claims often appear only indirectly through records created by the institution they challenged.

The comparison also reveals the distinct evidentiary strengths of military history. Muster rolls, orders, correspondence, and proceedings make the sequence of events more readily reconstructable than the motives of each sepoy. The same regiment could contain soldiers who objected to a particular command, soldiers who opposed mutiny, and others whose decisions changed during confrontation. When an inquiry summarized the regiment's collective sentiments, it could conceal disagreement. Neither Vellore nor Barrackpore shows that the entire Company's Indian army shared identical views. Indeed, the suppression of resistance relied on troops who continued to obey, a fact fundamental to the colonial military system's resilience.

Section 68.3

Sannyasi and Fakir Resistance in Bengal, c. 1760s–1800

The Company's acquisition of the Bengal diwani in 1765 altered the means through which revenue was collected and movement policed. Officials increasingly interpreted armed itinerancy as a threat to secure taxation and commercial order. Recurrent violence, crop failures, and the famine of 1770 made rural conditions more precarious, although famine should not be treated as a sufficient explanation for every confrontation. Tax demands, restrictions on access to customary routes and resources, conflict with revenue agents, and attempts to control armed groups contributed to the changing situation. A chief who had previously negotiated payments with a provincial authority could find that a Company official now classified the same demand as extortion.

Majnu Shah is prominent in accounts of organized fakir resistance. His networks operated across northern Bengal and adjoining areas, drawing on relationships among followers, villages, and religious establishments. Other individuals and groups acted independently or cooperated episodically, and the duration and geographic reach of action varied. Fighting included attacks on collections and establishments connected with revenue administration, skirmishes with Company detachments, and movement through terrain where officials could not easily project continuous force. Such tactics benefited from knowledge of paths, rivers, villages, and seasonal conditions, but cannot be presumed to imply a permanently coordinated regional army.

Company correspondence often used the vocabulary of robbery for these expeditions. Violence and forcible collection did occur, and victims could include local people, so an adequate account must not romanticize every act. Nevertheless, the legal label assigned by the government concealed a political dispute about who could command revenue, legitimate armed protection, and the right to travel. Existing religious affiliations also do not explain every decision. Ascetics and fakirs could cooperate across religious lines; local villagers might support them for practical reasons; and separate groups could compete with one another. The social composition was therefore more complicated than a narrative of a single Hindu or Muslim religious revolt.

A particular difficulty is identifying participants' articulated goals. Some later narratives treat the movement as an early freedom struggle, while colonial materials depict predatory bands. Neither characterization, used alone, establishes the intentions of those involved. Historians compare correspondence, descriptions of routes and financing, local traditions, and the chronology of administrative restrictions. They also ask whether sources composed long after the events have consolidated distinct confrontations into one recognizable story. Respect for resistance does not require attributing to eighteenth-century actors the institutions or language of a nineteenth- or twentieth-century nation-state.

By the turn of the nineteenth century the particular patterns of Sannyasi–Fakir armed opposition had diminished as Company military and administrative authority expanded, though no single date marks the end of every related network. The case reveals a defining feature of early colonialism: conquest changed the meaning of established economic and religious practices. Activities previously negotiated with local rulers could be criminalized under a new government claiming sole authority over taxation and organized force. The same tension would reappear in different forms among fortified southern chiefs and village military communities, even where the participants shared neither faith nor organizational tradition.

These episodes invite a wider comparison with the history of early Company revenue. Control of Bengal's fiscal resources gave officials the means to maintain troops, but claiming public security required interventions that disrupted earlier arrangements for armed mobility. A state that treated all unauthorized collection as illegal was asserting a far-reaching monopoly over revenue and coercion. That monopoly could protect cultivators against predation, yet it also denied older institutions the means of financing themselves. Historians should therefore ask not only who attacked Company stations, but also who gained or lost protection when itinerant groups were restricted, and whether communities assessed the change uniformly.

Section 68.4

The Polygar Wars and Resistance in Southern India, c. 1799–1805

Veerapandiya Kattabomman, associated with Panchalankurichi, became a prominent opponent during disputes over tribute and demands by Company officers. The confrontation intensified in 1798–1799. Company troops attacked his stronghold; Kattabomman was captured and executed in October 1799. Subsequent commemorations have made him a symbol of anticolonial courage, but interpretation should begin with the concrete dispute over revenue, recognition, and the obligations of a local chief. Accounts of speeches attributed to him often derive from later dramatic and popular representation rather than stenographic contemporary witnesses. His death did not end local opposition, and the destruction of fortified power created new incentives for others to form alliances.

Resistance expanded in 1800–1801 and involved leaders associated with Sivaganga and neighboring areas, including Periya Marudhu and Chinna Marudhu. The brothers had experience in the politics of Sivaganga and connections to the restoration of Queen Velu Nachiyar's authority in an earlier period. Their project attempted to mobilize discontented chiefs, soldiers, and communities beyond one estate. A proclamation posted at Tiruchirappalli and Srirangam in June 1801 has long been associated with Chinna Marudhu. Later printed renditions call for unity against foreign domination, but their exact wording, language of transmission, and relationship to any surviving original require documentary caution. The proclamation is evidence of an attempt to frame resistance in wider political terms, not proof of modern national organization.

The fighting was shaped by forts, local supply networks, forests, and the ability to attract military retainers whose livelihood depended on older political arrangements. Company forces combined siege operations with the recruitment of local allies and intelligence from those hostile to the resisting chiefs. A coalition could disrupt communication or threaten isolated posts but remained vulnerable when Company detachments concentrated, controlled routes, or secured cooperation from rival authorities. The defeat of Sivaganga resistance in 1801 was followed by executions and confiscations; the Marudhu brothers were executed in October of that year. Campaigns and pacification measures continued in adjoining areas into the early nineteenth century. The outcome was not only a military victory but a reduction of independent regional military power.

The politics of commemoration illuminate the source problem. In local memory the polygars and Marudhu leadership became embodiments of resistance to domination, while Company reports tended to present campaigns as restoration of lawful order against refractory chiefs. Each interpretation selects aspects of a complex conflict. A rigorous account should document the Company's expansion of revenue and judicial claims, the existing inequalities within chiefly systems, and the interests of villagers who had to provide labor, grain, or shelter to armed men. The fact that a chief opposed colonial rule does not mean every inhabitant of his territory supported his leadership or benefited from it.

The Polygar Wars demonstrate that colonial military expansion had to overcome indigenous structures of authority and armed service. Once forts were dismantled and hereditary claims narrowed, regional society did not immediately become administratively uniform: older loyalties, local credit networks, and memories of dispossession persisted. The wars provide a comparison with Odisha's paikas and Kittur's succession conflict, where defenders of established political rights also confronted a government that claimed ultimate power to define legitimate authority. They equally caution against a purely dynastic narrative, since chiefs depended on recruits, cultivators, artisans, and women whose histories are far less visible than those of celebrated leaders.

Military transformation also had consequences for service households. Dismantling a palaiyam meant more than removing a chief from a fort: scribes lost patrons, artisans lost commissions, soldiers lost employers, and villagers faced new officials collecting dues. Some men entered Company service or transferred allegiance; others remained attached to displaced households. These possibilities help explain why resistance was uneven even within one former chiefdom. They also show why a campaign's formal conclusion did not end all political consequences. When researching the region students should compare Company administrative histories with Tamil narratives, inscriptions, and local commemorations rather than taking a single celebratory or punitive account as exhaustive.

Section 68.5

The Paika Rebellion of 1817: Khurda, Bakshi Jagabandhu, and Resistance in Odisha

Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra Bhramarbar Ray, remembered as Bakshi Jagabandhu, was a former commander associated with the Khurda establishment. Accounts link his role to loss of land or revenue rights and to opposition among dispossessed military and landed groups. His prominence should not obscure the broader coalition that formed in 1817. Paikas, village leaders, cultivators, and participants from Kandha communities in the Ghumusar region took part for reasons not always identical. People who had lost customary authority were joined by households burdened by new demands. Explaining this alignment requires showing how political subordination and agrarian pressures affected each other rather than treating the rebellion as a single protest against taxation.

In March 1817 insurgents moved against Company positions and symbols of its authority. The rising spread from areas connected to Ghumusar and Khurda toward Puri, where religion and the status of the Jagannath temple were important to regional political legitimacy. Roads, forests, temples, and local settlements affected the movement of armed groups. Company officers confronted an opposition able to exploit knowledge of the area and draw support from several social networks. The initial phase took authorities by surprise, demonstrating that victory in 1803 had not created reliable political consent. Troops subsequently retook key locations, used intelligence and local alliances, and dispersed organized groups.

The principal armed movement was suppressed, but the aftermath extended beyond a single season. Jagabandhu remained beyond immediate Company control for years before accepting terms in the 1820s. The government altered elements of its administrative approach while continuing to assert ultimate sovereignty. Local memories of the uprising persisted, and the paika identity came to signify a defense of regional dignity. The combination of military suppression and negotiated accommodation resembles methods employed elsewhere: officials sought to remove the capacity for coordinated resistance while securing enough local cooperation to govern districts they could not occupy permanently with large forces.

Debate over whether 1817 should be called a first war of Indian independence illustrates both the importance of remembering Odisha and the danger of historical ranking. Opposition to British power had occurred earlier in Bengal, southern India, and other regions; moreover, the coalition's participants were engaged in a variety of disputes regarding Khurda's political order, land, and revenue. Describing them as defending regional sovereignty does not diminish their achievements. Rather, it makes visible their actual institutions, alliances, and grievances. The significance of the Paika Rebellion lies in the scope of its challenge after conquest and in its demonstration that newly governed populations could mobilize well beyond the circle of a displaced ruler.

A further question concerns the social outcomes of restoration. Had the rebels prevailed, would hereditary privileges, peasant obligations, and relations between forest communities and courtly elites have remained unchanged? The archives rarely allow a definitive answer. This uncertainty matters because it prevents historians from equating resistance to colonial power with an identical program of equality for all participants. The movement instead supplies a concrete example of the multiple stakes of political transformation: command, ritual authority, agricultural resources, and local recognition were bound together. The contest was over who could define those relationships after 1803.

The salt question illustrates how distinct forms of government intervention could converge. Controlling a commodity's manufacture or distribution affected prices and livelihoods, while restrictions on established production could become symbols of foreign interference. The specific measures changed over time, and the Company did not invent all existing restrictions, so the history requires chronology rather than broad generalization. In the same way, Jagabandhu's dispossession must be related to the administration of his particular estate, not presented as though every paika held identical rights. These distinctions make the coalition's breadth more remarkable: people with different institutional positions nevertheless found grounds for common action.

Paika is associated with martial service and landholding in particular Odisha political institutions. Palaiyakkarar refers to regional chiefs in southern India who held military and revenue claims; Company records often called them polygars. Neither term is a generic synonym for a peasant or soldier, and their regional histories must not be conflated.

Section 68.6

Kittur, Rani Chennamma, and Sangolli Rayanna: Succession and Regional Sovereignty, 1824–1831

Rani Chennamma, the leading figure in the resistance, defended the court's claim to determine its own succession and administration. In 1824 tensions culminated in armed confrontation with Company representatives and troops. The defenders initially resisted successfully, and the death of the Company's local political agent St John Thackeray became a major episode in colonial accounts. The Company's military superiority was not sufficient to prevent these first reverses. The fort's defense and the political authority of the queen depended upon local officers, garrison members, and networks of support that made her more than an individual heroic figure. Negotiations and disputes about prisoners also shaped the conflict, revealing differences between the Company's asserted legal rights and those accepted at Kittur.

A larger Company operation ultimately defeated the defenders. Chennamma was imprisoned at Bailhongal, where she died in 1829. The defeat did not eliminate opposition: Sangolli Rayanna, associated with the Kittur forces, continued armed resistance through smaller mobile operations. His methods reflected a different balance of force once the defended court had fallen. Rather than relying on a fort and a recognized administration, his supporters operated through rural relationships, knowledge of terrain, and attempts to unsettle Company authority. He was captured and executed in 1831. The continuing campaign illustrates the importance of distinguishing a ruling house's military defeat from the end of allegiance to its political project.

One major chronological error must be avoided. Dalhousie's later doctrine of lapse, commonly associated with annexations from the late 1840s, did not determine the events of 1824 at Kittur. Company officials were already intervening in dynastic successions and disputing adoptions; those earlier practices created precedents but were not a retrospective application of a policy later articulated with greater prominence. This distinction matters when evaluating the changing forms of paramountcy. Sovereignty could be eroded through individual decisions, treaties, residency supervision, and force before a named doctrine was invoked in later disputes.

Kittur's memory has been shaped by commemorations that honor Chennamma as a woman resisting a powerful colonial regime. That recognition is historically meaningful, but a scholarly account must also explain the interests of the adopted heir, court factions, military followers, local peasants, and Company officials. The danger of biography-centered narratives is not that they praise courage; it is that they replace an institutional conflict with a simple duel between a queen and empire. The choice of what records survived also matters. Company accounts preserve officers' reports and military operations more consistently than the voices of rural supporters who continued to supply Rayanna's network.

The Kittur case thus links the southern wars over hereditary power with the later annexations examined in Chapter 65 and the crisis approaching 1857. It demonstrates how an apparently technical question of inheritance was inseparable from military power and political legitimacy. It also adds a different kind of female leadership to the historical record: Chennamma exercised sovereign authority in a contested court rather than merely supporting a movement directed by men. Her case will be compared with more dispersed forms of women's participation in §68.16, where evidence comes less from governmental correspondence and more from community histories.

Another important distinction concerns adoption itself. In South Asian ruling households adoption could establish succession under established traditions, but recognition by a superior power might still be politically disputed. Company officials increasingly treated such recognition as a matter of their own discretion. A disagreement over an heir could thus become a test of paramountcy: who had the final right to declare the ruler legitimate? The Kittur dispute shows that this question predated any later standardized annexation doctrine. It also helps explain why other princely households viewed Company intervention anxiously even when they had not yet faced direct annexation. Succession was an institution of government, not merely domestic law.

Section 68.7

Bhil Communities, Western India's Frontier, and Resistance to Colonial Expansion

The defeat of the Marathas in 1818 altered the political setting. Company officers attempted to police roads, establish revenue authority, and reduce armed activity categorized as predation. Chiefs who had negotiated service or payments with several powers faced a government asserting exclusive jurisdiction. Administrative campaigns could threaten livelihoods, customary revenues, and freedom of movement even when officers described them as measures of public safety. The resulting resistance was not one Bhil rebellion, but a sequence of locally distinct actions involving different leaders and interests. Treating all Bhil groups as a single homogeneous enemy repeats a classificatory error embedded in colonial reports.

The terrain shaped conflict. Mountain routes, forests, small fortified places, and knowledge of seasonal water and paths enabled mobile groups to avoid concentrated Company forces. Detachments could seize settlements or pursue identified leaders without necessarily gaining permanent control of the wider landscape. Colonial proclamations offered rewards for cooperation and threatened punishment for attacks on communications and revenue. Yet the administration also recognized that military pursuit alone was expensive. Officers negotiated with chiefs, provided allowances, and sought to recruit local men into supervised forces where their knowledge might strengthen colonial policing rather than opposition.

Accommodation could be a means of preserving community interests under new conditions rather than proof that earlier resistance had lacked legitimacy. A leader accepting an allowance might secure continued influence over followers, even while losing the capacity to negotiate independently with multiple rulers. Recruitment could provide wages and status to men whose older military opportunities had diminished, but it also placed some local groups in the service of state campaigns against others. The distinction between repression and incorporation was therefore porous. Colonial government combined punishment, dependency, patronage, and delegated security in efforts to translate episodic control into durable administration.

Descriptions of Bhil communities in colonial reports often attributed resistance to inherent lawlessness or supposed cultural backwardness. Such language justified intrusive policing and obscured the changes wrought by conquest. Nonetheless, the historian must not reverse the stereotype by treating every armed raid as an explicitly anticolonial political act. Interpersonal disputes, competition among chiefs, resource scarcity, and older military habits could also be involved. The analytical task is to identify which actions arose from particular decisions about revenue and government, and which reflected conflicts that predated the Company's arrival but were now governed under new legal categories.

The western Indian case shows how different frontier environments complicated colonial rule. The Company sought regular taxation and unimpeded commerce, yet its authority depended on negotiations with people whose power grew from mobility, military service, and environmental knowledge. For communities affected by the campaigns, the distinction between an independent local chief and a state-appointed security intermediary could be decisive. The pattern of changing alliances has parallels with Company policy in the Khasi Hills, while the material concerns over land and customary resources foreshadow the distinct history of the Kol and Santhal uprisings. Similar methods of negotiation and coercion should not be mistaken for identical regional histories.

Environmental history adds another dimension. Forest access could support fodder, fuel, food, hunting, and minor produce that did not always pass through cash markets. Changes to policing and recognized property could affect these resources without producing a conventional revenue dispute. Colonial descriptions of uncultivated land often underestimated its economic importance to residents. A route protected for merchant traffic might cut across territory where local communities exercised customary control. The government defined security through the movement of commerce and troops; inhabitants might define it through reliable access to land, recognized chiefly mediation, and freedom from intrusive patrols. These competing definitions made policy conflict politically charged.

Section 68.8

The Jungle Mahals, Bhumij Resistance, and the Colonial Label “Chuar”

Contemporary records used the term Chuar, often pejoratively, for diverse groups participating in late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century uprisings. It did not designate one uniform people, and modern historians should not reproduce it without explanation. Bhumij communities were central in parts of the region, but the resistance also involved displaced or threatened landholders, cultivators, and retainers with different claims. Revenue reassessments, changes in intermediary rights, debt, and disputes over forest use could bring these groups into temporary alliance. The result was neither purely an ethnic conflict nor solely a landlord protest. It was a cluster of struggles over the ways colonial authority reordered an established regional society.

Forest cover, difficult routes, scattered settlements, and the connections of leaders to local populations made conventional military suppression difficult. A garrison's occupation of a town did not guarantee that revenue could be collected reliably in surrounding districts. Rebels could move among settlements, attack symbols of Company administration, seize supplies, and avoid prolonged confrontation with better-armed forces. Villagers might provide shelter willingly or under pressure, while others sought protection from raids. Officials used military expeditions, intelligence, rewards, and administrative changes to disrupt networks of support. The record's emphasis on violence should be read alongside evidence of the disputes that made such mobilization possible.

Local leaders' motives must be reconstructed individually. Some defended hereditary offices or established rights to dues, while others protested the economic burdens generated by new assessments and transfers of land. These goals could coincide during fighting but diverge once negotiations began. A settlement restoring an allowance to a chief might not relieve a tenant's rent burden; a reduction in taxation could leave contested local authority unresolved. The diversity of claims explains why the history cannot be represented simply as rebels versus the Company. Different people opposed different aspects of the new political order, and some collaborated with officials against local rivals.

The colonial vocabulary created lasting difficulties. Once reports categorized opponents as Chuars, dacoits, or habitual disturbers, those labels could be repeated by later administrators as though they constituted accurate ethnography. The terms reveal the governing state's demand to distinguish ordered cultivation from disorderly mobility and recognized authority from illegitimate force. Historians therefore examine land registers and judicial correspondence together with regional traditions, archaeological evidence, and local histories. The aim is not to remove conflict from the past, but to restore the political and economic context that the label tended to suppress.

Placed beside Kittur and Odisha, the Jungle Mahals challenge any map that marks Company control as complete whenever an army gained formal sovereignty over a district. Administrative power had to be remade through repeated confrontations, compromises, and local intermediaries. These struggles also prepare the reader for the Kol uprising: the expansion of outside property claims, policing, and debt relationships could produce profound conflicts in societies where land and political authority were closely connected. The movements were not identical, but the comparison reveals a persistent problem of colonial expansion into regions whose legal and social organization did not fit Company assumptions.

Time also matters in the Jungle Mahals. The episodes often grouped under the same heading occurred amid successive shifts in Company revenue policy and political alliances. A protest in the 1760s could not have been responding to precisely the same administrative rules as a confrontation decades later. Later histories that merge them into one continuous rebellion create a recognizable regional narrative but risk obscuring changing leaders and grievances. Students should reconstruct a localized chronology from identifiable records before claiming a single coherent organization. Doing so also enables comparison with Chotanagpur, where a different set of nineteenth-century legal and landholding arrangements contributed to rebellion.

Section 68.9

Adivasi Land, Forests, Customary Authority, and Colonial Transformation

Landholding arrangements were equally diverse. Some villages recognized corporate rights grounded in settlement by particular lineages, alongside individual cultivation and obligations to local chiefs. Other societies combined forest access, shifting agriculture, settled fields, and seasonal labor. The rights to graze animals, gather fruit, collect timber, hunt, or clear land could be shared among households under customary restrictions. There was no single Adivasi tenure system. Colonial legal instruments, however, tended to ask who was the proprietor, who paid government revenue, and what boundaries could be entered in a register. Such questions made some rights visible while rendering others difficult to prove to an official unfamiliar with local history.

The expansion of revenue settlements introduced new intermediaries and opportunities for credit and accumulation. Moneylenders and traders provided cash, grain, and goods that were often useful in agricultural expansion and new markets. Yet interest, disputed accounts, pledges of produce, and pressure from landlords could also transform dependency into alienation of land or labor. Police officers and court personnel, whose fees and decisions were difficult for villagers to challenge, could compound these effects. Economic change was therefore connected to political power: a local grievance that might once have been heard by a village council or chief could now pass through a distant court operating in an unfamiliar language and under rules enforced by armed agents.

Forest and hill societies were also affected by roads, military passage, and commercial extraction. Company officers sought communications between distant territories and access to markets or frontier posts. A road could promise trade while threatening a community's control over who crossed its land. Policing of movement could criminalize forms of hunting, travel, or armed escort regarded locally as legitimate. Changes in forest access and the status of military chiefs altered both livelihood and sovereignty. The issue was not simply material loss: the right to decide how territory was used was itself a form of political authority.

Religious dimensions need explanation on their own terms. Sacred groves, ancestral relations, community spirits, ritual specialists, festivals, and moral obligations provided ways of interpreting unjust government and organizing action. They were not merely disguises for economic grievances. A community might understand invasion and exploitation as violations of a divinely sanctioned order, while simultaneously demanding relief from moneylenders or police. Historians who translate all sacred claims into class language may miss why participation was compelling, how leaders established authority, and what kind of justice rebels intended to create. Conversely, describing resistance as irrational religious excitement ignores the obvious political and fiscal disputes recorded in contemporary accounts.

Relations with outsiders were never exclusively hostile. Chiefs negotiated recognition and allowances, traders made agreements with village families, and residents sought protection from rival forces. Some communities benefited from particular markets while others bore the costs. During uprisings, household decisions concerning refuge, provisions, or cooperation could depend on local histories and immediate security. Colonial authorities frequently depended on information and service supplied by residents of the very territories they claimed to be pacifying. Such arrangements explain why social communities cannot be divided into a universally resistant indigenous population and universally collaborating outsiders.

The cases that follow illustrate three different political situations: Chotanagpur, where land alienation and altered chiefly relationships were decisive; the Khasi Hills, where roads and the sovereignty of hill polities stood at the center of conflict; and Damin-i-Koh, where agrarian settlement, commercial extraction, policing, and religious reform converged in the Santhal Hul. Their shared feature is not one ethnicity or ideology but the transformation of local authority under expanding Company rule. Comparison permits generalization only when the distinctive institutions, causes, and aspirations of each movement remain visible.

A second methodological difficulty concerns the term customary. It can suggest a timeless practice, yet village institutions themselves changed through migration, marriage, new cultivation, alliances, and disputes. Communities were not static guardians of an untouched past. Some members could support new land registrations to protect their holdings, while others feared that written recognition of one family's claim would extinguish shared access. Colonialism often intensified such conflicts by giving particular classifications legal force. Explaining agency requires recognizing that people evaluated the opportunities and dangers of administrative change differently, even when they belonged to the same named community and shared a broad commitment to autonomy.

Historical Vocabulary

Adivasi, Diku, and Hul

Adivasi is an important modern umbrella term with historically variable applications. Diku has been used in accounts of Chotanagpur for outsiders associated with exploitative relationships. Hul is the name commonly applied to the 1855–1856 Santhal uprising. The words belong to different social and historical contexts and should be defined rather than treated as interchangeable symbols.

Section 68.10

The Kol Uprising of 1831–1832: Chotanagpur, Land Alienation, and Colonial Rule

During the early nineteenth century, outsiders described in records as dikus, including certain landlords, farmers of revenue, creditors, and agents, gained economic and administrative positions in portions of the region. The term did not designate a single religious or ethnic enemy, and the practices of particular individuals mattered more than a universal contrast between locals and migrants. Increased rent, forced labor, indebtedness, pressure on customary rights, and the perceived failure of colonial justice accumulated into grievances. These grievances intersected with conflicts over political appointments and interference with local rulers. The result was a crisis of legitimate authority, not simply a spontaneous refusal to pay an assessment.

Violence spread in 1831–1832 across affected districts of Chotanagpur. Munda, Oraon, Ho, and other participants were involved in different areas and phases; the colonial label Kol grouped them together in a way that can conceal distinct objectives and leadership. Budhu Bhagat figures prominently in many accounts, while other leaders organized followers within their own territorial networks. Attacks targeted houses and establishments associated with oppressive intermediaries and the new order, including property and people identified with outside power. As in other uprisings, this violence affected individuals who had varied degrees of personal responsibility for larger structures of coercion. Its occurrence must be narrated without converting entire communities into a collective aggressor.

The terrain of wooded hills and dispersed villages initially hindered Company operations. Armed groups moved through settlements where kinship and local knowledge could provide support. Colonial forces gathered detachments, coordinated with officials and local intermediaries, and used superior firepower and intelligence to break resistance. The suppression involved substantial killing, pursuit, and punishment; published figures differ and should not be reproduced as uncontested totals. The rebuilding of authority required more than battlefield victory, because disputes about land and district government survived the defeat of organized resistance. The rebellion became part of the case for changes in administration and special treatment of the region.

Surviving sources require care. The Bihar State Archives identifies an 1832 document concerning insurrection in Chota Nagpur, while British administration generated reports designed to account for the disruption. Those records reveal official categories and sometimes describe abuses but cannot by themselves convey every participant's motives. Later community histories and regional scholarship restore neglected actors, although they must be assessed for chronology and the purposes of commemoration. Jagdish Chandra Jha's study of the uprising offers a scholarly starting point for reconciling government documentation with local contexts. A source window should not invent an eyewitness declaration merely because the archive catalogue announces an insurrection file.

The chronology also separates the Kol uprising from Birsa Munda's Ulgulan at the turn of the twentieth century. The two histories share concerns over land, autonomy, and colonial intervention but occurred under different administrative, religious, and political conditions. Retrospective comparison is legitimate; conflation is not. Nor should one assume that the 1831–1832 movement was designed as a precursor of 1857. Its actors confronted the specific local rearrangements of power that accompanied Company rule, and their claims are best understood within that setting.

Chotanagpur thus illustrates the central problem of colonial legal authority in regions where property and governance had long been intertwined. A state that promised predictable land rights could assist powerful intermediaries and undermine locally recognized obligations. Courts and registers did not merely describe existing arrangements; they could strengthen particular claims and weaken others. Violence erupted when those affected concluded that regular remedies no longer protected them. The uprising's suppression demonstrated the reach of Company force, while its causes revealed the limits of government based on categories that made the social relationships of the governed difficult to recognize.

The rebellion also raises the question of how communities understood the entry of newcomers. Economic contact had long involved exchange between hills and plains, and opposition cannot be attributed simply to the presence of strangers. Conflict sharpened when people using new legal or administrative advantages displaced locally recognized claims, imposed dues, or employed police power against established residents. The term diku becomes politically significant in this context, identifying oppressive relationships rather than a timeless prohibition on outside contact. Distinguishing commercial integration from coerced alienation makes the causes clearer and avoids reproducing a romantic opposition between allegedly isolated tribes and the modern economy.

Section 68.11

Khasi Resistance, U Tirot Sing, and the Northeastern Frontier, 1829–1833

David Scott and other political officers negotiated with Khasi leaders, including U Tirot Sing of Nongkhlaw, about permission to build the proposed route. Negotiations concerned not only the road but also claims over adjoining territories and the conditions on which particular chiefs would consent. Some leaders saw advantages in trade or in British assistance against rivals; others feared that the route would enable troops, tax claims, and permanent interference. Because the hills contained distinct polities, the response was not unanimous. A specialist account must name the community and chief concerned rather than narrating a single Khasi decision to accept or reject British control.

The breakdown of arrangements led to conflict in 1829. A Company post at Nongkhlaw was attacked in April, and British officers were killed. Company expeditions followed, but confronting an opponent in rugged terrain proved difficult. Groups associated with Tirot Sing used local knowledge to disrupt communications and evade sustained engagement, while Company troops attempted to hold routes and seek allies among other hill chiefs. The war exposed the difference between securing official consent on paper and exercising practical control through difficult country. A road that administrators called an improvement had become a symbol of unresolved political sovereignty.

The opposing parties did not have equal resources, and military pressure eventually undermined organized resistance. Accounts place Tirot Sing's surrender or capture in 1833 and his subsequent confinement in Dhaka, where he died in 1835. Some Khasi communities continued to negotiate or resist aspects of Company influence after his defeat. Later Jaintia and Garo conflicts must be kept separate from this particular war, even though they belong to the broader history of nineteenth-century northeastern colonial expansion. Modern memorialization honors Tirot Sing as a defender of Khasi freedom; historical analysis adds attention to the councils, territorial claims, and political alternatives that made his choices intelligible.

Relating the war only to military tactics would miss its central political dimension. The issue was the authority to admit soldiers and workers, to determine the terms of passage, and to settle claims over land connecting hills and plains. Commerce, security, diplomacy, and sovereignty were inseparable. Some residents might profit from a better route while others feared that military movement would bring policing and taxation. These disagreements did not arise from ignorance of the benefits of a road; they reflected divergent judgments about who would control those benefits and enforce the conditions under which the road operated.

Recent scholarship using the correspondence of David Scott and Khasi chiefs highlights the range of local decisions and the dangers of describing the region simply as a frontier subdued by European arms. Local rulers corresponded, bargained, and sometimes exchanged rights as they pursued their own interests. British officers depended upon political information and alliances that they did not generate independently. This evidence complicates accounts that portray all Khasi leaders as uniformly resistant or uniformly dependent. Historical geography matters as well: the location of markets, passes, villages, and proposed roads determines why conflict unfolded where it did.

The Khasi resistance should therefore be compared with western Indian hill politics rather than collapsed into it. Both involved routes, mobility, chiefs, and the Company's reliance on local alliances. Yet Khasi polities retained distinctive institutions, and the immediate focus on a strategic corridor differed from the land-revenue and creditor conflicts of Chotanagpur. Such differences show why the broad rubric of Adivasi rebellion is useful only as an introduction. The actual history must be reconstructed from each community's political institutions, claims to territory, and surviving evidence of action.

The chronology of political settlement after the fighting also needs qualification. Company influence expanded through separate agreements and relationships rather than one uniform annexation of every Khasi polity at the moment of Tirot Sing's defeat. Some institutions and councils retained internal authority under varying conditions. Such arrangements could be regarded by officers as indirect rule and by local communities as constrained continuity. The difference mattered to the way political claims were remembered and defended. Students should compare the dates of military operations with the dates and contents of particular political agreements; otherwise a map labeled British territory may falsely imply identical administrative power across the hills.

Section 68.12

The Santhal Hul of 1855–1856: Sidhu, Kanhu, and the Crisis of the Damin-i-Koh

Grievances accumulated through oppressive interest and account-keeping, disputed landholding, rent collection, labor demands, police corruption, and the difficulty of obtaining justice from courts and officials. The colonial record identifies mahajans, revenue and police intermediaries, landlords, and in some accounts railway-related abuses. But it is misleading to imagine that every non-Santhal resident held the same position, or that every Santhal household experienced contracts in identical ways. The central conflict concerned a structure of enforceable dependency in which people felt that their labor and land were being appropriated without just redress. Violations of moral relations, including indignity and coercion, were as important as particular monetary sums.

At Bhognadih in late June 1855, an assembly associated with Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu proclaimed a new direction. Their brothers Chand and Bhairav also figure in accounts of mobilization. The movement drew on extensive village communication and on a powerful religious understanding of authority. The Thakur and other spiritual agencies were not incidental decorations attached to an economic protest; they helped define the justice that leaders believed should replace oppressive rule. The language of divine sanction authorized collective action and could make promises of a reordered moral world credible to participants. Treating religious conviction merely as the manipulation of gullible peasants repeats colonial prejudice, while reducing the Hul to a purely class-based revolt leaves the leaders' own stated purposes unexplained.

As mobilization spread, groups attacked establishments and individuals associated with their grievances and confronted Company detachments. Roads, forest paths, local markets, and village relationships shaped the movement of people and information. The Company concentrated troops and mounted operations in 1855–1856, suppressing resistance through a combination of military action and reorganization. Many people died, and reports contain large numbers, but figures vary by source and counting conventions; a scholarly textbook should not adopt the most dramatic estimate without methodological explanation. Violence affected noncombatants as well as fighters, and the consequences for village families extended far beyond the defeat of armed groups.

An unusually important source is the recorded examination of Kanhu, preserved through colonial proceedings and reproduced in historical documentary collections. Later scholarship distinguishes several examinations and warns that the date and title appearing in one printed compilation may not correspond exactly to the original interrogation record. These reports are precious because they preserve elements of the leaders' claims, including injustice and religious authority, but Kanhu's words were filtered through questioning, translation, and the circumstances of detention. They should not be treated as transparent autobiography. A documentary window should identify the particular edition and avoid representing an editorial paraphrase as a collated testimony.

The Company's own public explanation of the Hul was divided. A parliamentary discussion on 21 July 1856 referred to complaints against moneylenders, corrupt police and revenue personnel, and negligence by local officials; it also repeated an interpretation of religious commitment as fanaticism. Reading these elements together demonstrates how an official could acknowledge serious grievances and still deny the political meaning of Santhal religious claims. The record also described new arrangements for administering the affected country. Postrebellion changes to the governance of the Santhal Parganas testified to the failure of prior mechanisms while preserving the Company's power to determine the remedy.

Contemporary and later interpretations compete. Colonial writers often presented the Hul as disorder inflamed by superstition, nationalist accounts placed it within the history of resistance to British authority, and scholars including Ranajit Guha and Peter B. Andersen have investigated the insurgents' political language and religious world. Andersen's study especially challenges explanations that overlook Thakur and the moral reforms imagined by Santhal participants. These interpretations illuminate different aspects of evidence and should be assessed in relation to the documents they actually use. Community memories concerning Sidhu, Kanhu, and other figures preserve dimensions of sacrifice and agency not reducible to official categories, even where details of specific episodes remain disputed.

The Hul ended in military defeat by 1856, but it cannot be characterized as a failed rehearsal for 1857. It had its own leadership, geography, grievances, cosmology, and demands for justice. Its scale disclosed the dangers of allowing colonial intermediaries to exercise unchecked power in newly settled territory. Its aftermath revealed that administration could be revised after violence without relinquishing sovereignty. Above all, the Hul challenges historians to study religious meaning and economic coercion together, recognizing that the participants' struggle was for a different moral and political order as well as relief from particular debts and official abuses.

The Hul also offers a case study in the consequences of producing historical statistics. A colonial officer might count killed insurgents after an engagement, while a later account might estimate losses over an entire district or include deaths during displacement. Numbers copied without their definitions and source dates can create a false appearance of certainty. Similar caution applies to claims about the number of participants at Bhognadih or the exact dates of leaders' capture. The strongest conclusion is not a single unverified total but the movement's extensive participation, severe suppression, and enduring political consequences. Source criticism strengthens rather than diminishes recognition of the suffering involved.

Section 68.13

Peasant Resistance: Revenue, Rent, Debt, and Local Collective Action

Peasant communities had means of communication and collective organization that were not normally described as political institutions in Company records. Kinship networks, market gatherings, irrigation associations, village councils, and relationships with religious figures could coordinate decisions about payment, migration, and protest. Written petitions and delegations to officials were forms of resistance when they challenged a claim to revenue or asked for the dismissal of abusive intermediaries. The refusal to cultivate contested land or the withdrawal of labor could impose costs on landholders even without armed confrontation. These methods did not always become spectacular uprisings, which explains why histories based only on military reports overlook much of the everyday contest over authority.

Credit could be both the means of survival and a source of dependency. Households borrowed for seed, weddings, food during a failed harvest, or advances needed to plant a commercial crop. Repayment demands did not inevitably mean exploitation, because creditors assumed risks and productive borrowing could benefit cultivators. Nevertheless, unequal power over accounts, pledges, and access to courts made some arrangements coercive. When officials supported seizure or execution of debt judgments, a grievance against an individual creditor could become opposition to colonial law itself. In other situations groups blamed a local landlord rather than the Company and asked Company officers to intervene. Economic conflict therefore did not dictate one fixed political allegiance.

The social category peasant conceals divisions. Some cultivators owned or held durable rights in land; others rented under insecure terms, worked as sharecroppers, or depended on seasonal wages. Village elites might lead collective opposition to a tax that threatened their own surplus, while laborers had little reason to defend that elite's continuing claims over work or produce. Caste, gender, religious identity, and regional custom influenced who spoke for a village and who bore the costs of decisions. Historians should ask whether a protest preserved a local hierarchy, sought changes to it, or contained competing positions within the same movement.

Peasant actions could escalate when formal redress failed. The arrest of an organizer, a dispute over police violence, or a crop failure could transform petitions into attacks on records, warehouses, or revenue officers. Armed resistance frequently depended on local leaders who could assemble followers and obtain supplies, but the initial demands were often specific and negotiable. Colonial responses ranged from concessions and revised assessments to deployment of troops and collective punishment. The governing state's decisions about which petitions to hear and which gatherings to criminalize helped determine whether conflict intensified. Comparative analysis must therefore place official agency alongside popular grievances.

This chapter does not reproduce the detailed economic history of land systems; its concern is the politics of collective action. Peasants did not always use the vocabulary of sovereignty, yet a dispute over who had the right to decide rent, arrest a debtor, or dispossess a household was a dispute about legitimate authority. When such grievances aligned with military retainers, religious leaders, or forest communities, a local protest might become a larger regional rebellion. The Kol and Santhal cases exemplify that possibility, while the Faraizi movement illustrates how a sustained religious association could articulate claims on behalf of cultivating households without beginning as a conventional army.

The difference between peaceful and violent action was not always clear in official language. A crowd gathered to prevent seizure of crops could be described as riotous before anyone attacked a person. Conversely, a landlord's hired retainers might use force that officials regarded as lawful enforcement. Courts classified conduct through categories reflecting recognized property rights and the authority of those requesting intervention. Historians therefore examine who was entitled to call on police, what kind of evidence was accepted, and which forms of coercion remained unnamed. That analysis connects the economic institutions of Chapter 66 with the political conflicts traced here.

Section 68.14

The Faraizi Movement: Religious Reform and Agrarian Politics in Eastern Bengal

Religious reform meant changing particular practices, institutions, and relationships. Faraizi teachers criticized ceremonies and forms of authority they considered inconsistent with their understanding of Islamic obligation. This could generate dispute with established religious figures and with landlords who demanded payments for ceremonies or occasions that followers regarded as illegitimate. The movement's religious language was not simply a mask for material concerns; it furnished a framework within which obligations could be evaluated and a community could organize around a common discipline. Adherence might affect prayer, ritual behavior, social solidarity, and decisions about whom to obey in ordinary life.

After Shariatullah's death in 1840, his son Dudu Miyan became especially prominent. Under his leadership the relationship between Islamic reform and the claims of peasant cultivators became more explicit. Followers challenged particular exactions by zamindars and other rural powerholders, and organizers mobilized communities in disputes over rent and customary dues. A slogan commonly associated with the movement links a claim to land to the person who cultivates it, illustrating the radical potential of arguments grounded in labor. Modern scholarship emphasizes that such claims were not a mechanical consequence of earlier Hanafi juristic doctrine; they arose as religious reasoning interacted with the political economy of agrarian Bengal.

The character of the movement cannot be summarized as Hindu landlords versus Muslim tenants. Religion shaped participation and opposition, but landholding groups and rural communities were internally diverse, and the economic interests of particular people were not determined simply by confession. Some landlords were Muslim; some cultivators maintained relationships with established authorities; and disputes could turn on the legality of dues rather than on religious difference alone. Company officials were often involved as magistrates and police authorities, whether or not followers regarded colonial government as their principal opponent. The struggle for protection against coercion could therefore be conducted through legal petitions at one moment and through organized defiance at another.

Sources produced by missionaries, Company officials, rival religious leaders, and later historians have sometimes interpreted the movement through the narrow categories of fanaticism, social unrest, or peasant revolt. Each category captures only part of the history. The movement created durable networks of teaching and solidarity, and its members articulated norms that had consequences for economic relationships. A contemporary observer's fear of disorder is evidence of official anxiety but does not automatically describe participants' purposes. Likewise, attributing an identical political intention to every follower exaggerates the degree of centralized organization that can be proven from the surviving records.

The Faraizis show how religious authority could become a resource for bargaining in the countryside. Unlike a court-centered rebellion over succession, the movement connected dispersed households by rules of practice and shared moral claims. Its development under Dudu Miyan illustrates how organizational goals could change with conditions. By the 1850s eastern Bengal remained a setting of competing landlord, planter, commercial, and peasant interests, but that history was not a simple path to the uprising of 1857. The significance of the movement is that it makes visible a form of collective action in which social reform, religious conviction, and agrarian protest were inseparable without being reducible to one another.

One distinctive feature of the Faraizi case is that reform could create discipline across communities dispersed through the delta. Leaders' religious teaching helped followers evaluate which payments were legitimate and how obligations to landlords should be understood. Organization did not require the continuous occupation of a capital or the possession of a formal state treasury. Yet local branches could disagree over action, and adherence to a religious program did not automatically imply participation in every rent protest. Evaluating such movements requires reconstructing institutional ties and differences of practice rather than assigning a unitary intention to all who shared a religious identity.

Section 68.15

Mappila Uprisings in Malabar, c. 1836–1855

Mappilas were Muslim communities with a long history in the Malabar coastal and inland economy. Their social positions ranged from merchants and property holders to tenants and agricultural laborers, and they did not form one uniform class. Uprisings commonly grouped in the colonial archive as Mappila or Moplah outbreaks began in 1836 and recurred in different places through the nineteenth century. Before 1857 they were localized events rather than one continuous war. Particular confrontations involved grievances connected with tenancy, eviction, humiliation, police action, or religious identity. Their immediate sequences must be reconstructed from case-specific evidence rather than inferred from later, larger conflicts.

Colonial descriptions frequently emphasized religion and characterized participants as fanatics. This was a politically useful explanation because it located violence in the character of the governed rather than in the institutions of landholding and justice. Yet religion cannot simply be removed from the history either. Religious figures, understandings of martyrdom, community solidarity, and reactions to perceived threats to sacred life could shape motivations and the willingness to confront superior force. The question is how religious ideas interacted with the circumstances of agrarian conflict, not whether one must choose a single material or spiritual cause for every incident.

The historian K. N. Panikkar's work is especially important for treating the outbreaks as part of the changing relations between landlords, tenants, and colonial authority. Other scholarship has examined religious organization, local leadership, and the way British officers recorded coercion and violence. These interpretations need comparison because not every Mappila participant occupied the same tenant position or understood their conduct in identical terms. Some inhabitants of Muslim communities did not support an uprising; some grievances involved other Muslim actors; and the actions of a small group cannot be used to characterize every member of a religious population.

Company repression involved police investigations, armed expeditions, punitive measures, and attempts to prevent repetition. Reports often focused on the immediate violent episode and the need to restore order, while landlords and local officers produced competing accounts of its cause. Peasants' claims appear only indirectly in many surviving files. Asking whose testimony was recorded and under what circumstances is as necessary here as in the Vellore investigations. In particular, an officer who diagnosed religious excitement might also describe an eviction or long-standing rent dispute without treating that fact as causally central; historians must read beyond the report's stated conclusion.

Malabar's pre-1857 outbreaks should not be confused with the much later rebellion of 1921. Similar regional institutions and memories created connections across time, but the later movement belonged to changed conditions of imperial politics and mass mobilization. The value of examining the earlier episodes is their demonstration that apparently ordinary legal decisions about tenancy could become matters of collective honor and political authority. A colonial court could be regarded as impartial by one litigant and as the enforcement arm of a hostile landlord by another. When those judgments intersected with religious belonging, the resulting conflicts could not be comprehended through either agrarian economics or theology alone.

The larger historical setting also includes the ways colonial authorities sought to classify violence. Reports were written for readers concerned with imperial security, and the term outrage could suggest an exceptional disturbance rather than a recurrent social conflict. The choice of label influenced whether recommendations focused on police repression or on investigating tenants' claims. Later scholars' attention to agrarian relationships supplies a corrective without denying the importance of participants' religious language. The task is to read the official diagnosis beside its own evidentiary details, identifying where descriptions of an eviction, grievance, or local insult sit uneasily with a conclusion that attributes everything to fanaticism.

Section 68.16

Women, Households, Community Networks, and the Social Organization of Resistance

Rani Chennamma at Kittur provides a case in which a woman's political authority is unmistakable. She led decisions over succession, negotiation, and armed defense as an actor within the institution of rulership, not merely as the symbolic mother of an army. Her presence challenges assumptions that formal power was held only by men, although the exceptional visibility of a queen should not be taken as evidence that ordinary women enjoyed equivalent rights. The political position of a ruling household differed profoundly from that of a tenant, servant, or cultivator. Comparison is useful precisely because it reveals unequal opportunities for exercising authority even among people who confronted colonial intervention.

Santhal community histories remember Phulo and Jhano as courageous women connected with the Hul. These traditions matter to present-day historical identity and may preserve aspects of participation obscured in Company proceedings. A scholarly chapter must nevertheless distinguish specific claims recoverable from contemporary records from details transmitted or elaborated in later commemorations. The distinction is neither contempt for oral history nor an endorsement of official silence. It permits the historian to state confidently that women took part in the social world of the uprising while being careful about particular incidents, numbers of opponents killed, or speeches attributed to named individuals.

Religious networks enabled communication across settlements in ways not dependent upon written correspondence. Fakirs, ascetics, local teachers, village elders, and ritual specialists could convene followers and carry news. Market days, marriages, seasonal labor migration, and pilgrimage routes likewise moved information. The Faraizi movement demonstrates how shared practices generated solidarity without a permanent military headquarters. The Khasi and Bhil cases demonstrate the significance of people able to guide others through difficult terrain. These examples should not be merged into one universal underground organization. Their strengths followed from different institutions, and participation could be temporary, reluctant, or divided.

A household's relationship to rebellion was often shaped by risk. Colonial troops might requisition supplies or punish communities suspected of providing shelter; insurgents could demand assistance from villagers whose families feared retaliation. Some people chose to leave contested districts, while others attempted neutrality or mediated between sides. The archive may describe such conduct as betrayal, collaboration, or cowardice, but the historian must ask what alternatives were available. A woman managing a household during a campaign could be making decisions with immediate consequences for survival even if no official ever recorded her name. Similar pressures affected children, elderly people, and laborers dependent on access to fields.

Community memory and official documentation can be read in dialogue. Songs may reveal what later generations valued; petitions may show stated grievances; military reports may locate operations and identify punishments. Each source supports different levels of certainty. The central conclusion is that rebellion was a social process, not merely the movement of armed leaders between named battles. Recognizing households, gendered labor, and regional networks changes the questions historians ask: how was food obtained, how was authority justified, who made arrangements for flight, and who sustained collective action after a leader was captured? These are not secondary details but conditions without which organized resistance could scarcely endure.

Women's historical visibility also depends on the survival of material evidence. A ruler's letters, official orders, or negotiations may establish her political decisions directly. An ordinary woman's work is more likely to appear in household accounts, compensation records, court testimony, oral narrative, or a passing reference in an officer's report. These differences demand different levels of certainty, not different assumptions about human agency. The analytical goal is to explain how social reproduction and collective action depended upon people outside the small group of formally named leaders. Attention to that dependence broadens political history without inventing events for people whose individual experiences are unknown.

Section 68.17

Political and Social Pressures in the 1850s: The Company State Approaches a Crisis

The annexation of Awadh in 1856 is a particularly important development. The Company justified intervention through allegations of misgovernment, while critics questioned its political basis and effects. The kingdom's rulers, courtiers, military households, landlords, and numerous dependents were linked through networks of service and patronage. Removing the court and reorganizing administration affected livelihoods and expectations far beyond the ruling family. Many sepoys of the Bengal Army had ties to Awadh and surrounding districts, making the relationship between military service and home society especially significant. Yet the exact connection between these circumstances and the following year's uprising must be investigated in Chapter 69, not assumed here as a simple mechanical consequence.

Legal and social reform also produced competing reactions. Company educational policies, missionary activities, judicial decisions, and regulations affecting religious practices could expand opportunities for some and provoke apprehension among others. As Chapter 67 demonstrated, Indian intellectuals were active participants in these debates rather than passive recipients. Rumors of religious interference acquired force because the Company had genuine power to issue regulations concerning military appearance, education, marriage, and public institutions. Officers who regarded a reform as limited or benevolent did not necessarily understand how communities interpreted its implications for the future. Political suspicion therefore cannot be dismissed solely as ignorance or malicious agitation.

Economic grievances crossed the same social boundaries. Revenue settlements changed the ways cultivators and landlords negotiated obligations, and courts could reinforce claims that had previously been locally contested. Merchants and creditors offered services important to production but could also wield disproportionate power when supported by colonial administration. The Santhal Hul exposed violent dissatisfaction with such relationships in a territory that officials had expected to benefit from planned agricultural settlement. The Kol uprising, the Faraizis, and rural protest elsewhere showed that apparently technical fiscal changes could become disputes about who had the right to exercise power over land and people.

A longer perspective also reveals limits to continuity. The army of 1856 was not the Madras garrison of 1806, and the political order of Awadh was not equivalent to Kittur three decades earlier. Recruitment, military organization, communications, and regional alliances had changed. Different communities remembered earlier conflicts in different ways, and many did not share a program for challenging colonial rule. The historian may identify structural patterns—such as unequal authority, rumor, and insecure rights—without assuming that all participants belonged to an evolving centralized movement. The problem of explaining 1857 begins precisely where these recurrent patterns intersected with conditions peculiar to the later crisis.

This section therefore stops in 1856. It identifies military dependence on Indian recruits, widespread political dispossession, conflicts over courts and revenue, and competing understandings of religious authority as conditions that made Company rule vulnerable. It does not narrate the cartridge controversy, Meerut, Delhi, or the subsequent campaigns. Those subjects require an account of their own origins, immediate decisions, political leadership, and changing military geography. The conclusion to Part XII is that a formidable territorial government could be powerful in formal institutions while remaining deeply dependent on acceptance, negotiation, and coercion at the local level.

The comparative history cautions against treating 1856 as a single moment of generalized expectation. Some communities had recently experienced military defeat, others were negotiating a new settlement, and others lived under Company institutions long established. Economic expansion and education created beneficiaries as well as opponents. What made the empire vulnerable was not universal hatred but the possibility that particular grievances could align across institutions and regions under new circumstances. That alignment requires evidence from 1857 itself. The earlier record establishes a range of available political practices and memories, but it does not predict when or where they would converge.

Section 68.18

Methods and Networks of Resistance: Petitions, Proclamations, Armed Forces, and Local Alliances

Petitioning occupied an important place in the repertoire. Memorials to magistrates, rulers, or political agents could demand a reduction of revenue, restoration of privilege, or reconsideration of an official decision. Their language often adopted the categories of the institution addressed, which can make a petition appear more deferential than the grievance underlying it. Proclamations performed a different function: they publicly announced an interpretation of authority and invited action, as with the Marudhu-associated declaration of 1801. The meaning of either document depends on its circumstances, audience, and preservation. A proclamation copied into a colonial file may have been selected because officials regarded it as especially threatening.

Armed movements exploited their environments. Forts and walled towns supported defenders such as the polygars and the Kittur court, but could become traps when Company artillery and reinforcements arrived. Forests and hills provided shelter and knowledge advantages to smaller mobile groups, as in western India, Chotanagpur, and the Khasi Hills. Rivers and mountain passes could be routes for commerce, supply, and political authority as well as barriers to military pursuit. In every case food, ammunition, medical care, and the willingness of local households to assist were essential. A dramatic battle description cannot explain why a movement expanded or failed unless it considers these logistical relationships.

Coalitions were often fragile because participants wanted different outcomes. A dispossessed chief could seek restoration of his office, while cultivators joining him might expect relief from rents imposed by the same older order. A community might welcome an attack on one landlord but oppose violence against merchants supplying needed credit. Rival local rulers could enlist the Company against an insurgent neighbor. British success frequently depended on obtaining intelligence, supplies, and manpower from some Indian actors even as it fought others. The history of alliances is therefore an essential corrective to stories of two internally united camps facing one another across a fixed line.

The Company responded with methods designed to separate movements from supporters. Officers offered pardons or allowances, recognized cooperative chiefs, seized stores and correspondence, pursued leaders, and punished settlements accused of harboring rebels. Some policies attempted to address grievances, while others consolidated surveillance and military power. Negotiated surrender was not always the same as reconciliation: a chief might accept terms to preserve the lives of followers without endorsing the regime's legitimacy. Conversely, a local official who reduced a levy could win real support even while the broader political structure remained colonial.

Methods also changed within movements. Early protests over rent or dress could escalate into armed confrontation after arrests, failed negotiations, or sudden violence. Once regular forces were defeated, surviving supporters might disperse, hide, or adopt smaller mobile operations. This evolution helps explain Sangolli Rayanna's campaign after Kittur's fall and the varied histories of armed groups in forest regions. A comparative historian should not judge movements exclusively by whether they seized territory or permanently removed Company officers. Their capacity to alter policy, impose administrative costs, create enduring memories, or expose internal divisions may be historically significant even when they ended in military defeat.

Documentary survival influences the apparent prominence of methods. Armed attacks usually generated urgent official correspondence and can be dated precisely, while prolonged negotiations or the quiet withholding of labor may leave fragmented traces. A historian who reconstructs only battles risks treating the most visible conduct as the most important. In several cases, the unsuccessful petition or the threatened loss of recognition was the immediate prehistory of violence. Establishing that sequence changes causal interpretation: resistance becomes a response to unresolved relationships rather than an inexplicable outbreak. It also makes clear why settlements and concessions belong within the history of political action.

Section 68.19

Colonial Repression, Administrative Responses, and the Historiography of Early Resistance

The aftermath of Vellore demonstrates administrative inquiry and the reversal of contested regulations; Odisha shows prolonged negotiation following armed suppression; western Indian hill districts illustrate recruitment and allowances; and the Santhal Hul led to significant reorganization of territorial administration. These examples suggest that colonial responses could combine punishment and institutional adaptation. A state that altered its policy after revolt might implicitly acknowledge a grievance while still representing opposition as criminal behavior. Reforms could improve access to official justice for some people, but they could also make colonial supervision more intensive. Whether administration became more legitimate to residents is a separate question from whether it became more efficient for the government.

Historiography has moved through several overlapping interpretations. Colonial military and revenue writers often emphasized disorder, supposed fanaticism, and the restoration of property or law. Nationalist authors later interpreted regional figures as heroes of resistance to foreign rule, making visible political courage neglected in earlier accounts. Regional scholars emphasized particular languages, communities, and political institutions. Subaltern studies, especially the work of Ranajit Guha, asked how insurgent peasants understood authority and collective action rather than treating them solely as subjects acted upon by landlords or nationalist elites. None of these approaches automatically supplies a final answer; each makes particular sources and questions prominent.

The categories themselves shape interpretation. Mutiny is an administrative classification for soldiers who reject command. Peasant rebellion identifies one kind of social participation but may obscure the role of chiefs, ritual specialists, or artisans. Adivasi uprising can acknowledge indigenous political claims yet risks suggesting a homogeneous social category that did not exist in that form in 1831 or 1855. Anticolonial can properly describe opposition to Company power without implying modern nationalist organization. The challenge is to choose terminology that makes a historical claim explicit and then test that claim against what participants did, demanded, and communicated.

The surviving archive is not evenly distributed. The Company had resources to record correspondence, keep accounts, preserve court proceedings, and publish defenses of policy. Many opponents used oral communication, and their papers were more likely to survive when seized by officials. Recorded testimony may have been translated, summarized, and shaped by interrogation. Community memory and later scholarship help correct imbalance but also require criticism of their own genres and dates. In a source study, identifying the catalogued file is only a first step: historians must distinguish the fact of its existence from claims about individual statements that remain uncollated. Editorial paraphrases should never be dressed as literal extracts.

Comparative evaluation should address three questions. What institutional change precipitated opposition, and what evidence identifies the participants' stated grievances? How did movements organize action, and whose interests did their leadership represent? What did repression and subsequent administrative adjustment change for residents? These questions prevent a list of rebellions from becoming disconnected commemorative sketches. They also guard against a retrospective narrative that moves inevitably toward 1857. The longer history reveals repeated conflicts about political authority, but each confrontation had its own local chronology and possibility of a different outcome.

The editorial treatment of archives is itself an ethical responsibility. When a historian cannot inspect a manuscript directly, identifying its collection and describing the likely questions it poses is legitimate. Attributing a specific quotation, folio number, or detailed testimony to that manuscript without examination is not. Source windows should consequently separate verified catalogue metadata from analysis offered by the textbook editor. This distinction is especially important for statements taken during detention or trial, where translation and power asymmetry may affect the text. An openly qualified paraphrase can support valuable historical inquiry without pretending to reproduce an authentic original utterance.

Section 68.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

Adivasi movements made these conflicts particularly visible. In Chotanagpur, changes in property claims and the power of outside intermediaries interacted with local leadership and customary authority. In the Khasi Hills, the right to control a road through independent polities became central to war. In Damin-i-Koh, the Santhal Hul arose from oppression linked to settlement, debt, policing, and the demand for a just moral order articulated in religious terms. These were not interchangeable examples of supposedly primitive communities rejecting modernity. Each was an intelligible political response to a different combination of institutions and grievances.

The movements of eastern Bengal and Malabar further complicate rigid categories. Faraizi organization connected Islamic reform with tenants' claims against particular exactions, while Mappila outbreaks developed in a region where religious identity, agrarian dependency, and colonial justice intersected. Neither can be understood through theology alone, and neither should be reduced to a purely economic protest. Similarly, the categories ruler, soldier, peasant, Adivasi, and religious adherent often overlapped within coalitions. Participants negotiated, petitioned, migrated, fought, and sometimes accepted settlement. Resistance existed across a continuum of action rather than exclusively in armed episodes.

The Company's reactions illuminate its political character. Military force could destroy a fort or disperse an assembly, but stable government required intelligence, local officials, fiscal arrangements, and cooperation among residents. Repression might be followed by reform, negotiated recognition, or stronger surveillance. Indian auxiliaries, intermediaries, and allies remained central to the colonial state's ability to prevail against other Indians. Those facts undermine the notion of a uniformly foreign army conquering a uniformly united population. They do not diminish the inequalities of a political order in which ultimate authority and the power to define legality rested with the Company.

Historical interpretation is a further part of the synthesis. Colonial descriptions such as dacoit or fanatic cannot stand as neutral explanations, while the commemoration of patriotic heroes must not become a substitute for evidence of particular objectives. A modern national independence movement cannot simply be projected backward into every conflict. Yet local defense of sovereignty and customary rights could still be anticolonial, especially where participants explicitly opposed Company rule. Recognizing that distinction allows historians to value resistance without inventing a common plan or claiming that one uprising was the first to oppose British authority.

By 1856, Company power extended over vast territories and had faced repeated military, social, religious, and political challenges. The annexation of Awadh added new pressures to an already complex order, but neither earlier rebellions nor these tensions made the crisis of 1857 predetermined. The next chapter must explain its distinctive immediate causes, locations, leaders, and changing objectives. Chapter 68's central conclusion is therefore conditional and historical rather than teleological: the rebellion of 1857 arose in a society with a long experience of contested colonial rule, but it was not the inevitable culmination of a single continuous movement. Chapter 69, The Rebellion of 1857, opens Part XIII and investigates that new crisis on its own terms.

The comparison also exposes the limits of linear progress narratives. An administrative reform that followed an uprising might reduce a particular abuse while introducing fresh forms of oversight. A new road could benefit trade and threaten the authority of a hill polity. Military recruitment could provide employment while extending coercive state power. Political choices cannot therefore be graded only by whether they moved toward a later idea of independence or modernity. They must be understood through the possibilities and constraints available to participants at the time. This chapter prepares students to analyze the rebellion of 1857 with the same historical precision and caution.

Chapter Summary

Company rule encountered opposition wherever conquest, new property categories, administrative coercion, and the reorganization of military service altered established relationships. The resulting movements were not one continuous national struggle. Their participants ranged from sepoys and princely households to peasants, religious networks, and peoples defending varied land and political institutions in hill and forest regions.

Vellore and Barrackpore demonstrate how military service conditions could become matters of collective grievance. The Polygar Wars, Paika Rebellion, and Kittur resistance were closely associated with threatened hereditary political authority. In western India and the Jungle Mahals the expansion of revenue and police government interacted with the mobility and institutional claims of local communities.

The Kol uprising, Khasi resistance, and Santhal Hul show different forms of the crisis created by colonial expansion into regions with complex local government. Land alienation, creditor power, roads, police intervention, and religious understandings of justice could become political causes. Faraizi and Mappila movements add the need to analyze religious and agrarian concerns together rather than force an exclusive explanation.

Women and nonelite households remained important to the supply, communication, and endurance of movements, despite their unequal visibility in official documents. The Company usually suppressed armed rebellion but often revised its administration or relied upon negotiation and local allies. Repression did not itself establish the legitimacy of the political order imposed.

The historical task is to distinguish the participants’ own goals, colonial explanations, later nationalist commemorations, and modern scholarly interpretations. The year 1857 followed a long history of conflict, but its outbreak and campaigns require separate explanation. Chapter 69 begins Part XIII.

Chapter Reflection

From Recurrent Resistance to the Crisis of 1857

The recurring conflicts examined in this chapter show that Company sovereignty was never merely a matter of military victory and a colored boundary on a map. Government had to operate through landlords, soldiers, village institutions, merchants, police, and local alliances. Its measures could bring security or opportunities to some and dispossession or coercion to others, provoking different kinds of resistance.

Historical judgment requires attention to what participants sought and to the records through which their struggles have been remembered. A mutiny over conditions of service, an uprising over land, a war over a road, and a religiously informed struggle for justice are not interchangeable. Their differences make the larger history of colonial power more comprehensible.

Chapter 68 closes Part XII. Chapter 69, The Rebellion of 1857, opens Part XIII and asks how a new crisis crossed social and military boundaries in a manner distinct from, though historically connected to, earlier experiences of Company rule.
