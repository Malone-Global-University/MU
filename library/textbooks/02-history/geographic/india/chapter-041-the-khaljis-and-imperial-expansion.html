Part VIII — Islamic Frontiers and the Delhi Sultanate

The Khaljis and Imperial Expansion

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present
Delhi and its frontiers, 1290–1320

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1303, the sultan of Delhi was hundreds of kilometers from his capital, directing the siege of the great hill fortress of Chittor in Rajasthan, when a Mongol army appeared outside Delhi itself. Alauddin Khalji's best troops were with him, far away. According to the Persian chronicler Ziya al-Din Barani, writing decades later, the sultan hurried back, found his capital poorly prepared, and entrenched his forces at Siri, a site northeast of the older city that he would soon turn into a fortified royal capital. For about two months the Mongols remained in the region. Then they withdrew. Delhi had survived, but the episode exposed the central dilemma of Alauddin's reign: the more widely the sultan's armies campaigned, the more vulnerable his base became, and the more money, men, and discipline the state had to extract in order to remain secure.

Chapter 40 explained how the Delhi Sultanate, born of the Ghurid conquests examined in Chapter 39, became a durable political system under the Mamluk (Slave) sultans, developing the iqta system, an imperial capital, and the military defenses of the age of Balban. This chapter asks what happened when the rulers of that state tried to make it imperial. Between 1290 and 1320, the Khalji dynasty carried Delhi's armies into Gujarat, Rajasthan, Malwa, the Deccan plateau, and, in raiding expeditions, as far south as the Pandya country. At the same time, Alauddin Khalji (reigned 1296–1316) built a more coercive and more fiscally ambitious government than Delhi had known before, one that measured and taxed land, inspected soldiers and their horses, supervised the nobility through informants, and regulated prices in the capital's markets.

The chapter therefore studies three developments that were bound together. The first is a political transformation: the replacement of the Mamluk aristocracy by a new military elite in the "Khalji revolution" of 1290, followed by Alauddin's violent seizure of the throne in 1296 and his reshaping of sultanate kingship. The second is territorial expansion, driven by military ambition, plunder, the need to defend against Mongol invasions, and the desire for resources. The third is administrative and economic experimentation, much of it designed to finance and discipline an unusually large standing army. These developments cannot be understood separately, for the armies required revenue, the revenue required control of the countryside, the control of the countryside required loyal officials, and the loyalty of officials required surveillance and coercion.

The chapter also insists on a distinction that is easy to blur. Delhi's armies reached far more places than Delhi governed. Gujarat and Malwa were brought under provincial administration; Ranthambore and Jalore became Delhi-held fortresses and garrisoned strongholds; Devagiri was first subordinated as a tributary and later more directly controlled; Warangal and Dwarasamudra accepted tributary obligations; and the Pandya country was raided, plundered, and left to its own politics. A student who treats every campaign as a permanent annexation will misunderstand both Khalji power and its limits.

Our evidence is rich but uneven, and it requires constant critical reading. Barani's Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi (completed in the 1350s) is the major narrative of Alauddin's policies, but Barani wrote some sixty years after the events, as a moralist with strong views about kingship and a reformer's interest in the past. Amir Khusrau, the great Persian poet of the Delhi court, wrote contemporary court prose and verse praising the sultan's campaigns. Isami's Futuh al-Salatin (1350), composed in the Deccan, gives a different vantage. Inscriptions, coins, and the surviving buildings of Delhi supplement these. Sanskrit, Prakrit, and regional-language sources from the Rajput, Yadava, Kakatiya, Hoysala, and Pandya worlds offer other perspectives, though many of them were composed generations after the events.

By the end of the chapter students should be able to explain how the Khaljis changed the scale and character of the Delhi Sultanate; how the institutions of Alauddin's reign worked and what they cost; why the dynasty collapsed within four years of Alauddin's death in 1316; and why historians of the period must separate court rhetoric, later chronicle tradition, and regional memory from what the evidence will bear. Chapter 42 turns to the Tughlaq dynasty that inherited, and soon overextended, the Khalji legacy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political circumstances that allowed the Khalji dynasty to replace the Mamluk house in 1290, and assess why a simple "Turks versus Afghans" explanation is inadequate.
  2. Trace Alauddin Khalji's path from governor of Kara to sultan, including the Devagiri raid and the assassination of Jalal al-Din Khalji.
  3. Analyze how Alauddin reshaped sultanate kingship, including his relationship with religious scholars, his titles, and his reliance on coercion.
  4. Evaluate the Mongol threat to Delhi and explain how defense against it shaped Khalji military and fiscal policy.
  5. Describe the conquests of Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Malwa and distinguish the evidence of Persian court sources from later regional tradition.
  6. Compare annexation, provincial administration, military occupation, tribute, and raiding as distinct forms of Delhi's authority in the Deccan and the south.
  7. Explain how land measurement, revenue demands, the dagh and chehra systems, and market regulations were connected.
  8. Assess Barani's account of Alauddin's policies through source criticism, identifying what it can and cannot establish.
  9. Analyze the social costs and uneven effects of Khalji policy on peasants, intermediaries, merchants, soldiers, nobles, and enslaved people.
  10. Explain the succession crisis of 1316–1320 and the factors that destroyed the Khalji dynasty.
  11. Evaluate whether territorial expansion made the Delhi Sultanate stronger, more vulnerable, or both, using comparison with the Mamluk state.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did Alauddin Khalji justify and sustain his rule after seizing power through murder?
  3. How did the Mongol threat shape the military, fiscal, and territorial choices of the Khalji state?
  4. What is the difference between conquering, raiding, extracting tribute from, and governing a region, and why does it matter for evaluating Khalji "empire"?

  8. Why did a state that had defeated Mongol armies and conquered or subordinated much of the subcontinent fall apart within four years of its greatest ruler's death?

Historical Context

By 1290, the Delhi Sultanate had existed for about eighty years as a political system centered on the Indo-Gangetic plain. Founded by the former slave-commanders of the Ghurid conquests (Chapters 39 and 40), it had survived the early succession struggles, the rise of the iqta system, and the Mongol pressure of the thirteenth century. Under Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Balban (reigned 1266–1287), the court had stressed hierarchy, ceremony, and the suppression of rivals, and the army had been concentrated on the northwestern and Doab frontiers. Balban's government projected an image of imposing royal power, but its territorial reach remained largely confined to the north, and much of Rajasthan, Malwa, Gujarat, and the Deccan lay beyond its direct control.

Those regions were governed by other powers with their own long histories, many examined in earlier chapters. The Chauhans (Chapter 35) had been broken by the Ghurid victories of the 1190s, but Rajput rulers continued to hold fortified strongholds such as Ranthambore, Chittor, Siwana, and Jalore. Gujarat, wealthy from its ports and textile and trade networks, was ruled by the Vaghelas. In the Deccan, the Yadavas of Devagiri, the Kakatiyas of Warangal, and the Hoysalas of Dwarasamudra had built wealthy temple-centered kingdoms (compare Chapters 31 and 37), while the Pandyas dominated the far south and its pearl and horse-importing ports. These states were not weak or backward targets; they were prosperous, militarily capable, and enmeshed in rivalries with one another that Delhi's commanders would exploit.

Two external forces shaped the period. The first was the Mongol world. After the thirteenth-century conquests of Chinggis Khan and his successors, Mongol-descended khanates controlled much of Central Asia and Iran. From their bases in Afghanistan and Transoxiana, Mongol armies repeatedly invaded the Punjab and threatened Delhi. The second was the broader Indian Ocean and Eurasian horse trade (Chapters 49 and 38): sultanate armies depended on cavalry horses imported from Central Asia, Iran, and Arabia, which made the frontiers of Sindh and Multan, and the horse-importing ports of the south, strategically important.

Students should keep in mind the nature of the evidence. Persian chronicles written at or near the Delhi court emphasize royal power, military victories, and the sultan's piety or its absence. They are valuable and often detailed, but they are not neutral records. The numbers they report for armies, plunder, and casualties are frequently implausibly large, and the motives they assign to rulers are interpretations rather than documents. The chapter therefore treats figures cautiously, distinguishes contemporary from later sources, and notes where modern historians disagree.

Section 41.1

The Delhi Sultanate after Balban: Crisis and Political Opportunity

The death of Ghiyath al-Din Balban in 1287 left Delhi without a secure successor. Balban's own intended heir, his son Muhammad, had been killed in 1285 fighting Mongol raiders in the northwest, an episode that reminds us how heavily Mongol pressure weighed on the sultanate even before the Khaljis. Balban had then designated his grandson Kai Khusrau, but powerful nobles at court set this aside and installed another grandson, the young Muizz al-Din Kaiqubad, apparently because they preferred a ruler they could manage. The strong personalized authority Balban had constructed rested heavily on the sultan himself, and it did not survive him intact.

Kaiqubad's reign (c. 1287–1290) exposed the fragility of the system. According to Barani, the young sultan gave himself over to pleasure while court factions contended, and a powerful official named Nizam al-Din dominated affairs until he was removed. A paralytic stroke further weakened the sultan. Nobles then raised Kaiqubad's infant son Kayumars to the throne, and the factions of the court, broadly divided between established Mamluk (slave-descended) commanders and others, struggled for control. A court dominated by competing patrons, a child-sultan, and a divided military elite created exactly the opening that an ambitious and experienced commander could use.

That commander was Jalal al-Din Khalji, an old frontier general of Khalji origin who had served as a military governor in the Punjab and held a high military position under the Mamluk sultans. His career illustrates a feature of the sultanate that earlier chapters have noted: the Delhi elite was not a closed caste but a shifting body of military men drawn from slaves, free Turkic soldiers, Persian-speaking officials, and immigrants from across Afghanistan and Central Asia. The political crisis after Balban was thus not only a dynastic accident but also the moment when a different part of that military world demanded a share of power.

Section 41.2

The Khalji Revolution of 1290

In 1290, a party of Khalji commanders and their supporters moved against the court. Kaiqubad was killed, and Jalal al-Din Firuz Khalji was enthroned at Kilokhri, an unfinished palace-city on the Yamuna just outside Delhi, in the middle of the year. The Delhi aristocracy, still attached to the Balbanid order, was reluctant to accept the new ruler, and, according to Barani, Jalal al-Din did not at first enter the old city but governed from Kilokhri until opposition subsided. The transition has traditionally been called the "Khalji revolution," a phrase popularized in the twentieth century by historians who saw in it a broadening of the ruling elite.

What kind of revolution was it? Barani, writing as an admirer of the old Turkic nobility, described the event as the end of Turkish rule and the arrival of men who were not truly Turks, whom he sometimes associated with "Afghans." Modern historians complicate this picture. The Khalaj were a people of Turkic origin long settled in what is now southern Afghanistan, and Khalji soldiers had served under the Ghurids; the most famous earlier example, Muhammad Bakhtiyar Khalji, had conquered Bihar and Bengal around 1204 (see Chapter 39 and Chapter 47). Their rivals regarded them as outsiders partly because of status and differing political networks rather than because of any simple ethnic divide. The Khalji period did involve a real change in elite composition and political culture, with a widening of access to high office, but "Turks versus Afghans" is a later and politically loaded simplification.

The more important point may be what the revolution reveals about the sultanate's political logic. Legitimacy in the Delhi Sultanate did not rest on hereditary succession alone. A ruler held power by military success, the loyalty of commanders, patronage, and a recognized relationship with the broader Islamic world, including the Abbasid caliphal tradition and religious scholars. The Khaljis showed that this system could accommodate a new faction, but they also showed how violent, personalized, and unstable transitions could be, a pattern that would recur in 1296, 1316, and 1320.

Section 41.3

Jalal al-Din Khalji: Kingship, Conciliation, and Political Opposition

Jalal al-Din's reign (1290–1296) was shaped by his advanced age and his chosen style of rule. Barani presents him as a mild, pious, and forgiving sultan who sought to win over opponents rather than destroy them. He kept many nobles of the old regime in office, and when Malik Chhajju, a nephew of Balban and governor of Kara, rebelled in 1290, Jalal al-Din defeated him but treated him and his followers leniently. Barani, who valued strong and intimidating kingship, criticizes this clemency as dangerous weakness. Modern historians are more cautious: conciliation may have been a calculated response to a regime still insecure in Delhi, with a thin base among the old nobility.

His military record was mixed. In 1291 he besieged Ranthambore but withdrew without taking the fort, reportedly judging the cost in Muslim lives too high, a decision that suggests either scruple or realism about how hard Rajasthan's fortresses were to take (the fort would be captured by Alauddin in 1301, see Section 41.9). In 1292 the sultan's forces defeated a Mongol invasion, and some Mongols, according to the sources, converted to Islam and settled near Delhi. These "New Muslims" (a term used for Mongol converts in the Delhi army) would play a violent role in Alauddin's reign (Section 41.8). The sultan also captured Mandawar and took action against a Sufi, Sidi Maula, whose circle was accused of conspiracy and who was executed, an incident Barani used to question the sultan's judgment.

The most consequential development was the rise of the sultan's nephew and son-in-law, Ali Gurshasp, later Alauddin. Appointed governor of Kara (in the eastern Doab) in 1291, he raided Bhilsa in 1292, bringing back plunder and, importantly, tales of the greater wealth of the Deccan. Jalal al-Din rewarded him with Awadh in addition to Kara. The sultan's trust in a young, ambitious, and well-resourced relative would prove fatal. The reign of Jalal al-Din illustrates a recurring political problem: a ruler who lacked a strong army and a fearsome reputation tolerated, even enriched, the very commander most able to overthrow him.

Section 41.4

Alauddin's Rise: Devagiri, Wealth, and the Coup of 1296

Alauddin's expedition to Devagiri is the hinge on which Khalji history turns. Persian sources report that, from Kara, he marched with a force of several thousand horsemen through Chanderi and the Vindhya region into the Yadava kingdom, reaching Devagiri, the capital of Ramachandra (Ramadeva), by surprise. Ramachandra's main army was away, and the Yadava king, besieged in the fortress, agreed to pay a very large ransom in gold, silver, pearls, and other valuables, with an arrangement for future tribute. The date of the expedition is debated: some older accounts place it in 1294, but most modern scholars place it in 1296, shortly before Alauddin seized the throne. Students will find both dates in the literature.

The expedition's significance was threefold. It showed that a Delhi commander could cross the Vindhyas and extract treasure from a major Deccan kingdom; it supplied Alauddin with money to raise and pay a private army; and it forced a confrontation with Jalal al-Din, who wished to receive his nephew and the plunder at Kara. Barani reports that Alauddin deceived the sultan about his plans and then, as Jalal al-Din approached with a small escort, arranged his death. In July 1296, as the elderly sultan was greeting him on the bank of the Ganges, Alauddin's followers struck him down. Alauddin immediately proclaimed himself sultan.

Taking the throne required winning Delhi. The late sultan's widow, Malika-i Jahan, had raised a younger son, Qadr Khan, to the throne in the capital as Rukn al-Din Ibrahim, passing over his elder brother Arkali Khan, who was at Multan as governor and, according to Barani, was bitterly resentful of the decision. Alauddin advanced with his plunder-financed army and, according to Barani and Isami, distributed gold to soldiers, using catapults to scatter coins near the city to win followers. Rukn al-Din Ibrahim fled to Multan; Alauddin entered Delhi in the autumn of 1296, and he sent Ulugh Khan and Zafar Khan against Multan, where both princes were captured. They were blinded or otherwise eliminated, along with others who had supported the old house. The seizure of power thus rested on murder, money, and military command, and these origins shaped Alauddin's attitude toward legitimacy, wealth, and loyalty for the rest of his reign.

Section 41.5

Alauddin Khalji and the Recasting of Sultanate Kingship

Alauddin had to establish legitimacy for a regicide. He did so through a combination of generosity, military success, ceremony, and fear. His coins and inscriptions carried grand titles, among them one that likened him to Alexander (Sikandar-i Sani, "the second Alexander") and another that styled him as the right hand of the caliphate, linking the sultan to the Abbasid caliphal tradition. These titles were not empty: Delhi's rulers drew authority from association with the wider Islamic world as well as from conquest, and the language of universal conquest suited an army that was beginning to campaign across the subcontinent.

His relationship with religious scholars (ulama) is one of the most discussed topics in the period. Barani preserves anecdotes, which cannot be independently verified in their detail, portraying Alauddin as insisting that statecraft was a matter of practical necessity rather than of consulting the jurists on every issue. In one famous exchange, the sultan questions the chief jurist (qazi) of Bayana about the law of punishment, and then says, in effect, that he does what he judges good for the state and leaves the question of its legality to God. Barani also reports that Alauddin, flush with early success, contemplated founding a new religion, but was dissuaded by his counselor Ala al-Mulk, who told him that prophecy could not be created by kings. Whether or not these stories are exact, they capture a perception shared by Barani's readers: Alauddin's government was unusually independent of the scholars' guidance, which Barani found both effective and troubling.

The practical base of Alauddin's kingship was a new inner circle. He promoted relatives and trusted associates, including Ulugh Khan, Nusrat Khan, Zafar Khan, and Alp Khan, and later slave commanders such as Malik Kafur, and he removed or neutralized many of the nobles associated with the earlier regimes. Some of these were executed or blinded; others lost their lands and offices. In the first years of his reign Alauddin also faced a series of rebellions, including the plot of his nephew Akat Khan and revolts by other kinsmen, which Barani interprets as proof that the existing political system had failed to restrain ambition. Alauddin responded not only with punishment but also, as later sections show, with structural measures aimed at removing the causes of rebellion as he understood them.

Section 41.6

Mongol Invasions and the Defense of Delhi

The Mongol challenge to Delhi was serious, recurring, and decisive for Khalji policy. The Chaghatayid Mongols, ruling Central Asia and with forces based in Afghanistan, had raided the Punjab for decades, and in the early 1290s their pressure on the sultanate's northwestern marches continued. After Alauddin's accession, there was a rapid succession of invasions. In 1297–1298, a Mongol army was defeated near Jalandhar by Alauddin's commander Zafar Khan, who also recovered the frontier fortress of Sehwan in Sindh from a Mongol-linked commander. In 1299, a much larger Mongol army under Qutlugh Khwaja, a Chaghatayid prince, advanced as far as the outskirts of Delhi. The two forces met at Kili, and although the Delhi army held the field, the distinguished commander Zafar Khan was killed in the fighting, and the Mongols withdrew. Persian sources give enormous numbers for the Mongol force, but historians treat such figures with caution.

The most dangerous invasion came in 1303, when Alauddin's main army was occupied at Chittor. A Mongol commander named Taraghai advanced rapidly and besieged Delhi for about two months. Alauddin hurried back with a force and entrenched at Siri, relying on a defended camp and the old strategy of avoiding open battle while the Mongols' supplies and patience ran out. The Mongols withdrew, but the crisis led Alauddin to fortify Siri as a permanent capital with strong walls, to strengthen frontier fortresses in the Punjab, and to garrison the northwestern marches with experienced commanders. Among them was Ghazi Malik, later the first Tughlaq sultan, who became warden of the frontier based at Dipalpur.

Mongol invasions continued in 1305–1308. A large Mongol force under Ali Beg and Tartaq was intercepted and defeated near Amroha in 1305, and later invasions by commanders such as Kabak and Iqbalmand in 1306–1308 were also repulsed. Captured Mongol soldiers, according to Barani, were killed or enslaved in large numbers, a practice intended as deterrence. After 1308, serious Mongol incursions largely ceased during Alauddin's reign, partly because of the Khalji military response and partly because of political troubles within the Mongol world. The point for this chapter is that the Mongol threat served as both a cause and a justification for the creation of a much larger, better-paid, and more closely supervised army, with consequences that extended well beyond defense.

Section 41.7

Military Organization and the Financing of Expansion

Alauddin's army was larger, more centrally controlled, and more expensive than those of his predecessors. Persian chroniclers give figures for his cavalry in the hundreds of thousands, but these are probably inflated; what modern historians accept is that the sultan maintained a large standing force around Delhi, paid in cash from the treasury rather than relying exclusively on assignments of land revenue. This was a significant shift. Under the earlier iqta system (Chapter 40), commanders supported troops from the revenue of land assigned to them, a method that spread costs but also gave officers independent resources and loyalties. A core army paid directly by the sultan was more dependable, but it required reliable and enormous cash income.

The principal arm was cavalry. Horses came from Central Asia, Iran, and Arabia through Multan, Sindh, and the northwest, and sultanate commanders valued them as strategic resources; the historian Simon Digby has shown how closely the sultanate's military capacity was tied to the horse trade. War elephants were important, especially in sieges and in the Deccan. Siege warfare involved catapults (manjaniq), earthen ramps and mounds, covered approaches called sabat, and long blockades, and Amir Khusrau's accounts give vivid, if rhetorical, descriptions of such operations against Ranthambore and Chittor.

Supply and finance were the real constraints. Armies campaigning in Rajasthan or the Deccan depended on grain carried by merchant caravans and on forage in regions the army might not control. Plunder, ransoms, and tribute from conquered regions covered some costs but could not do so reliably. The more the state relied on campaigns for wealth, the more it needed ever new campaigns, a dynamic that explains both the Gujarat and Devagiri expeditions and the later administrative efforts to extract revenue from the Doab. This chapter's later sections show that Alauddin's revenue, inspection, and market policies were attempts to solve the problem of paying and supplying a large army on a sustained basis.

Section 41.8

The Conquest of Gujarat, 1299

Gujarat was one of the wealthiest regions of western India. Its ports, above all Cambay (Khambhat), linked the interior to the Arabian Sea trade with the Persian Gulf, Arabia, and East Africa (see Chapters 38, 47, and 49), and its merchants, Hindu, Jain, and Muslim, handled cotton textiles, indigo, and other goods. In the 1290s it was ruled by the Vaghela king Karna (Karandeva II). In 1299, Alauddin sent a large army under his brother Ulugh Khan and the wazir Nusrat Khan, and Persian sources report that the Vaghela forces were quickly defeated. Karna fled, reportedly to the Yadava court at Devagiri, and the invaders entered the capital region.

The conquest brought both plunder and political incorporation. Delhi's forces sacked wealthy towns and seized the treasure of the royal family. Cambay was plundered, and the temple of Somnath, which had been damaged in earlier raids and rebuilt, was again attacked and its image broken, an act that Persian sources celebrate as a triumph for Islam and that must also be understood as a demonstration of political sovereignty, since the destruction of a regime's major shrine was a way of declaring its power broken. Karna's wife was captured and later married Alauddin, and a young slave captured at Cambay, Malik Kafur, entered the sultan's service. Gujarat then became a province governed by Delhi's appointees, with Alp Khan as its governor for many years, although some Vaghela elements held out in outlying districts.

The campaign also revealed the tensions inside the army. When the returning force was ordered to hand over the state's share of the plunder, a revolt broke out among the "New Muslims," the Mongol converts serving in the Delhi army, who resented the demand and the harsh methods used to enforce it. Alauddin's response, according to Barani, was ferocious: the rebels were suppressed, and a wave of retribution in Delhi killed large numbers of New Muslims and their families, with women and children among the victims. The numbers Barani gives are uncertain, but the episode marks a clear example of the human cost of the state's efforts to control the army and its wealth. Later, an independent Gujarat Sultanate would emerge from the province Delhi created (Chapter 47).

Section 41.9

Ranthambore and Chittor: Warfare in Rajasthan

Rajasthan's hill fortresses posed a different military problem from the open plains of the Doab. Ranthambore, held by the Chauhan ruler Hammira (Hammiradeva), had defeated Jalal al-Din's attempt in 1291. In 1301 Alauddin sent Ulugh Khan and Nusrat Khan against it, and then came in person. Nusrat Khan was killed during the siege, struck, according to the chronicles, by a missile from the walls. The fort fell after a prolonged blockade, and Hammira was killed. Amir Khusrau, whose Khaza'in al-Futuh narrates the conquest in ornate prose, presents the victory as a triumph of the sultan's arms. The fall of Ranthambore removed the most prominent Chauhan stronghold and opened the way for further operations in Rajasthan.

Chittor, the seat of the Guhila rulers of Mewar, fell in 1303. It was besieged for several months, and Khusrau, who is generally thought to have been present with the army, reports its capture in August of that year, adding that thousands of the defenders were killed after the fall. The sultan's son Khizr Khan was placed in charge and the fort was briefly renamed Khizrabad, a sign of Delhi's intention to hold it. The ruler of Chittor at the time is generally identified in later Guhila genealogies as Ratnasimha. Delhi's hold on Mewar did not last, and within a few decades Guhila-Sisodia rulers had regained control, so Chittor illustrates conquest without durable integration.

Rajasthan also illustrates the problem of evidence. The best contemporary accounts are Persian court texts that exalt the sultan's victories; regional traditions, composed generations or centuries later, remember the same events as epics of resistance and sacrifice. The best-known of these is the story of Padmini, the queen said to have been the cause of the attack on Chittor, a tale found in Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat, composed in Awadhi in 1540, more than two centuries after the siege. Amir Khusrau's account of Chittor does not mention her, and no contemporary source supports the story. It should be treated as a later literary and historical tradition, important for understanding how Rajput memory developed (see Chapter 35) but not as evidence for what happened in 1303. Later traditions of jauhar, collective self-immolation by women when a fort was about to fall, are likewise elaborated in later literature and should be separated from what contemporary sources actually record.

The sources allow some insight into why Ranthambore mattered. Barani and Isami report that Hammira had given refuge to leaders of the 1299 "New Muslim" revolt (Section 41.8) and that Alauddin's demand for their surrender was refused. This supplied both a pretext and a political stake: a fortress that sheltered fugitives from the sultan's own army challenged his authority over his soldiers. Jalal al-Din's withdrawal in 1291 had also left Ranthambore as an unsettled affront. Khusrau's description of the siege, though rhetorical, refers to the construction of earthen and timber works and the use of catapults against the walls, which indicates a methodical blockade rather than a single assault. Such sieges were won by exhausting supplies and morale as much as by breaching walls, which is why they took months and cost commanders like Nusrat Khan their lives.

Chittor's strategic position helps explain the effort devoted to it. The fortress overlooked routes linking Delhi and Ajmer with Malwa and Gujarat, so control of it helped secure communications with the provinces conquered in and after 1299. Khusrau's report that thousands were killed after the fall indicates a deliberate punishment of a garrison and population that had resisted for months, and it may also have been meant to impress other fortress holders; like his figures elsewhere, the number should be read as rhetoric of scale rather than a count. Delhi's installation of Khizr Khan and the renaming of the fort reflect an intention to hold it, but the intention proved unsustainable: Chittor was costly to garrison, remote from the Doab, and surrounded by Guhila kin groups and chiefs whose followers survived the siege.

Regional sources show how differently these wars were remembered. The Sanskrit Hammira Mahakavya, composed probably in the fifteenth century at the Tomar court of Gwalior, celebrates Hammira as a model warrior-king and shaped the later image of Ranthambore. Later bardic chronicles, and the nineteenth-century compilation of James Tod's Annals and Antiquities of Rajasthan, which drew on them, made sieges such as Chittor emblems of heroic sacrifice. These texts are valuable for the history of Rajput identity and political memory (Chapter 35), but they were composed for patrons who wanted ancestors of heroic stature, and they differ from the Persian accounts in details of motive, leadership, and outcome. Historians therefore use them to understand how lineages and courts remembered defeat, not to reconstruct the events of 1301 and 1303 where contemporary evidence exists.

Section 41.10

Malwa, Siwana, Jalore, and the Consolidation of Northern Conquests

After Chittor, Alauddin's commanders extended Delhi's control through central India and western Rajasthan. In 1305, Ain al-Mulk Multani, a senior administrator and commander, defeated the Paramara ruler of Malwa, Mahlakadeva, and his chief minister, and took the principal towns of the region, including Ujjain, Mandu, Dhar, and Chanderi. Malwa, a rich agricultural zone that linked the Gangetic plain, Gujarat, and the Deccan, became a Delhi province. The evidence for the conquest comes from Barani, Khusrau, and Isami; the administrative letters known as the Insha-i Mahru are not part of it, because they were written by a different official (see the note on the two Ain al-Mulks below).

In western Rajasthan, Alauddin personally led the capture of Siwana in 1308, defeating the Chauhan ruler Sital Deva. Jalore, held by the Songara Chauhan ruler Kanhadadeva, was taken around 1311, though the dates and details of that campaign come largely from later regional traditions and are less secure. Delhi placed garrisons and governors in these strongholds. Yet control remained thin. Fortresses could be held by garrisons, but the surrounding countryside depended on local chiefs and landholding communities, and officials faced the problem of collecting revenue in regions with difficult terrain and strong local lords.

These conquests formed a chain of strongholds and provinces linking Delhi to Gujarat and Malwa, a strategic and commercial achievement. Gujarat's coastal wealth could now flow toward the capital, and the routes through Malwa gave access to the Deccan. They also revealed limits: Delhi's officials relied on garrisons, military pressure, and cooperation from local chiefs, and none of these was permanent. The consolidation of the north thus prepared the way for the Deccan expeditions, but it also showed that conquering a fortress and governing its hinterland were very different tasks.

Students should be alert to a frequent confusion in the literature. The general who conquered Malwa, Ain al-Mulk Multani, is a different person from Ain al-Mulk ibn Mahru, whose collection of letters (Insha-i Mahru) survives; ibn Mahru belongs to the later Tughlaq period, and his correspondence therefore cannot be used as direct evidence for the 1305 conquest or for Alauddin-era provincial administration. Peter Jackson's political and military history of the sultanate discusses the confusion. For Malwa, as for most Khalji provinces, even basic questions of how conquered regions were governed rest on court narrative rather than on surviving administrative documents.

Malwa mattered because of geography. Its plateau commands the routes between the Gangetic plain, the Narmada valley, Gujarat, and the Deccan, and its towns, Ujjain, Dhar, Mandu, and Chanderi, controlled agricultural surplus and trade. The Paramara kingdom had already been battered by Yadava, Chahamana, and earlier sultanate attacks, and its ruler's defeat in 1305 was made easier by the fragmentation of local power. Ujjain, an old religious and astronomical center, now came under Muslim governors, an example of how conquest altered the ritual as well as the political geography of a region, though the sources say little about how local temple communities, merchants, and landholders adapted.

In practice, control of a province meant assigning a governor, usually holding the region as an iqta or its equivalent, supplying him with troops, and expecting him to collect revenue, remit a surplus, and suppress disorder. In the Rajasthan strongholds, the garrison commander was both a soldier and a revenue collector, dependent on supplies from the surrounding countryside and vulnerable if local chiefs refused to cooperate. Local rulers had options: submission and payment, flight to another fortress, alliance with a rival, or resistance. The same lineages that lost Ranthambore or Siwana often reappeared as rulers elsewhere, so the Khalji conquests redistributed power among Rajput and other regional lineages rather than extinguishing them. The result by about 1311 was a network of Delhi-held points and provinces that looked impressive on a map and depended heavily on garrison strength.

Section 41.11

Devagiri and the Yadavas: From Raiding to Subordination

Devagiri, the Yadava capital on a rocky hill in the northwestern Deccan, was the gateway to the peninsula. Alauddin's raid of 1296 had established a relationship of ransom and promised tribute with Ramachandra, but the Yadava ruler did not pay reliably, and Karna of Gujarat had taken refuge in nearby territory. In 1307 Alauddin sent Malik Kafur against Devagiri. Ramachandra was defeated, taken to Delhi, and received with honor; he was restored to his kingdom with a title, and, according to Persian sources, gave a daughter in marriage to the sultan. The incident displays a characteristic Khalji method: a defeated ruler might be kept in place as a tributary and ally rather than removed, provided he supplied wealth, supplies, and support for campaigns farther south.

Devagiri then served as the forward base for operations in the Deccan. When Kafur marched against Warangal in 1309, Ramachandra provided provisions and guides. But the arrangement did not last. After Ramachandra's death, his son Shankaradeva stopped sending tribute, and in 1313 Kafur marched again and defeated him. Shankaradeva was killed, and Devagiri came under much closer Delhi control, with Kafur remaining there for a time. A revolt in 1315 led by a Yadava relative, Harapaladeva, was eventually suppressed under Alauddin's successor, Mubarak Shah, who had the rebel executed in 1318. In the Tughlaq period (Chapter 42), Devagiri would be renamed Daulatabad and become, briefly, the capital.

Devagiri therefore illustrates movement along a spectrum: raid, ransom, tribute, restored tributary kingship, and then military occupation and provincial rule. The stages were not inevitable or planned from the start, and they were shaped by local politics as much as by Delhi's intentions. Yadava rulers also had their own interests: they sought to preserve their kingdom, to use Delhi's strength against their rivals, and to avoid destruction. Treating them as passive victims misses this agency.

Why did Delhi treat Devagiri differently from Warangal? Devagiri lay on the corridor from Malwa and Gujarat into the Deccan, and its position made it the logical forward base for every later expedition; keeping it under a cooperative or subordinated ruler secured supply, intelligence, and a place to rest. Ramachandra's restoration in 1307, as the sources report it, allowed Delhi to use Yadava authority to keep local order while demanding provisioning and tribute. Isami's account, written in 1350 in the circle of the newly founded Bahmani sultanate (Chapter 46), views these events from a Deccan setting and shows how later Deccan Muslim rulers remembered the Khalji campaigns as the beginning of Muslim power in the region; it should be read alongside Barani's Delhi perspective.

Yadava inscriptions, together with the writings associated with the Yadava minister Hemadri and with Marathi devotional authors traditionally dated to the late thirteenth century, show a rich courtly and religious culture in Maharashtra under Yadava rule (Chapter 48 treats devotional movements more fully). That culture did not vanish with the Delhi conquest, but royal Yadava patronage ended and the region's political center shifted toward Delhi's governors. The shift illustrates a general point: the Khalji conquests changed the framework of patronage and power in the Deccan long before they changed its languages, religions, or agrarian routines.

Section 41.12

Malik Kafur and the Kakatiyas of Warangal

The Kakatiya kingdom, with its capital at Warangal in the Telugu country, was one of the richest and best organized polities of the eastern Deccan, with an extensive network of tanks (irrigation reservoirs), temples, and fortresses. It was ruled by Prataparudra (Rudradeva II). In late 1309, Malik Kafur left Delhi with a large army, marched via Devagiri, and invaded the Kakatiya territory. After a siege of the fort of Warangal, whose outer walls were of earth and the inner of stone, Prataparudra agreed to terms in early 1310.

Barani describes the submission in symbolic terms: the Kakatiya ruler sent out a golden image of himself with a chain around its neck, signifying his surrender, and then agreed to deliver a large payment of treasure, elephants, and horses, as well as annual tribute. Kafur returned to Delhi with immense wealth, and Alauddin rewarded him. The campaign shows again the distinction between plunder and rule: Delhi did not annex Warangal; instead, it imposed a heavy indemnity and tributary obligations, leaving the Kakatiya government intact. Warangal remained under its own ruler until the early 1320s, when a Tughlaq prince, the future Muhammad bin Tughluq, captured it (Chapter 42).

The Warangal expedition also demonstrates the limits of northern military power in the eastern Deccan. The distance from Delhi was enormous, supply lines were long, and the army depended on the cooperation of the Yadavas in Devagiri. The campaign succeeded because Kafur could concentrate a large force, exploit rivalries, and make the cost of resistance high, but it did not create the administrative presence needed to govern the region. Prataparudra's submission was a diplomatic and military settlement, not the creation of a province.

The Kakatiya state shows why submission, rather than conquest, was Delhi's practical outcome. Kakatiya rule depended on a network of warrior chiefs (nayakas), tank-irrigated agricultural villages, and temple endowments recorded in Telugu and Sanskrit inscriptions, and the dynasty's authority was strongest in the royal core around Warangal. A Delhi army could besiege the fort, but it could not displace this network without a lasting occupation, which the distances involved made impractical. Prataparudra's submission preserved his dynasty and the structure of local rule, while the indemnity returned to Delhi as plunder and tribute. After Tughlaq forces took Warangal in the early 1320s, Kakatiya rule ended, and within roughly a decade nayaka leaders, notably of the Musunuri family, led a movement that expelled Delhi's governors from much of the Telugu country. The episode shows how thin imposed rule could be when local military networks survived.

Section 41.13

The Hoysalas, the Pandyas, and the Southern Campaigns

In 1310–1311, Kafur led what was probably the most distant expedition any Delhi army had yet undertaken. He marched first against the Hoysalas, whose capital Dwarasamudra (modern Halebidu) lay in the Karnataka plateau. The Hoysala ruler Ballala III submitted after the city was taken and agreed to tribute and cooperation, a pattern similar to that of Devagiri and Warangal. The Hoysala dynasty remained in power, though weakened, and its eventual decline created space for new states in the region, including Vijayanagara (Chapter 45).

Kafur then moved into the Pandya country (Ma'bar in Persian usage), where a dynastic quarrel between two sons of the Pandya king had broken out. Persian sources suggest that one claimant appealed for outside help, giving Delhi a pretext. Kafur's army reached Madurai in 1311, but the main Pandya ruler fled, and the army plundered the region rather than conquering it. A later tradition, associated principally with the historian Firishta (c. 1600) rather than with Barani or Khusrau, holds that Kafur reached Rameswaram and built a mosque there; the report, the identification of the supposed building, and its location are disputed, and the problem is discussed in Muhammad Habib's introduction to his translation of Khusrau's Khaza'in al-Futuh. Kafur returned to Delhi with a very large amount of booty; Barani's figures for elephants, horses, and gold are huge, and, as elsewhere, they should be regarded as indicators of scale rather than exact counts.

Why the Pandya country? The wealth of the southern ports and temples was one motive. Another may have been the horse trade: Marco Polo and the Persian historian Wassaf both describe the large imports of Arabian and Persian horses into Pandya ports, a trade of obvious interest to a state whose power depended on cavalry. Southern sources, including Tamil inscriptions, show the disruption caused by the raids and the Pandya political crisis that followed, but not any lasting Delhi administration. The southern campaigns thus extended Delhi's military reputation and filled its treasury, but they did not extend its government.

The southern campaigns reveal the limits of a conquest based on mobile cavalry. Kafur's army moved quickly and struck wealthy centers, but at such distances from Delhi it could not remain. The Hoysala and Pandya ruling houses survived, sometimes weakened, and southern inscriptions record the dynastic quarrels and local reorganization that followed. The Pandya succession dispute shows that Delhi exploited a division already present rather than creating it, though the arrival of a northern army probably deepened the crisis by weakening Pandya authority in the temple towns and ports of the region at a moment of political contest.

Comparing the cases clarifies why Delhi's outcomes differed. Devagiri was near enough to be supplied and garrisoned and was needed as a base, so it moved from tribute toward annexation. Warangal and Dwarasamudra were beyond reliable supply, had strong local military networks, and could pay tribute at moderate cost to Delhi, so submission sufficed. The Pandya country was farthest, yielded wealth without offering the possibility of control, and was left to its own politics. In each case the decisive variables were distance, supply, the cohesion of the local state, and the availability of a cooperative intermediary, rather than any consistent Delhi plan to annex the south. Temple towns and ports bore the plunder, and the merchants of the Coromandel and Malabar trades, who depended on stable ports and predictable kings, experienced the raids as a disruption whose full effects the sources do not allow us to measure.

Section 41.14

What Did Khalji Expansion Actually Establish?

It is now possible to compare the forms of authority Delhi established. In Gujarat and Malwa, Delhi appointed governors, garrisons, and revenue officials, and these regions became provinces, though local resistance and the practical limits of administration meant control was uneven. In Rajasthan, Delhi held major fortresses such as Ranthambore and Jalore with garrisons, while regional chiefs and local populations continued to exercise power in the countryside; Chittor was held only briefly. In the Deccan, Devagiri moved from tribute toward direct control, while Warangal and Dwarasamudra accepted tribute and a degree of subordination without Delhi taking over their governments. In the far south, the Pandya country was raided and plundered but not placed under Delhi's authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribute, Suzerainty, and Annexation

Tribute refers to payments, goods, or services given by one ruler to another as an acknowledgment of the latter's superior power. Suzerainty describes a relationship in which a dominant ruler claims overlordship while the subordinate continues to rule his own territory. Annexation means the incorporation of territory into the dominant state's own administration, with officials appointed by the conqueror. In practice the boundaries among these were fluid: a tributary could be annexed later, as happened at Devagiri, or could cease paying when the conqueror's army withdrew.

The distinction matters because chronicles often describe any victory as a conquest. Historians must ask whether Delhi appointed governors, collected revenue, issued coins, stationed troops, or merely received payments, since each indicates a different degree of control.

Section 41.15

Land Revenue, Measurement, and the Ganga-Yamuna Doab

Territorial expansion and a large standing army required reliable revenue, and the most important source was land. According to Barani, Alauddin ordered the measurement of cultivated land, at least in the Doab and adjoining regions, and set the state's demand at approximately half of the produce. Land in the Doab was largely brought under direct administration (khalisa), meaning its revenue went to the royal treasury rather than to assignees. The sultan's officials supervised assessment and collection, and a special office was created to pursue arrears.

Historical Vocabulary
Khalisa, Kharaj, and Iqta

Khalisa refers to land whose revenue was collected directly for the royal treasury. Kharaj was the term used in Islamic legal tradition for a land tax; in sultanate practice it generally meant the state's share of the agricultural produce. Iqta, examined in Chapter 40, was an assignment of the revenue of a territory to a military officer (muqta), who used it to maintain troops and administer the region, remitting any surplus. Alauddin enlarged the khalisa while still relying on iqta assignments elsewhere; the two systems coexisted.

Barani also describes a series of measures directed against the rural intermediaries who stood between the peasant and the state: village headmen and local magnates, called khuts, muqaddams, and chaudhuris. These groups had enjoyed privileges, including exemption from certain taxes and the ability to pass burdens to ordinary cultivators. Alauddin's rules, in Barani's account, removed many of these advantages and made them pay on the same basis as others. Additional taxes on houses and on grazing animals were imposed. Barani, whose sympathies lay with the courtly elite, reports that these policies drove the rural elite into poverty and kept them from displays of wealth, a claim that reflects his own view of social order.

How far did these policies reach? Barani's description centers on the Doab and the regions near Delhi, and modern historians generally doubt that measurement and a uniform half-share demand were applied across the Gujarat, Malwa, Rajasthan, or Deccan territories. Tributary kingdoms paid tribute, not regular revenue. Even in the Doab, the capacity of the state to measure and assess land depended on local officials, accountants, and village intermediaries whose cooperation was needed. Historians such as W. H. Moreland, Irfan Habib, and others have debated how systematic the measurement was and how long the high demand lasted. The safest conclusion is that Alauddin intensified revenue extraction in the central territories and strengthened supervision, while the overall system remained uneven.

Assessing land by measurement required a chain of personnel: surveyors, village accountants, and revenue officials able to compare the measured area with the actual crops. Barani presents a bureaucracy kept under close audit, in which officials' accounts were inspected and arrears pursued by the mustakhraj, and he says that service as a revenue clerk lost its attraction because the penalties for error or dishonesty were severe. This should be read partly as a moral portrait: Barani valued strict accountability and approved of the policy's effect on rural magnates he distrusted. What can be said more cautiously is that the sultan's government tried to narrow the gap between what cultivators produced and what the state knew they produced, and that this required the cooperation, or coercion, of local accountants and headmen, who understood the village and could resist or conceal.

The evidence for revenue practice is thin. No village-level assessment records from Alauddin's reign survive, and detailed revenue documents appear only in much later periods (see Chapter 55 for the Mughal agrarian system). Historians therefore reason from Barani's narrative, from comparison with later systems, and from what is known of the Doab's agriculture, which depended on monsoon rainfall and on wells and other local irrigation. A demand of half the produce could be collected, if at all, only in good harvest years and in villages close enough to Delhi for enforcement; elsewhere, magnates and assignees probably continued to retain a share. The policy's significance lies less in its precise rate than in its direction: toward greater central control of rural surplus at the expense of intermediaries and assignees.

Section 41.16

Soldiers, Horses, Salaries, and Military Supervision

A paid army creates opportunities for fraud: soldiers could be listed but absent, horses could be borrowed for inspection, and inferior animals could be substituted for good ones. Alauddin's response, as Barani describes it, was a system of inspection. Each soldier was recorded in a descriptive roll (chehra), and each horse was marked with an official brand (dagh). The office responsible for musters and the army's registers, headed by the ariz-i mamalik, was a major post. Horses were inspected for quality, and troops who did not meet standards could be dismissed or punished. The system is believed to have owed something to earlier Central Asian and Mongol practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Dagh and Chehra

Dagh means a brand or mark; in the Delhi Sultanate the term referred to the branding of horses to certify that an animal had passed inspection and belonged to the army's rolls. Chehra literally means "face" and referred to the descriptive roll recording each soldier's appearance and details. Together they were tools for preventing fraud in payment and in the supply of cavalry. Later rulers, including Sher Shah and the Mughals (Chapters 50 and 51), revived and adapted these methods.

Barani reports an annual salary of 234 tankas (silver coins) for a cavalryman, with an additional allowance for a second horse. The figure is a reported one: historians disagree about which military grade it describes and how the payment for additional horses was calculated, so it should not be taken as an unquestioned universal wage for every ordinary trooper. It may nevertheless indicate the order of magnitude of pay in the central army. The significance lies in the connection between salaries and prices: a soldier's pay was kept relatively low by the standards of what the sultan might otherwise have had to pay, and the market regulations discussed in the next section were intended, according to Barani, to make it possible for soldiers to live on this pay. Whether the system worked as described is uncertain, but the logic is clear: a large paid army required low and stable prices, honest musters, and reliable remittance of revenue to the treasury.

The dagh and chehra systems addressed a real administrative problem. In a force paid in cash and recruited from many sources, officers who mustered troops could inflate their numbers, borrow horses for inspection, or accept bribes to pass inferior animals, and Barani describes such malpractice and Alauddin's strictness with the muster officials. Because a trained warhorse was costly and had to be imported, the quality of the horse mattered as much as that of the rider, and the sultan's concern to examine animals reflects the cavalry's dependence on a long-distance trade (Section 41.7). How consistently these procedures were applied outside the Delhi army, and during campaigns, is uncertain; the central army was most subject to inspection, while troops serving under provincial governors followed their commanders' own arrangements. Historians therefore see the system as a significant attempt at bureaucratic control whose actual reach we cannot measure.

Section 41.17

Market Regulations, Grain Supplies, and Price Controls

The market policies for which Alauddin is best known are described mainly by Barani and, with differences, by Isami. According to these accounts, the sultan fixed prices for grain, cloth, sugar, oil, horses, slaves, cattle, and other goods sold in Delhi. Separate markets were organized: a grain market, a market for cloth and other manufactured goods (the sarai-i adl), and a market for horses, slaves, and cattle. The grain market was supervised by an official, the market superintendent (shahna-i mandi), and the markets as a whole fell under a department headed by a senior official. Merchants were registered and, in some accounts, supplied with advances of state funds so that they could bring goods, with Multani merchants prominent. Grain was collected as revenue in kind from the Doab and stored in state granaries, to be released in times of scarcity. Transporters of grain, often called banjaras, were organized in groups and held responsible for delivery.

Enforcement relied on intelligence officers and market superintendents who checked weights and prices and reported to the sultan. Barani reports harsh penalties for cheating, including the cutting of flesh from a seller equal to the amount of a short weight, a detail that cannot be independently verified and may reflect his desire to dramatize the sultan's severity. Barani also gives specific prices for wheat, barley, rice, horses, and slaves, and states that prices remained stable even in years of poor rainfall. Historians accept that something like a price-control regime existed, but they question how complete it was, how long it lasted, and how far beyond Delhi it extended. The state's ability to supply grain depended on the revenue demand in the Doab, a link that shows how the agricultural and market policies fitted together.

Three features of the system deserve separation, because Barani's narrative blends them. First, there were the markets themselves: the grain market, the sarai-i adl, and the market for horses, slaves, and cattle. Second, there were the officials: a market superintendent for the grain market, a head of the market department, subordinate inspectors, and intelligence agents who reported prices; Barani names individual officers, but the overall organization is known only from his account. Third, there was the supporting system: collection of revenue in kind in the Doab, storage in state granaries, control of grain transporters, and credit advances to selected merchants. Without the third element the first two could not have operated, since price controls without a source of supply create shortages. This is why historians treat the regulations as an expression of the revenue and military systems of Sections 41.15 and 41.16 rather than as an isolated experiment in economic policy.

The social effects of the regime were uneven. Barani reports that during droughts grain was rationed by household in the capital, a feature that suggests the system's priority was Delhi's population and army. Merchants and grain carriers, who dealt with a state able to punish and to reward, had strong incentives to comply. Peasants who supplied the grain as revenue had no comparable protection, and nothing in the sources suggests that low prices in Delhi benefited cultivators elsewhere. The system's gains were thus concentrated in the capital and its army, while its costs were widely distributed.

Historical Debate

Why Did Alauddin Regulate Prices, and What Did the Policies Achieve?

The question: Were Alauddin's market regulations primarily a military and fiscal measure to support a large army, a broader instrument of political control, or an exaggerated tradition that grew in Barani's retelling?

Interpretation A — A military-fiscal solution: Barani himself explains the policy as a means of making it possible to maintain a large army, under Mongol threat, on moderate salaries. Historians such as W. H. Moreland and many later textbook accounts accept this connection, pointing to the links among revenue in kind, state granaries, and soldiers' pay, and to the timing of the policy after the major Mongol invasions of 1299–1303.
Interpretation B — A limited and coercive regime: Other historians, including Irfan Habib, emphasize that the controls were largely confined to Delhi and its neighborhood, that they depended on forced revenue collection from the peasantry and on harsh supervision of merchants, and that they were hard to sustain without the sultan's personal intimidation. They also note that Barani, writing long afterward, may have idealized or exaggerated the policy's success, and that the policy collapsed under Alauddin's successor.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely heavily on Barani and Isami, with some support from Amir Khusrau and from administrative language preserved in later sources. Independent price lists, market records, or documentary evidence from the period are scarce, which limits how far the debate can be resolved.

Why it matters: If the policy was a coherent solution to an army-financing problem, it indicates sophisticated state-building and a close link between military and economic institutions. If it was narrowly confined and enforced by fear, it suggests a state that could achieve short-term results in the capital without building durable institutions.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence that would help determine whether price controls operated outside Delhi, and explain why Barani's explanation of the policy should be tested rather than accepted outright.

Section 41.18

Controlling Nobles, Wealth, and Political Opposition

The early revolts of Alauddin's reign shaped one of the most distinctive elements of his government: an effort to eliminate what he saw as the causes of rebellion. Barani describes a set of measures that he says were devised after the sultan consulted trusted advisers. They included resuming land grants and religious endowments given by earlier rulers, which brought more land and revenue under the crown; creating an extensive network of informants who reported on nobles, officials, and ordinary people; prohibiting the sale and drinking of wine and intoxicants in Delhi; and forbidding nobles to hold social gatherings, form marriage alliances, or visit one another without the sultan's permission.

These measures were intended to deprive nobles of the wealth, leisure, and social ties that could support conspiracy. Confiscation of grants affected not only the military elite but also religious families and scholars who had held tax-free lands. Informants made private life dangerous for the elite, and punishments, including imprisonment, blinding, and death, were severe. The measures illustrate the sultan's belief that political stability depended on surveillance and fear. They also had effects Alauddin may not have intended: a court in which nobles could not trust one another, and where the most capable commanders were either kinsmen or slaves dependent on the sultan, was a court with few independent supporters if the sultan weakened.

The political consequences became visible after Alauddin's illness. The system worked as long as the sultan was physically capable and feared; it did not create institutions that could survive his incapacity. In this sense the very success of his methods of control contributed to the crisis of 1316 (Section 41.21).

Barani's list of four measures is better read as a retrospective explanation than as a document of policy. He presents the sultan reflecting, with advice from Ala al-Mulk (Barani's own uncle), on four causes of disorder: the ruler's ignorance of what his subjects were doing; wine-drinking and the gatherings it encouraged; the intimacy of nobles who formed ties through marriage and visits; and the wealth that gave them independence. The tidy list suggests that Barani, writing long afterward, organized scattered measures into a coherent program. Even so, the individual actions are plausible: confiscation of grants (milk, inam, and waqf) increased crown revenue, and a network of informants operating through the markets, the nobility, and the army gave the sultan information at the cost of trust.

The effects on religious and scholarly groups were mixed. Families who held revenue-free grants lost them, while Sufi communities such as that of Nizam al-Din Auliya, whose hospices lived on voluntary offerings rather than state grants, continued to attract followers from across Delhi society, and tradition holds that such saints kept their distance from the sultan. The evidence comes chiefly from later hagiographical texts written to present the saints as independent of royal power, so students should treat these accounts as part of the Sufi community's own memory.

Section 41.19

Peasants, Merchants, Soldiers, and the Social Costs of Empire

Khalji expansion and administration affected different groups unequally. Peasants in the Doab and neighboring regions faced a heavy revenue demand and additional taxes, and local intermediaries lost privileges. For ordinary cultivators the burden was likely severe, especially in poor harvest years, though the extent to which the stated half-share of produce was actually collected is uncertain. Merchants faced registration, supervision, and punishment for violations, though some, such as those who could obtain state advances or who supplied the capital's markets, may have profited. Urban consumers in Delhi, particularly soldiers and others living on cash wages, benefited from stable prices, at least according to Barani.

Soldiers experienced mixed conditions: regular pay and, for some, a share of plunder, but also rigorous inspection and punishments for fraud. Mongol converts, the "New Muslims," suffered terrible violence after the revolt of 1299. Enslaved people formed a visible part of the economy. Delhi's markets sold slaves, many captured in war, including Mongols, and military slaves such as Malik Kafur could rise to high command, while most enslaved people, including women and children taken in campaigns, lived in domestic or laboring conditions about which the chronicles say little. Conquered regions bore the costs of plunder, destruction of temples and wealth, and heavy tribute, though the experience differed between a regional king who retained his throne as a tributary and a town sacked by an army.

Any balanced assessment must therefore include both administrative achievement and human cost. Alauddin's government mobilized resources, defended the capital against repeated invasion, and created institutions that later rulers studied. It did so through violence, surveillance, and extraction that fell most heavily on those with the least power to resist. The sources, written largely by and for the elite, provide only glimpses of the experiences of peasants, laborers, and the enslaved.

Labor and construction add another dimension. The building of Siri, the Hauz-i Alai, and the Qutb extensions required large numbers of skilled masons and laborers, and probably conscripted labor, though the sources rarely say so; conditions are inferred from the scale of the works and from parallels in other premodern states. Women appear in the sources chiefly as marriage ties and spoils: women of defeated royal houses, such as the Vaghela queen Kamala Devi captured in 1299, were absorbed into the sultan's household, and Amir Khusrau's romance Ashiqa (written about 1315) retells the courtship of Khizr Khan and Devaladevi, a Gujarat princess, in verse. Such stories show how conquest shaped marriage politics and how the court turned captives into literary subjects, while saying little about how the women themselves experienced capture.

Institutional Workshop

How the Khalji Machine Fit Together

Barani's account, if accepted, describes a chain of linked institutions. Students should trace how each step depended on the others:

  1. Revenue: measurement and a high demand in the Doab fill the treasury and the state's granaries.
  2. Army: a large cavalry force is paid in cash; dagh and chehra ensure that the sultan pays for real soldiers and real horses.
  3. Markets: state grain stocks and price controls keep the cost of living low enough for soldiers to survive on fixed pay.
  4. Control of elites: informants, confiscation, and restrictions on assemblies discourage rebellion by those who might exploit military or financial weakness.
  5. Expansion: plunder and tribute from conquests add to revenue, but also increase the army's size and the need for supervision.

Everyday Life and Social History

Markets, Garrisons, and Households in Khalji Delhi

For most inhabitants of the Doab and the capital region, the political events of the age reached them through taxes, grain, and soldiers. Villages bore the weight of assessment and the presence of officials; households in Delhi lived with spies, restrictions on assembly, and the rhythms of markets where the price of grain mattered more than the sultan's titles. A soldier's household depended on pay, the quality of his horse, and the results of inspection. Women's lives are poorly documented, though the sources show them as targets of political violence when families were punished and as victims of capture and enslavement in war.

Climate and environment also mattered. The Doab's fertility made it the fiscal center of the sultanate, but grain supply depended on the monsoon; Barani's claim that prices remained steady even in drought suggests either the effectiveness of state granaries or the persistence of a tradition of the sultan's power. Historians must be cautious in using such claims as evidence for how ordinary people actually fared.

Section 41.20

Delhi under the Khaljis: Court, Architecture, and Historical Writing

The Khalji sultans transformed the physical and cultural landscape of Delhi. Siri, the fortified city begun around 1303, became the royal residence and military center; parts of its walls survive. The Hauz-i Alai, a large reservoir later known as Hauz Khas, supplied water. At the Qutb complex, Alauddin began an enlargement of the Quwwat al-Islam mosque and an ambitious tower intended to surpass the Qutb Minar, which was left unfinished. The Alai Darwaza, a gateway completed in 1311, bears inscriptions naming the sultan and is notable for its use of red sandstone and white marble, its carved decoration, and its dome and arches built on principles familiar from the wider Islamic world. The building shows the court's links with Persianate architectural traditions and its desire to display imperial power.

Court culture was Persianate and cosmopolitan. Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), the most famous poet of the age, wrote court histories and verse, including the Khaza'in al-Futuh (1311), and was closely associated with the Chishti Sufi master Nizam al-Din Auliya. Sufi communities (Chapters 44 and 48) occupied an independent religious space in Delhi that sultans respected and sometimes courted. Coins issued by the sultan, in silver tanka and copper or billon jital, and inscriptions on buildings carry his titles and help historians understand how the court wished to present him.

The historical evidence for the period must be read critically. Khusrau wrote as a court poet, and his prose celebrates the sultan's victories in conventional rhetorical form. Barani wrote about sixty years later, in a period of different political conditions, with strong views about the proper relationship between kings and religion, and he described Alauddin's reforms with admiration and criticism. Isami wrote from the Deccan and offers a perspective less centered on Delhi. Later writers such as Firishta (c. 1600) drew on earlier works but added their own interpretation. For regional events, Sanskrit, Telugu, Tamil, and Kannada inscriptions and later epic or prose traditions offer other accounts. No single source provides a complete or unbiased picture.

Primary Source Window

Barani on the Purpose of the Market Regulations

Source type: Persian narrative history (Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi) · Author: Ziya al-Din Barani, courtier and historian · Date: completed c. 1357, roughly sixty years after the events · Place: Delhi

Context: Barani was born into a family of Delhi officials, and his uncle served Alauddin. He wrote after a long career at the courts of later sultans, and his history is also a work of moral and political argument about how kings should rule. His chapter on Alauddin's market regulations is the principal narrative account of those measures.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim translation): Barani explains that the sultan, concerned that the army required to resist the Mongols and to campaign could not be maintained on salaries sufficient for high prices, turned to fixing the price of grain, cloth, and other necessities at low levels, so that soldiers could live on modest pay. He then describes the system of controlled markets, the officials who supervised them, the role of merchants and grain transporters, and the penalties for those who cheated or broke the rules.

Analysis: The passage tells us how a well-informed member of the Delhi elite understood the policy: as a response to military necessity, achieved through state supervision and punishment. It also shows Barani's tendency to treat the success of royal policy as a measure of the king's power. It does not allow us to verify actual prices, how far controls extended beyond Delhi, how the system worked in years of poor harvest, or how merchants and consumers experienced it. It also does not tell us what peasants paid to supply the state's grain. Barani writes for an audience of court readers and may idealize the system, especially in contrast with the weaker rule of Alauddin's successors.

Section 41.21

The Death of Alauddin and the Crisis of Succession, 1316–1320

Alauddin's final years were marked by illness and political disorder. As the sultan weakened, Malik Kafur, who had risen from slave to commander of the southern campaigns, became the dominant figure at court. Kafur is reported to have turned the sultan against his sons and relatives; Alauddin's eldest son Khizr Khan was imprisoned during the sultan's last illness, and Alp Khan, the governor of Gujarat, was executed in about 1315. Alauddin died in early January 1316. Kafur placed a young son, Shihab al-Din Umar, on the throne and acted as regent; in the period of his ascendancy Khizr Khan was blinded, according to the chronology reconstructed by Peter Jackson shortly after Alauddin's death rather than before it. Within about a month Kafur himself was killed by palace guards loyal to Alauddin's family.

Another son, Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah, was enthroned and reigned until 1320. He reversed many of Alauddin's severe policies, releasing prisoners and returning confiscated lands, and, according to Barani, relaxed the price controls and the discipline of the army. He adopted the elevated title of caliph, an assertion of independence from the caliphal tradition that earlier sultans had acknowledged. Barani, who disapproved of his personal conduct, portrays his rule as marked by indulgence and misplaced trust in his favorite, Khusrau Khan, a convert of Gujarati origin who led expeditions to Devagiri (1317) and Warangal (1318) and received high rank.

In April 1320, Khusrau Khan assassinated Mubarak Shah in the palace and took the throne, killing members of the royal family. His brief rule met opposition from the Delhi aristocracy and from provincial commanders. Ghazi Malik, the warden of the northwestern frontier, marched on Delhi and defeated Khusrau Khan in battle in September 1320. Ghazi Malik was proclaimed sultan with the title Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, ending the Khalji dynasty. The speed of the collapse, from a dynasty that had defeated Mongols and subdued kingdoms to extinction within four years, is the central problem of the chapter's final sections.

Why did the succession crisis destroy the dynasty? Part of the explanation lies in the structure of Alauddin's court. His methods had thinned the ranks of independent nobles and concentrated power in family members and favorites whose loyalty was personal. Malik Kafur could dominate precisely because he had no lineage or faction of his own to threaten the ruling house, which made him attractive to the sultan and unacceptable to the princes and household guards who saw him as a usurper. The palace guards (paiks) who killed him, and the courtiers who elevated Mubarak Shah, show that real power lay in the palace and the capital's garrison rather than in the provincial armies. Mubarak's reversal of Alauddin's severity, whether from conviction or from the need to win support, loosened the controls that had held the system together and, according to Barani, led to higher prices and weaker discipline.

Khusrau Khan's coup raises a problem of interpretation. Barani, hostile to him, describes the new regime in religious terms, as a reaction against Muslim rule by the Hindu followers of the usurper. Some modern historians read the episode more cautiously, as a palace and factional struggle in which a favorite of lower-status origin, supported by troops who had risen under the Khaljis, challenged an aristocracy that felt displaced; the religious language of Barani's narrative reflects his own concerns and those of the elites who opposed Khusrau Khan. Ghazi Malik's victory in 1320 drew on the strength of the frontier army and the support of nobles who rejected the usurper, and it illustrates how legitimacy in this period was conferred by those who controlled the military and the capital.

Section 41.22

The Khalji Legacy: Expansion without Permanent Integration

The Khalji legacy was both substantial and unstable. Territorially, Delhi's reach extended to Gujarat, Malwa, the Rajasthan fortresses, and the Deccan, and its armies had demonstrated that the plateau and even the far south were accessible to northern forces. Administratively, innovations in revenue assessment, military inspection, and market supervision offered models that later rulers, from Muhammad bin Tughluq to Sher Shah and Akbar (Chapters 42, 50, and 51), would adapt. Culturally, the court's patronage of architecture, poetry, and Sufi communities contributed to the development of Indo-Persian culture (Chapter 44).

Yet the system rested on conditions that were difficult to reproduce. Continuing military success provided plunder and tribute; a strong and feared sultan held together a court of dependent commanders; a combination of coercion and fixed pay kept the army loyal. Provinces and tributaries accepted Delhi's authority when its forces were present and could revolt when they were not. The state's dependence on one ruler's personal control left it vulnerable to illness, succession disputes, and palace intrigue.

What remained after 1320? Gujarat, Malwa, and Devagiri remained Delhi provinces for at least some time, and the Tughlaq conquest of Warangal (Chapter 42) built on Kakatiya submission and Khalji precedents. Chittor, Ranthambore, and other Rajasthan strongholds were lost or contested, and tributaries lapsed. Alauddin's regulations were weakened or reversed under Mubarak Shah, and the machinery of cash-paid armies and price control had to be re-created, adapted, or abandoned by the Tughlaqs. Many of the institutions studied in Sections 41.15 to 41.18 survive in the sources as models and precedents, which is part of why Barani and later writers wrote about them: they were arguments about how rulers should behave as much as descriptions of how they did.

Historians have framed the Khalji period in different ways. Older accounts often stressed either Alauddin's greatness as an empire-builder or his despotism, and they tended to read the period through modern concerns with state power or communal relations. More recent work, such as Peter Jackson's political and military history of the sultanate, has paid closer attention to the limits of the sources, to the Mongol frontier, and to the personal and factional character of sultanate politics. This chapter follows that more cautious tradition: it treats Khalji power as real and consequential, but also as dependent, uneven, and difficult to measure.

Cause and Effect

Why Khalji Expansion Did Not Secure the Dynasty

Long-term causes

The sultanate's reliance on personal military authority and plunder; the Mongol threat that required a large army; the political legacy of slave and factional elites; the wealth of neighboring regional kingdoms.

Short-term causes

Jalal al-Din's weak control; Alauddin's Devagiri plunder and coup; early rebellions that led to coercive control of nobles; the opportunity created by rich, divided Deccan kingdoms.

Immediate effects

Territorial expansion; a large paid army; heavy revenue demands; a fearful nobility; a treasury enriched by plunder and tribute.

Long-term consequences

Models of administration for later states; but also provincial instability, dependence on one ruler, and a succession crisis that ended the dynasty within four years of Alauddin's death.

Historical complexity: The collapse cannot be reduced to Alauddin's illness, Kafur's ambition, or Mubarak Shah's character. It reflected structural weaknesses: a system built on coercive personal control, a court without independent institutions, a fragile fiscal basis, and the thinness of Delhi's hold over distant regions.

Section 41.23

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has asked how the Khalji rulers transformed the territorial reach and governing capacity of the Delhi Sultanate, and why their unprecedented military and administrative achievements failed to secure the permanence of their dynasty. The answer begins with comparison. The Mamluk state described in Chapter 40 had been durable but regionally limited: it had protected the Doab and Punjab, built the iqta system, and defended against Mongols, but its authority did not extend deep into Rajasthan, Gujarat, Malwa, or the Deccan. The Khalji state was larger in reach, better financed from central lands, and more intrusive in its control of soldiers, nobles, and markets. It was also more dependent on the personal power of a single ruler.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Balban to Alauddin

What Changed

  • The ruling house and part of the elite changed from the Mamluk dynasty and its slave-descended nobility to a Khalji-led coalition and then Alauddin's inner circle.
  • Territorial reach expanded from the north to Gujarat, Malwa, Rajasthan, and tributary relationships in the Deccan, with raids into the far south.
  • The central army grew and was paid in cash; inspection by dagh and chehra was introduced.
  • Revenue demands in the Doab were intensified, with a greater share of land under the crown.
  • Market regulation and surveillance of nobles became instruments of government.

What Stayed the Same

  • Power remained military, personal, and dependent on cavalry and the ruler's reputation.
  • The Mongol threat shaped strategy before and after 1290.
  • Persianate court culture, Sufi communities, and the iqta system remained features of the sultanate.
  • Succession remained violent and contested.

Who experienced change most directly: Delhi nobles faced the new surveillance; Doab cultivators and rural intermediaries faced new revenue demands; conquered regions faced invasion and tribute. Many villages beyond the central territories experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows a state that grew in power without creating institutions that could survive a weak ruler.

The synthesis can be stated in four points. First, the Khaljis changed the sultanate's scale: their conquests and tributary relationships linked Delhi to western and peninsular India more extensively than any earlier sultanate government. Second, they changed its governing capacity, at least in the central territories, through revenue measurement, military inspection, and market control. Third, they did not produce uniform integration: provinces, garrisons, tributaries, and raided regions represented different kinds of authority, and many were unstable. Fourth, the dynasty's strength was concentrated in the sultan and a small circle of dependents, so illness and succession disputes could destroy in months what had been built over two decades.

Whether expansion made Delhi stronger or more vulnerable is best answered: both. It gave the sultanate wealth, prestige, and military experience, and it extended Delhi's influence into regions that would later be centers of the Bahmani and Vijayanagara states (Chapters 45 and 46) and of the regional sultanates (Chapter 47). It also increased the need for revenue and loyalty beyond what the system could provide without extraordinary coercion. Chapter 42 shows how the Tughlaqs, heirs to this inheritance, attempted even larger experiments and encountered the limits of sultanate empire.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Khalji dynasty's thirty years in power (1290–1320) and argued that its central achievement was to expand the military, territorial, fiscal, and administrative ambitions of the Delhi Sultanate, while failing to give that expansion permanent institutional form.

The Khalji revolution of 1290 followed the collapse of Mamluk authority after Balban and brought to power Jalal al-Din Khalji, whose conciliatory style and limited success proved inadequate against the ambitions of his nephew Alauddin. The transition cannot be reduced to a conflict of ethnic groups; it reflected a change in elite composition and political networks within the military society of the sultanate.

Alauddin's raid on Devagiri in 1296, with its large plunder, funded his seizure of the throne through the murder of Jalal al-Din. He then recast kingship through grand titles, independence from the advice of religious scholars, and a new inner circle of relatives and slave commanders, while suppressing rebellions with severity.

Repeated Mongol invasions between 1297 and 1308, including the siege of Delhi in 1303, shaped his policies. The sultan fortified Siri, strengthened the northwestern frontier, and expanded a cash-paid standing army, whose cost required new revenue and supply arrangements.

Khalji armies conquered Gujarat (1299), took Ranthambore (1301) and Chittor (1303), and brought Malwa, Siwana, and Jalore under Delhi's control. Persian court sources celebrate these victories, while regional traditions, such as the later story of Padmini, remember them differently and should not be mistaken for contemporary evidence.

In the Deccan and south, Malik Kafur's expeditions subordinated Devagiri, forced Warangal and Dwarasamudra into tributary obligations, and raided the Pandya country. These campaigns did not create a uniformly governed empire: annexation, military occupation, tribute, and plunder remained distinct forms of authority.

Alauddin's administrative measures, as described by Barani, included land measurement and high revenue demand in the Doab, branding of horses and descriptive rolls for soldiers, regulation of Delhi's markets, and surveillance and confiscation to control the nobility. These institutions were linked by the aim of supporting a large army, but their reach, duration, and effects remain debated, and they imposed heavy costs on peasants, intermediaries, merchants, soldiers, and the conquered.

The Khalji court produced important architecture, including Siri and the Alai Darwaza, and a Persianate literary culture exemplified by Amir Khusrau. The historical record depends on court chroniclers such as Khusrau and Barani, whose purposes and perspectives require critical analysis.

After Alauddin's death in 1316, Malik Kafur's ascendancy, Mubarak Shah's reversal of policy, and Khusrau Khan's coup destroyed the dynasty within four years, and in 1320 Ghazi Malik founded the Tughlaq dynasty. The collapse showed that a state dependent on a single ruler's coercive control and on continuing conquest could grow rapidly but could not easily survive his absence.

The Khalji experience stands as both foundation and warning for later rulers: it demonstrated what Delhi could attempt, and it showed what its institutions could not yet sustain. Chapter 42 follows the Tughlaq attempts to extend and reform that inheritance.

Key Terms

The dynasty (1290–1320) founded by Jalal al-Din Firuz Khalji; the Khalaj were a Turkic-origin people long settled in what is now southern Afghanistan.

Chapter Reflection

The Khalji period matters because it shows how far a medieval South Asian state could extend its power in a single generation, and how fragile such extension could be. Delhi's armies reached the Deccan plateau and the far south, its administrators experimented with measurement, inspection, and market regulation, and its court produced architecture and literature of lasting importance. These achievements make the era a major turning point in the political history of the subcontinent.

The period also teaches a methodological lesson. Almost everything students know about Alauddin's government comes from court writers who praised, feared, or later idealized him. Learning to separate Persian chronicle, regional memory, and modern interpretation is essential to understanding not only this chapter but all of the sultanate and Mughal periods that follow, and to resisting the political uses to which stories such as the Padmini legend have been put in modern times.

The long-term significance of the Khaljis lies in what they made thinkable and what they could not sustain. Their conquests drew Delhi into the politics of the Deccan and the south, contributing to the circumstances in which Vijayanagara, the Bahmani Sultanate, and the regional sultanates arose (Chapters 45 to 47). Their administrative methods anticipated the experiments of later rulers. And their collapse showed that territorial reach and fiscal ambition, without institutions that could outlast one ruler, produced a powerful but unstable state.

Chapter 42 now turns to the Tughlaqs, who inherited the Khalji frontier, the Khalji army, and the Khalji problem of governing a sultanate that reached farther than its institutions could hold.
