Indira Gandhi, the Emergency, and India's Democratic Crisis

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the early hours of 26 June 1975, police in Delhi and other cities began arresting leaders of the opposition. By morning the President of India had signed a proclamation of Emergency, the Cabinet had been convened after the fact, and the Prime Minister was addressing the country by radio. Within days the great majority of newspapers were working under censorship, thousands of people were in detention without trial, and the courts had been told that detainees could not ask them to enforce their fundamental rights. All of this was done under the authority of constitutional provisions drafted by the same Constituent Assembly that had created India's parliamentary democracy.

The Emergency of 1975–1977 is the most serious constitutional crisis in the history of the Republic of India. It lasted twenty-one months. It was proclaimed by a prime minister who had won one of the largest electoral mandates in the country's history only four years earlier, and it was ended, in March 1977, by the same prime minister's decision to hold a general election, which she lost. That sequence — extraordinary popular legitimacy, extraordinary concentration of power, suspension of ordinary politics, and then defeat at the polls — is what makes the Emergency both a story of democratic failure and a story of democratic survival. This chapter is responsible for explaining both halves of that story and for resisting the temptation to tell only one of them.

The chapter begins in 1966, when Indira Gandhi became prime minister as a compromise choice of senior Congress leaders who expected to guide her. It traces how she defeated those leaders, split the party that had led the independence movement, nationalized the major banks, and won in 1971 a campaign built around the promise to remove poverty. It then examines how the political position that seemed unassailable after the 1971 war with Pakistan, narrated in Chapter 92, eroded between 1973 and 1975 under inflation, scarcity, allegations of corruption, mass movements in Gujarat and Bihar, and finally a High Court judgment that declared the Prime Minister's own election void. The second half of the chapter analyzes how the Emergency functioned: through constitutional amendment, censorship, preventive detention, a compliant judiciary at the apex, the informal authority of the Prime Minister's younger son Sanjay Gandhi, and campaigns of sterilization and urban clearance whose human costs fell most heavily on the poor.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, this is not a biography of Indira Gandhi. Her decisions are central, but the Emergency also required the cooperation, silence, or acquiescence of ministers, senior civil servants, police officers, party officials, judges, editors, and ordinary citizens, and understanding why so many complied is as important as understanding why one person chose to impose it. Second, the Emergency was legal in a narrow sense: the proclamation was made under Article 352 as the Constitution then stood, and the Supreme Court accepted important consequences of it. That legality is itself the historical problem, because it shows that a constitution may contain the means of its own suspension. Third, documented repression should not be confused with contested interpretation. That people were detained without trial, that newspapers were censored, and that sterilization and demolition drives caused deaths and suffering are matters of public record, including the findings of an official commission. What remains debated are causes, motives, proportions, and meanings.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Court judgments, constitutional texts, parliamentary statements, official returns of the Election Commission, and the reports of the Shah Commission of Inquiry survive, alongside memoirs, journalism, diaries, and oral testimony. Each has limits. Judgments establish legal reasoning but not what happened in a police station. Official statistics record targets and returns but not consent. The Shah Commission was appointed by a government with a political interest in the findings, though its documentary record is among the most important sources for the period. Memoirs written after 1977 are shaped by what authors wished to be remembered for having done, or not done, during the Emergency. The chapter therefore treats sources as evidence to be weighed rather than as verdicts.

The chapter also marks a boundary within Volume VI. Chapter 92 narrated the Bangladesh Liberation War and the war of 1971 as events in regional and international history; this chapter takes up only their consequences for Indira Gandhi's domestic authority. Chapter 94 examines the Green Revolution, caste politics, regional parties, the Assam Movement, the Punjab crisis, Operation Blue Star, and Indira Gandhi's assassination, and these subjects are introduced here only where necessary. The Janata government is studied here for its work of democratic repair and its immediate fragmentation, not as a full administration.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain how a democratically elected government accumulated and used extraordinary power, why institutional safeguards failed in 1975–1976, why electoral competition nevertheless proved capable of reversing the crisis, and why constitutional reform after 1977 reduced but did not eliminate the risk of executive overreach. The chapter's central question is therefore: how did India's democratic institutions permit the concentration and abuse of executive power during the Emergency, and how did electoral competition, political opposition, and constitutional reform contribute to democratic recovery?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Indira Gandhi's initial dependence on Congress leaders gave way, between 1966 and 1971, to personal control of the party and the government.
  2. Analyze the Congress split of 1969, bank nationalization, and the 1971 Garibi Hatao campaign as linked steps in the redefinition of political authority.
  3. Distinguish between political messaging about poverty removal and measurable socioeconomic outcomes in the early 1970s.
  4. Assess the combined effects of inflation, scarcity, the 1973 oil-price shock, and corruption allegations on public confidence in the government.
  5. Compare the Gujarat and Bihar movements and evaluate the relationship between mass protest and parliamentary opposition.
  6. Distinguish the findings of the Allahabad High Court on 12 June 1975 from a criminal conviction, and a conditional stay from a reversal.
  7. Explain the constitutional mechanics of Article 352 and Article 359 and the difference among the Emergency proclamations of 1962, 1971, and 1975.
  8. Evaluate the effects of the 38th, 39th, and 42nd Amendments, press censorship, preventive detention, and the ADM Jabalpur decision on constitutional checks.
  9. Interpret the role of Sanjay Gandhi as informal authority, and analyze the sterilization and urban-clearance campaigns using quantitative and documentary evidence while distinguishing procedures from coercion and deaths.
  10. Describe how class, gender, region, and occupation shaped experiences of and resistance to Emergency rule.
  11. Analyze the 1977 election, the formation of the Janata Party, and the first transfer of national power away from Congress.
  12. Evaluate the legacy of the Emergency, including the 44th Amendment, and explain why constitutional reform did not eliminate future risks of executive overreach.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a leader chosen in 1966 as a compromise gain, within five years, an authority that her chosers had not intended her to have?
  2. In what ways did the Congress split of 1969 change the relationship among the party organization, the government, and the Prime Minister?
  3. Why did a landslide victory in 1971 not secure the Prime Minister's political position for the long term?
  4. To what extent were the Gujarat and Bihar movements responses to economic distress, and to what extent were they challenges to the legitimacy of the government itself?
  5. Was the Emergency primarily the result of an immediate crisis of leadership in June 1975, or did it reveal deeper weaknesses in India's political and constitutional institutions?
  6. What does it mean that the Emergency was constitutional in form but disputed in legitimacy?
  7. Why did so many institutions — Parliament, most of the press, much of the bureaucracy, and the Supreme Court — fail to check executive power, and where did resistance occur?
  8. How should historians distinguish the scale of sterilization procedures from the scale of coercion, and what evidence supports each claim?
  9. How did the Emergency affect rural and urban, poor and affluent, male and female, Hindu, Muslim, and other communities differently?
  10. Why did electoral competition succeed in ending the Emergency, and why did the reforms that followed not eliminate the possibility of its repetition?

Historical Context

The constitutional order Indira Gandhi inherited was the one analyzed in Chapters 88 and 89. India was a parliamentary federal republic with a written constitution, universal adult franchise, an independent Election Commission, and a Supreme Court empowered to enforce fundamental rights. The Indian National Congress had won every general election from 1952 to 1962 and had governed almost every state. This dominance was real but should not be mistaken for the absence of politics: Congress was a coalition of regional leaders, caste and landholding interests, trade unionists, and ideological currents, and its internal bargaining was the principal arena in which national policy was decided. Jawaharlal Nehru's death in May 1964 and Lal Bahadur Shastri's in January 1966 therefore posed not only a question of who would lead but a question of how a dominant party would reproduce itself without a founding leader.

The economic setting was difficult. India fought wars with China in 1962 (Chapter 92) and Pakistan in 1965, suffered two successive poor monsoons in 1965 and 1966, and depended on imported food grain, notably from the United States, at a time when American policy was sensitive to Indian positions on Vietnam. The third Five-Year Plan had strained resources, foreign-exchange reserves were low, and in June 1966 the government devalued the rupee under pressure from creditors, a decision that was unpopular across the political spectrum. The state's capacity for administering a planned, licensed economy was also under growing criticism, from the left for failing to redistribute and from the right for restricting private enterprise.

Constitutional law was in flux. The Fundamental Rights chapter guaranteed property as a fundamental right until the 44th Amendment (enacted 1978–79), and the courts repeatedly struck down land-reform and nationalization measures; Parliament responded by amending the Constitution. The central constitutional question of the 1960s and early 1970s was therefore whether Parliament's power to amend the Constitution was limited. In the Golaknath case of 1967 the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not abridge fundamental rights, and in Kesavananda Bharati in April 1973 a divided bench of thirteen judges held that Parliament could amend the Constitution but not alter its "basic structure." The tension between a Prime Minister committed to rapid social change through parliamentary majorities and a judiciary protecting existing constitutional limits shaped the decade and recurs throughout this chapter.

Finally, the Emergency cannot be understood without noticing the constitutional text on which it relied. Article 352, as it stood in 1975, allowed the President to proclaim an Emergency if satisfied that "a grave emergency exists whereby the security of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened, whether by war or external aggression or internal disturbance." The President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, headed by the Prime Minister. Article 359 allowed the President, during an Emergency, to suspend the right to move courts to enforce specified fundamental rights. Neither article had been used for internal political reasons before 1975. The Constitution's framers had accepted such powers, with some misgivings, for conditions of genuine crisis; Chapter 93 examines what happened when they were invoked in a political crisis whose nature was disputed.

Section 93.1 · Division I — Indira Gandhi's Rise and the Concentration of Power, 1966–1972

Indira Gandhi's Political Ascendancy: Succession, Congress Leadership, and the Struggle for Authority

Lal Bahadur Shastri died at Tashkent on 11 January 1966, hours after signing a declaration with Pakistan's President Ayub Khan that ended the war of 1965. For the second time in less than two years, the Congress party had to choose a prime minister without a recognized national leader. Gulzarilal Nanda served as acting Prime Minister while the party's senior organizational figures, a group journalists called the Syndicate, looked for a successor. The Syndicate's most influential member was K. Kamaraj, the Tamil Nadu leader who had become Congress president in 1963 and had been central to Shastri's selection. Others included S. Nijalingappa of Mysore, Atulya Ghosh of Bengal, S. K. Patil of Bombay, and N. Sanjiva Reddy of Andhra Pradesh.

The alternative to Indira Gandhi was Morarji Desai, a former finance minister with an independent political following and, to the organization's leaders, an unwelcome temperament. Indira Gandhi was the daughter of Jawaharlal Nehru, had served as Congress president in 1959, and was then minister of information and broadcasting. She had a national name, no independent organizational base, and a reputation, in the eyes of the leaders who backed her, for being manageable. On 19 January 1966 the Congress parliamentary party chose her in a secret ballot, by 355 votes to 169, and she was sworn in on 24 January. Critics, among them the socialist leader Ram Manohar Lohia according to the most common attribution, described her as a "dumb doll" put up by others. The description should be treated as a political jibe rather than an assessment, but it correctly captures the expectation of those who selected her: that real authority would remain in the organization.

Her first year gave those expectations some support. The monsoons of 1965 and 1966 had failed in large parts of the country, with severe scarcity in Bihar, and India relied on imported food grain, especially under United States Public Law 480 arrangements. In June 1966 her government devalued the rupee by roughly one-third, a step undertaken in the context of negotiations with the World Bank and the United States and widely resented as capitulation to external pressure. The government also faced linguistic and communal pressures, including the reorganization of Punjab into a Punjabi-speaking state and the new state of Haryana in November 1966, and a violent demonstration outside Parliament in the same month demanding a ban on cow slaughter. Chapter 94 examines the longer history of Punjab and regional politics; here it is enough to note that these were problems of governing a fragile coalition rather than of exercising personal authority.

The general election of February 1967 weakened her further and the party more. Congress retained a Lok Sabha majority, with 283 of 520 seats, far below the 361 it had won in 1962, and lost power in or lost its majority in a number of states, including Tamil Nadu, where the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won office, and Kerala, West Bengal, Orissa, and Punjab. Several senior party figures lost their own seats. The party's electoral base was fragmenting, and in several northern states opposition coalitions and defectors formed governments. Morarji Desai was admitted to the Cabinet as deputy prime minister and finance minister as a price of party unity.

Even in this weakened position, three developments pointed toward later centralization. First, the Prime Minister's Secretariat, headed after 1967 by the Principal Secretary P. N. Haksar, became an alternative source of policy advice to the party organization and to the Cabinet, drawing in economists and administrators who shared a commitment to a larger public sector and redistribution. Second, she began to cultivate younger Congress members, trade unionists, and former socialists and communists, such as Mohan Kumaramangalam and Chandra Shekhar, who favored a more radical program than the Syndicate. Third, she began to find that foreign-exchange constraints and the opposition of the organization limited her room for maneuver, and that the language of poverty and social ownership offered a way to define a political identity independent of it.

The point for the chapter's argument is that Indira Gandhi's authority was not an inheritance. It was created, between 1967 and 1971, through a series of confrontations in which she appealed over the heads of party leaders to legislators, to the electorate, and to a program. The next section examines the most important of those confrontations.

Section 93.2

The Congress Split of 1969: Party Organization, Presidential Politics, and Personal Leadership

After 1967 the Congress organization and the Prime Minister were formally allies but practically rivals. The Syndicate controlled the Congress Working Committee and, through it, the machinery of party nominations and the party's positions in many states. The Prime Minister controlled the government and, increasingly, the patronage and prestige that went with it. Disagreements between them concerned policy, particularly the pace of nationalization and the treatment of big business, and also the allocation of power: who could appoint chief ministers, who could decide the party's candidates, and who would speak for Congress in Parliament.

The decisive test came with the presidency. President Zakir Husain died on 3 May 1969, and the presidential election that followed became a vehicle for a broader struggle. At the party's session at Bangalore in July 1969 the Syndicate, over her objections, secured the nomination of N. Sanjiva Reddy, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, as the official Congress candidate. The Prime Minister had favored the Scheduled Caste minister Jagjivan Ram. Reddy's selection was also read as a signal that the organization intended to constrain her. In the same weeks she removed Morarji Desai from the finance portfolio, retaining the department herself, and Desai resigned from the government.

Vice-President V. V. Giri then resigned in order to stand as an independent candidate. The Prime Minister did not formally campaign against the official nominee, but she called on Congress members to vote according to their "conscience" rather than the party whip, a signal that was widely understood. The vote in August 1969 was close, and V. V. Giri won after the counting of second preferences. For the Syndicate it was a public humiliation, and for the Prime Minister it was a demonstration that, in an election conducted by members of legislatures, her influence could outweigh the formal authority of the party's governing organs.

The consequences were quick. Nijalingappa, then Congress president, accused the Prime Minister of indiscipline and, in November 1969, the organizational leaders expelled her from the party. A majority of Congress members of Parliament, however, remained with her. The party divided into two organizations. One, usually called Congress (R) for "Requisitionists," because its adherents had requisitioned a meeting of the party, followed Indira Gandhi. The other, Congress (O), the "Organization" Congress, was led by Nijalingappa, Kamaraj, Morarji Desai, and other senior figures. Congress (R) governed as a minority government, relying on outside support from the Communist Party of India, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, and other parties until the election of 1971.

The split changed the character of Indian politics in ways that mattered for 1975. It ended the model in which the party organization nominated and constrained the prime minister. In the new Congress (R) the legitimacy of every office flowed from the leader. Internal party elections became rarer, and senior positions increasingly depended on personal loyalty. Many of the experienced state and national leaders who might have disagreed with her in 1975 had gone into the other party or into political eclipse. The split also gave her a language for later conflicts: that those who opposed her stood for vested interests, privilege, and obstruction of the poor.

Historians disagree about how far this outcome was designed. Some portray the Prime Minister as a skilled political strategist who recognized from 1967 that her survival required the removal of the Syndicate. Others emphasize that she responded to successive opportunities and provocations, and that the Syndicate's own misjudgments, notably the nomination of Reddy, accelerated the process. The two accounts are not mutually exclusive. What is not disputed is that by the end of 1969 India had a governing party whose unity depended on one person, and that the Prime Minister's authority no longer depended on the approval of the party's senior organizational leaders.

Section 93.3

Bank Nationalization and the Expansion of State Economic Authority

On 19 July 1969, within days of Morarji Desai's departure from the finance ministry, the government nationalized fourteen of India's largest private commercial banks. The measure was carried out by an ordinance, issued under Article 123 of the Constitution, by the Vice-President acting as President, and addressed to the banks that held deposits above ₹50 crore. The Prime Minister announced the decision by radio the same evening. A note she had prepared for the party's Bangalore session, titled "Stray Thoughts on Bank Nationalisation," had laid out the justification: that private control of banking had skewed credit toward large industrial houses, neglected agriculture and small enterprise, and left the ownership of significant economic resources in a few hands.

The economic argument rested on several observations. A government policy of "social control" over banks, adopted in 1967, had not visibly changed their lending. Many large banks were closely linked to industrial families, and critics argued that deposits mobilized from the public were being channeled to affiliated companies. Rural India, where most Indians lived, had few bank branches, and agricultural credit remained in the hands of moneylenders at high rates of interest. Nationalization, the government argued, would permit credit to be directed toward priority sectors, notably agriculture, small industry, and exports, and expand the branch network into areas that private banks had judged unprofitable.

The political argument was at least as important as the economic one. The ordinance came in the middle of the conflict with the Syndicate and the presidential contest, and it was widely read as a move to seize the program of the left. It was welcomed by Congress members who favored a larger role for the state, by the Communist Party of India, and by many in the public, and it left Congress (O) opposed to a popular measure. The same measure was criticized by the Swatantra Party, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, much of the business community, and some economists, who argued that it concentrated credit allocation in a state bureaucracy open to political direction.

The Supreme Court intervened almost immediately. R. C. Cooper, a shareholder and director in one of the affected banks, challenged the ordinance and the act of Parliament that replaced it in August 1969. On 10 February 1970 an eleven-judge bench decided, by a majority of ten to one, that the legislation was invalid, principally because the principles it laid down for compensation to the banks' shareholders did not satisfy the constitutional requirement of compensation for compulsory acquisition and because it singled out fourteen banks without adequate justification. The decision did not deny the state's power to nationalize; it held that the particular legislation did not meet the Constitution's requirements. The government responded with a fresh ordinance and a new act that revised the compensation provisions, and then with constitutional amendments, the 25th in 1971 among them, limiting judicial review of compensation for acquired property.

Bank nationalization had durable effects, and they were mixed. In the following two decades the bank branch network expanded several-fold, including in rural areas, and priority-sector lending directed credit toward agriculture and small enterprises that had been underserved. Nationalized banks also became vehicles for political direction of credit, and in later decades they accumulated bad loans and were criticized for inefficiency. A second wave of nationalization followed in 1980. The measure was not reversed until the liberalizing reforms examined in Chapter 98, and even then the public-sector banks remained dominant.

Within the argument of this chapter, bank nationalization illustrates two patterns. It showed that a programmatic appeal could reorganize the political field: the Prime Minister turned a dispute about party authority into a contest between "the poor" and "vested interests." And it opened a conflict with the Supreme Court over the limits of parliamentary power that would run, through the property cases, the privy-purse case, Kesavananda Bharati, the Allahabad judgment, and finally the Emergency amendments, to 1977 and beyond.

Section 93.4

Garibi Hatao: Populism, Redistribution, and the General Election of 1971

Congress (R) governed as a minority from late 1969, and its leaders understood that a durable majority required an electoral mandate. Between 1969 and 1970 the government pursued several symbolic and substantive measures, among them the abolition of the privy purses and privileges granted to the former princely rulers. The purses had been guaranteed in the merger agreements of 1947–1949 (Chapter 87) and in the Constitution. In September 1970 a constitutional amendment to end them passed the Lok Sabha but failed in the Rajya Sabha by a narrow margin. The government then issued a presidential order withdrawing recognition from the rulers, and in December 1970 the Supreme Court struck the order down in the Madhav Rao Scindia case. On 27 December 1970 the Prime Minister recommended the dissolution of the Lok Sabha, more than a year before its term ended, and the general election was set for early March 1971.

The decision to hold the national election early had a structural consequence. Lok Sabha and state assembly elections, which until 1967 had broadly coincided, were now separated, and the national campaign could be fought on national themes under a single leader's name, without the complications of state-level contests. The Prime Minister toured the country extensively, and the campaign was organized around two phrases. Her opponents, a "Grand Alliance" of Congress (O), the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party, and the Samyukta Socialist Party, whose members could agree on little else, offered the slogan Indira Hatao, "remove Indira." Her answer was Garibi Hatao, "remove poverty." The contrast turned a contest of parties into a referendum on a person, and gave her a theme that her opponents' coalition of conservatives, communal parties, and socialists could not match.

The substantive promises were more ambitious than the slogan. The party's platform spoke of land ceilings, curbs on concentrations of economic power, measures against monopolies, and expansion of the public sector, together with the abolition of princely privileges. The Prime Minister's speeches addressed the rural poor, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, minorities, and the urban unemployed more directly than the Congress of the 1950s had. Her support included many who had not been part of the Congress coalition, and the party appealed to voters across the divisions of caste and region that had been fragmenting its electoral base since 1967.

The result was decisive. Congress (R) won 352 of the 518 contested seats with about 43.7 percent of the vote, against 283 seats and about 40.8 percent for the undivided Congress in 1967. The Grand Alliance did badly; Congress (O) won only sixteen seats. The Communist Party of India and the Communist Party of India (Marxist), the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Jana Sangh, and Swatantra each won a few dozen seats or fewer. Congress (R) now had more than two-thirds of the Lok Sabha, enough on its own to pass constitutional amendments subject to the Rajya Sabha and state ratification where required. After the war of December 1971 (Chapter 92), the 26th Amendment abolished the privy purses, completing one of the promises that had provoked the election.

Historians distinguish carefully between the success of the campaign and the success of the program. The campaign's effect on political identity was real: it identified the Prime Minister with the poor and gave Congress (R) a new coalition. The measurable effect on poverty was limited. Official estimates by the Planning Commission, which are themselves debated, put the share of the population below the poverty line at more than half in the early 1970s and showed little decline before the end of the decade. State land-ceiling laws were passed but implemented unevenly, and surplus land distributed was small relative to the number of landless households. The Fifth Five-Year Plan, launched in 1974, made poverty removal an explicit objective, but the economic crisis of 1973–1975 (§93.6) reduced its ambitions. The gap between promise and outcome is part of the background to the discontent examined later.

The 1971 election thus established a pattern that would shape the Emergency: a leader whose authority rested on a direct electoral relationship with the public, a party dependent on that leader, and a program whose results could not match its rhetoric. Voters had given an exceptional mandate, and they could withdraw it.

Historical Vocabulary
Privy Purse

A privy purse was a tax-free annual payment guaranteed by the government to the former rulers of princely states in return for their integration into India in 1947–1949, together with certain privileges such as titles and personal immunities. The arrangements were incorporated in the Constitution. By 1969 they were a small part of the national budget but a large symbol: critics regarded them as an inherited privilege inconsistent with equality, while defenders regarded them as a solemn commitment made when the rulers surrendered sovereignty.

The debate over privy purses mattered to this chapter because it combined constitutional law, political symbolism, and a conflict between Parliament and the Supreme Court. Its resolution, by the 26th Amendment in December 1971, also showed the government's capacity to change the Constitution once it had a large majority. Students should not equate privy purses with the broader history of the princely states, treated in Chapters 71 and 87.

Section 93.5 · Division II — Political Opposition and the Breakdown of Consensus, 1972–1975

The High Point of Indira Gandhi's Authority: Victory, Legitimacy, and Centralization

The war of December 1971 and the creation of Bangladesh, narrated in Chapter 92, raised the Prime Minister's standing to its highest point. Many Indians credited her with a decisive military and diplomatic success, and opposition leaders from several parties publicly praised her. In March 1972 Congress won large majorities in the state assembly elections in most states. The party that had split in 1969 now claimed to be the national Congress, and the old organization's leaders were largely eclipsed. The Prime Minister was, by any measure, the most powerful figure in the country. The question for this chapter is what she did with that power, and how its use shaped the crisis that followed.

The first answer is centralization. With her own authority the basis of Congress (R), she increasingly selected state chief ministers from Delhi rather than allowing legislators or state party leaders to choose them, and she removed those who developed independent bases. Chief ministers became more dependent on the national leader and less on their own supporters, a pattern that reduced the party's responsiveness to local conditions and made state governments vulnerable to dismissal when they displeased the center. Organizational elections within the party were postponed or replaced by nominations. By the mid-1970s the Congress president, D. K. Barooah, was reported to have said that "India is Indira and Indira is India," a formulation that, whether or not literally accurate, captured how personalized the party had become. Within the government, the Prime Minister's Secretariat and a small group of advisers took many decisions that earlier prime ministers would have taken through Cabinet or party committees.

The second answer concerns the judiciary. The decision in Kesavananda Bharati on 24 April 1973, by a narrow majority of seven to six, held that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution but could not destroy its "basic structure." Two days later the government announced that A. N. Ray, a judge who had been in the minority, would become Chief Justice, superseding three more senior judges, J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde, and A. N. Grover, all of whom had been in the majority. Ministers, notably Mohan Kumaramangalam, had spoken of the need for a judiciary "committed" to the social philosophy of the Constitution. Critics saw the supersession as an attempt to penalize judges who had ruled against the government and to discourage others. The practice of choosing Chief Justices by seniority was thereby weakened, and a second supersession followed in January 1977 (§93.12).

The third answer concerns legal powers. Two sets of provisions would become central. One was the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), passed in 1971, which allowed preventive detention for reasons related to the security of the state and public order. The other was the declaration of Emergency made on 3 December 1971, during the war with Pakistan, on the ground of external aggression. That proclamation was never revoked when the war ended. It remained in force through 1975, which meant that a state of Emergency already existed in a formal sense when the second proclamation, on grounds of internal disturbance, was issued. The significance of this fact lies in what it made possible: a government that wished to use emergency powers would find them already in its hands. These instruments are examined in §§93.9 and 93.12.

Finally, the Prime Minister's position encouraged a reading of political opposition as obstruction rather than as legitimate disagreement. The language of the 1971 campaign, which had presented her opponents as defenders of privilege, was easily extended to those who criticized her government or her family's associates. The opposition parties were weak and divided in 1972, and many observers, including some foreign ones, regarded Indian politics as having entered a phase of one-party dominance under a new leader. That judgment was premature, but it shaped how the government responded when opposition revived. The tendency to treat challenge as conspiracy, which would be prominent in the Prime Minister's own account of June 1975, had roots in the confidence of these years.

Section 93.6

Economic Distress, Inflation, Corruption Allegations, and Public Dissatisfaction

The government's popularity did not survive the economic conditions of 1972–1975. The costs of the refugee crisis and the war of 1971 had strained public finances, and a serious drought in 1972–1973 reduced food-grain output and pushed up prices. In 1973 the government took over the wholesale trade in wheat, hoping to ensure procurement and stable prices, but the measure disrupted markets and was abandoned in 1974. The country's reliance on imported fertilizer, petroleum, and food grain left it exposed to external shocks, and the largest of these arrived in October 1973, when the Arab oil-exporting states imposed an embargo and the price of crude oil roughly quadrupled over the following months.

The effects on an economy as dependent on imported oil as India's were severe. Standard estimates of wholesale prices show inflation running at about 20 percent or more a year in 1973–1974 and again in 1974–1975, with food, fuel, and fertilizer prices rising fastest. Industrial production stagnated, overall growth was slow, and urban consumers and wage earners, whose real incomes fell, were particularly affected. The government responded with measures such as compulsory deposits from wage earners and restrictions on wage and dividend increases, which were unpopular with the very groups that had been important to the 1971 coalition: salaried employees, industrial workers, and the urban middle class. Unemployment among educated young people was a further source of grievance, and the contrast between the rhetoric of 1971 and the conditions of 1974 sharpened it.

Allegations of corruption compounded economic hardship. Several cases kept the issue before the public. The Nagarwala case of 1971, in which a large sum of money was withdrawn from a Delhi bank branch on the strength of a telephone call purporting to be from the Prime Minister's office, ended with the death in custody of the central figure and unresolved questions. Maruti Limited, a company established in 1971 under the control of the Prime Minister's son Sanjay Gandhi to manufacture a small car, attracted allegations about the way it received licenses and land, although it produced no cars at this time. Licence-permit cases involving Congress politicians, including the case of Tulmohan Ram, a Bihar Member of Parliament accused of taking money for import licenses, were publicized by the opposition and the press. How far each allegation was true is not equally well established, and the strongest evidence from the Emergency period itself comes from the Shah Commission, which examined Maruti and other matters (§93.19). What is clear is that, in the public mind, the government had acquired a reputation for corruption, and that the opposition made this a central theme.

Political violence added to the sense of crisis. The assassination of Railway Minister L. N. Mishra, a close political associate of the Prime Minister from Bihar, in a bomb attack at Samastipur in January 1975, produced allegations and counter-allegations, and the investigation became a matter of controversy. Revolutionary left-wing movements in parts of Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, and Bihar, repressed through police action in the early 1970s, had left a legacy of detentions and killings that shaped the attitude of many intellectuals toward the state.

Dissatisfaction with prices, scarcity, and corruption did not by itself produce a challenge to the government's legitimacy. It created the conditions in which one could develop. Economic discontent was widespread but diffuse. What gave it political direction was the ability of student groups, trade unions, and opposition parties to link it to demands for change in government. That connection was made in Gujarat and Bihar in 1974, and it is the subject of the next section. The distinction matters for interpreting the Emergency: the government could justifiably point to genuine economic difficulties and to political agitation, but the question of whether these amounted to a threat to the security of India, the constitutional test for the Emergency, is a different one.

Section 93.7

The Gujarat and Bihar Movements: Jayaprakash Narayan, Students, Workers, and Political Mobilization

The Nav Nirman ("reconstruction") movement in Gujarat began in December 1973 with protests by engineering students in Ahmedabad against an increase in hostel mess charges, in a state where food and cooking-oil prices had risen sharply and where the Congress government of Chimanbhai Patel was widely accused of corruption. The protests spread rapidly to other colleges and cities, drew in teachers, professionals, and middle-class families, and developed into a demand for the resignation of the state government. Police firing and the burning of public property contributed to violence, and the army was called in at points. On 9 February 1974 Chimanbhai Patel resigned, and in March the state assembly was dissolved. Morarji Desai, from Gujarat, took up the demand for fresh elections and undertook a fast in April 1974. Elections were held in June 1975, and the opposition front formed by Congress (O), the Jana Sangh, and other parties won enough seats to form a government, with the result announced on 12 June 1975, the same day as the Allahabad judgment.

The Gujarat movement was significant for a reason beyond its local effects. It showed that a Congress state government could be removed by mass protest outside the legislative process, and that a demand for dissolution of an assembly, made through demonstrations, could succeed. For the opposition it offered a model; for the central government it supplied evidence that agitation could undermine authority.

In Bihar a similar movement began in March 1974, when students demonstrated at the state assembly against corruption, high prices, and unemployment. The movement lacked a national leader until students asked Jayaprakash Narayan, known as JP, to lead it. JP, a socialist leader of the independence movement who had withdrawn from party politics in the 1950s to join Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan (land-gift) movement, accepted on condition that the movement remain non-violent. In April 1974 he took charge, and on 5 June 1974, at a rally in Patna, he called for "Total Revolution" (sampoorna kranti), a moral, social, economic, and political transformation that went beyond the replacement of one government by another. The movement called for the dissolution of the Bihar assembly, organized strikes and non-cooperation, and developed parallel structures of "people's committees." In November 1974 police charged a procession led by JP in Patna, and the sight of an elderly leader being struck became a symbol for the movement.

The movement's composition and ideology were complex. Many participants were students and young people with no party affiliation. Others came from the Socialist Party, the Jana Sangh with its affiliated Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) cadre, Congress (O), and other organizations. The presence of Hindu nationalist organizations, whose organizational skill was important to the movement's reach, was criticized by the Communist Party of India, which opposed the movement as a reactionary challenge to a progressive government, and was cited by the government as evidence of a conspiracy. JP himself insisted that the movement was non-partisan. Historians therefore describe it as a coalition of grievances and groups whose common ground was opposition to the existing government, not a unified ideological movement.

The third mobilization was industrial. In May 1974 the All-India Railwaymen's Federation, led by the socialist trade unionist George Fernandes, called a strike over wages, bonuses, and hours that lasted about three weeks from 8 May. It involved hundreds of thousands of railway workers, by some estimates well over a million, and brought parts of the rail network to a halt. The government responded with mass arrests of union leaders and workers, evictions from railway quarters, and the use of the army and paramilitary forces to run essential services, and the strike ended without achieving its demands. Its suppression, in the very month of the first Indian nuclear test at Pokhran (18 May 1974), left a legacy of bitterness among organized labor.

The relationship between mass protest and parliamentary opposition was never settled. JP's movement did not seek power for any single party, yet it created the conditions in which opposition parties found a common platform, and its supporters invited them to unite. In 1975 the opposition coalitions formed in Gujarat showed the electoral potential of such unity. The government, for its part, saw in the movements a challenge to an elected government by extra-parliamentary means. Both interpretations were present in India in 1974–1975, and the Emergency would be justified, by its authors, on the second.

Section 93.8

The Allahabad Judgment and the Constitutional Crisis of June 1975

While the street movements unfolded, a legal case that began in 1971 approached its conclusion. In the general election of March 1971 Indira Gandhi had been returned from Rae Bareli in Uttar Pradesh, defeating the socialist candidate Raj Narain by a large margin. Raj Narain filed an election petition in the Allahabad High Court alleging that she had committed corrupt practices, in violation of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. The case, argued for the petitioner by the lawyer Shanti Bhushan, proceeded for years, and in March 1975 the Prime Minister appeared in court as a witness, the first occasion on which an Indian prime minister had done so.

On 12 June 1975 Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha delivered his judgment. He rejected several of the allegations, including those that involved bribery and distribution of goods to voters. He found, however, that she had committed two corrupt practices under section 123(7) of the Act, the obtaining of assistance for the furtherance of her election prospects from a government servant. The first concerned Yashpal Kapoor, an official in the Prime Minister's Secretariat who had worked on her campaign and whose resignation from government service, the court held, had not taken effect when he began that work. The second concerned the use of state government employees to construct rostrums and arrange electricity and loudspeakers for her meetings. The court declared the election void and, under the Act, disqualified her from contesting elections for six years. Justice Sinha stayed the operation of his order for twenty days to permit an appeal.

Three distinctions are essential. First, the judgment concerned electoral law, not criminal law. The Prime Minister was not convicted of a crime or sentenced to imprisonment; she was found to have violated rules governing the conduct of elections, rules that, as the court noted, were technical in some respects. Supporters pointed out that the offenses were minor relative to the sanction of removal from office, which is why some commentators at the time and since have described the judgment as one on a technicality. Critics replied that the law applied to every candidate and that the misuse of official resources in an election was precisely what the law was meant to prevent. Second, the judgment did not by itself remove her as Prime Minister. A prime minister must be a member of one of the Houses of Parliament, and the voiding of her election raised a question about her continuation that depended on the outcome of an appeal. Third, the stay that followed was a conditional stay, not a reversal.

On 24 June 1975, Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer, sitting as vacation judge of the Supreme Court, heard the appeal and granted a stay of the High Court's order. The stay was conditional. It allowed her to continue as a Member of Parliament and as Prime Minister, but she could not vote in Parliament or draw a salary as a member until the appeal was decided. It left the judgment standing and her position legally uncertain. Opposition leaders argued that the Prime Minister had lost the moral and legal authority to continue and demanded her resignation. Many Congress leaders and organizations staged demonstrations of support, and the party's leadership insisted that she should stay. Her advisers, including Siddhartha Shankar Ray, the Chief Minister of West Bengal and a lawyer, were in contact with her on the constitutional options.

The legal crisis coincided with a political one. On 12 June, the Gujarat election results showed the opposition front ahead, a blow to the Congress government at the center. Opposition parties, led by JP, announced a campaign of nationwide non-violent civil disobedience, beginning on 29 June, to demand the Prime Minister's resignation. On 25 June, at a large rally in Delhi, JP called on the police and the armed forces not to obey illegal orders. The government treated the statement as an incitement to mutiny; JP's defenders stated that he had referred to unlawful orders only. The interpretation of this speech, and of the threat posed by the proposed agitation, would become the central point in the debate about whether the situation justified the Emergency (§93.9).

By the night of 25 June, therefore, the Prime Minister faced a High Court judgment that, unless overturned, would remove her from office; an appeal that gave her a conditional reprieve; a massive and growing opposition movement demanding her resignation; and an electoral defeat in Gujarat. What she did next, and the legal and political choices available to her, are the subject of Division III.

Section 93.9 · Division III — The Emergency and the Suspension of Democratic Freedoms, 1975–1977

The Declaration of Emergency: Article 352 and the Events of June 25–26, 1975

On 25 June 1975 the Prime Minister, according to the accounts that historians have been able to assemble from memoirs, testimony to the Shah Commission, and later interviews, discussed with a small group of advisers how to respond to the situation described in §93.8. Siddhartha Shankar Ray, the Chief Minister of West Bengal and a barrister, advised her that Article 352 could be used to proclaim an Emergency on the ground of internal disturbance, and he is generally credited with drafting the legal basis. Others in the group included her additional private secretary R. K. Dhawan and, among ministers and officials, those closest to her. The Home Minister, K. Brahmananda Reddy, and the full Cabinet were not, according to most accounts, consulted in advance.

The legal steps were brief. Late that evening the Prime Minister went to President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, who signed the proclamation, as the Constitution required that he act on the advice of the Council of Ministers. During the night police in Delhi and other cities arrested opposition leaders, among them Jayaprakash Narayan, Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L. K. Advani, and, notably, some Congress members, such as Chandra Shekhar, who had criticized the government. Reports indicate that electricity to the Delhi newspaper offices on Bahadur Shah Zafar Marg was cut off that night, which delayed the next day's editions. At about six in the morning on 26 June the Cabinet met, was informed, and approved the proclamation, and the Prime Minister then broadcast to the nation on All India Radio. In her broadcast she presented the measure as a response to a conspiracy to disrupt the government and the economy, and to incitement of the armed forces and police, and assured listeners that ordinary life would continue.

Article 352 as it stood in 1975 allowed the President, if satisfied that a grave emergency existed "whereby the security of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened, whether by war or external aggression or internal disturbance," to proclaim an Emergency. The proclamation had to be laid before Parliament and would lapse after two months unless approved by resolutions of both Houses; Parliament approved it in late July 1975. The consequences were far-reaching. Under Article 358, the freedoms in Article 19 were automatically suspended during an Emergency declared on grounds of war or external aggression, and under Article 359 the President could suspend the right to move courts to enforce specified fundamental rights. The Union government acquired the power to give directions to the states on any matter, and Parliament could legislate on subjects reserved to the states.

The relationship between the 1975 proclamation and the earlier ones is often misunderstood. India had experienced two previous Emergencies: one proclaimed on 26 October 1962 during the war with China on the ground of external aggression, and lifted in January 1968; and one proclaimed on 3 December 1971 during the war with Pakistan, also on grounds of external aggression. The 1971 proclamation had never been revoked. The proclamation of 25 June 1975 was therefore a second, additional proclamation, on a new ground, of internal disturbance, and for twenty-one months two proclamations were in force at once. The use of the existing 1971 proclamation for domestic political purposes, such as the suspension of Article 19 rights, was one reason why the legal position of the Emergency was complex. It also meant that the machinery of emergency power had been available for years before it was used in this way.

Historical Vocabulary
"Internal Disturbance"

The phrase internal disturbance appeared in Article 352 as one of three grounds on which the President could proclaim a national Emergency, the others being war and external aggression. It was not defined in the Constitution. During the debates of the Constituent Assembly, some members had objected to such a broad term, and Dr. B. R. Ambedkar defended the emergency provisions as necessary and as subject to parliamentary control. Between 1950 and 1975 the phrase had not been used as a ground for proclamation.

The vagueness of the phrase meant that it could be applied to a wide range of situations, from armed insurrection to widespread agitation. In 1978 the 44th Amendment replaced it with "armed rebellion," a narrower expression. The change shows how the framers of the amendment understood the problem: not that Emergency powers should not exist, but that the executive's discretion to declare an Emergency on political grounds should be sharply limited.

Cause and Effect

The Proclamation of the Emergency

Long-term causes

Personalization of the Congress party after 1969; conflict between executive and judiciary over property, privy purses, and basic structure; retention of the 1971 Emergency and preventive-detention legislation; economic distress after 1972; erosion of the 1971 mandate.

Short-term causes

The Gujarat and Bihar movements; the railway strike; the Allahabad judgment of 12 June 1975; the conditional stay of 24 June; the opposition's announced campaign of civil disobedience; JP's rally of 25 June.

Event

Proclamation of an Emergency on the ground of internal disturbance on 25–26 June 1975, advised by the Prime Minister and a small circle and signed by the President.

Immediate effects

Arrest of opposition leaders; pre-censorship of the press; suspension of judicial remedies for detainees; bans on organizations; extension of Parliament's powers.

Long-term consequences

The 1977 defeat of Congress; constitutional amendments of 1977–1979; the growth of judicial doctrines on basic structure and personal liberty; enduring political memory of the Emergency.

Historical complexity

The Emergency was neither inevitable nor the product of one decision alone. It required structural conditions, a personal crisis, and the willingness of others to implement it.

Why no single cause is enough: An account that attributes the Emergency solely to the Allahabad judgment ignores why the legal instruments existed and why the institutions did not resist. An account that attributes it solely to institutional weakness ignores the timing and the role of individual decisions.

Historical Debate

Did the Emergency Arise from a Leadership Crisis or from Deeper Institutional Weakness?

The question: The documented repression of the Emergency is not in dispute. What is debated is why it happened: whether it was chiefly the response of a particular leader to an immediate threat to her position, or whether it revealed deeper weaknesses in the institutions of Indian democracy.

Interpretation A — A crisis of leadership and personal survival: The timing of the proclamation, thirteen days after the Allahabad judgment and one day after the conditional stay, points to the Prime Minister's personal predicament as the trigger. The decision was taken by a very small circle, without prior Cabinet discussion. The Shah Commission concluded that the situation did not justify the extraordinary measures, and the Emergency's early measures, such as the arrest of leaders and censorship, were aimed at the opposition and at her critics, including within Congress. On this view the Emergency was a choice, and another leader might have chosen differently.
Interpretation B — Institutional weakness and structural permissiveness: The Constitution contained the instruments of suspension and detention, and the 1971 proclamation was already in force. The Congress party had become dependent on one person, the opposition lacked a stable parliamentary alternative, the Supreme Court's authority had been challenged by supersession, and the federal system allowed the center to remove state governments. Without these conditions, the decision of June 1975 could not have been implemented as it was. On this view the Emergency expressed longer-term features of the political system.

Evidence: Interpretation A relies on the chronology, memoirs, and the Shah Commission's record of decision-making. Interpretation B relies on constitutional texts, the history of centralization since 1969, and comparison with the later behavior of institutions. A third position, offered by the government at the time and by some defenders, holds that a real threat to public order and stability existed. Historians have generally treated this as a claim to be tested against the evidence of the movements' non-violent character and the capacity of ordinary law.

Why it matters: If the Emergency was mainly a leadership crisis, the lesson concerns the character of individual leaders and the strength of checks on personal ambition. If it expressed institutional weakness, the lesson concerns constitutional design and political structure. The two views are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine them: structural conditions made it possible, and individual decisions made it happen.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence that support each interpretation, and explain what further evidence would help decide how much weight each should receive. Do not treat the documented facts of repression as part of the debate.

Constitutional chronology: 1975 versus later safeguards

The written Cabinet-advice requirement in Article 352 was introduced by the Forty-fourth Amendment after the Emergency. It must not be read back into the rules operating on 25 June 1975. The lack of prior Cabinet consultation is a historical issue documented by the post-Emergency inquiry; it is not proof that the later written-advice clause already applied.

Section 93.10

Emergency Government: Executive Centralization, Parliament, and Constitutional Amendment

The Emergency did not abolish the institutions of government; it changed how they worked. Parliament continued to meet and pass legislation, but many leading opposition members were in detention or in hiding, those who were present could not freely communicate their speeches to the public because of censorship, and legal protection for the reporting of parliamentary proceedings was withdrawn. The Cabinet continued to exist but decisions were concentrated in the Prime Minister's house and in a small circle. The Lok Sabha, elected in 1971 for five years, had its term extended, first in 1976 and again later that year, by Parliament's own votes, a step possible during an Emergency and one that meant that members were sitting beyond the term for which voters had chosen them.

The federal balance shifted too. The Union could now direct state governments, and a number of states were governed by chief ministers whose authority depended entirely on Delhi. Where Congress did not govern, the center acted. In January 1976 the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government of Tamil Nadu under M. Karunanidhi was dismissed and President's Rule imposed, with many DMK activists arrested; in March 1976 the Janata Front government of Gujarat, which had come to power in the election of June 1975, fell after losing its majority and the state came under President's Rule. The tools of Article 356, which allows the dismissal of a state government where constitutional machinery has failed, were thus combined with Emergency powers.

The most consequential changes were constitutional. The Emergency government had a large majority in Parliament and few members to oppose it, and between August 1975 and December 1976 it amended the Constitution several times. The 38th Amendment, passed in August 1975, placed the President's satisfaction in proclaiming an Emergency, and ordinances issued during one, beyond judicial scrutiny. The 39th Amendment, also of August 1975, removed from the courts all questions about the elections of the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, and Speaker, which would be decided by a body determined by Parliament, and inserted Article 329A, which declared that the election of the Prime Minister of 1971 was valid regardless of the Allahabad decision. Parliament also amended the election law retrospectively to remove the grounds on which Justice Sinha had decided against her.

The Supreme Court examined these measures in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain, decided on 7 November 1975 by a bench of five judges. The Court struck down the clause of Article 329A that would have validated the Prime Minister's election without judicial examination, on the ground that it violated principles that formed part of the basic structure of the Constitution, including free and fair elections, the rule of law, and the separation of powers. But it also held that her election was valid under the election law as retrospectively amended, and so set aside the Allahabad order. The result was paradoxical: the Court affirmed limits on Parliament's amending power and, in the same judgment, ensured that the Prime Minister kept her seat. The judges were divided in their reasoning, which reflected both legal differences and the circumstances in which the Court was working.

The 42nd Amendment, passed by Parliament in November 1976 and enacted by the end of the year, was the most extensive change since 1950, and is often called a "mini-Constitution." It added the words "socialist" and "secular" and "integrity" to the Preamble, inserted a list of Fundamental Duties of citizens, gave the directive principles of state policy priority over certain fundamental rights, restricted the powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts to review central and state laws, extended the term of the Lok Sabha and state assemblies from five to six years, allowed the deployment of central armed forces in states, and sought to place amendments of the Constitution beyond judicial review. Some of its provisions, such as the Fundamental Duties and the Preamble's added words, were enduring. Others were designed to ensure that no court could again question measures of the kind that the Emergency government favored.

Section 93.11

Press Censorship, Information Control, and the Suppression of Political Expression

The Emergency government moved at once against the press. Censorship was imposed through orders under the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules and through the issuing of guidelines by a Chief Censor, under which newspapers had to submit material on political matters for clearance before publication. The restrictions covered reports of arrests, of opposition statements and activities, of strikes and protests, and anything that might be construed as criticism of the government or of the Emergency itself. Newspapers were also forbidden to report the censorship. Further laws followed: the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act of 1976, and the removal of legal protection for faithful reports of parliamentary proceedings.

Control of the flow of news extended beyond the ordinary press. In January–February 1976 the four main Indian news agencies, the Press Trust of India, the United News of India, Hindustan Samachar, and Samachar Bharati, were merged into a single agency, Samachar, a step that gave the government control over the supply of news to papers across the country. All India Radio and Doordarshan, already state-owned, were used to present the government's view. Publications, advertisements, and newsprint allocations were also instruments: the government could reward or penalize papers through its advertising and the allocation of scarce resources, and officials made clear that disfavored editors risked these pressures. Several foreign correspondents were expelled or had their accreditation withdrawn, including the BBC's Mark Tully in 1976, and foreign papers' reports were closely monitored.

Responses within the press varied. A few publications resisted openly. On the morning after the proclamation some newspapers, including the Delhi edition of the Indian Express, published blank or nearly blank editorial columns as a protest, and its proprietor Ramnath Goenka became a symbol of resistance and a target of government action over the following months. Journalists were among those detained, including Kuldip Nayar, who was arrested in July 1975, and reported numbers of detained journalists ran into the hundreds. Some periodicals closed rather than publish under censorship, or were forced to cease publication. Others found ways to reduce the effect of censorship through indirect references or by leaving gaps, until the censors forbade that practice.

Most newspapers, however, complied. Many proprietors and editors judged that opposition would bring closure, loss of advertising, or prosecution, and some supported the Emergency. The politician L. K. Advani, himself detained, said after the Emergency that the press had been asked only to bend but had crawled. His remark, which has often been quoted, expresses a bitterness that is widely shared; it should also be read as a political judgment by a leader who had been in prison, and historians stress that the choices facing editors were difficult and unequal. A distinction is useful. Institutional accommodation was widespread, but individual acts of resistance, in the form of defiance, of leaks, and of the circulation of underground news-sheets, were also common. Cultural expression was subject to control, too: films such as Kissa Kursi Ka, a satire on politics, had their prints seized and destroyed, and the film Aandhi was banned for a time, while musicians and other performers who declined to cooperate with the government's publicity found their work kept off state radio.

The effect of censorship was not only to silence opponents. It also meant that policymakers, and many citizens, could not learn what the Emergency's policies were doing. Reports of sterilization abuses, police firing, and demolitions did not reach the wider public in the usual way, and the government itself operated without the information that a free press would have supplied. Historians sometimes suggest that this contributed to the decision to hold elections in 1977, since the government may have believed, on the basis of the reports it was shown, that its policies were more popular than they were. The claim should be treated as a hypothesis, but it highlights a general point: censorship damages the information available to the censor as well as to the public.

Section 93.12

Political Detention, Civil Liberties, and the Judiciary during the Emergency

The arrests of 25–26 June 1975 were the first of many. Detentions were made under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), under the Defence and Internal Security of India Rules, and, for those accused of smuggling and economic offenses, under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act. The total number detained is not known precisely. Contemporary figures and later estimates generally put it at more than one hundred thousand over the course of the Emergency, and a recent scholarly synthesis estimates nearly 110,000 people imprisoned over the period, but totals vary with the detention law, definition of a political prisoner, and reporting period; this cumulative estimate is not the number held under MISA at a single date. The detainees included leaders of the main opposition parties and many Congress dissenters; students; trade unionists; journalists; and many local workers and activists with no national profile. Organizations such as the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the Jamaat-e-Islami, and the Communist Party of India (Marxist-Leninist) were banned.

Conditions of detention varied. Some leaders were held in relatively comfortable circumstances, while others experienced overcrowded prisons, ill-treatment, and, in some cases, torture. Deaths in custody occurred. Among the best-known cases was that of P. Rajan, a student at the Regional Engineering College in Kozhikode, Kerala, who was arrested in March 1976 and died in police custody; his father, Eachara Warrier, filed a petition for habeas corpus, and after the Emergency the Kerala High Court's findings contributed to the resignation of the state's Chief Minister, K. Karunakaran, in 1977. The film actress and activist Snehalata Reddy died in January 1977, shortly after being released on parole from prison in Bangalore, where she had been held in connection with the Baroda dynamite case. These cases illustrate the physical risk to detainees and also the impact of the later inquiries.

The central legal question was whether the courts could do anything. The legal mechanism for challenging unlawful detention was the writ of habeas corpus, a petition to a High Court under Article 226 or to the Supreme Court under Article 32. On 27 June 1975 the President issued an order under Article 359 suspending the right to move any court for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Articles 14 (equality before the law), 21 (protection of life and personal liberty), and 22 (protection against arbitrary arrest and detention), and, in January 1976, an order extended the suspension to Article 19. A constitutional right and the means of enforcing it are different things. The Emergency orders did not, on their face, abolish the rights; they suspended the right to ask a court to enforce them.

Several High Courts nevertheless heard petitions from detainees. At least nine High Courts, including those of Allahabad, Bombay, Delhi, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Punjab and Haryana, and Rajasthan, held in various forms that detention could be challenged where the detention order did not comply with the law, or that a detainee still had some remedy despite the Presidential order. The government appealed to the Supreme Court, and in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla, decided on 28 April 1976, a bench of five judges ruled by four to one in the government's favor. The majority, A. N. Ray, M. H. Beg, Y. V. Chandrachud, and P. N. Bhagwati, held that while the Presidential order was in force no person had a right to move a court to challenge a detention on the ground that it violated the law, including MISA itself, and that Article 21 was not a source of rights independent of the Constitution. During the argument the government's counsel was reported to have accepted that, on his reading, even an unlawful killing by an official could not be questioned in court while the order was in force, an answer that became notorious.

Justice H. R. Khanna dissented. He held that the right to life and liberty did not originate in Article 21 alone, that the state had no authority to deprive a person of life or liberty without the sanction of law, and that the suspension of the right to move courts for enforcement did not remove the rule of law. The dissent was widely understood at the time and afterward as a statement of principle by a judge who knew its likely cost. In January 1977 the government appointed Justice M. H. Beg Chief Justice, superseding Khanna, who was the senior-most judge, and Khanna resigned. The majority judgment was widely criticized. Some of those who had joined it, such as Justice Bhagwati, later expressed regret. In 2017 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court, in the privacy case Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, stated that the majority opinions in ADM Jabalpur were seriously flawed, and the decision is now regarded as overruled.

The judiciary's record during the Emergency was thus mixed. High Courts that heard detainees' petitions did so against pressure, and some judges were transferred between High Courts in 1976 in ways that were widely understood as punitive. The Supreme Court accepted a reading of the Constitution that made it unable to protect liberty during the Emergency. Both facts belong to the history. The first suggests that judicial independence did not wholly disappear; the second suggests that constitutional guarantees, when the means of enforcing them can be suspended, depend on whether the ultimate court is prepared to enforce them anyway.

Historical Vocabulary
Preventive Detention

Preventive detention is the holding of a person without trial, not as punishment for an offense already committed but to prevent a person from acting in a way that the state considers harmful, such as endangering security or public order. The Indian Constitution, in Article 22, permits preventive detention under law, while setting out safeguards that ordinarily include communication of the grounds and review by an advisory board. It is therefore an exception to the usual rule that detention requires a trial, and it was already controversial in the Constituent Assembly.

The Maintenance of Internal Security Act of 1971 was a preventive-detention statute. Its use during the Emergency was extended to political opponents, and the safeguards that normally applied were weakened by amendments and by the suspension of the right to petition courts. Students should distinguish preventive detention from punitive detention after conviction, and should distinguish the existence of a legal power from the way it is used.

Section 93.13 · Division IV — Coercive Modernization, Social Control, and Everyday Life

Sanjay Gandhi: Informal Political Authority and the Five-Point Programme

Sanjay Gandhi, the Prime Minister's younger son, was in his late twenties at the time of the Emergency. He held no ministerial or parliamentary position. His public identity came from Maruti Limited, the car company he had founded in 1971, and from his leadership of the Youth Congress, the party's youth wing, which he built into a large organization in 1975 and 1976. Yet his influence over government decisions was far greater than his formal position suggests, and the Shah Commission and many other accounts document that officials, ministers, and chief ministers acted on his wishes. The contrast between formal and informal authority is a key feature of Emergency politics.

His authority operated through networks. Ministers and officials who cultivated him included Bansi Lal, the Chief Minister of Haryana who became Defence Minister in December 1975, Vidya Charan Shukla, who became Minister of Information and Broadcasting in 1975, Om Mehta, the Minister of State for Home Affairs, and R. K. Dhawan, the Prime Minister's private secretary. In Delhi the Lieutenant Governor, Krishan Chand, and officials of the Delhi Development Authority, notably its Vice-Chairman Jagmohan, carried out programs that reflected his priorities. The Youth Congress provided cadres who could mobilize crowds, pressure officials, and enforce programs. The Prime Minister defended him publicly and allowed his role to expand, though historians differ about how much she controlled him or approved his actions.

On 1 July 1975, the Prime Minister announced a Twenty-Point Programme of economic and social measures. It included the abolition of bonded labor (given legal force through an ordinance in October 1975), a moratorium on rural debt, a review of minimum agricultural wages, the distribution of house sites to the landless, enforcement of land-ceiling laws, and action against tax evasion and smuggling. The programme gave the Emergency a language of social reform and linked it to the 1971 slogans. Its implementation varied widely, and some of its provisions, such as the abolition of bonded labor, were more effective on paper than in practice. Around the same time Sanjay Gandhi promoted his own Five-Point Programme, which was associated particularly with the Youth Congress. Accounts of its contents differ, but the five points are usually listed as family planning, the abolition of the dowry system, the planting of trees, adult education or literacy, and the eradication of casteism. In popular memory, the Five-Point Programme has become identified with its most coercive components: sterilization and slum clearance.

Historians have offered different interpretations of the programme. Some stress its modernizing and technocratic character: the Five Points addressed real social problems, from population growth to dowry, and they reflected a belief that social change required decisive, disciplined action rather than democratic deliberation. Others emphasize that Sanjay Gandhi's approach combined authoritarianism, personal ambition, and indifference to the effects on those who were its targets. The interpretations are not necessarily incompatible: a program with legitimate objectives can be carried out by illegitimate and harmful means. This is the central issue in §§93.14 and 93.15, where the objectives of population control and urban planning meet the realities of coercion.

The role of Sanjay Gandhi also illustrates a wider pattern. The Emergency's centralization did not produce a coherent bureaucratic dictatorship; it created a system in which authority flowed through personal connections, and in which officials had to guess the wishes of those close to the Prime Minister. This made some people more powerful than their formal posts warranted and made the system arbitrary. The Shah Commission concluded that Sanjay Gandhi had exercised authority without any official position and had given directions to officials on matters of government, including the use of state power in Delhi and elsewhere. The finding does not resolve the question of the Prime Minister's own responsibility, but it documents the informal channels through which the Emergency operated.

Section 93.14

The Sterilization Campaign: Population Policy, Targets, Coercion, and Human Rights

India had adopted a national family-planning programme in 1952, among the first governments in the world to do so, and by the 1960s it emphasized contraception, intrauterine devices, and voluntary sterilization, particularly vasectomy, which is simpler and cheaper than female sterilization. Mass vasectomy camps, with cash incentives, were held in several states from the late 1960s, notably in Kerala and Gujarat. Population growth was widely seen, by Indian planners and by foreign advisers and donors, as a barrier to economic development. The population had passed 500 million, and the census of 1971 showed continued growth. Family planning before the Emergency was thus an established if limited policy, with incentives rather than compulsion at its center.

During the Emergency the policy changed in kind and scale. In April 1976 the government announced a National Population Policy, which raised financial incentives for sterilization, increased the weight given to population in the allocation of central funds to states, and encouraged states to consider legislation that would introduce compulsion. In practice the main change was the use of administrative targets, set by the center and magnified by states and districts. Officials, from health workers and teachers to police and revenue officers, were assigned quotas of sterilization "cases" to be produced, and their pay, promotion, and standing could depend on meeting them. Those who produced fewer than required could have their salaries withheld or be transferred.

The pressure passed down the chain to the public. Incentives included cash payments, land allotments, loans, and priority in housing plots, and disincentives included denial of ration cards, medical treatment, licenses, or other services to those who did not have sterilization certificates, according to reports and the Shah Commission's record. In some places, accounts and testimony describe police and officials surrounding villages, detaining men, and taking them to camps. The targets fell most heavily on poor, rural, and marginalized groups, among them landless laborers, Scheduled Castes, and in some regions Muslims, who were least able to resist officials and who were often sought as the easiest "cases." Camps were often poorly equipped, and surgeries performed in haste and in unhygienic conditions led to infections and deaths. Women were less often the target of the sterilization drive than men, but the effects on families fell on women as well.

Resistance occurred, and sometimes ended violently. At Muzaffarnagar in Uttar Pradesh, in October 1976, police fired on a protest against sterilization and related excesses, and dozens were reported killed, though the dates and numbers given in accounts differ. In several states there were protests, flight from villages, and refusal to cooperate, as well as evasion through bribery and fraud: some officials met targets by sterilizing people who did not meet the intended criteria, including unmarried persons and the elderly.

The figures need careful handling. Official figures presented to the Lok Sabha in 2025, based on the Shah Commission's reports, record sterilization targets and achievements by year. For 1975–1976 the target was about 2.49 million, and about 2.62 million procedures were reported. For 1976–1977 the target was about 4.26 million, and about 8.13 million procedures were reported, so that the total across the two years exceeded ten million. Other scholarly compilations give somewhat different figures for 1976–1977, commonly about 8.3 million, and one account records that 1.7 million procedures were carried out in September 1976 alone. The same Shah Commission record notes 548 complaints of sterilization of unmarried persons and 1,774 deaths linked to sterilization, though some sources give slightly different totals. What the figures cannot tell us is the proportion of procedures that were coerced. They record achievement, not consent. The number of deaths and complaints is a minimum of documented harm, not an estimate of all harm, since many people had no means of making complaints. Historians therefore avoid two errors: treating every procedure as forced, which the evidence does not show, and treating the number of recorded complaints as a measure of the coercion that occurred, which it cannot be.

The campaign ended in early 1977 when the elections were announced, and its political consequences were considerable. In northern India, where the drive had been most intense, Congress suffered its heaviest defeats (§93.18). The effect on family-planning policy was long-lasting. After 1977 the program was renamed family welfare, and governments avoided target-driven sterilization for a time, though targets and incentives returned in different forms. International human-rights attention has since treated the campaign as an example of what can happen when population policy is pursued through coercive administrative pressure. The case remains central to debates on reproductive rights and the proper limits of state action in the name of development.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village and a Quota: Reconstructing Pressure from Below

Note: this feature is an analytical reconstruction drawn from patterns documented in official records and scholarship. It does not describe a named village or person.

Consider how a district sterilization target reached a village. A district health officer was assigned a quota, divided it among blocks, and passed it to the schoolteachers, patwaris (village revenue officials), and health workers of each village. Each had a figure to meet and a reason to meet it: salaries, promotion, or a transfer might depend on it. The most accessible candidates were people who depended on the officials: landless laborers, tenants, and members of marginalized communities who could not easily refuse a person with authority over a ration card, a land record, or a job.

Villagers' responses are known through memoirs and the later testimony of those involved. Some men agreed in return for a cash payment that was meaningful to a poor household. Others left the village when a camp was announced. Some paid officials to be recorded as sterilized or to be left alone, and others were taken by force. Families dealt with the consequences: loss of work after the operation, infections, and, in some cases, a loss of social standing. Women bore the burden of care and sometimes of the stigma attached to a household affected by the program.

The reconstruction shows that the campaign worked through the relationships of ordinary administration, not by the use of a special apparatus. This is why it was so effective in meeting targets and so difficult to resist. It also shows why historians distinguish carefully between the objectives of a policy and the experiences of those who carried it out and those who were subject to it.

Section 93.15

Urban Clearance and Forced Eviction: Delhi, Turkman Gate, and State Development Policy

The second major campaign associated with the Five-Point Programme was the clearance of unauthorized settlements and the "beautification" of cities, above all Delhi. Delhi had grown quickly since 1947, partly through refugee settlement and partly through rural migration. Hundreds of thousands lived in jhuggi-jhopri (slum) clusters and in unauthorized colonies, often on land owned by public agencies, and the city's planners had long regarded them as a problem. The Delhi Development Authority (DDA), whose Vice-Chairman from 1975 was Jagmohan, was the principal agency for demolition and resettlement.

During 1975 and 1976 bulldozers cleared large areas, including parts of the old city. Residents were typically given little notice, and those who qualified for resettlement were allotted small plots, often of about twenty-five square yards, in newly created "resettlement colonies" on the outskirts, such as Trilokpuri, Nand Nagri, and Jahangirpuri. The new colonies lacked basic facilities, and the distance from places of work made earning a livelihood difficult. Reports and testimony describe a connection between eligibility for plots and sterilization, with people being encouraged or pressed to undergo sterilization in order to qualify, so that the two campaigns reinforced one another. Estimates of the number of people displaced in Delhi during the Emergency range from several hundred thousand upward, with some accounts citing about 700,000; these totals are uncertain and should be treated as approximations.

The best-known episode took place at Turkman Gate in Old Delhi, a Mughal-era gateway next to a densely populated Muslim-majority neighborhood. The demolition of structures began there on 13 April 1976. The area was also the site of a family-planning camp, and local residents were angry both about the demolitions and about pressure to be sterilized. On 19 April a crowd gathered to protest, and the confrontation with police and demolition squads ended in police firing. The government's statement to the Lok Sabha on 27 April 1976 said that three people had died on the spot and three more later of their injuries, six in all. Evidence given to the Shah Commission by police witnesses suggested a higher number, with one officer acknowledging at least eight deaths and another suggesting at least twenty, and independent researchers and residents have put the number much higher, in some accounts in the hundreds, claims that cannot be verified. Because the press was censored, the event was not reported in the way it would normally have been.

Residents of the area were moved to resettlement colonies, where many lived in makeshift shelters for years. The Shah Commission, which looked into the demolitions, concluded that they had been carried out without due process and with a disregard for law and procedure by officials anxious to meet the wishes of Sanjay Gandhi. The Commission named him, Jagmohan, and a number of other senior officials in connection with the events. Jagmohan later denied wrongdoing and went on to a long public career, a fact that has contributed to continuing disagreement about responsibility.

The episode shows the tension between two ideas of the state. One is the planning state, committed to order, public health, and the removal of what it regards as obstacles to modern urban life. The other is the rights-bearing citizen, who has legal claims to housing, notice, and due process. During the Emergency, the suspension of judicial remedies and the censorship of the press removed the mechanisms by which those claims could be asserted. The distinction between the objectives of urban planning and the means used to achieve them is not a matter of opinion: the question of whether cities should be reorganized is a policy question, but the denial of notice, compensation, and legal recourse to those displaced is a question of rights. Later Indian courts, in cases about the rights of pavement and slum dwellers, developed a jurisprudence of livelihood and due process that can be understood in part as a response to experiences like these.

Section 93.16

Indian Society under Emergency Rule: Class, Gender, Region, and Resistance

The Emergency was not experienced uniformly. For some in the urban middle class and among salaried employees, the early months seemed to bring order: trains ran more punctually, government offices saw less absenteeism, and prices, after the high inflation of 1973–1975, eased as a good monsoon in 1975 restored agricultural output and the economy grew strongly in 1975–1976. Supporters of the Emergency, among them some businessmen, many civil servants, and the Communist Party of India, which then supported the Congress government, saw it as a way to restore discipline and carry out reform, and the Gandhian leader Vinoba Bhave described it as a period of discipline, an anushasan parva. These responses were real and are part of the history, and later accounts that treat all Indians as united in opposition are inaccurate.

For others, the effects were quickly negative. Industrial workers lost the right to strike, and bonuses were restricted. Peasants in the poorest groups were the main targets of sterilization quotas. Urban poor communities faced demolition. Muslims in Old Delhi and in parts of north India, and Scheduled Castes in many places, experienced the campaigns most severely. Women faced the consequences of sterilization and detention in different ways: women political activists were detained, among them the former rulers Gayatri Devi and Vijaya Raje Scindia, and many women in poor households bore the burden of caring for husbands and sons affected by the programs. Students and young activists were detained or pursued by police. Government employees were pressed to carry out the programs and to meet targets, and those who refused might be transferred, denied promotion, or disciplined; the Shah Commission's record shows both compliance and the fear that produced it.

Regional differences were significant. The campaign of sterilization and slum clearance was most intense in Delhi and in northern states such as Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, and Bihar, where the Youth Congress and the state governments were most closely aligned with the central leadership, though large numbers of procedures were reported from other states as well. In southern states, in general, enforcement was less coercive, and Congress fared much better in the elections of 1977. Political pressure also varied: Tamil Nadu saw the dismissal of its government and the detention of DMK activists, including Karunanidhi's son M. K. Stalin, and in Kerala the Rajan case showed the reach of police power. In Punjab the Akali Dal organized protests against the Emergency, and tens of thousands were reported to have courted arrest, a subject that Chapter 94 takes up in the context of Punjab politics.

Resistance took several forms. Political leaders went underground, and groups such as the Socialist Party's networks and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's volunteers distributed bulletins and organized satyagrahas, in which people courted arrest in public protest. George Fernandes, who evaded arrest until June 1976, became the central figure in the Baroda dynamite case, in which activists were accused of plotting to damage government property. Subramanian Swamy escaped to the United States, from where he campaigned against the Emergency. Citizens' groups, among them Citizens for Democracy, which JP helped establish, and Indian communities abroad lobbied foreign governments and press. Lawyers, judges, and High Courts hearing habeas petitions practiced a form of institutional resistance. And ordinary people resisted in small ways: spreading rumors and jokes, hiding relatives, refusing to attend camps, and sheltering fugitives. Much of this resistance is hard to document precisely, which is itself a consequence of censorship.

Taken together, these accounts support a more complex interpretation than either of the two stories often told about the Emergency, one of universal fear and one of popular support. Many people complied out of fear, habit, or self-interest. Some actively supported the measures. A smaller number resisted openly, often at serious personal cost. A large number experienced the Emergency less as a constitutional question than as a change in the conditions of their daily lives. These differences shaped how the election of 1977 would be decided.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Newsroom, the Office, and the Neighbourhood

Note: this is an analytical reconstruction of everyday conditions from published evidence, not an account of particular individuals.

For many people the main daily effects of the Emergency were small and cumulative. In a newspaper office, copy had to be taken to a censor before it could be printed, and anything critical would be cut, which meant that editors learned to anticipate the censor's objections. In a government office, officials learned that to be seen to cooperate was safer than to ask questions; targets and reports replaced discussion. In a neighbourhood, residents learned to be careful about what they said to strangers and what they wrote in letters, and rumor took the place of the news that the press could not carry.

Historians reconstruct these conditions from memoirs, oral testimony, and documents such as the Shah Commission's files. The evidence is uneven: it tends to favor those who later had the means to publish accounts, and the voices of the poorest are often heard only through officials' records. The aim is not to dramatize the period but to see how a legal framework of suspended rights became a set of ordinary habits of caution.

Section 93.17 · Division V — The Election of 1977 and the Recovery of Parliamentary Democracy

The Decision to Hold Elections: Political Calculation and Opposition Reorganization

On 18 January 1977 the Prime Minister announced on All India Radio that the Lok Sabha would be dissolved and a general election held in March. The Lok Sabha's term, already extended, would otherwise have run on, and the Emergency could in principle have continued indefinitely. Political prisoners began to be released within days, restrictions on public meetings were eased, and press censorship was relaxed, though the Emergency itself remained in force until after the election. The decision surprised most observers, and its explanation remains one of the most debated questions about the period.

Several explanations have been offered, and they are not mutually exclusive. One stresses misjudgment. Reports from the intelligence services and from Congress officials, filtered through a system in which few people wished to bring bad news, may have led the Prime Minister to believe that the party would win comfortably. A second stresses legitimacy: the Emergency had been justified as a temporary response to a crisis, and the Prime Minister, who had always described herself as a democrat and who cared about India's standing abroad, may have wanted to restore a formal mandate. A third points to the extension of the Lok Sabha's term, which could not be continued indefinitely without further constitutional steps and growing resentment. A fourth stresses personal and family considerations, including her discomfort with the direction of the regime and with the role of Sanjay Gandhi, though the evidence for the last is thin and contested. Historians generally accept that miscalculation played a large part, but the exact weight of each factor cannot be established from available sources.

The opposition, released from detention and mostly without organization or money, had to move quickly. On 20 January 1977 the leaders of four parties, Congress (O), the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the Bharatiya Lok Dal, and the Socialist Party, agreed to fight the election together, and on 23 January the Janata Party was formally constituted, with Morarji Desai as chairman and Charan Singh as vice-chairman. Jayaprakash Narayan, ill and recently released, was the movement's moral center, and he pressed the parties to unite under one flag and one symbol, which was that of the Bharatiya Lok Dal. The merger brought together groups of different ideology, from Hindu nationalists to socialists and Gandhians, who shared an opposition to the Emergency and little else.

The most important political event of the campaign came from within the ruling party. On 2 February 1977 Jagjivan Ram, the senior Scheduled Caste minister, resigned from the Cabinet and the Congress along with H. N. Bahuguna and Nandini Satpathy and formed the Congress for Democracy, which allied with the Janata Party. The defection signaled that leaders who had remained within the system were willing to leave it, and it was significant for the voters of the north, among whom Jagjivan Ram had a following. Other alignments were mixed: the Communist Party of India allied with Congress, as did the AIADMK in Tamil Nadu, while the Communist Party of India (Marxist), which had opposed the Emergency, contested separately in many places and adjusted seats with the opposition in some.

The campaign was short, lasting about two months, and was held in unusual conditions. The opposition had to contend with the lingering effects of censorship, the state's control of radio and television, the shortage of money, and the difficulty of communicating after years of repression. Yet the issue was clear and could be understood without much explanation: the election, in the opposition's presentation, was a choice between democracy and dictatorship. JP and other leaders drew large crowds, and the sterilization campaigns and other excesses, which the censorship had previously made hard to report, became subjects of open discussion in the north. The Prime Minister defended the Emergency's achievements and stressed order, discipline, and economic progress. Her opponents' difficulty was to convince voters that they were united enough to govern; hers was to convince them that the government that had ruled for twenty months deserved renewal.

The decision to hold an election did not itself guarantee a fair one. Observers noted the ability of the Election Commission, which continued to function and to administer the polls, to organize the voting in a country of some 320 million voters, and the electoral machinery was a crucial institutional asset. The legitimacy of the election as a contest was recognized by almost all participants, and that recognition, perhaps more than any single legal device, helps explain what happened next.

Section 93.18

The General Election of 1977: Congress Defeat and the First National Transfer of Power

Voting took place between 16 and 20 March 1977, with a turnout of about 60.5 percent. The result was a decisive defeat for Congress. Of 542 seats, Congress won 154, with about 34.5 percent of the votes cast, compared with its 352 seats and 43.7 percent in 1971. The Janata Party, whose candidates mostly stood under the Bharatiya Lok Dal symbol, won 295 seats with about 41.3 percent, enough on its own for an absolute majority; the Communist Party of India (Marxist) won 22 seats, the Communist Party of India 7, and the AIADMK, allied with Congress, 18. Indira Gandhi lost her own seat at Rae Bareli to Raj Narain, the petitioner in the Allahabad case, by 55,202 votes, and Sanjay Gandhi lost at Amethi.

The pattern of the vote was strongly regional. In much of north India, the area of the most intense sterilization and slum-clearance campaigns, Congress was almost wiped out: it won no seat in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Delhi, or Himachal Pradesh, and a single seat in each of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. In the south, Congress and its allies did well: the party won the great majority of seats in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, and fared far better in Tamil Nadu and Kerala than in the north. Western India was mixed, with Congress winning a larger share in Maharashtra and Gujarat than in the Hindi-speaking states but losing much ground. Scholars commonly argue that the difference reflects the varying intensity of enforcement, the history of the Congress organization in different regions, and the strength of regional parties and leaders. Such explanations are plausible and supported by correlations, but they are interpretations, not facts derived from the returns, and individual constituencies often differ from the regional average.

The relation between votes and seats was also a feature of the outcome. Congress's share of the vote fell by about nine percentage points, but the number of its seats more than halved, a consequence of the first-past-the-post system in which the winner in each constituency takes the seat. The opposition's decision to unite under one symbol meant that votes against Congress were concentrated rather than divided, which magnified the effect of a modest national swing in the north into a landslide. The point shows why electoral arrangements matter for the political consequences of public opinion.

The transfer of power followed quickly and peacefully. The Cabinet recommended the revocation of the Emergency on 21 March, and the proclamations of 1975 and 1971 were withdrawn. President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed had died on 11 February 1977, and B. D. Jatti, the Vice-President, was acting President. On 24 March Morarji Desai, at the age of eighty-one, was sworn in as Prime Minister at the head of the first national government not led by Congress. Accounts of the final days of the Emergency include stories that the Prime Minister considered ignoring the verdict, and these have appeared in memoirs and journalism, but reliable evidence for such a decision is lacking, and the documented record shows an orderly transition.

The election was a milestone. It showed that the electorate could remove a government that had suspended ordinary politics, and that the machinery of elections, the parties, and the norms of peaceful transition remained strong enough to carry out the change. It was not a guarantee of future stability, however. The winning coalition was an alliance of convenience, the Emergency had demonstrated how much depended on individuals, and the institutional safeguards that had failed in 1975–1976 had not yet been repaired. Democratic recovery was an achievement that remained to be consolidated.

Section 93.19

The Janata Government: Constitutional Repair, Accountability, and Political Fragmentation

Morarji Desai's government included Charan Singh as Home Minister, Atal Bihari Vajpayee as External Affairs Minister, L. K. Advani as Information and Broadcasting Minister, Jagjivan Ram as Defence Minister, and George Fernandes as Industry Minister. Its first acts were to dismantle the Emergency's apparatus. Detainees who had not been released were freed, bans on organizations were lifted, censorship was ended, and the government pledged to restore constitutional rights. Parliament passed the 43rd Amendment in 1977, repealing several provisions of the 42nd that had limited judicial review, and in December 1978 passed the 44th Amendment, which became law in 1979 (Primary Source Window below).

The government also tried to establish accountability. In May 1977 it appointed the Shah Commission, whose reports, issued in 1978, are discussed in §93.16. The Janata government pursued cases against some who had been involved in Emergency excesses. In October 1977 Indira Gandhi was arrested on corruption charges and released the next day because the case was not sustained. In December 1978, after a parliamentary privileges inquiry, the Lok Sabha expelled her from the House and she was briefly jailed. Sanjay Gandhi and some officials faced prosecutions, with limited outcomes. Many observers, including some in the Janata government, concluded that the effort at accountability was uneven and did not produce a clear judicial finding of responsibility, and that the proceedings strengthened Indira Gandhi's claim to be a victim of vindictiveness.

Not all the Janata government's measures strengthened the federal and democratic norms it had promised to restore. In April 1977 Charan Singh, as Home Minister, advised the dismissal of the Congress governments in nine states, on the ground that they had lost the confidence of the electorate in the Lok Sabha elections, and the Supreme Court declined to interfere. The step, which used Article 356 against governments that retained assembly majorities, was criticized as inconsistent with the party's own principles, and it showed that the instruments of central control that had been used in the Emergency years were attractive to those who inherited them.

The more serious problem was internal. The Janata Party was an alliance of four parties and the Congress for Democracy, led by men who had been rivals for decades, among them Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, and Jagjivan Ram, each of whom believed he had a claim to the premiership. There were sharp ideological differences, including over the status of members of the Jana Sangh who were also members of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, a controversy about "dual membership" that became a central cause of conflict. The government's economic policy was cautious, and its ability to carry out structural reform was limited by internal disagreements. Meanwhile, the Congress party split again in January 1978 when Indira Gandhi and her supporters formed what became Congress (I), and she won a by-election at Chikmagalur in Karnataka in November 1978, returning to Parliament and to national politics.

By 1979 the government was losing its majority. Charan Singh and his supporters left the party in July, and Morarji Desai resigned on 15 July 1979. Charan Singh formed a government with the support of Congress (I) from outside, but when Congress (I) withdrew its support in August he resigned without having faced the Lok Sabha, and Parliament was dissolved. In the election held in January 1980 Congress (I) won 353 seats, and Indira Gandhi returned as Prime Minister on 14 January 1980. The reasons for that outcome, including the record of the Janata government and the political conditions of 1980, belong to Chapter 94, and this chapter takes the return only as a transition.

Within the terms of this chapter, the Janata period had two lessons. It demonstrated that the democratic recovery could be completed in constitutional terms: the Emergency had been ended, the main amendments reversed or narrowed, and the principles of basic structure and of judicial protection of personal liberty reaffirmed. It also showed that recovery did not guarantee stable government: the coalition that defeated the Emergency could not hold together to govern, and its collapse allowed the leader whose actions had led to the Emergency to return through the same electoral process.

Documentary paraphrase of reported findings (not a quotation): The Commission recorded, among other things, that the decision to proclaim the Emergency was taken without prior Cabinet consultation; that detentions and censorship were used against political opponents; that Sanjay Gandhi had exercised authority without holding office; that the Turkman Gate demolitions had been carried out without proper legal procedure; and that large-scale sterilization had resulted in complaints and deaths, including 548 complaints concerning unmarried persons and 1,774 reported deaths.

Analysis: The Commission's reports are one of the most important sources for the Emergency because they draw on official files and the testimony of officials, many of whom had been directly involved. They have limits. The Commission was created by a government that had defeated the Emergency's authors and had political reasons to expose them; its main subject, the Prime Minister, did not fully cooperate; and its findings reflect a legal inquiry into misconduct, not a complete social history. Where its record is consistent with other evidence, such as court records and parliamentary statements, historians treat it as strong evidence.

Excerpt and paraphrase: The amended Article 352 allows a proclamation where the security of India is threatened "by war or external aggression or armed rebellion," replacing "internal disturbance." The amendment also required that the Cabinet communicate its decision to the President in writing, that Parliament approve a proclamation within one month by a special majority, and that the rights protected by Articles 20 and 21 could not be suspended during an Emergency. It removed Article 329A and restored five-year terms for legislatures, and deleted property from the list of fundamental rights.

Analysis: The returns are quantitative evidence of an election's outcome but not its causes; the amendment is evidence of what its drafters believed had gone wrong. By tying emergency powers to armed rebellion, requiring Cabinet responsibility, and protecting Articles 20 and 21, it targeted the specific mechanisms used in 1975. It did not address every risk: Article 356, preventive-detention laws, and the informal concentration of authority in a single leader remained. The amendment also reflected the preferences of the Janata coalition, including the removal of property as a fundamental right, which was not an Emergency issue.

Historical Debate

Why Did Electoral Competition End the Emergency Despite Weakened Institutional Safeguards?

The question: The courts, Parliament, and most of the press had failed to check the executive in 1975–1976, yet an election in 1977 removed the government. What explains the success of electoral competition?

Interpretation A — The resilience of electoral institutions and norms: Even during the Emergency, the Election Commission, the party system, and the expectation that governments derive legitimacy from elections remained in place. The Prime Minister had always claimed democratic legitimacy and had no mechanism, apart from an indefinite extension, to avoid an election. The strength of these norms, built over a quarter-century, is argued to have made the return to elections natural once the decision was taken.
Interpretation B — Miscalculation and contingent circumstances: The Prime Minister's decision to call an election rested on misleading information and misplaced confidence; the opposition's unity was assembled in weeks and was far from inevitable; and Jagjivan Ram's defection came at a crucial time. On this view the outcome was contingent, and a different set of choices could have extended the Emergency.
Interpretation C — Voters' response to lived experience: The pattern of the vote, strongest against Congress where sterilization and demolition were most intense, suggests that voters, especially poor and rural voters, used the election to reject the coercive policies. Some quantitative studies of the campaign relate its intensity to electoral change, though correlations of this kind depend on the quality of the data and do not exclude other factors.

Evidence: A relies on the continuity of the electoral machinery and the statements of political actors. B relies on memoirs, intelligence reports, and the chronology of January–February 1977. C relies on the returns and on district-level statistics. The three approaches address different aspects of the same event and are generally combined by historians.

Why it matters: If democracy was restored mainly because of institutional resilience, then the protection of electoral procedures is a priority. If it was contingent, democracies cannot rely on the same outcome. If voters acted on experience, then the quality of information and the freedom to report abuses are crucial.

Student task: Explain how the three interpretations would differ in their predictions about what would have happened if the Emergency had lasted three more years, and identify evidence that would test them.

Section 93.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Democracy, Constitutional Power, and the Legacy of the Emergency

This chapter has traced a path from the succession of 1966 to the return of Indira Gandhi in 1980, with a central argument: that a democratically elected government accumulated extraordinary power, used constitutional emergency provisions to suppress opposition and civil liberties, and was subsequently defeated through the electoral system it had constrained. The problems that bind the twenty sections together are electoral legitimacy, political centralization, constitutional safeguards, bureaucratic obedience, mass resistance, and accountability.

Electoral legitimacy. Indira Gandhi's authority was not conferred by the party hierarchy but won through confrontation and elections: the split of 1969, the campaign of 1971, and the victory of that year gave her a mandate of unusual strength. That mandate was real and was also the basis of the claim that opposition was obstruction. When the legitimacy of that mandate was questioned by the Allahabad court and by mass movements, the government reacted by suspending the competitive politics through which a mandate is tested. The 1977 election, held under conditions that were not wholly free but sufficiently open, shows that the same source of legitimacy could be withdrawn.

Centralization. Power was concentrated in stages: control of the party, the selection of chief ministers, the shaping of the judiciary, the retention of the 1971 Emergency, and the growth of the Prime Minister's Secretariat and informal networks. The Emergency of 1975 did not create this concentration but used it, and the role of Sanjay Gandhi shows how it produced authority that bypassed constitutional office.

Constitutional safeguards and their failure. The safeguards intended to limit emergency power were Parliament, the courts, the press, the federal system, and the requirement of ministerial advice. Each failed or was circumvented. Parliament sat without its opposition, the press was censored, the Cabinet was informed after the event, the states were subject to central direction, and the Supreme Court in ADM Jabalpur accepted the suspension of judicial remedies. The partial resistance of High Courts, the basic-structure judgment in the Raj Narain case, and Justice Khanna's dissent show that the system was not wholly without resources. The lesson is that safeguards depend on the willingness of those who hold them to use them, as well as on their legal form.

Bureaucratic obedience and mass experience. The Emergency's policies were carried out by civil servants, police, teachers, and health workers, many of whom complied because of fear, ambition, or conviction, and some of whom resisted. The sterilization and demolition campaigns show how administrative systems can transmit pressure from the top to the poorest people with little regard for consent. The experiences of different classes, regions, and groups, set out in §93.16, did not produce a single reaction, but their cumulative effect was visible in the vote of 1977.

Resistance and accountability. Resistance came from detained leaders, underground activists, a few editors, some judges, and many ordinary citizens, and it was assisted by the reconstitution of the opposition as the Janata Party. Accountability afterward was partial: the Shah Commission documented abuses, the 43rd and 44th Amendments reformed the Constitution, and a few individuals faced legal proceedings, but no systematic judicial settlement followed, and the principal figure returned to office within three years.

Why democracy was restored through elections, and why safeguards were not enough. The restoration of democracy depended on the survival of an electoral system that the government itself did not abolish. It rested on the legitimacy of elections as a source of authority, on the capacity of the opposition to unite, and on voters' willingness to use the ballot. The reforms that followed made some of the abuses of 1975 legally harder, by narrowing the grounds for Emergency, requiring Cabinet responsibility, and protecting Articles 20 and 21, and the Supreme Court's later doctrine on basic structure and its rejection of the ADM Jabalpur reasoning increased protection of liberty. But constitutional reform could not remove the underlying conditions of executive dominance: a strong Prime Minister with a disciplined majority, a system of preventive detention, and the instrument of central intervention in the states under Article 356. Later chapters trace the continued use of such instruments in the 1980s and 1990s.

The chapter's themes connect to its neighbors. Chapter 92 showed how a victorious war strengthened the Prime Minister; Chapter 94 will show how Congress, returning to power in 1980, faced a transformed political landscape of agrarian change, caste and regional politics, and crisis in Punjab and Assam, in which the Emergency was a continuing reference point. The Emergency is remembered in India as a warning, and also, in contested ways, as a symbol invoked by parties in later political argument, a subject taken up in Chapter 111.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Constitution of 1950 to the Constitution of 1979

What Changed

  • The Congress party moved from an organization-centered coalition to a leader-centered party after 1969
  • The relationship between Parliament and the courts was transformed by the property cases, Kesavananda Bharati, and the Emergency amendments
  • The Constitution acquired the Fundamental Duties and new Preamble words in 1976, and lost property as a fundamental right in 1978–1979
  • The grounds for emergency changed from "internal disturbance" to "armed rebellion," with new procedural limits
  • National power passed away from Congress for the first time, and Indian politics became more competitive

What Stayed the Same

  • Elections, the Election Commission, and universal adult franchise continued to function
  • Preventive detention and Article 356 remained in the constitutional system
  • Poverty, regional inequality, and agrarian distress continued despite the slogans of both Congress and the Janata Party
  • The influence of personal leadership in Indian party politics endured

Who experienced change most directly: Detainees, the displaced, the sterilized, journalists, and opposition politicians experienced the Emergency most directly, while many in the urban middle class experienced it indirectly. Who experienced more continuity: Those whose lives were little touched by the campaigns, and the poor who remained poor under all governments. Why the pattern matters: It shows that constitutional change can occur without changing the conditions that made abuse possible.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how a democratically elected prime minister gained and exercised extraordinary power between 1966 and 1977, how that power was used to suspend democratic freedoms, and how elections restored a measure of democratic competition. Its central question was how India's institutions allowed the concentration and abuse of executive power during the Emergency, and how electoral competition, opposition, and constitutional reform contributed to recovery.

Indira Gandhi became Prime Minister in January 1966 as a compromise candidate of Congress organizational leaders. Economic difficulty, devaluation, and the weak result of the 1967 election left her dependent on them. Between 1967 and 1971, through the conflict over the presidency, the split of 1969, bank nationalization, and the Garibi Hatao campaign, she converted that dependence into personal authority. The election of 1971, in which Congress (R) won 352 seats, gave her a mandate that few Indian leaders had had.

The high point of her authority in 1971–1972 coincided with a series of developments that would later facilitate the Emergency: the selection of chief ministers from Delhi, the supersession of senior judges after Kesavananda Bharati, the enactment of MISA, and the retention of the 1971 Emergency proclamation. Meanwhile inflation, scarcity, the oil shock, and allegations of corruption eroded public support, and between 1973 and 1975 movements in Gujarat and Bihar, led in Bihar by Jayaprakash Narayan, and the railway strike, challenged the government.

The legal crisis came on 12 June 1975, when the Allahabad High Court found that Indira Gandhi had committed corrupt practices under the Representation of the People Act and voided her election. This was an electoral-law finding, not a criminal conviction, and on 24 June the Supreme Court granted only a conditional stay, which allowed her to remain in office but without a vote in Parliament. The opposition announced a campaign of civil disobedience. On 25–26 June, on the advice of the Prime Minister, the President proclaimed an Emergency under Article 352 on the ground of internal disturbance, in addition to the external Emergency of 1971 that remained in force.

The Emergency worked through law. Opposition leaders and many others were detained under MISA and related rules, roughly 110,000 over the period in one scholarly synthesis, with official and scholarly counts differing by category and date; the courts were told that detainees could not ask them to enforce fundamental rights; the press was censored and the news agencies merged; and the 38th, 39th, and 42nd Amendments reduced judicial review and strengthened the executive. The Supreme Court in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain applied basic-structure limits but upheld her election, and in ADM Jabalpur, by four to one, accepted the suspension of habeas corpus remedies, with Justice Khanna dissenting. Several High Courts resisted, and the 2017 Puttaswamy decision later repudiated the majority view.

Sanjay Gandhi, without formal office, exercised extensive informal authority through the Youth Congress and a network of ministers and officials. The Twenty-Point Programme and his Five-Point Programme joined social-reform objectives to coercive methods. Official figures record about 2.6 million sterilization procedures in 1975–1976 and about 8.1 million in 1976–1977, with reported complaints and deaths documented by the Shah Commission; these categories measure different things, and the number of procedures cannot be equated with the number that were forced. Demolitions in Delhi, including the violence at Turkman Gate in April 1976, displaced large numbers of poor residents.

The Emergency was experienced very differently by different groups. Some middle-class urban people and officials welcomed the order it seemed to bring, while the rural and urban poor, minorities, workers, and political activists bore its heaviest costs. Enforcement was most intense in Delhi and parts of north India. Resistance took forms from underground organizing and satyagraha to the judicial resistance of High Courts and everyday evasion.

In January 1977 the Prime Minister announced elections, whose reasons historians attribute to a combination of miscalculation, desire for legitimacy, and constitutional pressures. The opposition united as the Janata Party, joined by Jagjivan Ram's Congress for Democracy. In March 1977 Congress won 154 seats against Janata's 295, and the party lost every seat in several northern states. The Emergency was revoked and Morarji Desai became Prime Minister, the first national leader not from Congress.

The Janata government released detainees, ended censorship, established the Shah Commission, and passed the 43rd and 44th Amendments, which narrowed the grounds for emergency, required Cabinet responsibility, and protected Articles 20 and 21. But it was divided, it used Article 356 against Congress state governments, and it collapsed in 1979, leading to the return of Indira Gandhi in January 1980. The Emergency's legacy is both a warning about how legal powers can be turned against liberty and an illustration of the capacity of electoral competition and constitutional reform to restore democratic politics, although not to remove every risk of executive overreach.

Chapter Reflection

The Emergency matters because it shows that a democracy can be disabled without being abolished. Elections, courts, and a constitution existed throughout, and the suspension of ordinary politics was carried out through procedures that a constitution had provided. This is a harder lesson than the story of a coup, because it asks students to look for the conditions in which legal powers can be turned against the purposes they were meant to serve.

The chapter also reveals how personal and structural histories interact. A single leader, a particular legal crisis, and a small circle of advisers made the decision, but the decision could be implemented only because the party, the bureaucracy, the police, and much of the press complied, and because the courts at the highest level declined to stand in the way. At the same time the history shows resilience: a High Court judge, a dissenting Supreme Court justice, underground activists, ordinary voters, and an Election Commission that kept functioning made the restoration of competitive politics possible.

The consequences extended well beyond 1977. The Emergency shaped debates over executive power, judicial independence, press freedom, population policy, and urban displacement, and its memory became a resource in later political argument. Within the six-volume sequence, it forms a hinge: it followed the confidence of the first postcolonial generation, as narrated in Chapters 88 and 89, and it preceded the more fragmented and regionalized politics of the 1980s examined in Chapter 94.

Students who understand this chapter should be able to resist two easy conclusions: that India's democracy was never in danger, and that it was destroyed and then rescued by a single event. The more accurate account is one of vulnerability, resistance, and partial repair, and of the continuing need to ask who holds power, what limits it, and who can enforce those limits when they are tested.
