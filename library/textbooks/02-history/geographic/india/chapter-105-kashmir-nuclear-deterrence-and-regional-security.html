Kashmir, Nuclear Deterrence, and Regional Security

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

On the morning of April 22, 2025, tourists were gathered in Baisaran, a high meadow above the Kashmir resort town of Pahalgam, reachable only on foot or by pony. Armed men emerged from the surrounding pine forest and killed twenty-six people, twenty-five of them visitors and one a local pony handler. Within forty-eight hours, India had suspended a water-sharing treaty that had survived three wars; Pakistan had closed its airspace to Indian aircraft; and the two nuclear-armed states were exchanging fire across the Line of Control. Within three weeks, missiles, drones, and aircraft had struck targets deep inside both countries, and a ceasefire had been announced on May 10, 2025, after a crisis that many observers regarded as the most dangerous between the two states since 1999 or even 1971.

That sequence was not an aberration. It was the latest and most intense iteration of a pattern that has structured South Asian security since the early 1990s: a militant attack linked, by India, to Pakistan-based organizations; an Indian threat or use of military force; Pakistani denials and counter-threats; international intervention, especially by the United States; and a halt that leaves the underlying dispute untouched. This chapter asks why India and Pakistan have repeatedly approached major war even though both have possessed nuclear weapons since May 1998, and how the strategic involvement of China has complicated the regional security environment.

The chapter's argument rests on three analytical commitments. First, Kashmir cannot be treated merely as a territorial prize contested by two capitals. It is a region of many peoples, languages, religions, and political aspirations, whose civilians have borne the most continuous costs of the conflict. The Kashmir Valley, Jammu, Ladakh, Pakistan-administered Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Gilgit-Baltistan, and the China-administered Aksai Chin have different histories and no single shared political preference. Second, nuclear deterrence is not synonymous with peace. Deterrence appears to have helped prevent total war between India and Pakistan, but it has not prevented limited war, mobilization, terrorism, or dangerous misjudgment; and some scholars argue that it has made such lower-level violence more attractive. Third, the India–China frontier is a distinct strategic problem, governed by a different line, different history, and different logic from the India–Pakistan Line of Control, though by the 2020s the two problems had become increasingly entangled in Indian strategic thinking.

The historian of this subject faces unusual evidentiary difficulties. Official statements on all sides are instruments of policy as well as sources of information. Casualty figures, battlefield claims, and accounts of who initiated or mediated a ceasefire are disputed even among participants. Intelligence assessments are classified; attribution of attacks rests partly on judicial findings, partly on government allegations, and partly on claims by militant organizations that may themselves be strategic. The chapter therefore distinguishes consistently among documented events, claims by governments, and scholarly inference. Because the narrative extends to November 20, 2026, the final sections also describe developments whose outcomes are unknown; these are presented as ongoing rather than settled.

The chapter follows the approved five-division structure. Division I examines Kashmir from the origins of the insurgency in 1989 to the present, centering the experience of its inhabitants. Division II traces nuclearization, the Kargil War, the 2001–2002 standoff, and the diplomatic opening of the 2000s. Division III follows terrorism and military retaliation from the Mumbai attacks of 2008 through the confrontation of May 2025. Division IV analyzes Kashmir's constitutional transformation after 2019. Division V places the India–China frontier, China–Pakistan cooperation, and nuclear strategy in a single regional framework and concludes by connecting security to the wider transformation examined in Chapter 106.

The chapter's position in the series matters. Chapter 87 examined the accession of Jammu and Kashmir and the first India–Pakistan war; Chapter 92 examined the wars of 1962, 1965, and 1971; Chapter 97 traced nuclear programs and the origins of SAARC; Chapter 101 followed Pakistan's domestic politics; and Chapter 104 treated the Himalayan and Indian Ocean states. The present chapter draws on those narratives only as necessary. It also leaves river-basin hydrology and environmental analysis to Chapter 109 and the wider global alignments of the United States, Russia, and the Gulf to Chapter 110.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the origins of the Kashmir insurgency of 1989–1990 and distinguish its local political causes from external support.
  2. Distinguish among Kashmiri nationalism, pro-Pakistan militancy, Islamist transnational organizations, and moderate autonomist politics.
  3. Describe the geographic and political diversity of the former princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, including the areas administered by India, Pakistan, and China.
  4. Analyze the human consequences of prolonged militarization for civilians, distinguishing documented abuses from contested allegations.
  5. Trace the nuclearization of South Asia in 1998 and explain the logic and limits of nuclear deterrence.
  6. Compare the crises of 1999, 2001–2002, 2008, 2016, 2019, and 2025 in terms of trigger, escalation, restraint, and resolution.
  7. Evaluate competing arguments over whether nuclear weapons prevented a wider war at Kargil and in later crises.
  8. Assess the opportunities and failures of India–Pakistan diplomacy between 1999 and 2008.
  9. Interpret the constitutional changes of August 2019 and the December 2023 Supreme Court judgment, including the difference between electoral restoration and constitutional restoration.
  10. Distinguish the Line of Control from the Line of Actual Control and explain the development of the India–China frontier dispute through 2026.
  11. Explain how China–Pakistan strategic cooperation shapes India's perception of a two-front problem without treating either state as an instrument of the other.
  12. Evaluate the strengths and limits of the evidence available for recent military confrontations.

Historical Context

The princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, ruled by the Dogra Maharaja Hari Singh, was among the largest of the princely states that faced a choice at the end of British paramountcy in 1947. Its population was diverse: a Muslim majority overall, concentrated in the Kashmir Valley and parts of Jammu, but with large Hindu populations in Jammu, Buddhists in Ladakh, and Shia and Ismaili communities in the northern mountains. After tribal-militia and Pakistani irregular forces entered the state in October 1947, the Maharaja signed an Instrument of Accession to India, and the first India–Pakistan war followed. The ceasefire line established under United Nations auspices on January 1, 1949, divided the state; Chapter 87 treats these events in detail.

The subsequent decades established the structures that this chapter inherits. India maintained that accession was legally complete; Pakistan, citing the UN Security Council resolutions of 1948–1949 that envisaged a plebiscite, maintained that the final status of the state remained unresolved. A plebiscite was never held. Within the Indian-administered part, Article 370 of the Indian Constitution (1949) gave the state a special autonomous status, which was progressively eroded through presidential orders after 1953, when Sheikh Muhammad Abdullah, the dominant Kashmiri leader, was dismissed and arrested. The wars of 1965 and 1971 (Chapter 92) left Kashmir unresolved, and the Simla Agreement of July 1972 converted the 1971 ceasefire line into the Line of Control (LoC) and committed both states to settle differences bilaterally. Pakistan has since insisted that Simla does not extinguish the Kashmir question as an international dispute.

Three further contexts shape the chapter. The first is the strategic environment of the end of the Cold War. The Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan in 1989 released experienced fighters and organizational networks that had been supported by Pakistan and its partners during the 1980s (Chapter 95), some of which were redirected toward Kashmir. The second is nuclear development. India's 1974 test and Pakistan's covert program (Chapter 97) created by the 1990s a situation of undeclared nuclear capability that became overt in 1998. The third is China. After the war of 1962, China held Aksai Chin; in 1963 Pakistan ceded to China the Shaksgam Tract, and China and Pakistan developed what both describe as an all-weather partnership. The India–China boundary, unlike the Kashmir LoC, was never converted into an agreed line.

Historians of this subject rely on a mixture of sources: official statements and diplomatic records; Indian parliamentary and judicial records; Pakistani military and civilian memoirs; reports by the United Nations, human-rights organizations, and journalists; satellite imagery; and, for the most recent events, press reporting that has not yet been tested against archives. Students should expect more uncertainty here than in chapters where decades of archival work exist, and should treat any claim about an incident in the nuclear era as the product of a particular institutional vantage point.

Division I — Kashmir: Insurgency, Territorial Dispute, and Civilian Experience, 1989–2026

Section 105.1

The Kashmir Dispute and the Origins of the Insurgency

The armed insurgency that began in the Kashmir Valley in 1989–1990 had both a long history and an immediate political trigger. The long history was the unresolved status of the state after 1947. India's position rested on the Maharaja's Instrument of Accession and on the Constituent Assembly of Jammu and Kashmir, which in 1956 adopted a constitution declaring the state an integral part of India. Pakistan's position rested on the United Nations resolutions of 1948–1949, which called for a plebiscite once Pakistani forces withdrew, and on the argument that a Muslim-majority state should have gone to Pakistan. India in turn argued that Pakistan had never completed the withdrawal that the resolutions required first. Neither argument was ever tested by a vote of the state's inhabitants.

For Kashmiris, the central political experience of the decades after 1947 was the repeated reduction of promised autonomy. Article 370 and the 1952 Delhi Agreement had implied that Delhi's powers would be limited to defense, foreign affairs, and communications. But after Sheikh Abdullah's dismissal and imprisonment in 1953, successive state governments depended on Delhi's support, and through presidential orders the central government extended most Union powers and many constitutional provisions to the state. The 1975 accord between Indira Gandhi and Sheikh Abdullah brought him back to power but accepted these changes as a fait accompli (Chapter 93). The state's political class thus came to be seen by many Kashmiris as dependent on New Delhi, while dissenting voices, notably the Jamaat-e-Islami and a variety of plebiscite-front sympathizers, were marginalized. Wars between India and Pakistan in 1965 and 1971 intensified the sense that Kashmir was a pawn of larger conflicts; the Simla Agreement of 1972, which converted the ceasefire line into the Line of Control and committed the two states to bilateral resolution, left no role for Kashmiri participation.

The immediate trigger was the state assembly election of 1987. A coalition of Islamic and other opposition groups, the Muslim United Front (MUF), contested against the alliance of Farooq Abdullah's National Conference and the Indian National Congress. The National Conference–Congress alliance won decisively, but many observers, journalists, and later Kashmiri participants alleged extensive manipulation, including arrests of MUF workers and irregular counting. Indian officials have disputed the extent of fraud, and it is difficult to establish the scale of manipulation precisely; but the perception that peaceful electoral routes were blocked proved historically consequential. Several young men who had worked for the MUF, including Muhammad Yasin Malik, who had been an MUF polling agent, and Mohammad Yusuf Shah, a candidate who later became known as Syed Salahuddin, later became prominent in the armed movement.

Other conditions converged. Unemployment among an expanding educated generation, resentment of corruption and of Delhi's influence, the global example of the fall of authoritarian regimes in 1989, and the Afghan jihad's demonstration that an armed insurgency could defeat a superpower provided a repertoire and an ideological climate. The execution of the Kashmiri separatist Maqbool Bhat in Delhi in 1984 had created a martyr for the cause of independence. Cross-LoC training and weapons soon followed, a subject examined in §105.2.

The tempo accelerated in late 1989. The kidnapping of Rubaiya Sayeed, daughter of the Union Home Minister, in December 1989, and the release of five detained militants in exchange, convinced many that the Indian state was vulnerable. In January 1990 the central government appointed Jagmohan Malhotra as governor, the state government resigned, and direct central control began. On January 19–20, 1990, house-to-house searches in Srinagar and large protests culminated on January 21 at Gawakadal bridge, where Indian paramilitary forces fired on a crowd of demonstrators; estimates of the dead range from several dozen to more than fifty. By the spring of 1990 hundreds of thousands of Kashmiris were taking part in demonstrations demanding azadi, a term meaning "freedom" that different groups defined differently, from independence to accession to Pakistan to greater autonomy.

The insurgency of 1989–1990 should therefore not be treated as a simple foreign invasion by proxy, nor as a spontaneous popular uprising without outside assistance. It was an indigenous political crisis that foreign actors, chiefly Pakistan's military intelligence apparatus, learned to exploit and shape. This distinction is critical for understanding the later trajectory: as the movement's local leadership was killed, co-opted, or marginalized, the proportion of non-Kashmiri militants rose, and the conflict increasingly became one that outside powers drove while Kashmiris suffered its effects.

Section 105.2

Armed Movements, Militancy, and Cross-Border Networks

The first phase of the insurgency was dominated by the Jammu and Kashmir Liberation Front (JKLF), founded in the 1960s–1970s and committed to the independence of all of the former princely state, India- and Pakistan-administered alike. The JKLF's leaders, including Amanullah Khan, who was based in Pakistan-administered Kashmir, and Yasin Malik in the Valley, drew on widespread popular sympathy in 1989–1990. But Pakistan's security establishment had little interest in an independent Kashmir. By 1990–1991 support shifted to organizations favoring accession to Pakistan, above all the Hizbul Mujahideen, formed in 1989 under the leadership of Muhammad Ehsan Dar, with Syed Salahuddin later becoming its supreme commander. The Hizbul Mujahideen was largely composed of Kashmiris and was linked to the Jamaat-e-Islami. A violent contest between the JKLF and the Hizbul Mujahideen, in which the former was weakened, followed. In 1994 Yasin Malik announced a unilateral ceasefire and the JKLF turned toward non-violent politics.

From the early 1990s a second type of organization became increasingly prominent: transnational Islamist groups, largely recruited from Pakistan's Punjab and from the Afghan-war milieu. Among them the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen and Harkat-ul-Ansar were early examples. The Lashkar-e-Taiba (LeT), the armed wing of the Markaz Dawa wal Irshad (later Jamaat-ud-Dawa), founded in Afghanistan's Kunar province in the late 1980s and headquartered at Muridke near Lahore, began operations in Kashmir around 1993 and developed a reputation for fidayeen or suicide-style assaults on military installations. Its leader, Hafiz Muhammad Saeed, maintained that its goal was the Islamization of society as well as the liberation of Kashmir. The Jaish-e-Mohammed (JeM) was founded in early 2000 by Masood Azhar, one of three militants whom India released in December 1999 in exchange for the passengers of a hijacked Indian Airlines aircraft, IC-814, which had been flown to Kandahar. These groups differed from the earlier Kashmiri movements in their organization, their sectarian and ideological orientation, their lack of local roots, and their willingness to attack targets beyond Kashmir.

The question of Pakistani state support is among the best-documented in the contemporary history of the region, yet it remains politically charged. India has consistently asserted that Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI) trained, armed, financed, and directed militant groups. Pakistan has consistently characterized its role as diplomatic, moral, and political support for the Kashmiri right to self-determination, denying direction of attacks. Evidence from outside sources, including statements by former Pakistani officials, including later remarks by Pervez Musharraf, acknowledgments in Pakistani media, judicial proceedings, and reports by Western governments and the United Nations, indicates that Pakistani agencies provided at least training facilities, safe havens, and logistical support in earlier decades, even though they did not necessarily control every operation or every group. By the 2000s, under international pressure, Pakistan banned several groups; but banned organizations reappeared under new names, a pattern that Indian and Western officials cited as evidence of tolerance. In the 2010s the international Financial Action Task Force placed Pakistan on its "grey list" for deficiencies in countering terror finance, from which it was removed in October 2022.

Terminology matters. Indian official usage typically calls the violence "terrorism," emphasizing cross-border sponsorship and the targeting of civilians. Pakistani official usage has often called the militants "freedom fighters" or avoided the term, and calls the conflict a freedom struggle. Many Kashmiris describe the period as an uprising, a resistance, or simply a conflict, while distinguishing between the violence of different groups. Scholars generally prefer neutral terms such as insurgency and militancy for the whole, while applying "terrorism" analytically to attacks that deliberately kill civilians, a category that includes acts committed by militants against Hindu villagers in Jammu and Kashmiri Pandits, against Sikhs at Chittisinghpura in March 2000, and against tourists at Pahalgam in 2025. This chapter follows that usage, and it notes that non-state violence by some militants against Kashmiri civilians, including those suspected of collaboration, is documented in Kashmiri and human-rights accounts.

The character of the insurgency changed over time. The 1990s saw large numbers of local recruits and the presence of Afghan veterans; the government responded in part by creating counter-insurgent militias (Ikhwan) from surrendered militants, which committed their own abuses. In the 2000s, after the 2003 ceasefire and increased fencing and surveillance along the LoC, the numbers of active militants fell, and the proportion of foreign fighters in the remaining groups exceeded that of Kashmiris, according to Indian official estimates. A new generation of locally recruited militants emerged after 2008–2010 protest cycles, and especially after the killing of the Hizbul commander Burhan Wani on July 8, 2016, whose social-media presence and funeral drew enormous crowds. The Resistance Front, which first appeared around 2019 and was described by Indian authorities as a front for the Lashkar-e-Taiba, claimed attacks in the 2020s, including, initially, the Pahalgam attack. The pattern across thirty-five years is clear: external organizations have repeatedly supplied weapons and doctrine, but each major turn in the conflict has also required local political conditions that made recruitment possible.

Section 105.3

Militarization, Human Rights, and Kashmir's Civilian Population

The Indian state's response to the insurgency was to deploy an enormous security presence. The Army, the Border Security Force, the Central Reserve Police Force, and, from 1990, the Rashtriya Rifles, a counter-insurgency force drawn from the Army, as well as the state police, operated in the Valley and parts of Jammu; the total number of security personnel in the state has been commonly estimated in the hundreds of thousands, though official figures are not published in detail. The legal framework included the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of 1990 applied to the state, which allows the use of lethal force on suspicion in disturbed areas and requires central government sanction for prosecution of personnel; the Jammu and Kashmir Public Safety Act of 1978, which allowed administrative detention without trial for up to two years in some categories; and other emergency or special laws. Indian officials defended these measures as necessary to confront an armed, externally supported insurgency; critics argued that they created impunity.

Human-rights organizations and the UN Human Rights Office documented a range of abuses by state forces: extrajudicial killings described as "encounters" that were staged; torture in detention; sexual violence, including the alleged mass rape at Kunan and Poshpora in February 1991, whose investigation remains contested; and enforced disappearances. The Association of Parents of Disappeared Persons has estimated several thousand cases (figures of 8,000 or more are cited by Kashmiri groups), while Indian authorities have disputed such numbers and attributed many missing men to crossing the LoC for militant training. A 2011 investigation by the Jammu and Kashmir State Human Rights Commission documented 2,730 bodies initially buried as unidentified at 38 sites in north Kashmir. Its investigators reported that 574 had subsequently been identified by local families, leaving 2,156 without identification at the time of the inquiry, and urged further investigation, including comparison with families of missing persons. Neither the existence of an unidentified grave nor the identification of a local resident, by itself, establishes how that person died or who was responsible; the report raised urgent evidentiary questions rather than resolving every disappearance case. In 2000 the killing of five villagers at Pathribal, presented as the killing of the militants responsible for the Chittisinghpura massacre, was later found by Indian central investigators to have been staged; the Supreme Court in 2012 closed the case without prosecution in the civilian courts, leaving the matter to the military justice system. In 2010 three villagers killed at Machil by Army personnel were presented as infiltrators; military courts later convicted some personnel, although the sentences were suspended on appeal. In 2018 and 2019 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights published reports on Kashmir calling for an independent international investigation; India rejected them as biased and as violating its sovereignty, and Pakistan welcomed them.

Militants also killed civilians in large numbers. Documented massacres include the killing of thirty-five Sikh men at Chittisinghpura in March 2000, of twenty-three Pandits at Wandhama in 1998, of villagers in Jammu's Kishtwar and Doda districts, and assaults on pilgrims to the Amarnath shrine. Local politicians, journalists, and members of religious communities were among the victims, and militants imposed social codes in some localities. The number of people killed in the conflict is disputed: Indian official figures cited in parliamentary replies are in the low-to-mid forty thousands, comprising civilians, security forces, and militants since 1990; Kashmiri and some non-governmental estimates are much higher. All such figures are provisional.

The displacement of Kashmiri Pandits is among the most politically and emotionally contested episodes. In 1989–1990, after killings of prominent Pandits and public threats broadcast from mosques and wall posters, a large majority of the Valley's Pandit population, a small Hindu minority of perhaps three to five percent, left for Jammu, Delhi, and elsewhere. Estimates of those who left vary widely, with numbers of one hundred thousand or more commonly cited and registered migrant families counted in the tens of thousands. Hindu nationalist narratives have often described the exodus as a genocide organized by Islamist militants, while some Kashmiri and other scholars point to the role of the governor's administration in facilitating departures and to the fact that Muslims also fled and were killed. Historians agree that the departure was real, that militants targeted Pandits, that fear was widespread, and that many displaced families lived for years in camps, while disagreeing about causation, scale, and what term best describes it. Return has been limited; employment packages, special townships, and, after 2019, new rehabilitation initiatives have drawn few families back, and killings of minority members in 2021–2022 renewed fears.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Conflict

For civilians in the Valley, security forces were a constant presence in the form of checkpoints, bunkers, nighttime curfews, and "crackdowns," in which men were assembled for identification. Schools were closed for extended periods, especially in the early 1990s and during the summers of 2008, 2010, and 2016; universities and the economy suffered; and tourism, historically the Valley's principal industry, collapsed in the 1990s and recovered unevenly after 2003, only to be disrupted repeatedly. Mental-health surveys by international medical organizations have found high rates of trauma-related symptoms. Families of the disappeared, known as "half-widows" when wives did not know whether their husbands were alive, faced legal and economic limbo. Protest, notably stone-throwing by young men in 2008, 2010, and 2016, was met with pellet shotguns, which blinded or injured hundreds, as documented by hospitals and the Indian state's own legal challenges. For Pandits, displacement meant loss of orchards, homes, and community networks. For soldiers and paramilitary personnel, deployment meant service in a hostile environment, with its own casualties and trauma. For villagers near the LoC on both sides, shelling and the construction of bunkers were routine in periods of crisis.

Section 105.4

Kashmir's Divided Geography and Competing Political Aspirations

"Kashmir" in common usage names the whole of the former princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, which covered roughly 222,000 square kilometers in 1947. Its parts differ in language, religion, and history. The Kashmir Valley, an alluvial basin around the Jhelum river, is overwhelmingly Muslim and Kashmiri-speaking, with a historic Sufi and Persianate culture and a small Pandit minority before 1990. Jammu division, in the Dogra homeland, includes the Hindu-majority plains and foothills, with significant Muslim populations, notably Gujjars, Bakarwals, and Pahari speakers, in the Pir Panjal and Chenab regions. Ladakh, a high-altitude cold desert, has a Buddhist majority around Leh and a Shia Muslim majority around Kargil. Together, these areas make up the Indian-administered part, which after 2019 comprises the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and of Ladakh.

The Pakistan-administered portion includes Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK), with its capital at Muzaffarabad, with Poonch and Mirpur regions that sent many migrants to Britain, and its own president, prime minister, and legislature, while Pakistan controls defense, foreign affairs, and finance matters. Gilgit-Baltistan, a mountain territory of Shia, Ismaili, and Sunni communities, rebelled against the Maharaja in November 1947 and joined Pakistan, but was not formally incorporated as a province; for decades it was governed as the Northern Areas, and a 2009 reform gave it a nominal self-government. Pakistan has refrained from integrating it fully, partly to preserve its legal position on Kashmir, and many inhabitants have demanded constitutional status. China administers Aksai Chin, a barren plateau on the Ladakh–Xinjiang frontier through which it built a road in the 1950s, a project that contributed to the border war of 1962, and holds the Shaksgam Tract, ceded by Pakistan in 1963 under an agreement that both stated would be revisited after a final settlement of Kashmir. India regards all of these areas as part of its territory, as stated in a 1994 parliamentary resolution; Pakistan regards the Indian-administered area as disputed.

Kashmir's Areas of Administration, the LoC, the LAC, and the Himalayan Frontier

Using a historical atlas that distinguishes de facto from claimed boundaries, students should locate:

  • The Kashmir Valley, Jammu division, Kargil, and Leh
  • Muzaffarabad, Mirpur, and Poonch in Azad Jammu and Kashmir
  • Gilgit and Skardu in Gilgit-Baltistan
  • The Line of Control and the Siachen Glacier, north of the LoC's end-point at map reference NJ9842
  • Aksai Chin and the Shaksgam Tract
  • The Line of Actual Control in Ladakh, Galwan, Pangong Tso, Depsang, and Doklam
  • Arunachal Pradesh and the McMahon Line

The LoC and LAC are different kinds of line: the former is a line agreed in 1972 on maps and demarcated on the ground, while the latter is an undemarcated and differently perceived limit of actual control. Maps in Indian, Pakistani, and Chinese publications draw the boundaries differently, and no single map is neutral.

These geographies correspond to differing political aspirations, which historians distinguish rather than collapsing into "pro-India" and "pro-Pakistan." There are at least five broad positions. Accessionist positions, strongest in much of Jammu and Ladakh and among sections of the Valley's population, accept the 1947 accession and seek greater integration with India; the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Congress have represented versions of this view. Autonomist positions, associated with the National Conference and the People's Democratic Party (founded in 1999), accept membership in India but demand the restoration of autonomy; the National Conference's 2000 autonomy resolution and the PDP's "self-rule" proposals are examples. Pro-Pakistan positions, represented by Syed Ali Shah Geelani, the Hizbul Mujahideen, and a section of the Hurriyat, favor accession to Pakistan. Independence positions, represented by the JKLF and by many Kashmiris who prefer a sovereign state, reject both. Finally, many residents of Ladakh and Jammu have articulated regional demands: Ladakhi Buddhists, who feared domination by the Valley, demanded Union Territory status from 1989, and the Hindu-majority Jammu region has periodically agitated for equal treatment and, at times, for statehood of its own.

Reliable measurement of these preferences is impossible, because no free and comparable plebiscite has been held, surveys in a conflict zone are unreliable, and electoral participation is an imperfect proxy: voters may take part in elections to obtain governance without endorsing accession, while boycotts may reflect either principle or intimidation. Participation has varied sharply: turnout in the April 2017 Srinagar parliamentary by-election was around 7 percent, while turnout in the 2008, 2014, and 2024 assembly elections was substantially higher. The Pakistani-administered areas have also had contested politics, including movements in AJK for rights and, in 2024, large protests over prices and elite privileges. The historical point is that Kashmir's inhabitants have never held one uniform view, and any account claiming to speak for "the Kashmiris" should be read with suspicion.

Historical Vocabulary
Azadi

The Urdu and Hindi word for "freedom," which became the main slogan of the Kashmir uprising in 1989–1990. It has been used with different meanings: independence for the whole of the former state, accession to Pakistan, or substantial self-rule within India. Its historical importance lies in its ambiguity, which allowed diverse groups to join one movement while holding incompatible goals. Students should not assume that the word identifies a single political program or that its users shared one definition.

Division II — Nuclearization, Kargil, and India–Pakistan Crisis Diplomacy, 1998–2010

Section 105.5

The Nuclear Tests of 1998 and the Transformation of Regional Strategy

Chapter 97 traced the nuclear programs of the two states; this section addresses only the transition to overt deterrence. India's 1974 test, described officially as a "peaceful nuclear explosion," prompted Pakistan's accelerated program under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto and, later, the enrichment work associated with Abdul Qadeer Khan at Kahuta. By the late 1980s and 1990s, both states were widely believed to possess the ability to assemble weapons at short notice, a condition often called opaque or "recessed" deterrence. In the 1990 Kashmir crisis, American officials believed that war might have involved nuclear risks, although the interpretation of that episode, sometimes called the Gates mission, is contested by participants. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 and the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty of 1996 had been rejected by India as discriminatory, and Pakistan had linked its own position to India's.

In May 1998, after the Bharatiya Janata Party led by Atal Bihari Vajpayee formed a coalition government, India conducted tests at Pokhran on May 11 and 13. Indian leaders gave several rationales, including the declining international disarmament regime, the perceived threat from China and from Pakistan's missile and nuclear program, and a desire for recognition. Pakistan, whose leaders had been weighing responses, conducted five tests in the Chagai hills on May 28 and a sixth on May 30, under Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif, who calculated that restraint would be politically and strategically costly. The United States and Japan imposed sanctions, and the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 1172 in June 1998, condemning the tests and calling on both states to stop weapons development. The sanctions were largely lifted after 2001, when the war in Afghanistan made Pakistan, and later India, more important to Washington; in 2005–2008 the United States and India concluded a civil nuclear agreement that effectively accepted India's status.

Doctrine developed unevenly. India published a draft nuclear doctrine in August 1999 and an official summary in January 2003, committing to "credible minimum deterrence," no first use against nuclear-weapon states, nonuse against non-nuclear-weapon states, and "massive" retaliation after any first strike on Indian territory or forces anywhere, with the possibility of nuclear response to biological or chemical attacks. Pakistan has never published a full doctrine. Its officials have said that nuclear weapons would be used if the state's existence were threatened, and have identified thresholds, including loss of a large part of territory, destruction of a large part of its forces, economic strangulation, or political destabilization, as outlined by General Khalid Kidwai of its Strategic Plans Division in 2001. Pakistan has rejected a no-first-use posture on the ground that it would invite conventional attack from a larger neighbor. Later, Pakistan developed short-range systems, such as the Nasr (Hatf-IX), tested in 2011, to deter limited Indian conventional incursions; this "full-spectrum deterrence" has been criticized for lowering the nuclear threshold and creating pressure for early delegation of authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Stability–Instability Paradox

A concept associated with the strategist Glenn Snyder, applied by later scholars to South Asia. It holds that when nuclear weapons make large-scale war too costly, they may increase stability at the highest level of violence while encouraging lower-level conflict, because each side believes the other will not escalate to catastrophe. Applied to Kashmir, the argument is that Pakistan could support militancy or limited incursions under a nuclear shield, while India could be deterred from retaliating at scale. Critics argue that the pattern is not proven, since the proxy conflict predated nuclear weapons, and that Indian military doctrines since 2001 have been designed precisely to find space for conventional response. The term is therefore best treated as a hypothesis, not a law.

The tests produced three strategic consequences. First, they ended the ambiguity that had prevailed since the late 1980s; both governments could now be held responsible for the use or non-use of deliverable weapons. Second, they inaugurated new and more difficult questions of command and control, early warning, and the possibility of accidental or unauthorized use in a region where the flight time of missiles is measured in minutes. Third, and most relevant for the chapter's central question, they created a context in which each state could claim that nuclear weapons protected it, while the other could regard the claim as a license for adventurism. The first major test of this problem arrived within a year.

Section 105.6

The Kargil War of 1999: Limited War under Nuclear Deterrence

On February 20–21, 1999, Atal Bihari Vajpayee traveled by bus to Lahore and signed, with Nawaz Sharif, a declaration promising dialogue and nuclear confidence-building. At almost the same time, Pakistani troops were occupying high-altitude positions on the Indian side of the Line of Control in the Kargil, Drass, Batalik, and Mushkoh sectors, in terrain at 4,000 to 5,500 meters. These positions were in unmanned gaps that both armies had customarily vacated in winter, since the snow makes them impossible to hold. The occupiers consisted of regular soldiers, including units of the Northern Light Infantry, a paramilitary force recruited from Gilgit-Baltistan, supported by militants; Pakistan initially described them as "mujahideen," a claim contradicted by the later admission of its own army's casualties and by the awarding of military honors.

The plan's objectives remain contested. Indian accounts treat it as an attempt to cut the Srinagar–Leh highway, to isolate the Siachen Glacier area, and to force negotiation on Kashmir. Pakistani officers, including General Pervez Musharraf, then Chief of Army Staff, described it as a tactical response to Indian incursions elsewhere in Kashmir and Siachen. Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif later claimed that he had been informed only late and without full detail, while Musharraf has maintained that the prime minister had been briefed; the question of how far civilian authority was aware remains unresolved in the public record. Indian intelligence did not detect the occupation until early May, when shepherds and patrols reported intruders; the first Indian patrol, led by Lieutenant Saurabh Kalia, was captured around May 3, and its members were killed, with bodies returned showing signs of mutilation, according to Indian reports that Pakistan has denied.

India launched Operation Vijay (Army) and Operation Safed Sagar (Air Force). The Indian Air Force began strikes on May 26, within strict orders not to cross the LoC; India lost two aircraft on May 27 and a helicopter soon afterward. The fighting was carried out by infantry and artillery against entrenched defenders who held the heights; the Army suffered significant casualties in frontal assaults before the introduction of Bofors howitzers and laser-guided bombs to destroy bunkers. Tololing, taken in June, and Tiger Hill, in early July, were turning points, alongside Point 4875 and the Batalik ridge. India's official toll was 527 killed and about 1,360 wounded; Pakistan has given lower figures for its own losses (several hundred killed), while Indian estimates are higher, and Sharif once claimed far larger numbers. The war occurred only a year after both states had tested nuclear weapons, and it stands as the first conventional war between declared nuclear powers.

Diplomacy was decisive. India's decision not to cross the LoC or to open a front elsewhere, despite military advice that such measures might ease the campaign, gave it considerable diplomatic advantage: the G8 meeting in Cologne in June condemned the violation of the LoC, the United States pressed Pakistan to withdraw, and China declined to support it. Reports emerged that Pakistan had moved nuclear-capable assets; the American analyst Bruce Riedel, who attended the July 4 meeting at Blair House between Sharif and President Bill Clinton, later wrote that Clinton told Sharif that he might be preparing for nuclear escalation and refused to intervene unless Pakistan withdrew. Pakistani officials have denied that nuclear alert was ordered. Sharif agreed to withdraw, and by late July the positions were vacated or recaptured. In October 1999, after Sharif attempted to dismiss Musharraf, the army overthrew his government (Chapter 101).

Historical Debate

Did Nuclear Weapons Prevent a Wider Kargil War?

The question: Was India's restraint at Kargil produced by the fear of nuclear escalation, and did nuclear weapons also make Pakistan's initial incursion possible?

Interpretation A — Deterrence worked, at a cost: Scholars such as S. Paul Kapur argue that Pakistan's confidence that India would not respond with full-scale war encouraged the occupation, while Indian restraint in staying on its side of the LoC showed that nuclear risks bounded the conflict. On this view, nuclear weapons both prevented a wider war and encouraged limited aggression, exactly as the stability–instability hypothesis predicts.
Interpretation B — Other factors explain restraint: Scholars such as Šumit Ganguly and Devin Hagerty stress that India had long avoided crossing the LoC, that its political goals were to hold the diplomatic high ground, and that the terrain, not nuclear fear, constrained horizontal escalation. Others argue that Pakistan's calculation depended on Indian military weakness and surprise, rather than on a nuclear shield, and that the war shows Indian willingness to fight conventional limited war despite the risks.

Evidence: Both sides depend on partial evidence: memoirs of Indian and Pakistani officers, American accounts, and inference from behavior. The existence and scale of any Pakistani nuclear preparations remain disputed, and Indian officials have said that India's decision to stay on its side was an element of strategy that preceded any perception of a nuclear threat.

Why it matters: If nuclear weapons deterred Indian escalation, later Indian doctrines that sought to find space beneath the nuclear threshold (Operation Parakram in 2001–2002; the strikes of 2016, 2019, and 2025) can be seen as attempts to overcome the Kargil lesson. If they did not, the case for such experiments is weaker.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence not yet available to historians that would help determine whether nuclear fear shaped decision-making in June 1999.

Kargil had lasting consequences. It discredited Pakistan diplomatically, harmed Sharif, and ended the Lahore process; it persuaded Indian leaders that nuclear weapons did not preclude conventional war; it produced the Kargil Review Committee (1999–2000) and subsequent Indian reorganization of intelligence and defense, including the creation of a Chief of Defence Staff position only two decades later, in 2020; and it contributed to the concept of limited war below the nuclear threshold. The next test was already two years away.

Section 105.7

The 2001 Parliament Attack and the 2001–2002 Military Standoff

On December 13, 2001, five armed men drove into the compound of India's Parliament in New Delhi in a car with official markings, and attacked security personnel with automatic weapons and grenades. They were killed within about half an hour; nine other persons, including security personnel and a gardener, died. Parliament was in session, though the Prime Minister and most members had left the building or were inside, protected by the guards. Indian authorities attributed the attack to the Lashkar-e-Taiba and the Jaish-e-Mohammed, with the assistance of Pakistan's intelligence agency; Pakistan denied involvement and demanded evidence. An Indian court convicted Mohammad Afzal Guru, a Kashmiri, and others; Afzal was executed in 2013, a decision that remains controversial in Kashmir. The attack followed the October 2001 bombing of the state assembly in Srinagar, and came in the weeks after the September 11 attacks, when Pakistan had changed its Afghan policy under American pressure.

India's response was to mobilize its military. Operation Parakram, begun in December 2001, moved several hundred thousand troops to the border and the LoC, as part of what Indian planners considered coercive diplomacy: India sought to compel Pakistan to end support for cross-border militancy, extradite wanted individuals, and act against organizations, but also considered limited military options. Pakistan responded by deploying its forces, with Musharraf stating that Pakistan would respond to any attack. The two armies, among the largest standing forces in the world, faced each other along the Punjab and Rajasthan border in a posture reminiscent of earlier crises, but now both held nuclear weapons.

The crisis had two peaks. In January 2002, under heavy American and British pressure, Musharraf delivered a speech promising to ban sectarian and militant organizations, including the JeM and LeT, and to prevent the use of Pakistani territory for terrorism; it was well received internationally, though the groups reorganized. In May 2002, an attack on an Indian Army camp in Kaluchak in Jammu killed about thirty people, many of them family members of soldiers; India again prepared for strikes, and tension peaked in late May and June as both sides issued warnings. The American Deputy Secretary of State Richard Armitage and British Foreign Secretary Jack Straw conveyed assurances from Musharraf that infiltration would end, and the United States and others urged restraint. Nuclear signaling occurred in the form of statements: Pakistani officials said they would not rule out first use; Indian officials said that nuclear threats would not deter them. In October 2002 India withdrew its forces.

Operation Parakram showed what coercion by conventional mobilization could and could not do. Pakistan's reduction of infiltration across the LoC, and Musharraf's later acceptance of a ceasefire in 2003, suggested a coercive effect, although the reasons for those decisions also included American pressure, fencing of the border, and Pakistan's own concerns. But India had not attacked; its mobilization had taken weeks, allowing Pakistan to prepare, and it cost considerable resources and lives, including soldiers lost in accidents and mine-laying (reported in the hundreds). Indian military planners concluded that a faster, more limited response was necessary; the resulting concept, informally known as "Cold Start" and never formally acknowledged by the Indian government, envisioned rapid integrated battle groups conducting shallow incursions. Pakistan's response was the development of tactical nuclear weapons. Pakistani strategists have claimed credit for deterrence; Indian critics argue that the crisis demonstrated the limits of conventional options. The central lesson, shared by both sides but interpreted differently, was that large conventional forces facing each other under nuclear conditions create pressures for early action and risks of miscalculation.

Section 105.8

From Confrontation to Dialogue: Ceasefires and Peace Initiatives

The period between 1999 and 2008 contains both of the worst and some of the most promising moments in India–Pakistan relations. The Lahore process of February 1999, abruptly ended by Kargil, was followed by the Agra summit of July 2001 between Vajpayee and Musharraf, which failed over the wording of references to Kashmir and cross-border terrorism. After Parakram, Vajpayee, speaking in Srinagar in April 2003, offered a "hand of friendship" to Pakistan, and in November 2003 the two sides announced a ceasefire along the LoC, the international border, and the Siachen Glacier, which, in practice, produced the quietest LoC in many years. At the January 2004 SAARC summit in Islamabad, Musharraf pledged that territory under Pakistan's control would not be used to support terrorism in any manner, and the two states agreed to a Composite Dialogue on eight subjects, which included Kashmir, Siachen, Sir Creek, the Wular barrage, terrorism, and trade.

The government of Manmohan Singh, which came to power in India in 2004, continued the process, and a back channel developed between Pakistani and Indian envoys, notably Tariq Aziz and Satish Lambah, on a possible framework for Kashmir. Musharraf offered in public statements a series of ideas: demilitarization, self-governance for both parts of Kashmir, soft borders, and a joint mechanism for management, sometimes called the four-point formula. Singh expressed the view that borders could not be redrawn but could be made "irrelevant." Pakistan's former foreign minister Khurshid Kasuri has stated that the two sides came close to an understanding by 2007; Indian officials have been more cautious, and no agreed text was published. The domestic political crisis that engulfed Musharraf in 2007 (the dismissal of the Chief Justice, the lawyers' movement, and the Red Mosque episode; see Chapter 101) weakened Pakistan's ability to conclude any agreement.

Concrete measures were achieved: bus services between Srinagar and Muzaffarabad (April 2005) and between Poonch and Rawalakot (2006), the first transport across the LoC since 1947; limited trade across the LoC from 2008, in the form of barter exchanges that included Kashmiri goods; the October 2005 earthquake, which killed tens of thousands in Pakistan-administered Kashmir and prompted opening of crossing points for relief; and a gradual increase in visas and exchanges. Confidence-building measures included prior notification of missile tests, agreed in 1999 and reaffirmed later, hotlines between military operations officials, and an agreement on reducing the risk of nuclear accidents in 2007. Attacks nevertheless occurred: the Samjhauta Express bombing in February 2007 killed about sixty-eight people, mostly Pakistani passengers, in an attack later linked by Indian investigators to Hindu extremist activists; militant attacks in Kashmir continued at reduced levels; and the Indian embassy in Kabul was bombed in 2008, an attack India linked to Pakistan.

Why did the process not yield a settlement? Several reasons stand out. The negotiators could discuss formulas, but no side could accept anything that appeared to cede its claims, especially since the Indian parliament's 1994 resolution, Pakistani public opinion, and the Hurriyat's differing positions limited flexibility. In Pakistan, the army retained control of policy on India and Kashmir, so that agreements depended on the military leader's own political position. In India, the coalition government had little capacity to risk public opposition. Spoilers, whether militant groups or political factions, could and did disrupt the process, and the incident that finally ended it, the Mumbai attacks of November 2008, came from precisely that quarter. Finally, the formulas under discussion left out many Kashmiris, who participated mainly through the separatist leadership's limited contacts with Delhi and Islamabad. The Composite Dialogue created genuine opportunities and showed that the LoC could be quiet; it did not address the structural difference between the sides' positions.

Division III — Terrorism, Military Retaliation, and Escalation, 2008–2025

Section 105.9

Terrorism, Cross-Border Militancy, and the Crisis of Attribution

By the late 2000s the militant landscape that India faced was different from that of 1990. The large indigenous insurgency had diminished; the dominant organizations were Pakistan-based groups with ideological commitments broader than Kashmir. The Lashkar-e-Taiba, which Pakistan banned in January 2002 and which reappeared as Jamaat-ud-Dawa with a large charitable and educational network, had expanded its recruitment from Punjab and developed relationships with international jihadist networks; the Jaish-e-Mohammed, which Pakistan also banned, was linked to attacks on Pakistani leaders as well as on India. The state's relationship to these organizations was uneven. Pakistani military leaders had long viewed some militant groups as instruments of policy against India, while others turned against the state: after 2007 the Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan and sectarian groups waged a violent campaign inside Pakistan that killed many thousands (Chapter 101). Pakistani officials have repeatedly cited these losses as evidence that Pakistan is itself a victim of terrorism; Indian officials have replied that the army distinguished between groups that threatened Pakistan and groups useful against India.

The problem of attribution lies at the center of every India–Pakistan crisis. After a mass-casualty attack, the target state must decide whether to hold the organization, the host state, or both responsible, and on what evidence. Sources of evidence include intercepted communications, forensic examination, the testimony of captured attackers, and, occasionally, claims of responsibility by the organizations themselves. Each has limits. Claims may be false, or retracted, as happened when the Resistance Front claimed and then reportedly disavowed responsibility for Pahalgam. Intelligence cannot normally be disclosed. Pakistani authorities typically ask for evidence, deny responsibility, and propose an independent or joint investigation; Indian authorities typically regard such offers as delaying tactics. In judicial terms, Pakistan has prosecuted some individuals, for example Hafiz Saeed, who was convicted of terror financing in 2020 and again in 2022, and Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi, but trials for the Mumbai attacks have progressed slowly, and Indian officials have judged them inadequate.

Attribution can also run the other way. Pakistan has accused India of supporting separatist violence in Balochistan and anti-state militants, citing the 2016 arrest of Kulbhushan Jadhav, an Indian former naval officer who was convicted by a Pakistani military court (the International Court of Justice in 2019 ordered review and reconsideration of the conviction), and of involvement in attacks such as the March 2025 hijacking of the Jaffar Express. India has rejected these claims. They illustrate the symmetrical suspicion that characterizes the relationship, and they warn students against treating any single government's accusation as a finding of fact.

Responsibility for violence was not identical across cases. Attacks such as Mumbai in 2008 are supported by extensive judicial and forensic evidence of Lashkar-e-Taiba's planning from Pakistan. Others, such as the 2019 Pulwama bombing, were claimed by the JeM, whose leader's organization operated openly in Pakistan until banned and was listed by the UN in 2019 after China ended its objections. Others still were carried out by local or small cells that had limited connection to formal organizations, as in several attacks in Kashmir in the 2020s. A historian should therefore be skeptical of explanations that treat all violence as the work of a single Pakistan-directed apparatus, as well as of explanations that attribute violence solely to local grievance. The political consequences of an attack, however, did not depend on legal certainty; after the 2000s, the central political question in India was whether a government that failed to respond forcefully would survive, and in Pakistan whether acceptance of Indian demands would be acceptable to the army and public. Terrorist organizations, aware of this dynamic, could in effect provoke crises.

Section 105.10

The Mumbai Attacks of November 2008

On the night of November 26, 2008, ten men arrived by sea in Mumbai, India's financial capital, having traveled from Karachi in a ship from which they transferred to a hijacked Indian fishing trawler, killing its crew, and then to inflatable boats. They split into pairs and attacked the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus railway station, the Leopold Café, the Taj Mahal Palace and Oberoi-Trident hotels, a hospital, and Nariman House, a Jewish center. The attacks lasted nearly sixty hours; they were broadcast live on television, and they targeted foreigners and Indians alike. At least 166 people were killed, excluding attackers, among them guests and staff at the hotels, railway passengers, police officers including senior anti-terrorism officials such as Hemant Karkare, and members of the National Security Guard who stormed the buildings. Nine of the attackers were killed; one, Ajmal Kasab, was captured, tried, and hanged in November 2012.

The investigation revealed an operation planned with care. The attackers carried satellite phones and communicated with handlers in Pakistan during the assault; intercepted calls, GPS equipment, and Kasab's confessions pointed to the Lashkar-e-Taiba. An American citizen, David Coleman Headley (Daood Gilani), who had worked with LeT and with an officer of the ISI, conducted reconnaissance in Mumbai before the attacks and was later convicted in the United States. Pakistan, after initial denials, acknowledged in early 2009 that parts of the plot had been prepared on its territory and arrested several suspects, including Lakhvi; but court proceedings in Rawalpindi were repeatedly delayed. Tahawwur Rana, a Pakistan-born Canadian businessman convicted in the United States for providing support, was extradited to India in April 2025 to face trial. India has repeatedly asserted the involvement of Pakistani state officials; the extent of that involvement beyond individual officers has not been publicly established in a court.

The response in India was a combination of public anger, an overhaul of security institutions (the creation of the National Investigation Agency, a coastal security scheme, a restructured counterterrorism network), and diplomatic pressure on Pakistan. India did not launch military strikes. Indian officials, including later memoirs by senior officials, have said that options were considered and rejected because of doubts about military effectiveness, the risk of escalation, and the desire to maintain international sympathy. This restraint became the object of political criticism, notably from the opposition Bharatiya Janata Party, which characterized it as weakness and later contrasted it with its own policy after 2014. The Composite Dialogue was suspended. Though a summit at Sharm el-Sheikh in July 2009 revived talks in principle, the sequence of meetings that followed, including those in Thimphu in 2010 and the resumption of a dialogue in 2011–2012, failed to produce progress, and incidents such as the January 2013 killing of Indian soldiers on the LoC, one of whom was reportedly beheaded, soured relations again.

Mumbai became a turning point in Indian security policy for several reasons. It showed that a small group with maritime access could paralyze a city of twenty million. It was seen as the clearest example of cross-border terrorism directed by a Pakistan-based organization against urban, civilian, and international targets. It marked the end of the optimism of the Composite Dialogue. And it created a political expectation that future major attacks would require visible retaliation: the question of what form that retaliation might take, if conventional war was precluded, would shape Indian strategic thinking for the next decade, culminating in the cross-border strikes of 2016, 2019, and 2025.

Section 105.11

Uri, Pulwama, Balakot, and Changing Military Responses, 2016–2019

After the Bharatiya Janata Party under Narendra Modi won a majority in 2014 (Chapter 100), the Indian government adopted a more assertive declared posture toward Pakistan. Early overtures, including Modi's invitation to Nawaz Sharif to his inauguration in 2014 and a brief stop in Lahore in December 2015, were followed by attacks on an Air Force base at Pathankot in January 2016, which India attributed to the Jaish-e-Mohammed. Then the killing of Burhan Wani on July 8, 2016, produced months of unrest in the Valley, in which more than ninety civilians died according to most reports and thousands of people, including many with pellet injuries, were hurt.

On September 18, 2016, four militants attacked an Indian Army brigade headquarters at Uri, close to the LoC, killing nineteen soldiers (the final count). India attributed it to the Jaish-e-Mohammed. Eleven days later, on September 29, the Indian Director General of Military Operations announced that Indian special forces had conducted "surgical strikes" on terrorist launch pads across the LoC, causing significant casualties among militants and their supporters. Pakistan denied that any such strikes had occurred, said that two soldiers had been killed in cross-border firing, and invited journalists to LoC areas. Independent verification was not possible, and Indian opposition parties and some analysts questioned the claim and the evidence, which was released only partially. The strikes were significant less for their military effect than for the doctrinal message: an Indian government publicly announced a cross-LoC operation and thereby dropped the policy of silence that had characterized earlier cross-border actions.

The next crisis was more serious. On February 14, 2019, a young Kashmiri man, Adil Ahmad Dar, drove a car with explosives into a convoy of the Central Reserve Police Force near Pulwama, killing forty personnel. The Jaish-e-Mohammed claimed responsibility. On February 26, Indian Air Force Mirage 2000 aircraft struck what India said was a major Jaish training camp near Balakot, in Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province, well outside Kashmir, the first Indian use of airpower across the international border in a non-war context since 1971. India's foreign secretary described it as a "non-military preemptive" strike, and Indian officials initially claimed that a large number of militants had been killed, a claim for which no public evidence was produced; Pakistan said the strike hit empty land, and independent analyses of satellite imagery did not corroborate large-scale damage.

On February 27, Pakistan Air Force aircraft attacked sites near the LoC in Jammu and Kashmir, an operation Pakistan called Swift Retort, and an air engagement followed in which an Indian MiG-21 flown by Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman was shot down; he ejected over Pakistani territory and was captured, after reportedly bringing down a Pakistani aircraft, a claim contested by Pakistan, with later reporting inconclusive. India also lost a helicopter, shot down by its own air-defense missile. Reports at the time indicated that Indian leaders were prepared to use missiles, and that Pakistan's National Command Authority was convened; both claims are uncertain. Abhinandan was returned on March 1 through the Wagah crossing, described by Prime Minister Imran Khan as a gesture of peace. American, Saudi, and other diplomacy intervened. Pakistan closed its airspace until July 2019.

The crisis of 2019 demonstrated several features of the changing pattern: India's willingness to strike across the international border; Pakistan's willingness to respond symmetrically, within a day; a rapid de-escalation partly produced by an accident of capture and the quick return of the prisoner; and the role of domestic politics, since the strike occurred a few weeks before India's general election, in which Modi's party won a larger majority. Indian officials have argued that the strikes re-established deterrence by showing that nuclear weapons did not preclude conventional retaliation. Pakistani officials have said that their response showed that deterrence still worked. The relevant question for historians is whether each side learned to escalate more or to avoid escalation: the events of 2025 suggest that both lessons were drawn.

Section 105.12

The Pahalgam Attack and the India–Pakistan Military Confrontation of 2025

Chapter 100 described India's political context in 2025: a third-term Modi government that depended on coalition partners, and a Kashmir that, after 2019, the government presented as normalized, with record tourist numbers. Chapter 101 described Pakistan's political context: a hybrid system in which the army, under General Asim Munir, was dominant, and in which former prime minister Imran Khan was imprisoned. Within this setting, on April 22, 2025, gunmen attacked tourists in the Baisaran meadow near Pahalgam. Survivor accounts reported by Indian media said that attackers questioned some victims about their religion before shooting; twenty-five tourists and a local pony operator, Syed Adil Hussain Shah, who was reported to have tried to resist, were killed. It was the deadliest attack on civilians in Jammu and Kashmir in years. The Resistance Front first claimed responsibility, and later reportedly distanced itself from the claim. Indian authorities attributed the attack to Lashkar-e-Taiba and to Pakistani sponsorship; Pakistan rejected the allegation, called for a neutral investigation, and said India had offered no evidence. Indian authorities later said that three men implicated in the attack were killed in an operation in July 2025.

On April 23, India's Cabinet Committee on Security announced a series of measures: the Indus Waters Treaty was to be held "in abeyance" until Pakistan "credibly and irrevocably" ended support for cross-border terrorism; the Attari integrated checkpost was closed; visas for Pakistanis were canceled; defense attachés were declared persona non grata and the High Commissions were reduced. Pakistan responded on April 24 by closing its airspace to Indian aircraft, suspending trade, and stating that any diversion of its water would be regarded as an act of war; it also said it might suspend bilateral agreements including the Simla Agreement. Troops exchanged fire across the LoC from the night of April 24–25. Both governments conducted missile tests and exercises; Pakistan's leaders warned that an Indian attack was imminent. Indian leaders, in public statements, promised that those responsible would be pursued "to the ends of the earth," and Prime Minister Modi indicated that the armed forces had been given operational freedom.

On the night of May 6–7, India launched Operation Sindoor, striking nine sites that it described as terrorist infrastructure of the Jaish-e-Mohammed and Lashkar-e-Taiba, including locations in Bahawalpur, Muridke, Muzaffarabad, Kotli, Bhimber, and Sialkot, in Pakistan and in Pakistan-administered Kashmir; India stressed that no military or civilian infrastructure was targeted. Pakistan described the strikes as an act of war, said that they hit mosques and civilian areas, and reported dozens of civilian deaths (Pakistani official figures cited about forty civilians killed and more than a hundred injured). Pakistani forces responded with heavy shelling in Poonch and other sectors, killing Indian civilians, and an air engagement occurred on May 7, in which Pakistan claimed to have shot down several Indian aircraft, including Rafales. India's Chief of Defence Staff acknowledged in late May that India had suffered aircraft losses, without giving numbers, and later Indian officials claimed to have destroyed several Pakistani aircraft; Pakistan has denied that claim. Independent verification of aircraft losses is incomplete.

Over May 8 and 9, Pakistan launched large numbers of drones at Indian cities and military sites (Indian officials said hundreds), and India reportedly struck air-defense installations around Lahore and elsewhere. In the early hours of May 10, India struck a series of Pakistani air bases, among them Nur Khan near Rawalpindi, Rafiqui, Murid, Sargodha, Sukkur, Bholari, and Jacobabad (Indian officials said eleven air bases), with air-launched and ground-launched missiles; Pakistan responded with what it called Operation Bunyan-um-Marsoos, using missiles and drones against Indian bases. The strikes on the Nur Khan base, close to the headquarters of Pakistan's Strategic Plans Division, which supervises its nuclear forces, drew particular attention; Pakistani officials denied that the National Command Authority had been convened, while some Indian officials described Pakistani nuclear rhetoric as bluff. Whether the strike represented an intentional signal or incidental targeting is disputed.

A ceasefire was agreed on May 10. The sequence of announcement remains contested. President Donald Trump publicly announced a "full and immediate ceasefire" before either government, and the Secretary of State and Vice President stated that the United States had helped; Saudi Arabia, the United Kingdom, Iran, and others also engaged. India's government stated that the arrangement was reached directly between the Directors General of Military Operations on their hotline, at Pakistan's request, and that mediation had not occurred on Kashmir or trade, a position repeated by Indian military leaders in 2026. Pakistan credited American mediation and thanked Trump, who repeatedly claimed credit and said that trade pressure had ended the war. Evidence on this dispute is inconclusive: official Indian accounts, Pakistani accounts, and American public statements differ in emphasis, and both sides probably had reasons to stop, among them the risk of further escalation and the strategic costs of continued strikes. Hours after the ceasefire, each accused the other of violations, but it largely held.

Casualties and outcomes were contested. Pakistani figures included roughly forty civilians killed by Indian strikes and several military personnel; Indian figures included civilians killed in cross-border shelling, concentrated in Poonch, and several service members. India claimed to have killed over a hundred militants and to have damaged airbases and radar; Pakistan claimed to have shot down Indian aircraft and to have struck Indian bases. Each side declared victory, and Pakistan promoted Munir to Field Marshal on May 20. Prime Minister Modi described Operation Sindoor as "paused" and stated that India would treat any future terror attack as an act of war, make no distinction between terrorists and their sponsors, and not accept "nuclear blackmail." Pakistan said it would respond to any Indian strike. India thus publicly articulated a more assertive deterrent and retaliatory policy, but its declared "new normal" did not establish an automatic operational rule: attribution of a subsequent attack, choice of targets, the risk of escalation, and political decisions would still determine whether force was used. Later episodes must be assessed on their own evidence rather than presumed to follow an invariant script.

The confrontation of May 2025 revealed several features of the risks of conventional escalation between nuclear-armed states. First, the thresholds for force had fallen: strikes across the international border into Punjab, an area far from Kashmir, were conducted at the start rather than as a late escalation. Second, the character of warfare changed: drones, loitering munitions, long-range missiles, and electronic warfare were used heavily, while information operations, much of it unreliable, circulated at speed. Fourth, ambiguity concerning nuclear signals persisted, and strikes near sensitive installations created the potential for misreading. Finally, the speed with which each side declared victory suggests that deterrence may be sustained by political narratives that allow leaders to end a conflict without conceding. India did not announce cross-border retaliation, an outcome that illustrates how the post-2025 doctrine's application depends on attribution to external actors and on political judgment. Division IV — Kashmir's Constitutional Transformation and the Politics of Legitimacy, 2019–2026

Section 105.13

Article 370 and the Historical Foundations of Jammu and Kashmir's Autonomy

Article 370 of the Indian Constitution, adopted in 1949 and placed in a part titled "Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions," limited Parliament's power to legislate for Jammu and Kashmir to matters specified in the Instrument of Accession (defense, external affairs, communications) and to others with the concurrence of the state government. Its drafting reflected the circumstances of the state's accession, which had been conditioned on a promise by Indian leaders that the people would decide the state's future constitution through a Constituent Assembly. The article provided, in clause 3, that the President could declare it inoperative on the recommendation of that Constituent Assembly. The Assembly met from 1951 to 1957, adopted the state's own constitution, and dissolved without recommending abrogation or amendment of the article.

Interpretations of what this meant have long diverged. Kashmiri autonomists, and many constitutional scholars, argued that the article represented a compact: the state had acceded on limited terms, and further integration required the consent of its representatives. Indian nationalists argued that the article was explicitly temporary, that it had hindered integration and development, that it discriminated against women and other residents, and that it provided a legal basis for separatism. Both arguments rest on historical claims that can be partly assessed. The word "temporary" appears in the heading and was understood by some of the framers as meaning a transitional arrangement; the Constituent Assembly's dissolution without recommendation left the legal status of clause 3 open to dispute, as the Supreme Court would later address.

Article 35A, inserted through a Presidential Order in 1954 following the Delhi Agreement of 1952, empowered the state legislature to define "permanent residents" and to confer on them exclusive rights concerning government employment, land ownership, and scholarships. It protected the state's demographic character, a concern shared across much of the Himalayan region (and in other states with similar protections), but critics argued that it discriminated against women who married outside the state, against refugees from West Pakistan who had settled in Jammu after 1947, and against Dalit communities such as the Valmikis who had been brought to the state as sanitation workers and were denied residence rights.

The practical autonomy of the state was progressively reduced. After 1953, and especially through the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 1954 and subsequent orders, most provisions of the Indian Constitution, and Union jurisdiction over most subjects in the Union list, were extended to the state; the offices of Sadr-i-Riyasat and Prime Minister were renamed Governor and Chief Minister in 1965; the Election Commission, the Supreme Court, and the Comptroller and Auditor-General acquired jurisdiction. Central governments dismissed state governments or imposed President's Rule repeatedly. By the 1990s, Article 370 had become, in the characterization of an Indian politician, a "tunnel" through which central laws passed, while the symbolic and residual protections remained. The National Conference's autonomy resolution of 2000, adopted by the state assembly, called for restoration of the pre-1953 position and was rejected by the Vajpayee government.

These provisions remained politically significant because they represented different things to different groups. For autonomists and many Kashmiris, they were the evidence of a legal promise and the minimum guarantee of identity. For separatists, they were a limited concession insufficient to address the question of self-determination. For the Bharatiya Janata Party and its ideological predecessor, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, whose founder Syama Prasad Mukherjee died in detention in Kashmir in 1953 while campaigning against the state's special status, the removal of Article 370 was an enduring commitment, included in electoral manifestos for decades. For residents of Jammu and Ladakh, perspectives varied: many in Jammu and some Ladakhis supported abrogation, while Kargil's Shia community and parts of the Valley opposed it.

Section 105.14

The Constitutional Changes of August 2019

After the 2019 general election, in which the Bharatiya Janata Party won its second majority, the government moved rapidly. In late July and early August, it deployed tens of thousands of additional troops to the state, canceled the Amarnath pilgrimage and told tourists and students to leave, citing a terror threat. On August 4, mobile phone networks, landlines, and the internet were shut off, and political leaders, including former chief ministers Omar Abdullah and Mehbooba Mufti, as well as hundreds of other politicians, activists, and lawyers, were detained or placed under house arrest. On August 5, Home Minister Amit Shah introduced in the Rajya Sabha a Presidential Order, which extended all provisions of the Indian Constitution to Jammu and Kashmir and amended Article 367 so that references to the state's "Constituent Assembly" would be read as references to its "Legislative Assembly." Because the state was under President's Rule at the time, Parliament exercised the legislature's powers. On the same day Parliament recommended that Article 370 cease to operate, and on August 6 a second Presidential Order declared its clauses inoperative except the one that applied the Constitution to the state; Article 35A was thereby removed. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, passed in both houses, took effect on October 31, 2019 and divided the state into two Union Territories: Jammu and Kashmir, with a legislature, and Ladakh, without one.

The government's stated rationale was threefold. Security: the existence of special status, it argued, had encouraged separatism and terrorism, and its removal would allow integration. Development: the arrangement had impeded investment and perpetuated dynastic politics, and removal would bring prosperity and rights to excluded groups, including women, refugees, and Dalits. National unity: Article 370 had been a temporary provision and a political anomaly. Critics offered objections of procedure and of principle. Procedurally, they argued that a state's status was changed without the consent of its elected representatives, by reinterpreting an article to bypass its own safeguards, and by downgrading a state, a step they claimed was not allowed by the Constitution's federal design. On human rights, they pointed to the communications blackout, which lasted months and, for high-speed mobile internet, until February 2021, the detentions, including detentions under the Public Safety Act, and the effect on hospitals, students, and businesses. The Supreme Court heard challenges to the shutdown and in January 2020 held that indefinite suspension of the internet was impermissible, but did not order immediate restoration.

Pakistan responded by downgrading diplomatic relations, expelling the Indian high commissioner, suspending bilateral trade, and suspending the Samjhauta and Thar Express trains; it brought the issue to the United Nations Security Council, which held closed consultations but took no decision. China criticized the creation of Ladakh as a Union Territory, since it included Aksai Chin in India's claim. The United States and other states generally described the matter as an internal Indian affair while expressing concern about restrictions. Subsequent measures extended the changes: new domicile rules in 2020 provided residency to many who had lived in the region for fifteen years or more, and land laws were amended to allow purchase by non-residents; a delimitation commission redrew constituencies in 2022, increasing the number of assembly seats from 83 to 90, with an additional six for Jammu and one for Kashmir, so that Jammu division holds 43 seats and Kashmir 47, and reserving nine seats for Scheduled Tribes for the first time. Critics argued that delimitation favored Jammu, where population is smaller; supporters argued that it corrected earlier imbalance.

Section 105.15

The Supreme Court, the 2024 Elections, and the Question of Statehood

The Supreme Court's judgment of December 11, 2023, discussed in the preceding section, came after four years of delays. A Constitution Bench headed by Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud unanimously upheld the revocation of Article 370's special status. Its opinions held that Jammu and Kashmir had not retained a residual sovereignty on accession, that the Constituent Assembly's dissolution did not prevent the President from acting under clause 3, and that Article 370 was a temporary constitutional provision. The Court nevertheless drew a crucial distinction within the 2019 process: it held that the attempted substitution of "Legislative Assembly" for "Constituent Assembly" through Article 367 in paragraph 2 of C.O. 272 was beyond the President's power under Article 370(1)(d). An interpretive clause could not be used to amend Article 370 indirectly. The Court separately sustained the application of the Constitution through the valid part of C.O. 272 and upheld C.O. 273 under Article 370(3), concluding that the President could act without a recommendation from the dissolved Constituent Assembly. The result was approval of the abrogation, not wholesale approval of every legal step by which the government had sought to achieve it. Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul's separate opinion recommended a truth and reconciliation commission to examine violations by state and non-state actors since the 1980s. The court did not decide on the legality of reducing a state to a Union Territory, noting the government's statement that it was temporary, but upheld the creation of Ladakh as a Union Territory, and directed that statehood be restored "at the earliest."

Elections followed in September and October 2024, the first assembly elections in the region since 2014. Voting took place in three phases between September 18 and October 1, and results, declared on October 8, gave the National Conference 42 of the 90 elected seats, the Bharatiya Janata Party 29, the Indian National Congress 6, and the People's Democratic Party 3, with the remainder won by independents and smaller parties. The BJP's seats came almost entirely from Jammu, while the National Conference, in alliance with Congress, won most seats in the Valley. Omar Abdullah took office as Chief Minister on October 16, 2024. Participation was higher than in the last several parliamentary elections, and many observers saw it as a reaffirmation of electoral politics, although separatist and some civil-society voices argued that voting for a Union Territory assembly did not constitute endorsement of the 2019 changes. In November 2024 the new assembly passed a resolution calling for the restoration of special status, which the Indian government and the BJP opposed, and a later resolution, in late September 2026, called for the immediate restoration of full statehood, from which BJP legislators walked out.

The distinction between electoral restoration and constitutional restoration is critical. The elected government of the Union Territory operates under the Reorganisation Act, as amended, and under rules of business that retain broad powers for the Lieutenant Governor, an appointee of the central government. The Lieutenant Governor controls police, public order, the administration of All India Services officers, and certain other subjects; the elected government's powers over these are limited, and the Lieutenant Governor can nominate five members to the assembly, a provision that has been contested. The Chief Minister has publicly complained about the limitations and about delays; supporters of the arrangement say that security requires central control until normalcy is confirmed. Restoration of statehood would give the elected government control over many of these matters, though not restore Article 370 or the pre-2019 autonomy, which the Supreme Court judgment made legally difficult.

As of November 20, 2026, statehood had not been restored. The central government has repeatedly stated that statehood will be restored at an "appropriate time," and Union ministers have indicated that it might happen soon, while the National Conference, the Congress, and others have protested delay, and the Chief Minister has asked when the "right time" will arrive. The status of Ladakh is also unsettled: Ladakhi groups, including the Leh Apex Body and the Kargil Democratic Alliance, have demanded statehood or protections under the Constitution's Sixth Schedule, and reported protests in Leh in September 2025 led to deaths and the detention of the activist Sonam Wangchuk. These developments should be treated as ongoing; their outcomes cannot yet be known.

Section 105.16

Kashmir after 2019: Society, Security, and Competing Futures

Assessing the effects of the 2019 changes is difficult because the evidence is contested and measured differently. Indian official data report a sharp decline in militant incidents, in stone-throwing, and in civilian and security-force fatalities. Non-governmental trackers such as the South Asia Terrorism Portal record fatalities falling from several thousand annually in the early 2000s to a few hundred in the late 2010s and lower levels in the 2020s, although a series of attacks on non-local workers and minority members in 2021–2022, attacks in Jammu's Rajouri and Poonch districts in 2023–2024, and the Pahalgam massacre show that violence persisted. Tourism rose strongly in 2022–2024 according to official figures, and dropped sharply after Pahalgam; many Kashmiri workers who depend on tourism lost income. The government has pointed to infrastructure projects, such as the Chenab rail bridge and railway links to the Valley, and to investment commitments, which have been slower than announced.

Civil liberties remain a concern. Journalists, human-rights defenders, and political figures have been detained or prosecuted under anti-terror and public-safety laws; Yasin Malik was sentenced to life imprisonment in 2022 on terror-financing charges; the leading separatist, Syed Ali Shah Geelani, died in 2021 under house arrest; the Hurriyat factions have largely ceased to function openly; and bans have been placed on organizations, including Jamaat-e-Islami. Media freedom in the region has been constrained by official advertising policy, press accreditation rules, and episodes of surveillance, according to press-freedom organizations; the government says that its actions reflect the rule of law. Residence and land rules, and the entry of non-local employees, have created fears among many Kashmiris of demographic change, comparable to the debate on Himalayan land protections, while Jammu's Hindu groups and refugee communities have gained rights they had long sought. Employment, particularly for educated youth, remains a major problem; unemployment rates are among the country's highest for graduates.

The crisis of 2025 had immediate effects. Shelling in Poonch killed civilians, led to the evacuation of border villages, and destroyed homes and a religious school; the Pahalgam attack prompted a large-scale search operation and reported arrests, and in the weeks after it local authorities demolished houses of families of alleged militants, a practice that critics called collective punishment. Kashmiris outside the region faced harassment in some Indian cities; and Kashmiri political parties condemned the Pahalgam attack, and many local people demonstrated against it, indicating that attacks on tourists did not represent popular sentiment. The tourism economy was hit hard, and its recovery was slow.

On Pakistan's side of the LoC, Azad Jammu and Kashmir experienced shelling in 2025 and political agitation over economic issues, and Gilgit-Baltistan's constitutional status remained unresolved. Pakistani authorities and political leaders continue to describe India's actions of 2019 as illegal and to describe Kashmir as the central unresolved question, and to raise it at international forums.

Division V — China, Nuclear Deterrence, and the Regional Security Order, 1990–2026

Section 105.17

China, the Himalayan Frontier, and the Line of Actual Control

The India–China boundary differs in kind from the India–Pakistan Line of Control. The LoC was drawn on a map by military representatives in 1949 and adjusted in 1972, and its alignment is not itself disputed, although the status of the territory it separates is. The India–China frontier has never been agreed. It consists of three sectors: the western sector in Ladakh, where India claims Aksai Chin, held by China since the 1950s; the middle sector along Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand; and the eastern sector, where China claims most of Arunachal Pradesh, which it calls part of southern Tibet, and which India administers up to the McMahon Line drawn at the Simla Conference of 1914. The war of 1962 (Chapter 92) ended with Chinese advances in both sectors and a unilateral Chinese withdrawal in the east. The term Line of Actual Control, used since the late 1950s, designates the limits of each side's physical control; it has not been exchanged on maps in most sectors, and perceptions of its alignment differ at several points, which is why patrols can meet and clash without either having crossed the other's "border."

For three decades after 1988, India and China managed their dispute through agreements. The 1993 Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity along the LAC, the 1996 agreement on confidence-building measures in the military field, which includes a ban on the use of firearms and explosives within two kilometers of the LAC, and the 2005 Agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles, which stated that a settlement should take into account each side's settled populations, and the 2013 Border Defence Cooperation Agreement formed a framework. Special Representatives of the two governments met regularly. Confrontations nevertheless occurred, at Depsang in 2013, Chumar in 2014, and, most seriously, at Doklam in 2017, where Indian troops entered territory claimed by Bhutan to stop Chinese road construction near the strategic Siliguri corridor; the 73-day standoff ended with mutual withdrawal in August 2017.

The crisis of 2020 was more severe. From April–May 2020, Chinese forces moved into areas in eastern Ladakh that India considered its side of the LAC, including the Galwan valley, the north bank of Pangong Tso, Gogra–Hot Springs, and approaches to Depsang. On June 15, 2020, a clash at Galwan, fought with clubs, stones, and improvised weapons, in line with the 1996 ban on firearms, killed twenty Indian soldiers; China acknowledged four fatalities in February 2021, though other estimates are higher. It was the first deadly India–China clash since 1975. Both sides deployed tens of thousands of additional troops, with heavy equipment, to the high plateau. India banned Chinese applications and restricted Chinese investment; trade nonetheless remained large. Corps-commander talks led to disengagement at some points in 2021–2022, with the creation of buffer zones in which patrols were suspended, but without restoring the pre-2020 situation at others.

In October 2024, the two governments announced an arrangement for patrolling and grazing in Depsang and Demchok, the last unresolved friction points, which both described as a step toward disengagement. Prime Minister Modi and President Xi Jinping met in Kazan on October 23, 2024; the Special Representatives met in Delhi in December 2024 and again in August 2025, and Modi visited China for a summit of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation in Tianjin at the end of August 2025. Direct flights and pilgrimages to Kailash-Mansarovar were resumed. On August 25, 2026, the 25th round of Special Representatives talks was held in Beijing between National Security Adviser Ajit Doval and Foreign Minister Wang Yi, which agreed to continue negotiations on a framework for settlement of the boundary question on the basis of the 2005 parameters, to set up an expert group on boundary delimitation and a working group on border management, to add military hotlines in the eastern and middle sectors and new meeting points for commanders, and to hold the next round in India in 2027, according to official Chinese and Indian statements.

These arrangements should not be described as a final border settlement. They manage the situation but do not agree a boundary; they do not demobilize the forces that were deployed in 2020; and China's naming of places in Arunachal Pradesh, which India rejects, and its construction of infrastructure and dual-use villages along the frontier, illustrate continuing contestation. For Indian planners, the lesson of Galwan was that the earlier assumption that agreements would hold had been wrong; for Chinese planners, it was that India's infrastructure building and the Union Territory status of Ladakh constituted changes to the status quo. Both sides have an interest in managing a costly confrontation, particularly as their economic ties and the broader international environment shifted after 2024. Students should note the contrast with Kashmir: here two large states negotiate bilaterally through institutions, while the people of the frontier, including Tibetans and Himalayan communities, are not parties to the talks.

Historical Vocabulary
Line of Control and Line of Actual Control

The Line of Control separates Indian- and Pakistan-administered Kashmir and was agreed in 1972 on the basis of a 1949 ceasefire line; it is mapped and, in most places, marked on the ground. The Line of Actual Control separates Indian- and Chinese-held areas, was never jointly delineated, and is perceived differently by the two sides. Because the names resemble each other, they are often confused; but the first is a line within a dispute over sovereignty that both states recognize as a line of administration, while the second is a limit of control within an unsettled boundary.

Section 105.18

China–Pakistan Strategic Cooperation and India's Two-Front Security Problem

China and Pakistan have maintained close relations since the early 1960s, when Pakistan's alignment with the United States weakened and both found India a rival: China after 1962, Pakistan since 1947. The 1963 boundary agreement ceded the Shaksgam Tract; the Karakoram Highway, completed in 1978, linked Xinjiang and Pakistan across the mountains; China became Pakistan's principal supplier of conventional arms and assisted its missile and nuclear programs, according to United States and other assessments. Pakistan, in turn, has supported China on Xinjiang, Tibet, and Taiwan, and served as an intermediary with the United States in 1971. Pakistani officials describe the relationship as "higher than mountains."

The economic dimension grew with the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), announced in 2015 as part of the Belt and Road Initiative, which involved Chinese financing of roads, energy projects, and the port of Gwadar on the Arabian Sea. Its route passes through Gilgit-Baltistan, which India claims as part of the state of Jammu and Kashmir; India has therefore refused to join the Belt and Road Initiative on grounds of sovereignty. CPEC also provoked problems for Pakistan, including debt concerns, security threats to Chinese personnel from Baloch separatists and other militants, and local grievances in Balochistan and Gilgit-Baltistan. China's tolerance for Pakistan's position was visible diplomatically: for ten years it placed technical holds on a proposal to list Masood Azhar under the UN Security Council's 1267 sanctions regime, lifting them in May 2019 after Pulwama and international pressure.

Military cooperation deepened. China supplies a large majority of Pakistan's major arms imports in recent years, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute's data, including the JF-17 fighter co-produced with Pakistan, J-10C fighters, PL-15 air-to-air missiles, HQ-9 air-defense systems, naval frigates, and submarines. During the May 2025 confrontation, Pakistan used Chinese-made equipment, and its performance was widely discussed. Indian officials, including a deputy chief of the army staff in July 2025, stated that China had provided Pakistan with live inputs during the conflict and that India faced "one border, three adversaries" (China, Pakistan, and Turkey); Chinese authorities have rejected such descriptions. Public evidence on the extent of intelligence or operational support is limited, and students should treat claims about it as assertions that are plausible but incompletely documented.

India's "two-front problem" refers to the possibility of simultaneous military pressure from Pakistan in the west and China in the north, a concern for which Indian military planners have prepared since the 1960s and which became more salient after 2020. It has influenced Indian procurement of aircraft, air defense, and mountain forces, the construction of roads and tunnels in border areas, and the creation of theater commands, a reform under discussion. It has also shaped Indian diplomacy, including closer ties with the United States, Japan, Australia, and other partners in the Quad framework, a subject belonging to Chapter 110.

The relationship must be understood in a balanced way. Pakistan is not a Chinese instrument: its leaders have sought also to restore relations with the United States and to balance dependence on China, as seen in Pakistan's engagement with Washington in 2025, and Pakistani and Chinese interests do not always coincide on militancy, Afghanistan, or debt. China, similarly, has interests in stability with India, as seen in the border arrangements of 2024–2026, trade, and the Special Representatives' process, and has avoided explicit military support in India–Pakistan crises. Conversely, India's tendency to see Beijing and Islamabad as a single adversary can simplify a more complex reality. The outcome is a triangular relationship in which each bilateral dispute affects the others, but is not reducible to them.

Section 105.19

Nuclear Deterrence, Crisis Management, and Regional Security through 2026

At the January 2026 reference date, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute estimated approximately 190 nuclear warheads for India and 170 for Pakistan. These figures are analytical estimates of inventories, not official declarations or counts of operationally deployed warheads, and must not be treated as precise or directly comparable measures of usable capability. India's forces included land-based Agni missiles of increasing range, aircraft, and a growing sea-based component: the nuclear-powered submarine INS Arihant (operational deterrent patrol announced in 2018) and a second, INS Arighaat, commissioned in 2024; it tested a multiple-warhead missile, Agni-5, in March 2024. Pakistan's forces included Ghauri, Shaheen, and Ababeel ballistic missiles, the Babur cruise missile, aircraft, and the short-range Nasr; it had tested a sea-launched cruise missile. Pakistan also announced a new Army Rocket Force Command in August 2025 to organize longer-range conventional rocket and missile strikes; this was not a replacement for its nuclear command and control system. Each has a separate nuclear command structure: India's Nuclear Command Authority, with a Political Council headed by the Prime Minister and an Executive Council headed by the National Security Adviser, and the Strategic Forces Command created in 2003; Pakistan's National Command Authority, established in 2000, supported by the Strategic Plans Division.

The central concepts are credible retaliation, escalation control, and the thresholds for use. India's declared no-first-use policy, with a pledge of "massive" retaliation, has been debated within India, and some strategists and officials have suggested reviews or described the policy as conditional on circumstances; no change has been formally announced. Pakistan's refusal to adopt no first use, and its development of tactical weapons, are meant to offset India's conventional superiority by threatening early escalation. These postures interact dangerously: Indian conventional planning has sought to operate below Pakistan's thresholds, Pakistan's tactical weapons are meant to lower them, and the compressed time between attack and response gives leaders little opportunity to verify intelligence. Added uncertainties include the vulnerability of dual-capable systems, since cruise missiles and aircraft can carry either conventional or nuclear weapons, the potential for cyber and drone attacks on command systems, and the integration of missile defense and long-range precision weapons.

Confidence-building measures offer partial safeguards. India and Pakistan have exchanged lists of nuclear installations every January 1 since 1992 under the 1988 agreement not to attack them; have notified each other of ballistic-missile tests since 2005; have maintained a hotline between Directors General of Military Operations; and signed in 2007 an agreement on reducing the risk from accidents. These measures operated in 2025: the cessation of hostilities was arranged through the DGMO channel. What they lack is a dialogue on doctrine, on the mixing of conventional and nuclear systems, or on strategic restraint, and the political relationship is currently frozen.

Primary Source Window

The India–Pakistan DGMO Joint Statement of February 25, 2021

Source type: Joint statement issued by the military operations chiefs of both armies · Date: February 25, 2021 · Availability: Indian government Press Information Bureau publication and United Nations Peacemaker document collection; see Documentary Sources below.

Context: After an escalation of LoC firing in 2018–2020 that killed civilians and soldiers, the two Directors General of Military Operations held a conversation over the hotline and issued an announcement that was unexpected. It occurred after the 2019 crisis and the Article 370 changes and without any visible political dialogue, and subsequent reporting suggested quiet back-channel contacts, which neither government has confirmed in detail.

Paraphrase of contents (not a quotation): The two officials agreed to observe strictly all agreements and understandings on cessation of firing along the LoC and other sectors from the midnight of February 24–25, 2021; reviewed the situation; agreed to address each other's core issues and concerns that might disturb peace; and agreed to use the hotline and flag meetings to resolve unforeseen situations.

Analysis: The statement is evidence of the role of military channels in risk reduction and of the fact that the 2003 ceasefire understanding, which had frayed, could be revived by agreement. It does not mention Kashmir or terrorism by name, and its wording permitted each side to present it as consistent with its own position. It shows that low-level stability along the LoC was possible without a political settlement. It also shows the limits of such arrangements: the understanding was tested after Pahalgam in 2025 and in subsequent exchanges. Historians cannot determine from the document alone what motivated the decision or what undertakings, if any, were exchanged privately.

The ceasefire of 2021 largely held until April 2025. The cessation of hostilities of May 10, 2025, was less comprehensive. By November 2026, the political relationship remained deeply strained. Pakistan's ban on Indian aircraft in its airspace was extended repeatedly, most recently to November 24, 2026; India's measures of April 2025, including the closure of the Attari crossing and the end of visas, remained generally in place; occasional exchanges of fire along the LoC were reported in 2025–2026 though not on the scale of the earlier war; and Indian forces continued counterinfiltration operations. At the same time, economic indicators showed some resumption of indirect trade, such as the growth of Pakistan's imports from India reported in November 2026, and, on November 20, a Pakistani minister visited India for the first time since the 2025 war, with no bilateral meeting announced. These developments should be treated as provisional.

The Indus Waters Treaty has become a security issue. The treaty of 1960, mediated by the World Bank, allocated the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan, with India permitted limited non-consumptive use and run-of-river hydropower. India's announcement on April 23, 2025, that the treaty would be held "in abeyance" until Pakistan ended support for cross-border terrorism, which has no explicit basis in the treaty's text, was matched by Pakistani statements that any stoppage or diversion of water would be an act of war. India has stopped sharing certain data and has pushed hydropower projects on the western rivers; physical constraints, including limited storage capacity, mean that India's ability to alter flows quickly is limited, as described in Chapter 109. In arbitration proceedings that India has refused to attend, a Court of Arbitration under the treaty issued awards in 2025 and May 2026 and, on August 31, 2026, a unanimous award that the treaty remains in force and that India's abeyance was not justified under the treaty or international law, together with an order of interim measures concerning the Ratle hydroelectric project on the Chenab. India rejected the court as illegally constituted and said that abeyance remains. The Court's finding is a determination in the arbitration proceedings, not an agreed resolution accepted by both governments. The continuing jurisdictional and compliance dispute has security significance because it introduces water, a resource on which tens of millions of Pakistanis depend, into the repertoire of coercion, which may increase the stakes of any future crisis.

Section 105.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The history traced in this chapter began in a political crisis within Kashmir, in the late 1980s, and extended through five crises between nuclear-armed states to a frontier confrontation with a third power. Several interacting processes explain why the pattern repeated. The first is the unresolved territorial dispute, which each state treats as a matter of national identity and legal right, and which has no settlement acceptable to both. The second is domestic political legitimacy: in India, governments of different parties have been judged on their response to terrorism and on their treatment of Kashmir, while in Pakistan, the army's institutional role and its definition of national security have been tied to the Kashmir question. The third is militant violence, which supplied triggers for crises, was sometimes tolerated or supported by one state, and could be calibrated by organizations seeking to disrupt diplomacy. The fourth is conventional military power, which gave each state options and, in India's case, a growing willingness to use them. The fifth is nuclear deterrence, which constrained the scale of war and, perhaps, encouraged lower-level violence. The sixth is diplomacy, which at times, as in 2003–2007, offered genuine opportunities.

Neither militarization nor negotiation permanently resolved the Kashmir conflict. Militarization reduced violence at times, as measured by fatalities, but did not remove its causes, and it produced documented human-rights costs and alienation. Negotiation produced real achievements, including ceasefires, transport links, and trade across the LoC, but foundered when attacks, domestic crises, and incompatible positions intervened. The constitutional change of 2019 resolved a long-standing Indian political demand and altered the legal framework, yet by 2026 the questions of statehood, representation, and the aspirations of Kashmir's different peoples remained open, and the Pakistani position that the dispute is international was unchanged.

Nuclear weapons both constrained and complicated conventional warfare. They appear to have helped keep each crisis from becoming a general war: India did not cross the LoC in 1999, and each crisis ended after limited use of force. But they did not prevent crises, and some of the lessons drawn from them, particularly after 2016, were that limited force is possible under the nuclear threshold and that retaliation is necessary to deter further attacks. The events of 2019 and 2025 suggest that the threshold for conventional force has been lowered, while the speed and technological complexity of modern warfare have shortened decision time. Deterrence has operated, but it has relied on political judgment and external intervention, which cannot be assumed to be available in a future crisis. The history does not support the conclusion either that nuclear weapons guarantee peace or that war is inevitable.

India's relations with Pakistan and China cannot be understood separately. Chinese support for Pakistan's military, the CPEC route through Gilgit-Baltistan, the Chinese presence in Aksai Chin, and the 2020 clashes have contributed to an Indian perception of a combined challenge, while India's improving ties with the United States and its own power have shaped Chinese and Pakistani perceptions. At the same time, the diplomatic process between India and China from 2024 to 2026 shows that two of the three relationships have moved toward managed stability even as the third remained confrontational, which shows that the triangle is not fixed.

The chapter's emphasis on the people of Kashmir should remain central to its conclusion. The Valley's residents, Pandits, Jammu's refugees, Ladakh's Buddhists and Shias, and the inhabitants of Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan have experienced decades of violence, displacement, constraints on rights, and uncertainty. Their preferences are not uniform and have rarely been consulted, and an account that treats them as objects of two states' policy is incomplete. The chapter's evidence also reminds students of the limits of present knowledge: many facts about the crises of 2019 and 2025, from casualty figures to nuclear signaling, remain contested, and the final consequences of the arrangements of 2024–2026 cannot yet be known.

The security environment described here forms part of the larger twenty-first-century transformation of South Asia: a region of rising economic weight, young populations, urban growth, and digital connectivity, but also of unresolved borders and high military expenditure. Whether the region's economies and societies can develop while those disputes persist is a question that Chapter 106 takes up, examining demography, cities, technology, and employment. Chapter 109 returns to the Indus system as an environmental and political resource, and Chapter 110 considers South Asia's place in a changing international order.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the unresolved Kashmir dispute, nuclear weapons, cross-border militancy, conventional military competition, and the India–China frontier shaped South Asian security from 1989 to November 2026. Its central question was why India and Pakistan repeatedly approached major war despite nuclear deterrence, and how China complicated the regional environment.

Division I began with Kashmir itself. The insurgency of 1989–1990 grew from the unresolved status of the state after 1947, the erosion of autonomy, and the disputed election of 1987, and acquired organization and weapons from Pakistan-based networks. Its leadership shifted from the independence-oriented JKLF to pro-Pakistan and transnational groups, whose attacks and the Indian state's heavily militarized response produced documented abuses, mass displacement of Kashmiri Pandits, and decades of civilian hardship. The former princely state comprises regions with different histories and aspirations, administered by India, Pakistan, and China, and no single political preference exists.

Division II traced the nuclear tests of May 1998, which made deterrence overt, and the Kargil War of 1999, the first conventional war between declared nuclear powers. Indian restraint at Kargil and the contested role of nuclear fear illustrate the stability–instability debate. The 2001–2002 mobilization showed the limits of coercion by large conventional forces under nuclear conditions. The ceasefire of 2003 and the Composite Dialogue produced real, if limited, gains in transport, trade, and confidence-building, but no settlement.

Division III followed the problem of attribution and the changing Indian response to terrorism. The Mumbai attacks of 2008 produced restraint but shifted the political climate. Uri and the 2016 strikes, and Pulwama and Balakot in 2019, introduced publicly acknowledged cross-border retaliation and reciprocal Pakistani action. The Pahalgam attack of April 22, 2025 led to the suspension of the Indus Waters Treaty, four days of missile, drone, and air operations beginning on May 7, and a ceasefire on May 10 whose mediation, casualties, and outcomes remain contested. The crisis showed lower thresholds for force, shorter decision times, and persisting nuclear ambiguity.

Division IV examined Article 370 and its erosion, the constitutional changes of August 2019, the Supreme Court's judgment of December 2023, and the assembly elections of 2024, after which an elected government headed by Omar Abdullah took office under a Lieutenant Governor with substantial powers. As of November 2026, statehood had not been restored, and the issue remained politically contested. Evaluations of the effects of 2019 depend on the indicators chosen and on political positions.

Division V turned to China. The India–China frontier, governed by the undemarcated LAC, was the site of the Doklam standoff of 2017 and the deadly Galwan clash of 2020, followed by the disengagement arrangements of October 2024 and further diplomatic progress through the 25th Special Representatives talks of August 25, 2026. These arrangements manage but do not settle the boundary. China–Pakistan cooperation, including arms transfers and CPEC, contributes to India's two-front concern, but each state acts on its own interests.

The chapter also assessed nuclear deterrence and crisis management. Estimated inventories of approximately 190 warheads for India and 170 for Pakistan in January 2026, differing doctrines on first use, short-range and dual-capable systems, and compressed decision times create risks that hotlines and notification agreements only partially address. The Indus Waters Treaty dispute added water to the security agenda.

The synthesis argued that territorial dispute, domestic legitimacy, militant violence, conventional power, nuclear deterrence, and diplomacy interacted so that neither militarization nor negotiation produced a permanent settlement. Nuclear weapons have limited the scale of war but have not prevented crises. India's relations with Pakistan and China are interconnected, though not reducible to one another.

Finally, the chapter has stressed historical caution. Many facts about recent crises are contested, official narratives conflict, and events through November 2026 are unfinished. The transition to Chapter 106 asks how South Asia's economic and social transformation proceeds in this security setting.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regional dispute, once embedded in a nuclear environment and tied to global politics, can be both dangerous and stubbornly durable. The crises described here were not simple repetitions: each began with different circumstances, involved different technologies and leaders, and produced different lessons. Yet the underlying structure of unresolved territory, contested legitimacy, militant violence, and mutual suspicion persisted.

The chapter also reveals how security politics shape the rest of South Asia's history. The Kashmir conflict has affected constitutional politics in India, military influence in Pakistan, the development of the Himalayan frontier, and the lives of ordinary people on both sides of the LoC. Its connection to the wider volume is clear: the nuclear tests of Chapter 97, the political changes of Chapters 99–101, and the water and climate issues of Chapter 109 all intersect with it.

For the six-volume series, the chapter continues a long history of frontiers, from the northwestern passes of the ancient and medieval centuries to the princely-state accessions of 1947, now reinterpreted in an era of missiles and drones. It warns against reading the past as a script: the outcomes of 1999, 2019, or 2025 were not inevitable, and the future is not determined by them.

Chapter 106 now turns from security to the structural forces transforming the region: demography, cities, technology, and employment, in a South Asia whose prosperity and stability remain bound to the questions studied here.
