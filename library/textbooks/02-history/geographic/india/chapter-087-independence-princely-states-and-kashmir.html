Independence, Princely States, and Kashmir

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 15 August 1947 two new Dominions came into legal existence, but neither could yet say with certainty where its authority ended. The boundary commissions had reported, the armed forces and civil services were being divided, and flags had been raised in Delhi and Karachi. Yet roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent’s territory lay in princely states whose rulers had not all decided what to do, and the largest of them, Hyderabad, Junagadh, and Jammu and Kashmir, would take months or years to resolve. Independence was a date in a statute. Sovereignty over a territory was something that still had to be made.

Chapter 85 explained how Britain, the Indian National Congress, the Muslim League, and other actors arrived at the decision to transfer power to two Dominions. Chapter 86 examined the territorial division of Punjab and Bengal and the violence, displacement, and loss that accompanied it. This chapter turns to a different but closely connected problem. Once the British Crown withdrew, who would exercise authority over which territories, with what legal title, and by what means? The answer depended on four processes that this chapter keeps analytically distinct: legal accession to a Dominion, administrative integration of a territory into that Dominion’s government, military control of ground and population, and political legitimacy, meaning the degree to which inhabitants and outside observers accepted the resulting arrangement. These processes were connected, but they rarely moved in step.

The princely states, which the British had governed indirectly through treaty relationships for generations, posed the sharpest version of this problem. Their rulers exercised real authority inside their territories, but the Crown had controlled their external relations, defense, and communications through the doctrine of paramountcy. The Indian Independence Act of 1947 ended that arrangement without telling the rulers what should replace it. Most of the states acceded to India or Pakistan within weeks, often after negotiation with Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel’s States Department, its secretary V. P. Menon, and Lord Mountbatten. A small number did not follow that path, and their cases show that incorporation could rest on negotiation, popular movements, economic and political pressure, or force.

Three cases stand at the center of the chapter because they reveal different relationships among a ruler’s choice, a population’s preferences, geography, and military power. Junagadh, a small Kathiawar state with a Muslim ruler and a largely Hindu population, acceded to Pakistan and was absorbed into India within months. Hyderabad, the largest state, sought an independent position, entered a standstill agreement with India, and was invaded in September 1948 in an operation that was followed by serious communal violence. Jammu and Kashmir, ruled by a Hindu Maharaja over a Muslim-majority population, became the scene of an uprising, an armed incursion from Pakistan, a disputed accession to India, a war, and an international dispute that remained unsettled in 1949 and has remained a central problem in South Asian politics since.

This chapter also asks students to treat the people of these territories as historical actors and not as the objects of decisions made in Delhi, Karachi, or London. Peasants in Telangana, the leaders of the Praja Mandals, the members of the Poonch rebellion, the followers of Sheikh Abdullah, the soldiers of the Gilgit Scouts, and the refugees who crossed shifting lines each had interests and political preferences of their own. Much of the evidence about them is incomplete, and much of it was produced by governments with a stake in the outcome. Official figures for votes, casualties, and displacement require particular care, and the chapter notes where they are contested.

The evidence for these events is abundant but unevenly reliable. It includes the text of the Indian Independence Act, instruments of accession and standstill agreements, correspondence among rulers, ministers, and the Viceroy or Governor-General, records of the Indian Defence Committee, United Nations resolutions and commission reports, memoirs by participants such as V. P. Menon and Pakistani and Indian army officers, and later scholarship that has reexamined each of these. Several key episodes, notably the exact sequence of events on 26 and 27 October 1947 and the scale of violence in Hyderabad in 1948, remain subjects of historical dispute. The chapter presents the documents, identifies who produced them and why, and distinguishes what they establish from what later commentary has claimed.

Chapter 87 closes Volume V. It shows how the end of empire produced two functioning but territorially contested states, and how the settlement of some questions, such as the incorporation of Junagadh and Hyderabad into India, coexisted with the failure to settle others. It also prepares the ground for Volume VI, which begins with Chapter 88’s examination of the Indian Constitution and the republic that Constituent Assembly members were designing even as these territorial struggles unfolded, and which returns to Kashmir and regional security in later chapters, especially Chapter 105.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the legal establishment of the two Dominions under the Indian Independence Act of 1947 and distinguish the legal date of 15 August from Pakistan’s observance of 14 August.
  2. Compare the administrative, military, financial, and institutional starting positions of India and Pakistan as successor states.
  3. Analyze the meaning and consequences of the lapse of British paramountcy for the princely states.
  4. Distinguish among an instrument of accession, a standstill agreement, a merger agreement, and full administrative integration.
  5. Assess the roles of Patel, V. P. Menon, Mountbatten, rulers, and popular movements in the incorporation of the states.
  6. Reconstruct the Junagadh crisis and evaluate the evidence for the 1948 referendum.
  7. Explain why Hyderabad’s negotiations with India broke down and analyze the political forces within the state, including the Nizam’s government, the Razakars, the Telangana rebellion, and the State Congress.
  8. Evaluate Operation Polo and the violence that followed, attributing and critically examining estimates of casualties.
  9. Describe the political geography and politics of Jammu and Kashmir under Dogra rule, distinguishing the Valley, Jammu, Poonch, Ladakh, Gilgit, and Baltistan.
  10. Trace the events of October 1947, distinguishing the Poonch uprising, the tribal incursion, the Instrument of Accession, its acceptance, and the Indian military airlift, and explain the documentary disputes surrounding them.
  11. Explain the course of the first India–Pakistan war and the United Nations’ attempts to secure a ceasefire, truce, and plebiscite, distinguishing proposals from implementation and the 1949 ceasefire line from the later Line of Control.
  12. Evaluate why Junagadh and Hyderabad entered the Indian Union while Jammu and Kashmir remained divided and contested, and what this shows about sovereignty, representation, and legitimacy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the legal creation of two Dominions not immediately produce two fully functioning, territorially secure states?
  2. What did paramountcy mean, and why did its lapse create both opportunity and danger for rulers?
  3. In what ways did accession, merger, and integration differ, and why does the distinction matter for understanding what rulers surrendered and when?
  4. How should historians weigh negotiation, inducement, popular pressure, and force in explaining the integration of the princely states?
  5. Whose consent mattered in Junagadh and Hyderabad: the ruler’s, the population’s, or the neighboring Dominion’s?
  6. How did events within Jammu and Kashmir, and not only decisions in Delhi and Karachi, shape the crisis of 1947?
  7. What do the documents establish about the Instrument of Accession and the Indian airlift, and where do historians disagree?
  8. How did the military geography of the Valley, Poonch, Jammu, Ladakh, and the northern areas shape the course of the war?
  9. What did the United Nations propose, what did India and Pakistan accept, and what was actually implemented?
  10. Why did independence settle sovereignty in some territories but leave it contested in others?

Historical Context

British India was never a single territorial state. By the 1940s it consisted of provinces under direct British administration and a large number of princely states under indirect rule. The states varied enormously: Hyderabad was larger than many European countries, while hundreds of others were tiny holdings, some of only a few villages. Estimates of their number differ because they depend on how a “state” is defined; V. P. Menon’s well-known count is about 565, while other compilations are somewhat lower, and this chapter treats the figure as approximate. Together they held roughly two-fifths of the territory of the subcontinent and about a quarter of its population. Rulers governed internally, subject to the supervision of British Residents and Political Agents, while the Crown, through the Viceroy acting as its representative to the states, controlled defense, foreign relations, and certain other matters.

Chapter 85 showed that by 1946 British and Indian leaders had agreed that paramountcy could not simply be handed to a successor government. The Cabinet Mission’s memorandum of May 1946 stated that it would lapse when British rule ended. The Indian Independence Act of July 1947 gave that position statutory form. At the same time, the states were embedded in networks of roads, railways, rivers, ports, and trade that did not respect their boundaries. Most were small, surrounded by territory that would belong to one of the new Dominions, and economically dependent on it. A few were large enough, or located sufficiently far from the likely centers of power, to contemplate independence or a choice of Dominion.

Politics inside the states had been changing. From the 1920s and especially after 1938, organizations known as Praja Mandals or State Peoples’ Conferences demanded responsible government and civil liberties, often with the sympathy of Congress leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru. In several states, including Hyderabad and Jammu and Kashmir, rival movements pursued competing political futures. Historians therefore treat the princely states of 1947 not as inert territories awaiting incorporation but as arenas of their own politics, in which rulers, ministers, landlords, peasants, communal organizations, and nationalist parties all pursued objectives.

The evidence base for this chapter is largely documentary. Official records include the Indian Independence Act, instruments of accession, agreements, correspondence, and United Nations resolutions and reports. Memoirs and retrospective accounts, including Menon’s The Story of the Integration of the Indian States, are valuable but were written by participants who had reasons to present their conduct favorably. Later scholarship, including work on Junagadh, Hyderabad, and Kashmir by historians such as Srinath Raghavan, Mridu Rai, Christopher Snedden, Sumantra Bose, and others, has revised many received narratives, and students should expect to encounter disagreement. Casualty and displacement figures for 1947–1948 are generally estimates, and they must be attributed to a source rather than presented as established fact. The chapter dates events using the common calendar; where documents themselves are dated differently from the events they describe, the difference is noted.

Independence and the Problem of Territorial Sovereignty

How the new Dominions inherited different territorial and constitutional problems, and why the princely states became central to postcolonial state formation.

Section 87.1

Independence in August 1947: Two Dominions and the Transfer of Power

The Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947 and fixed 15 August as the “appointed day” on which two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, would be set up in place of British India. Each Dominion would have its own Governor-General appointed by the Crown, its own legislature, and, for the time being, the Crown as its formal head of state. The Act ended the authority of the British Parliament over the new Dominions and removed the Secretary of State for India and the Indian Office from the structure of government. It did not, however, produce two finished constitutions. Each Constituent Assembly was to act both as a constituent body and as the Dominion’s legislature, and until a new constitution was adopted each Dominion would be governed under the Government of India Act of 1935, adapted for the purpose by the Governor-General. Chapter 88 examines how India moved from that arrangement to its own constitution in 1950, and Chapter 90 how Pakistan’s experience diverged.

The transfer of power occurred in two capitals over two days. In Karachi, Lord Mountbatten, as Viceroy, addressed Pakistan’s Constituent Assembly on 14 August, and the Dominion of Pakistan was inaugurated that day, which is why Pakistan observes 14 August as its Independence Day. Legally, both Dominions came into existence on the appointed day, 15 August, and Muhammad Ali Jinnah took the oath as Pakistan’s first Governor-General on that date. In Delhi, the Constituent Assembly met in the late evening of 14 August and Jawaharlal Nehru delivered his well-known speech on India’s “tryst with destiny” just before midnight. On 15 August Mountbatten was sworn in as the first Governor-General of the Dominion of India, at the request of Indian leaders, while a different arrangement prevailed in Pakistan, where Jinnah held both the governor-generalship and the presidency of the Constituent Assembly. The difference in dates is not a contradiction. It reflects the sequence of ceremonies and the symbolism each government chose to emphasize.

Independence did not immediately create two integrated national territories. The Boundary Commissions’ awards for Punjab and Bengal, discussed in Chapter 86, were announced on 17 August, two days after the Dominions began. Millions of people therefore celebrated independence without knowing on which side of a line their homes would fall. Within the new Dominions, much of the administrative apparatus that had been unified under the Government of India was being divided, and many of its personnel were in transit. The armed forces remained under a joint Supreme Commander, Field Marshal Claude Auchinleck, until the end of November 1947, a temporary arrangement that would later become a point of contention during the Kashmir crisis. British officers continued to serve in senior positions in both armies, and the Crown’s formal status as head of state meant that Britain retained a symbolic and diplomatic presence.

The legal framework therefore established sovereignty in principle but left its practical content to be worked out. The Dominions could legislate, conduct foreign relations, and eventually leave the Commonwealth, but they inherited a fiscal system, a communications network, and a security apparatus that had been designed for a single state. Nor did the Act settle the status of the princely states, which the next sections examine. Independence was best understood as the beginning of a process in which both governments tried to convert legal title into effective authority over territory, institutions, and populations, while contending with unprecedented displacement and violence. Where Chapter 85 asked how Britain’s departure was negotiated and Chapter 86 how Partition was experienced, this chapter asks how sovereignty was constructed after the departure.

Section 87.2

India and Pakistan as Successor States: Government, Military, Territory, and Administration

India and Pakistan began from unequal positions, and the inequality was institutional before it was political. India inherited the capital at Delhi, the central secretariat, most of the central government’s files and offices, the principal ordnance and industrial installations, and the continuing identity of the pre-existing state, so that it was generally treated as the principal successor to British India for purposes of international treaties and membership in international organizations. Pakistan, by contrast, was created from two territories separated by about a thousand miles of Indian territory, and it had to establish a capital at Karachi, new ministries, and many new institutions almost from nothing. Contemporary and later accounts describe shortages of office space, equipment, records, and trained staff in the first months. These conditions affected the capacity of Pakistani officials to handle the refugee crisis and the territorial questions that followed.

The division of assets and personnel was managed through the Partition Council, which allocated cash balances, stores, and other holdings, and through committees that divided the armed forces. The army was divided broadly in a ratio of about two to one in India’s favor, and individual soldiers and officers were given choices, subject to the religious composition of their units and the policies agreed. The process was complicated by the fact that many units were mixed and that very few ordnance factories and depots lay in the territory assigned to Pakistan. Civil servants of the Indian Civil Service and other services opted for one Dominion or the other, and Muslim officials who chose Pakistan, together with a small number of others, became the nucleus of its bureaucracy. A dispute over the division of financial assets, including a sum of Rs 55 crore due to Pakistan, flared in January 1948 and became part of the political context in which Mahatma Gandhi undertook his last fast, discussed in Chapter 86.

The political leadership of the two states reflected their different origins. In India, Nehru became Prime Minister and Vallabhbhai Patel Deputy Prime Minister with responsibility for Home Affairs and for the new States Department, a division of labor that gave Patel direct control of the machinery for dealing with the princely states. Mountbatten remained Governor-General until June 1948, when C. Rajagopalachari succeeded him, and he influenced policy on the states and Kashmir during that period. In Pakistan, Jinnah served as Governor-General and president of the Constituent Assembly, with Liaquat Ali Khan as Prime Minister, and Jinnah’s unique authority meant that for the first year formal constitutional offices and real power overlapped to an unusual degree. Neither government was monolithic, and disagreements within each, including between Nehru and Patel on matters such as Kashmir and Hyderabad, form part of the story told below.

The new states were therefore confronted at once by problems of territorial jurisdiction, fiscal capacity, public order, and administrative continuity. They had to decide how far to rely on British officers and on the existing legal framework, how to settle millions of refugees, how to defend uncertain borders, and how to deal with territories not yet assigned to either. Both governments also had to consider the effect of their decisions on each other. A policy that appeared defensive to one could look aggressive to the other, particularly in contested territories where each government had limited information about the intentions and capacities of its counterpart. This mutual suspicion, already intensified by the violence of Partition, shaped how both governments responded to the princely states.

Section 87.3

The Lapse of British Paramountcy and the Future of the Princely States

Before 1947, the princely states stood in a relationship to the Crown that cannot be described either as full sovereignty or as simple subordination. Treaties, engagements, and customary usage left rulers authority over internal administration, taxation, and justice, while the Crown, acting through the Viceroy and the Political Department, controlled the states’ external relations and defense, regulated succession, could depose rulers, and often intervened in internal matters through Residents. Both the Crown’s paramountcy and the rulers’ internal sovereignty were real. Lawyers and politicians nevertheless differed about how to characterize the relationship, and these differences became important when paramountcy was to end.

Section 7 of the Indian Independence Act provided that the suzerainty of the Crown over the Indian States would lapse on the appointed day, together with the treaties and agreements between the Crown and the rulers and the functions, obligations, and rights that flowed from them. The Act did not transfer these powers to the new Dominions. The practical consequence was that the rulers, in a legal sense, regained the powers the Crown had exercised over their external relations, including the capacity to enter into relations with either Dominion, while also losing the Crown’s protection. Section 7 also allowed for temporary arrangements to continue until new agreements could be made, which is why “standstill” agreements, preserving existing administrative arrangements in matters such as customs, posts, and railways, became common.

Interpretations differed. The Muslim League leadership, and Jinnah in particular, stated in June 1947 that the states were free to remain independent or to choose their association, and that Pakistan would respect their decisions. Congress and the Government of India took the position that the states could not claim independent international status and that geography and the interests of their populations pointed toward accession to the Dominion in which they were located. The British Government, for its part, declined to recognize the states as separate Dominions and urged rulers to accede, while acknowledging that Britain could not compel them to do so. The legal question of what a ruler could do was therefore entangled with the political question of what a ruler could survive doing.

Permanent independence was not a realistic option for most rulers. Most states were small, landlocked within one of the new Dominions, and dependent on its railways, ports, currencies, and markets. Defense was a more serious problem. Only a few states had armies of any significance, and none could resist a determined neighbor. Rulers also faced political movements within their states demanding responsible government and, in many cases, association with India or Pakistan. Some rulers nevertheless explored independence, others attempted to bargain over the terms of accession, and a few tried to play the Dominions against each other. Those cases, rather than the majority that acceded quickly, are the subject of the sections that follow.

Historical Vocabulary
Paramountcy

Paramountcy denoted the supremacy that the British Crown claimed over the Indian princely states. It rested on treaties, usage, and the practical power of the Crown’s representative, and it included control over defense, foreign relations, and communications, as well as authority to intervene in internal affairs where the Crown thought necessary. The related term suzerainty is used in the Indian Independence Act for the Crown’s overlordship.

The concept is easy to misread. Paramountcy was not the same as direct British sovereignty over the states, and its lapse did not amount to a grant of full international sovereignty to rulers. It created a legal vacuum that the rulers, the Dominions, and the Crown tried to fill by negotiation. Students should avoid equating the lapse of paramountcy with an “option of independence,” since the political and economic realities in 1947 made that option available in practice to very few.

Primary Source Window

The Indian Independence Act of 1947, Section 7, and the Lapse of Paramountcy

Source type: Statute of the United Kingdom Parliament · Issuing institution: British Parliament · Date: royal assent 18 July 1947; effective 15 August 1947 · Available: UK legislation archive

Context: The Act implemented the decision, negotiated in the months described in Chapter 85, to transfer power to two Dominions. Its drafters had to decide what to do with the states, whose treaty relations were with the Crown rather than with British India.

Paraphrase of Section 7(1)(b): On the appointed day, the Crown’s suzerainty over the Indian States ends, and with it the treaties and agreements between the Crown and the rulers, the functions the Crown exercised in relation to the states, and the Crown’s obligations toward them. The Act’s text, which should be consulted directly, speaks of suzerainty that “lapses.”

Analysis: The provision is authoritative for the legal position of the Crown and for the fact that no automatic transfer of paramountcy to the Dominions occurred. It does not tell historians what rulers would do, how Dominions would respond, or how populations would react. It is also the product of British parliamentary drafting, not of the rulers or the Dominions. The accompanying political statements by Mountbatten, Patel, Jinnah, and others are separate sources with their own purposes.

Section 87.4

Patel, V. P. Menon, Mountbatten, and the Negotiated Integration of the States

The Government of India answered the question of the states through a combination of persuasion and institutional design. After a 25 June 1947 decision by the Interim Cabinet to establish a States Department, Patel took charge of the new machinery, with V. P. Menon, a senior official who had served as Reforms Commissioner and constitutional adviser to the Viceroy, as its secretary. On 5 July Patel publicly appealed to rulers to accede. Patel brought political authority and a reputation for firmness; Menon brought a detailed grasp of constitutional law and the practical knowledge of how to draft instruments rulers could accept. Mountbatten, whose royal connections and personal relations with many rulers were an asset, was brought into the effort and, as Governor-General, lent it official weight.

The central device was the Instrument of Accession. Under a draft prepared by the government, a ruler would accede to the Dominion in respect of three subjects: defense, external affairs, and communications. In all other respects, the instrument preserved the ruler’s authority, and the states were assured that the Dominion would not demand more. Patel’s public statements emphasized that the arrangement left the rulers’ internal position largely untouched and asked for cooperation rather than subordination. In his address to the Chamber of Princes on 25 July 1947, Mountbatten argued that the states were bound to the Dominion they adjoined by geography and by long-standing economic and cultural ties, and that accession on the three subjects was a limited but necessary step. He also advised the rulers that there were practical limits to what he, as the Crown’s representative, could do for states that did not accede after the Crown’s departure.

The limitation of the initial instrument was not merely a drafting convenience. A state that signed it could remain governed by its ruler's courts, revenue officers, and local laws while surrendering specified responsibilities to the Dominion. This enabled rapid accession without demanding an immediate answer to disputes over representative government, property, or the ruler's future constitutional status. It also created a second negotiation: whether and when those internal powers would be transferred to ministries accountable to the population. Reading an accession document as though it were already a merger agreement erases this interval and overstates the finality of the August 1947 settlement.

By 15 August, according to Menon, nearly every state contiguous to India had signed the instrument or a standstill agreement, with the exceptions of Hyderabad, Junagadh, and Jammu and Kashmir. The pace reflected both the attractiveness of the terms and the leverage the government held. Several of the most celebrated cases illustrate the range of pressures. In Travancore, the dewan Sir C. P. Ramaswami Iyer announced in June 1947 that the state would be independent, but after an attempt on his life in July and political pressure from within the state, the Maharaja acceded on 30 July. The Nawab of Bhopal, Hamidullah Khan, who had been chancellor of the Chamber of Princes and had been close to Mountbatten, resisted for a time and sought to hold open the option of independence, but acceded shortly before independence after negotiation, and Bhopal was merged with India later. The Maharaja of Jodhpur, Hanwant Singh, whose state bordered Pakistan, was approached by Pakistani representatives, but Mountbatten and Menon argued that accession to Pakistan would create communal difficulties in a predominantly Hindu state, and he acceded. Mysore, Baroda, and many Rajputana states also acceded, often after discussions in which Menon and Patel explained that the terms would not be repeated later.

In the territories that lay within or adjoining Pakistan, the Government of Pakistan pursued a parallel process, with a different pattern. Bahawalpur and Khairpur acceded to Pakistan, as did the northwestern states of Swat, Dir, and Chitral. Kalat, in Balochistan, had a distinct treaty history, and its ruler, the Khan, declared the state’s independence at independence, a position Pakistan did not accept; Kalat was brought into Pakistan in March 1948 under circumstances that remain disputed among historians and that Chapter 90 addresses in the context of Pakistan’s state formation. The existence of this parallel case is a reminder that the incorporation of states was a problem for both Dominions, even though India’s states were far more numerous.

The integration of the states cannot be described accurately as entirely voluntary or entirely coercive. Rulers acceded because the alternatives were unattractive, and because they were offered financial guarantees, personal privileges, and assurances regarding their internal position. They also acceded because the government could signal that failure to do so would invite political agitation, administrative disruption, and the withdrawal of cooperation. Popular movements within states, often led by Praja Mandals sympathetic to Congress, strengthened the government’s hand. In exceptional cases, where the state was large, strategically important, or in dispute, the government was prepared to use force. The following sections examine those cases.

Historical Vocabulary
Accession, Merger, and Integration

An Instrument of Accession was a legal document by which a ruler transferred to a Dominion authority over specified subjects, usually defense, external affairs, and communications. A standstill agreement preserved existing administrative arrangements temporarily without transferring those powers. A merger agreement went further: the ruler ceded full authority over the state to the Dominion or agreed to its combination with other states or provinces, typically in exchange for a privy purse. Integration refers to the wider process by which a state’s laws, finances, administration, and political institutions were brought into the Dominion’s structure.

The terms describe stages, not synonyms. A state could be fully “acceded” while retaining its own internal government, as most states did in 1947, and its administrative incorporation could take years. Historians who use “accession” to mean the entire process obscure what rulers actually surrendered and when.

Contested Accessions: Junagadh, Hyderabad, and the Territorial Union

How the two most prominent contested accessions outside Kashmir raised questions of sovereignty, political representation, and state violence.

Section 87.5

Junagadh: Accession to Pakistan, Indian Intervention, and the 1948 Referendum

Junagadh was a state of modest size in Kathiawar, on the Arabian Sea coast of what is now Gujarat. Its population, which official censuses put at several hundred thousand, was predominantly Hindu, while its ruler, Nawab Mahabat Khan III, was Muslim. The state was not contiguous with Pakistan. It was surrounded by territory that would belong to India, and it was linked to Pakistan only by sea, across several hundred miles of the Arabian Sea. It also contained, or was entangled with, a number of smaller subordinate chiefships such as Mangrol and Babariawad, whose rulers claimed a measure of independence from Junagadh and, in August 1947, announced accession to India. Its Dewan from May 1947 was Sir Shah Nawaz Bhutto, a Sindhi politician and the father of the later Pakistani leader Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, whose influence on the Nawab is cited by historians as a significant factor in the state’s decision.

The Nawab decided around the time of independence to accede to Pakistan, and the accession was publicly announced and accepted by Pakistan on 15 September 1947. The Government of India objected at once. Its representatives argued that a state with a Hindu majority and a location entirely within Indian territory could not accede to Pakistan, that Junagadh’s economic and cultural ties lay with Kathiawar, and that the ruler’s decision did not represent the wishes of the people. Pakistan’s government replied that the Indian Independence Act gave rulers the power to accede and that the Nawab had acted within his rights. Each side thus invoked a different principle. India appealed to territorial contiguity and the preferences of the population, and Pakistan to the ruler’s legal authority, the very principle that India invoked soon afterward in Kashmir.

The Indian government did not rely on argument alone. Surrounding Kathiawar states acceded to India or turned to Indian authorities for protection, and Indian forces were placed on Junagadh’s borders. Economic pressure was applied: the state’s railways, postal services, fuel and supplies, which depended on India, were curtailed. In Bombay, on 25 September 1947, a group of Junagadh émigrés and Kathiawari political activists, led by Samaldas Gandhi, proclaimed a provisional government, the Arzi Hukumat, which claimed to represent the people of Junagadh and began to organize volunteers on the state’s borders. Within Junagadh, the Nawab’s administration faced mounting disorder, unrest in the countryside, and the departure of many officials. The Nawab himself left for Karachi in late October, and the Dewan, Bhutto, found himself unable to govern.

On 8 November, Bhutto wrote to the Government of India’s Regional Commissioner at Rajkot, asking the Indian government to take over administration, and Indian troops and officials entered Junagadh on 9 November 1947. The sequence illustrates how the four processes distinguished in this chapter could separate: Pakistan had accepted a legal accession, but it lacked military control of the territory and could do little to maintain it, while India exercised control without having, at that point, obtained a formal accession or a clear expression of popular preference. Pakistan protested that the Indian action was illegal and brought the matter to the attention of the United Nations Security Council as part of its broader complaint against India in 1948.

The Government of India then organized a referendum, held on 20 February 1948, in which the electorate was asked to choose between joining India and Pakistan. Official Indian figures reported that 190,779 votes were cast for India and 91 for Pakistan, out of a reported 190,870 votes cast. These figures are documented, but they must be read carefully. The referendum was conducted under Indian administration, after the Nawab and many Muslim officials and residents had left, and Pakistan did not accept its legitimacy. Historians do not treat the result as a neutral measurement of preferences, though many also note that the state’s Hindu majority, together with the political movement against the Nawab, probably would have produced a large majority for India under many arrangements. Scholarship has emphasized that Junagadh’s incorporation involved more pressure and complexity than conventional nationalist summaries suggest.

Junagadh matters for two reasons. It demonstrated that India was prepared to use economic pressure, political mobilization, and the threat or presence of force to prevent a state in its geographic midst from joining Pakistan, and it provided a precedent that later arguments about Kashmir could invoke. Pakistan observed that India had rejected the accession of a Muslim ruler of a Hindu-majority state on the basis of popular preference, which should, by the same logic, apply in a Muslim-majority state with a Hindu ruler. India observed that Junagadh’s accession had been to a Dominion with which the state had no contiguity and that the people had ultimately voted. Both arguments were coherent. They shaped the legal and rhetorical positions that each government would take over Kashmir.

Section 87.6

Hyderabad: The Nizam, Independence Claims, and the Breakdown of Negotiations

Hyderabad was the largest princely state, with an area comparable to a major European country and a population recorded in the 1941 census as about sixteen million. It occupied much of the Deccan Plateau and included Telugu-speaking regions in the east (Telangana), Marathi-speaking districts in the northwest (Marathwada), and Kannada-speaking districts in the southwest. The great majority of the population, perhaps 85 percent, was Hindu, while Muslims, who made up roughly a tenth of the population, held a disproportionate share of senior government posts, large landholdings, and army commissions. Urdu was the language of administration and of elite education, and the state’s court culture had a Persianate and Urdu-speaking character. The Nizam, Mir Osman Ali Khan, ruled as an autocrat in a state in which political parties and trade unions were restricted and in which many rural areas were dominated by jagirdars and other large landholders who exacted rent and forced labor.

Hyderabad was therefore a state of deep social inequality and of multiple political movements, and its population cannot be described as divided into two homogeneous religious camps. The Hyderabad State Congress, led by figures such as Swami Ramanand Tirtha, campaigned for responsible government and for association with India, and it drew support in Marathwada and parts of Telangana. The Communist Party and its allies in the Andhra Mahasabha, organizing in the Telugu-speaking districts, led a rural insurgency from 1946 that attacked landlords’ authority, redistributed land, and formed village committees; this Telangana rebellion was directed at the Nizam’s regime and its local agents as much as at any outside power. The Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen, led from 1946 by Qasim Razvi, defended the Nizam’s sovereignty and Muslim political primacy and organized the volunteer force known as the Razakars. Many Muslims, including some in the administration, did not support the Ittehad’s program, and many Hindu landholders and officials, for their own reasons, were wary of the Congress and the Communists.

The Nizam’s goal, in the months before independence, was to preserve the state’s autonomy and, if possible, to obtain a status that would allow it to remain outside both Dominions, perhaps with a connection to the Crown. He did not accede by 15 August. His negotiators, including his constitutional adviser Sir Walter Monckton, entered discussions with the Government of India in the autumn of 1947, and the two sides signed a one-year Standstill Agreement on 29 November 1947 that continued existing arrangements while negotiations went on. The agreement did not settle the main issue, and its interpretation was disputed. India saw it as a temporary bridge to accession. The Nizam’s government saw it as recognition of an interim independence and tried, in the months that followed, to strengthen its position by building up its military forces, seeking arms from abroad, and exploring access to a port through Portuguese Goa.

In the first half of 1948 negotiations over a more permanent settlement, in which Mountbatten took an active part before leaving office in June, failed to produce agreement. Indian officials argued that Hyderabad’s landlocked position, its Hindu majority, and the activities of the Razakars, which they said threatened public order and the Hindu population, made an independent Hyderabad untenable. The Nizam’s government replied that the Razakars were a volunteer force and that India was seeking to undermine the state’s authority. Border incidents multiplied, and the government in Delhi, increasingly convinced that the Nizam’s government could not or would not control the Razakars, moved toward a military solution. On 21 August 1948 Hyderabad appealed to the United Nations Security Council, though the case had little prospect of success. Jinnah’s death on 11 September removed any realistic possibility that Pakistan would support Hyderabad.

Section 87.7

Operation Polo, September 1948: Military Incorporation and the Violence That Followed

On 13 September 1948, Indian forces entered Hyderabad in an operation code-named Polo, which the Indian government described officially as a “police action,” on the ground that Hyderabad’s status was internal to India. The main forces were commanded by Major General J. N. Chaudhuri and advanced from the west, along the Sholapur–Hyderabad road, and from the east, from the direction of Vijayawada, with subsidiary thrusts from other directions. Hyderabad’s regular forces, led by Major General Syed Ahmed El Edroos, and the irregular Razakars were outmatched in numbers, equipment, and organization. The principal fighting lasted about five days. On 17 September the Nizam broadcast an order to cease resistance, his prime minister Laik Ali resigned, and on 18 September El Edroos formally surrendered to Chaudhuri, who became military governor.

The Indian government justified the operation by pointing to the disorder said to be caused by the Razakars, the failure of negotiations, and the need to protect the state’s population. Critics, including some Indian observers at the time, have argued that the military action was avoidable or disproportionate, while defenders have argued that the Nizam’s government had made a negotiated solution impossible. Both views rest on documentary evidence about the negotiations, and the debate is revisited in the Historical Debate feature at the end of §87.8. What is not in dispute is that India used its armed forces to end the independent position of the largest state and that the operation was followed by a period of serious violence.

During and after the operation, communal violence occurred in many parts of the state, particularly in the districts of Marathwada and in parts of Telangana. Muslims were attacked, killed, and driven from their villages by local mobs, sometimes with the participation or complicity of armed groups and, according to several accounts, of some Indian military personnel, while Razakar violence against Hindus, which had occurred before the operation, was also reported. The Government of India, anxious about the scale of the reports, asked a goodwill delegation led by Pandit Sunderlal, with Qazi Abdul Ghaffar and Maulana Abdullah Misri, to visit the state in November and December 1948. The committee reported in a confidential document that large-scale killings had occurred. Estimates attributed to it are commonly given as between about 27,000 and 40,000 deaths in the state during and after the operation, though the text itself reached the public only decades later and has been reproduced in different versions and discussed in A. G. Noorani’s work. Other writers have proposed higher totals, and some contemporary officials and later writers offer much lower figures.

Students should treat these figures with caution. They were not the findings of a judicial commission: the investigators described their visit as a goodwill mission, relied on a limited and non-random set of local inquiries, and did not conduct a complete death count. They are estimates, they cover a wider period of violence than the five days of military operations, they include both Muslim and non-Muslim victims in differing proportions depending on the source, and they were produced under circumstances that did not allow systematic counting. The report’s suppression also means that the debate over its findings was long conducted without access to its text. What the evidence supports is that the incorporation of Hyderabad was accompanied by substantial violence against civilians, concentrated on Muslims, that it was not a bloodless administrative integration, and that the Government of India was aware of its scale but chose not to publish the findings. Contemporary and later scholarship, including work by historians of Hyderabad and of the postcolonial Indian state, treats coercion and violence as integral to the story.

The violence after the military victory raises a distinct question from the legitimacy of the operation itself: whether forces entering a state to restore order protected civilians impartially once effective authority had changed. Military success could be counted in days and measured in occupied roads and headquarters; the protection of villagers, investigation of killings, and restoration of ordinary policing required a longer institutional effort. The Sunderlal material is important precisely because it brought reports of civilian harm to the attention of a government celebrating integration. Its evidentiary limitations do not justify discounting the violence it recorded; nor does its numerical range replace a district-by-district reconstruction from multiple archives.

After the surrender, Hyderabad was placed under a military governor, with the Nizam retained as a figurehead and, after the Constitution came into force in 1950, as Rajpramukh, a position he held until 1956. A civil administrator, M. K. Vellodi, took charge in 1949. The Ittehad was banned and Razvi was arrested; he was later released on condition that he leave India and went to Pakistan. The Indian army and police turned against the Communist insurgents of Telangana, who had in the previous two years controlled large areas, and the armed struggle continued in diminished form until the party abandoned it in 1951. Hyderabad’s later reorganization along linguistic lines, discussed in Chapter 89, ended its existence as a single administrative unit. For the purposes of this chapter, the central point is that Hyderabad’s incorporation required force and was followed by violence that a government committee itself judged serious.

Section 87.8

From Princely Territories to Administrative Unions: Merger, Reorganization, and State Formation

Accession in 1947 left most rulers in charge of internal administration. Within a few years that position changed. The Government of India, which had accepted the limited instruments as a first step, proceeded through a second phase of agreements in which rulers agreed to merge their states with neighboring provinces or to form new unions with other states. The motives included administrative efficiency, the inability of many small states to sustain modern government, and political pressure from Praja Mandals and from regional politicians who demanded responsible government and an end to princely autocracy. In Orissa and Chhattisgarh, for instance, small states were merged into neighboring provinces in the winter of 1947–1948, in some cases after popular movements and disturbances that the authorities used as a reason for intervention.

The new unions were formed by combining groups of states under a Rajpramukh, usually a former ruler, with an elected legislature and a ministry. Saurashtra (originally the United State of Kathiawar) was formed on 15 February 1948 from a large number of small Kathiawar states. Matsya Union, linking several states in eastern Rajasthan, followed in March 1948, and Madhya Bharat, formed around Gwalior and Indore, in May 1948. The Patiala and East Punjab States Union (PEPSU) was inaugurated in July 1948. Greater Rajasthan was constituted in stages through 1949, and Travancore–Cochin was formed in July 1949 after Travancore and Cochin agreed to merge. Other states, including Bhopal, were taken under central administration, and some in the northeast, such as Manipur and Tripura, merged in 1949. Mysore remained as a unit with a responsible government before being fully absorbed into the state system of the republic.

The terms of merger typically included a privy purse, a guaranteed annual payment to the ruler, together with the retention of personal property and certain privileges. These arrangements, which were later written into the Constitution of 1950, reduced rulers’ resistance but left a political and financial legacy. The total privy purse, calculated by Menon at a figure of several crores of rupees annually, was large in relation to the budgets of the new governments, and in 1971 the Indian Parliament abolished the purses by constitutional amendment, a development beyond this volume’s scope. For rulers, the bargain meant the loss of political authority in exchange for security of income and status. For their subjects, it meant, in many cases, the first experience of elected government, though also the continuation of existing inequalities in landholding.

Merger also posed a question of political representation. A ruler might sign an agreement relinquishing power without consulting cultivators, urban associations, or movements that had challenged the dynasty for years. In a union combining several states, citizens then had to navigate new boundaries of electoral districts, courts, revenue administration, and public employment. Local elites sometimes welcomed the prospect of wider markets and uniform institutions but resisted losing influence over appointments or taxation. The new unions were therefore not simply colored blocks on a political map; they were experiments in constructing viable administrations from territories with different legal systems and unequal capacities.

The differences among cases are instructive. In many states, integration was negotiated, with rulers accepting terms and Praja Mandals welcoming them. In others, popular mobilization, such as the movements in Orissa and the Mysore State Congress agitation, pushed rulers toward accession or merger. In Junagadh, pressure and intervention preceded a referendum. In Hyderabad, force settled the issue. The cumulative result was an Indian territorial state in which princely India had been absorbed within about three years. That result was not completed on Independence Day. It was the outcome of a multi-year political process, with different stages and methods in different places, whose full administrative consolidation extended into the 1950s and, as Chapter 89 shows, was followed by the linguistic reorganization of states.

Historical Debate

Was the Integration of the Princely States Principally Negotiated or Coercive?

The question: Should the incorporation of the states into India be understood chiefly as a series of negotiated agreements, or as a process in which pressure and force were decisive?

Interpretation A — Principally negotiated: More than five hundred states acceded within weeks on limited terms that left their internal authority intact, then agreed to mergers in exchange for privy purses. Few used force. The Government of India held out financial and personal guarantees, rulers accepted them, and the exceptional cases of Junagadh, Hyderabad, and Kashmir arose because of the specific decisions of their rulers. On this account the process was a remarkable administrative achievement in which the vast majority of states were integrated without violence.
Interpretation B — Pressure and coercion were structural: Rulers negotiated under the threat of popular agitation, economic dependence, and, in the last resort, military force, so that “consent” was constrained by the balance of power. Hyderabad was invaded, Junagadh was taken after a blockade, and violence accompanied both. Moreover, the principle of popular preference was invoked selectively, in Junagadh and sometimes in Hyderabad, but not applied consistently across cases. On this view the integration was less a negotiation among equals than the extension of one state’s authority over weaker ones.

Evidence: Both views draw on the Instruments of Accession and merger agreements, the correspondence of Patel, Menon, and Mountbatten, the records of the Praja Mandal movements, military records, and the Sunderlal report. They differ in how they weigh the majority of peaceful cases against the minority of violent ones, and in how they interpret “voluntary” action by rulers who knew the alternatives.

Why it matters: The interpretation shapes how historians understand the origins of the Indian state’s authority, the legitimacy of its territorial claims, and the relationship between consent and force in postcolonial state formation. It also matters for comparison with Pakistan’s approach to Kalat and with the Kashmir dispute.

Division III · §§87.9–87.12 · 1846 background; 1946–October 1947

Jammu and Kashmir: Competing Sovereignties and the Crisis of 1947

How the state’s political geography, internal movements, ruler’s decisions, and military invasion produced the most consequential territorial dispute of independence.

Section 87.9

Jammu and Kashmir under Dogra Rule: Geography, Population, and Political Authority

The princely state of Jammu and Kashmir was a creation of the nineteenth century. After the First Anglo-Sikh War, the Treaty of Amritsar of 16 March 1846 transferred to Gulab Singh, the Dogra ruler of Jammu who had served the Sikh court at Lahore, the hill territories between the Indus and the Ravi, including the Kashmir Valley, in return for a payment of seventy-five lakh Nanak Shahi rupees and recognition of British supremacy. Gulab Singh and his successors, of whom Hari Singh, who succeeded in 1925, was the last to rule, built a composite state by conquest and annexation, adding Ladakh, Baltistan, and, through a combination of Dogra and British action, the northern frontier region of Gilgit. Many Kashmiri political movements later described the Treaty of Amritsar as a “sale” of the people, a characterization that was itself political, but it indicates how the origin of the dynasty was remembered.

It is essential to distinguish the Kashmir Valley from the larger state. The Valley, a densely populated basin around Srinagar watered by the Jhelum, was overwhelmingly Muslim and Kashmiri-speaking, with a small but influential Hindu Pandit minority. Jammu, the Dogra homeland to the south, contained a mixed population in which Muslims predominated in the western districts and Hindus, many of them Dogras, in the east, with Punjabi, Dogri, and Pahari languages. Poonch, a jagir held by a collateral branch of the dynasty and later brought under closer control, lay to the west and was inhabited by Muslim Pahari-speaking communities with many men who had served in the British Indian Army. Ladakh, to the east, was predominantly Buddhist in Leh and Shia Muslim in Kargil, with Tibetan-related languages; Baltistan was Shia Muslim and Balti-speaking; and Gilgit, in the far north, was a mountainous region with a mixture of Shia, Ismaili, and Sunni communities and languages such as Shina. The Gilgit Agency, a frontier region, had been leased to the British in 1935 and was returned to the Maharaja on 1 August 1947. The census of 1941 recorded a population of about four million, a majority of it Muslim, but the proportions varied sharply from region to region.

The Maharaja’s authority was personal and monarchical. The state’s administration was dominated by Dogra Hindus and Kashmiri Pandits, and the army’s officer corps was largely Dogra. A representative body, the Praja Sabha, established in 1934, had a restricted franchise and little power. Land revenue and other taxation were heavy, and in many areas peasants held land under conditions that left them vulnerable to indebtedness and to demands for labor. Poonch had particularly burdensome local taxes. The result was a politics of grievance that cut across religion in some respects and along it in others, because the state’s Muslim majority was underrepresented in the services and in the landed elite.

The state’s economy and communications tied it to both sides of the future Partition boundary. The principal all-weather route into the Valley ran along the Jhelum valley road from Rawalpindi through Domel and Muzaffarabad and then to Baramulla and Srinagar. Rail connections ran to Jammu from Sialkot, and the Banihal cart road linked Jammu to the Valley through a pass that was often closed in winter. Timber, fruit, shawls, and other goods flowed toward the Punjab plains, and supplies of grain and manufactured goods flowed back. Trade routes through Gilgit and Ladakh linked the state to Central Asia and Tibet. The Radcliffe Award’s treatment of Gurdaspur district, discussed in Chapter 86, affected whether India would have a usable road link to the state, and scholars continue to discuss how far it influenced later decisions. By 1947 the state was thus a mosaic with strong external connections, and the term “Kashmir” in this chapter refers, unless specified, to the whole state and not only the Valley.

Section 87.10

Sheikh Abdullah, the National Conference, the Muslim Conference, and Competing Political Futures

Organized opposition to Dogra rule emerged after 1931, when protests in Srinagar and Jammu produced violence and the government appointed a commission to examine grievances. The Muslim Conference, formed in 1932 under the leadership of Sheikh Mohammad Abdullah, a school teacher educated at Aligarh, began as a platform for Muslim demands. In 1939 Abdullah and his colleagues renamed it the National Conference, opening its membership to non-Muslims and adopting a program of responsible government and land reform. The party’s 1944 manifesto, “Naya Kashmir” (New Kashmir), set out a program of economic and social reform. A rump Muslim Conference, led by Ghulam Abbas and sympathetic to the Muslim League, continued to exist and gained support in Jammu province and Poonch.

In 1946 Abdullah launched the “Quit Kashmir” movement, demanding an end to Dogra rule and the cancellation of the Treaty of Amritsar. He was arrested in May and sentenced to imprisonment. Jawaharlal Nehru, who had close personal and political ties with Abdullah and the National Conference, went to Kashmir in June to defend him and was himself arrested and expelled, an episode that deepened the personal relationship between the two men and the Congress’s commitment to Kashmir’s politics. The Muslim Conference declined to join the movement and in July 1947 passed a resolution in favor of accession to Pakistan. Abdullah was released in late September 1947.

These facts show that Kashmir’s politics cannot be summarized as a contest between two camps. Opposition to the Maharaja was not the same as support for accession to India or Pakistan. Abdullah’s party was anti-monarchical, secular in program, and allied with Congress, but its leaders did not at that stage commit unconditionally to India, and some of them sought an autonomous or independent status. The Muslim Conference favored Pakistan but did not command the support of the entire Muslim population. Many Hindus and Sikhs in Jammu supported the Maharaja or Hindu political organizations, and in the east of Jammu, groups influenced by Hindu nationalist politics were active. Ladakhi Buddhists, Shia communities in Kargil and Baltistan, and the peoples of Gilgit each had their own concerns. Political support for Sheikh Abdullah did not necessarily constitute consent to a specific international settlement, and recent scholarship emphasizes the diversity of Kashmiri preferences, which the surviving evidence permits historians to reconstruct only imperfectly because no plebiscite or reliable survey was ever conducted.

The 1944 Naya Kashmir program is particularly revealing because it linked a demand for political representation with proposals concerning land, labor, education, and the position of women. To supporters it offered an alternative to the patronage and inequality associated with Dogra rule; to critics it raised questions about who had authorized the National Conference to speak for diverse regions. The same program could be read as a proposal for internal transformation rather than a mandate on international accession. A historian who treats all support for social reform as approval of one Dominion will misread both the movement and the dilemmas facing its leaders in October 1947.

The relationship among Abdullah, Nehru, and Congress was nonetheless significant. Nehru regarded Kashmir’s relationship with India as a matter of principle as well as of strategy, as evidence that a Muslim-majority region could belong to a secular India, and he regarded Abdullah as the representative of the state’s people. These attitudes affected the decisions Indian leaders made in October. The Muslim League leadership, for its part, regarded the state’s majority-Muslim population and its geographic and economic connections as grounds for its inclusion in Pakistan and saw Abdullah as Nehru’s ally. These interpretations of the state’s identity were already in place before the crisis began.

Section 87.11

The Crisis of October 1947: Poonch, the Tribal Invasion, and the Collapse of the Maharaja’s Position

Hari Singh did not accede to either Dominion by 15 August. On 12 August he proposed standstill agreements to both India and Pakistan. Pakistan accepted, and postal and telegraph services continued under the arrangement. India did not sign, asking for further discussion. The Maharaja’s reasoning is not fully documented. Possible considerations included the risk of alienating the state’s Hindu or Muslim populations, a wish to preserve the dynasty’s autonomy, uncertainty about the future of the monarchy in either Dominion, and the hope that delay would allow the situation to clarify. He replaced his prime minister, Ram Chandra Kak, who was suspected of favoring independence, first with Janak Singh and, in mid-October, with Mehr Chand Mahajan, a jurist who had served on the Punjab Boundary Commission and who favored accession to India.

Within the state, authority was weakening. In Poonch, a revolt began in the spring and summer of 1947 against heavy taxation and local administration. Many of its leaders were former soldiers, and they organized armed resistance, to which the state’s forces responded harshly. By the autumn, Poonch had become a center of armed insurgency, with links to the Muslim Conference and to supporters across the Punjab border who provided arms and shelter. In September and October violence also affected Jammu and the western districts, with movements of refugees across the new Punjab boundary. The tensions of Partition were being transmitted across the state’s border, and the Maharaja’s government was losing control over areas on its western frontier. Pakistan’s authorities were also accused of restricting supplies and communications to the state; Pakistan denied the allegation, and historians consider the evidence unclear.

Poonch illustrates the danger of treating October's invasion as the beginning of all armed resistance. Veterans who had served in wartime armies brought military experience into a dispute already intensified by taxation, police action, and the upheaval of neighboring Punjab. Their grievances and political affiliations were not identical, and some localities experienced changes of authority before the tribal columns advanced toward the Valley. The chronology matters for assigning responsibility: the wider war linked pre-existing regional rebellion with outside intervention, but did not retroactively turn every participant in the Poonch rising into an agent of another state.

On or about 22 October, a force of armed Pashtun tribesmen entered the state from the Frontier, passing through Abbottabad and Domel into the Jhelum valley. Contemporary and later documents, including Indian official accounts, Pakistani memoirs by army officers such as Akbar Khan, and the reports of external observers, show that these lashkars were organized and transported with assistance from persons in Pakistan’s Frontier Province and from Pakistani officers and officials acting in some capacity. The extent to which the government of Pakistan, including its senior leadership, planned, authorized, or tolerated the operation is disputed. Pakistani accounts have emphasized the spontaneous indignation of the tribes at reports of attacks on Muslims in Jammu and Poonch and the part played by Poonchi insurgents. Indian accounts have emphasized official planning and complicity. What can be said is that the force would not have crossed the border and moved with the speed it did without logistical help from within Pakistan, and that the question of official responsibility is to be separated from the question of the conduct of those who participated.

The invasion rapidly broke the Maharaja’s position. State forces in the west, including Muslim troops who joined the insurgents, collapsed, and the commander at the Uri bridge, Brigadier Rajinder Singh, was killed while attempting to delay the advance. Muzaffarabad fell on 24 October, the Mahora power station that supplied Srinagar was seized and the city’s electricity cut off, and in the same days an administration styling itself “Azad” (Free) Kashmir was proclaimed in the western districts with Sardar Muhammad Ibrahim Khan as its president. On 26 and 27 October the lashkars reached Baramulla, where they looted the town and killed and abused civilians, including those at the St. Joseph’s mission, an episode that was widely reported. The time spent in Baramulla delayed the advance toward Srinagar, which lay some thirty-five miles farther on, and allowed the Indian airlift described below to begin before the capital fell. The Maharaja and his family left Srinagar for Jammu on the night of 25 October.

Students should distinguish several actors. The Poonch uprising was a local rebellion against the state’s government with its own causes. The lashkars were tribal irregulars organized with help from Pakistan. Azad Kashmir’s leaders sought a political identity separate from the Maharaja’s government and increasingly aligned with Pakistan. Pakistani authorities offered support and had their own strategic aims. The Maharaja’s forces were an army of the state, divided along communal lines, that disintegrated under pressure. The crisis of October was the product of all these forces, not of a single plan, and each of them reflects different accounts in later memory.

Section 87.12

The Instrument of Accession, 26–27 October 1947, and the Indian Military Airlift

By 24 October the Maharaja sent urgent requests for military help to the Government of India. V. P. Menon flew to Srinagar on 25 October to assess the situation, and the Indian Defence Committee, chaired by Mountbatten, met on 25 and 26 October. Mountbatten advised that India should not send troops into a state that had not acceded, since this would risk a confrontation with Pakistan on a legal footing that could be challenged. The Committee agreed on 26 October that if the Maharaja acceded, India would send forces, and that accession should be accompanied by a statement that the question would be referred to the people once order was restored. Menon flew to Jammu with the draft instrument, and the Maharaja, who had by then written to Mountbatten requesting assistance and offering accession, signed the Instrument of Accession, which bears the date of 26 October 1947. On 27 October Mountbatten accepted it, adding that the state’s accession would be settled by reference to its people once law and order had been restored and the invader expelled.

On the morning of 27 October the first Indian troops, a battalion of the Sikh Regiment under Lieutenant Colonel Dewan Ranjit Rai, were flown to Srinagar, followed by further battalions in an airlift that used military and requisitioned civil aircraft. The next day Nehru telegraphed Liaquat Ali Khan, setting out the Indian position, and the Indian government acted in cooperation with Sheikh Abdullah, whom the Maharaja had asked to form an emergency administration. Pakistan’s leadership rejected the accession as fraudulent, resting on violence and fraud, and argued that the Maharaja had already agreed to a standstill with Pakistan, had lost the confidence of his people, and had acted under the military protection of the Dominion he was joining. Jinnah, it is recorded, ordered the acting British commander of the Pakistan Army, Sir Douglas Gracey, to send troops into Kashmir. Gracey, citing the position of the Supreme Commander, Auchinleck, who warned that British officers would be withdrawn from both armies in the event of war, declined to carry out the order, and the plan was dropped for the time being.

The documents are not free of ambiguity. The accession instrument is in the standard form, with the three subjects of defense, external affairs, and communications, and with a clause that the state retained its authority in other matters. The letters of the Maharaja and Mountbatten’s reply form a related set. The text of Mountbatten’s acceptance has been frequently cited by India as acceptance of the accession, and by critics as evidence that accession was conditional, at least in political terms. The principal debates concern the timing and circumstances. The Indian government’s account, supported by Menon’s narrative, states that the instrument was signed on 26 October before troops were dispatched. Some scholars, including Alastair Lamb, have argued that the evidence does not show that the instrument had been signed before the troops’ departure on the morning of 27 October, and have pointed to inconsistencies in the sequence of travel that Menon described. Others, including Srinath Raghavan, have argued that the decision to send troops and the legal act of accession were so closely linked that the exact sequence does not change the broader picture, and that the instrument’s validity does not depend on the sequence. These disputes concern a day and a few hours, but they have been read as bearing on the legitimacy of the whole sequence.

Contemporary paperwork provides a way to test retrospective accounts without pretending that every discrepancy can be resolved. Nehru's letter of 27 October refers to receiving the signed accession papers from Menon and explains that the troop decision had been made the previous afternoon; a United States diplomatic report sent the following day described the signature as occurring on the 27th. Both are evidence of how the sequence was understood or reported at the time. Neither, by itself, is a minute-by-minute record of the ruler's movements, the physical transfer of documents, or the departure of the first aircraft. This is why the dated instrument, acceptance letter, cabinet decisions, correspondence, and later memoirs must be assessed separately.

Three things should be kept distinct. The first is the legal accession instrument, which in the form used by all states transferred limited subjects to India. The second is the political promise, made in the Mountbatten letter and repeated by Nehru in a broadcast on 2 November, that the people would be consulted after order was restored. The third is the military intervention itself, which was undertaken because India judged that the state’s capital was at risk. Each of these has been invoked in different ways. India has stressed the first, Pakistan has emphasized the circumstances in which it was obtained and has invoked the second to argue that the question was always open, and the United Nations later treated the dispute as one that should be resolved through an expression of the people’s will. The sections that follow show how these arguments played out in war and diplomacy.

Historical Debate

How Should the Accession and Division of Jammu and Kashmir Be Interpreted?

The question: What legal and political weight should historians give to the Maharaja’s accession, given the circumstances in which it was made, the promise to consult the people, and the military events?

Interpretation A — A legally valid accession in response to aggression: The Maharaja, as the ruler recognized by the Indian Independence Act, had the authority to accede. He did so by the standard instrument when the state was invaded by forces that came from Pakistan. India accepted accession and sent troops at the ruler’s request, with a political commitment to consult the people after order was restored. Pakistan’s help to the tribesmen violated the standstill agreement and international law. On this view the state’s accession was valid and the Pakistani presence was an occupation.
Interpretation B — A contested accession lacking clear legitimacy: The Maharaja acted after losing control of much of the state, in a context of communal violence, and under circumstances in which the timing and signature are disputed. His decision did not represent a population that was majority Muslim and whose political preferences were varied and unmeasured, and India itself acknowledged this when it promised a plebiscite. The Poonch uprising and the Azad Kashmir government show local opposition to the Maharaja. On this view the accession was provisional and the question was properly one for the people.

Evidence: The instrument, the Maharaja’s letter, Mountbatten’s acceptance, the Defence Committee records, Pakistani and Indian military memoirs, diplomatic correspondence, and later UN documents. Many scholars combine elements of both interpretations: they accept that a legal instrument was signed and that the Maharaja had formal authority, but note that legal accession did not settle the question of political legitimacy.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the origins of the dispute, the responsibility of each government, and the relationship between legal form, popular representation, and force. The chapter’s purpose is not to adjudicate between India’s and Pakistan’s positions but to show how each depends on particular documents, legal arguments, and interpretations of events.

The First India–Pakistan War and United Nations Intervention

The military conflict and the unsuccessful attempt to turn a ceasefire into an agreed political settlement.

Section 87.13

The First India–Pakistan War, 1947–1948: The Defense of the Kashmir Valley

The first weeks of the war were fought for the Valley. The airlift of 27 October brought troops to Srinagar’s airfield, and the first arrivals moved forward to meet the lashkars. Lieutenant Colonel Ranjit Rai, who commanded the first battalion, was killed in the fighting on the Baramulla road on 27 October. In the following days Indian reinforcements arrived, and on 3 November Major Somnath Sharma and his men were killed defending the approaches to the airfield near Badgam, an action for which Sharma received the first Param Vir Chakra. On 7 November Indian forces, supported by armored cars, defeated the main body of the tribal force at Shalateng, a few miles from Srinagar, and turned the tribesmen back. Baramulla was retaken on 8 November, and Uri on 13 November, which restored Indian control over the Valley’s main approaches from the west.

The terrain and the season shaped the campaign. The Jhelum valley road, the principal route into the Valley, ran through narrow gorges where small forces could delay larger ones. The Banihal Pass connecting Jammu with the Valley was liable to close in winter, which meant that the Valley’s supply depended on the airlift until a road could be improved and held. Mountains limited the use of armor and artillery, and in many areas the fighting depended on small units, porters, and mules. The tribal lashkars, motivated by the prospect of plunder and by religious and political appeal, were poorly suited to sustained operations against organized forces, and their conduct, including looting in Baramulla, alienated many Kashmiris and may have weakened their own cause. At the same time, the Indian forces were operating at the end of a long supply line, with limited numbers and under a command structure still shared with British officers.

Supplying troops was a political as well as a military problem. Aircraft that transported soldiers could not simultaneously carry the food, fuel, ammunition, and medical supplies required to sustain them, and every delay on the mountain road increased dependence on the airfield. Maintaining Srinagar therefore demanded cooperation among Indian commanders, civil transport staff, state officials, and local people who provided labor and information. This logistical perspective helps explain why the defense of the Valley cannot be reduced to the outcome of one battle. The ability to reinforce and provision a position after a tactical success could determine whether that success lasted through winter.

The war was not a single conventional conflict between two mobilized national armies from the beginning. Its character changed across several phases. In the first, from late October to early November, the combatants were irregular tribal forces, Poonchi insurgents, and the state’s remaining troops on the one side, and Indian army units airlifted to the Valley on the other. In the following months, the forces calling themselves the Azad Kashmir army, a mixture of local insurgents, former soldiers, and Pakistani officers and men on leave or unofficially attached, played the main role against India, and Pakistan’s regular army was formally uninvolved. That position changed in the spring of 1948, when Pakistani regular troops moved into the state, a development that Pakistani authorities acknowledged to the United Nations Commission in the summer, discussed in §87.14. Indian forces, in turn, expanded their operations beyond the Valley and eventually, in 1948, planned for the possibility of crossing into Pakistan itself, though they did not do so.

Diplomacy ran alongside combat. Mountbatten met Jinnah in Lahore on 1 November and the discussions yielded no agreement, since Jinnah proposed simultaneous withdrawal of Indian forces and tribesmen under a joint arrangement, which Indian leaders considered unacceptable while the invasion continued. Nehru’s broadcast of 2 November affirmed that the people of Kashmir would decide the state’s future under impartial auspices. Britain’s role was complicated: British officers served in both armies and, as noted, helped to prevent the direct entry of Pakistani regulars in October, and British and Commonwealth diplomats were consulted by both sides. By the end of 1947 it was clear that the war would not end quickly, and the Government of India, which had retaken the Valley but could not easily dislodge its opponents in the west, turned to the United Nations.

Historical Vocabulary
Ceasefire Line and Line of Control

The 1949 ceasefire line was the military line agreed at Karachi in July 1949 under United Nations auspices to mark the positions held by the Indian and Pakistani forces after the fighting. It was a line of military demarcation, observed by United Nations military observers, and not a recognized international boundary. The Line of Control is the term used after the Simla Agreement of 1972, which redefined the line that resulted from the December 1971 ceasefire at the end of the Bangladesh war, treated in Chapter 92. It followed the earlier line in many sections but was not identical to it.

Using “Line of Control” for 1949 is anachronistic and obscures a change in the diplomatic status of the line. This chapter uses “1949 ceasefire line” for the earlier period.

Section 87.14

The War across Jammu, Poonch, Ladakh, Gilgit, and Baltistan

The war was fought across a front of several hundred miles in very different environments, and the Valley was only one of its theaters. In the Jammu region and Poonch, fighting continued through the winter of 1947–1948. Mirpur, in the southwest, fell to the insurgents in late November 1947 and was followed by killings of Hindu and Sikh residents and the flight of survivors, an episode that features in the memory of displaced communities. Kotli and other towns changed hands, and Indian garrisons held on in Poonch town, which was besieged from late 1947 until the siege was lifted in November 1948. Jhangar fell to Pakistan-backed forces at the end of December 1947 and was recaptured by India in March 1948, while Indian forces won the battle at Naushera in February. Each locality had its own sequence, in which local rebels, refugees returning to their homes, and soldiers from both sides were involved.

In the north, events followed a different course. In Gilgit, which the British had returned to the Maharaja on 1 August 1947, the Gilgit Scouts, a local force commanded by the British officer Major William Brown, arrested the Maharaja’s governor, Brigadier Ghansara Singh, on 31 October and 1 November and declared that the region would join Pakistan. A local political leadership took formal charge, and a Pakistani political agent arrived by mid-November. The coup depended on both the British officer and the support of local leaders and troops who had their own reasons for opposing Dogra rule, and Pakistan accepted the region’s accession. The rulers of Hunza, Nagar, and Chitral also joined Pakistan. Baltistan followed a similar course, though more contested. The Gilgit forces, supported by local irregulars, moved toward Skardu, where the Dogra garrison held out until it surrendered in August 1948, and then toward Kargil and the road to Ladakh.

Ladakh therefore became a strategic problem. Leh, its principal town, was cut off by the winter and by the advance of Pakistani and Gilgit forces. Indian forces, with local support, held the area through improvised measures. Under the leadership of local figures including the Buddhist leader Kushok Bakula, an airstrip was prepared at Leh, and the first aircraft landed in May 1948. In November 1948, after a surprise advance in which light tanks were brought across the Zoji La pass at high altitude, Indian forces captured Zoji La, Dras, and Kargil and secured the route between the Valley and Ladakh. The achievement depended on the engineering of a road across mountains and on the season, and it determined that Ladakh would remain under Indian administration.

Pakistan’s military participation expanded in 1948. In May, Pakistani army formations moved into parts of the state, in order, Pakistani authorities explained to the UN Commission, to prevent an Indian advance toward Pakistan’s own territory. By the summer, the Commission’s members were told that Pakistani regular forces were in the state, which changed the diplomatic position by affirming that the conflict was no longer only a matter of irregulars. This, in turn, made the withdrawal of Pakistani forces central to subsequent UN proposals. Local forces and civilians suffered under the changing military control. Villages were destroyed or abandoned, communities were displaced, and many people were forced to choose between loyalty to different authorities, issues treated in §87.18.

The northern operations also show that a military map can conceal a complex political landscape. A district headquarters, an isolated fort, a caravan route, and a high-altitude pass could each have different significance to a local community and to the two governments. Control of Skardu or the roads toward Leh was not equivalent to administering every valley beyond it. The movement of soldiers depended on seasonal access and local assistance, while the establishment of civilian authority depended on the acceptance—or coercion—of people whose earlier relationship to the Dogra court had varied substantially. Later statements that whole northern regions simply 'joined' one country should be tested against the sequence of revolts, local rulership, military outcomes, and administrative decisions.

The northern regions and their peoples were historical actors in their own right and not simply territory contested between Delhi and Karachi. The Gilgit revolt, though influenced by outside actors, reflected local opposition to Dogra rule, the political weight of Shia and Ismaili communities, and the ambitions of local rulers and officers. The people of Ladakh, notably the Buddhist community, held distinct views about their future and about the Valley’s politics. The people of Baltistan, whose ties to Ladakh and to the Indus valley were centuries old, found themselves divided by a line that had no local origin. Scholarship on these regions, much of which draws on local sources, shows that their inclusion in one state or the other resulted from a combination of external decisions and local agency, and that the memory of 1947–1948 remains politically significant.

Section 87.15

India Appeals to the United Nations: The Security Council and Resolution 47

On 1 January 1948 the Government of India submitted a complaint to the Security Council under Article 35 of the Charter, charging that Pakistan was assisting the tribesmen and other raiders who had invaded the state, and asking that Pakistan be called on to stop. Pakistan replied in mid-January, denying the charge, accusing India of obtaining the accession by fraud and violence, and raising its own complaints, including the treatment of Muslims in India and the incorporation of Junagadh. The Council, in a series of meetings in which representatives such as Sir Zafrulla Khan for Pakistan and Gopalaswami Ayyangar for India argued their cases at length, widened the issue from one of Pakistani assistance to raiders to one of the state’s status. In its resolution of 17 January 1948 the Council urged both parties to refrain from actions that would worsen the situation. On 20 January, by Resolution 39, it established the United Nations Commission for India and Pakistan (UNCIP), to investigate the facts and mediate.

The Council’s further action came on 21 April 1948 with Resolution 47. The Council voted on the draft paragraph by paragraph; it took no single vote on the resolution as a whole. The frequently repeated tally of nine votes in favor and two abstentions belongs to Resolution 39 of 20 January, not Resolution 47. The resolution enlarged the Commission and recommended a sequence of measures. First, Pakistan was to use its best endeavors to secure the withdrawal from the state of tribesmen and of Pakistani nationals who did not normally reside there, and to prevent further intrusion. Second, India was to reduce its forces progressively to the minimum needed to support civil power, after the Commission had notified that the tribesmen had withdrawn and that the situation was normalizing. Third, India was to establish a plebiscite administration headed by an administrator nominated by the Secretary-General of the United Nations, with wide powers, and to arrange for a plebiscite on the question whether the state should accede to India or Pakistan. The resolution also called for a representative interim government in the state, and for the release of political prisoners and the return of refugees.

It is important to state accurately what Resolution 47 was. It was a recommendation of the Security Council, adopted under Chapter VI of the Charter, which concerns the pacific settlement of disputes, not a binding order. It specified a sequence of withdrawal, force reduction, restoration of order, and arrangements for a plebiscite, but it did not set a date for a vote and it did not instruct that an immediate plebiscite be held. Its terms left room for disagreement, particularly about the sequence and degree of withdrawal, the status of the Azad Kashmir forces and of the Indian-backed administration, and the role of Sheikh Abdullah’s government. India objected that the resolution treated aggressor and defender as equals and that it did not recognize the state’s accession. Pakistan objected to provisions that, in its view, left the Indian-supported Abdullah government in control of the arrangements for the plebiscite. Neither government accepted the resolution as framed.

Resolution 47's sequence explains much of the later impasse. Withdrawal of the irregulars and the relevant Pakistani nationals was expected to precede India's reduction of forces, after which conditions for a vote would have to be established. Each government questioned whether the other could be trusted to preserve security or political neutrality during that interval. The issue was not simply whether leaders endorsed the abstract principle of consulting the population; they disputed the institutions that would supervise the process and the armed power permitted to remain on the ground. In a territory that had recently seen invasion, communal violence, and displacement, those were substantive issues rather than procedural details.

The scope of the Council’s influence was constrained by the dynamics of the early Cold War, by British and American caution about alienating either party, and by the lack of a mechanism to compel compliance. The Commission, which arrived in the subcontinent in July 1948, had to operate in conditions in which the fighting continued and in which the parties differed on the order of events. The decision to treat the question as one to be settled by a vote of the inhabitants was a significant moment, because it internationalized the principle that the state’s final status was undetermined, an interpretation that India accepted in a limited, conditional sense and that Pakistan embraced fully.

Section 87.16

The UNCIP Proposals, the 1949 Ceasefire, and the Karachi Agreement

The Commission worked through the second half of 1948 in the subcontinent, interviewing officials and visiting both sides of the front. In August it learned that Pakistani regular forces were in the state, and on 13 August it adopted a resolution in three parts. The first called for a ceasefire order. The second set out principles for a truce agreement, which included the withdrawal of Pakistani regular troops and tribal forces, and, after the Commission certified that these withdrawals were taking place, the withdrawal of the bulk of Indian forces, with local authorities administering the evacuated areas under Commission observation. The third affirmed that the future status of the state would be determined in accordance with the will of the people, once the truce was implemented. These parts were intended as stages: the ceasefire would end the fighting, the truce would arrange for demilitarization, and consultation on a plebiscite would follow.

The parties responded with qualifications. India accepted the resolution subject to clarifications, in particular regarding the role of the Azad Kashmir forces, which it did not want to be recognized as a separate authority, and regarding the Indian army’s right to remain in sufficient strength to defend the state. Pakistan accepted it with reservations, including on the sequence of withdrawals. The truce negotiations that followed did not produce a final agreement on the withdrawal of forces. In December, however, the Commission put forward supplementary principles concerning the plebiscite, which both governments accepted at the end of the month, and which it adopted formally on 5 January 1949. These principles provided for a plebiscite administrator nominated by the Secretary-General and appointed by the state’s government, and for the administrator to have control over the state’s forces and administration for the purpose of the vote. Admiral Chester Nimitz of the United States was later named to the post, but never took it up.

The ceasefire took effect just before midnight on 1 January 1949, bringing the main fighting to an end. The Commission’s military observers, drawn from several countries, were deployed along the lines to monitor it. In the following months, military representatives of India and Pakistan met under the Commission’s auspices in Karachi and, on 27 July 1949, signed the military Karachi Agreement, which entered into force on 30 July. This agreement defined the ceasefire line in detail on maps, specified the positions each side would hold, and described the role of United Nations military observers in supervising it. The agreement traced the line through the mountainous state and, at its northern end beyond Khor, used the formulation “thence north to the glaciers.” The text did not delineate every segment in the high-glacier region, an ambiguity that acquired later strategic significance (Chapter 105). The ceasefire line should not be confused with the later Line of Control terminology. The observer group that supervised the line later became known as the United Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan (UNMOGIP), and its status was reaffirmed by the Council in 1951.

The July military agreement and the April political agreement signed in Karachi must also be kept separate in students' minds. The former was concluded by representatives of India and Pakistan with United Nations involvement to mark military positions; the latter concerned the distribution of administrative responsibilities between Pakistan and Azad Kashmir leaders and did not determine an internationally agreed border. They addressed different participants, territories, and powers. Confusing them can turn an administrative allocation into supposed international consent, or turn a ceasefire arrangement into evidence of a final political settlement. Neither reading follows from the texts.

It is crucial to distinguish what was implemented from what was proposed. The ceasefire was implemented. The line was agreed and observed. The truce arrangements, including the mutual withdrawal of forces and the demilitarization of the state, were not carried out, because the parties could not agree on the sequence and the status of the forces and administrations on each side. The plebiscite was never held. The question whether this failure was primarily due to India’s refusal to accept demilitarization on terms that would have exposed its position, to Pakistan’s refusal to withdraw its forces first, or to the structure of the proposals themselves has been a subject of continuing argument, and each government has presented the history in a way that assigns responsibility to the other. UN records document the proposals and observation machinery but do not by themselves settle responsibility. Later mediators, including General A. G. L. McNaughton and Sir Owen Dixon in 1949 and 1950, also failed to produce agreement.

Section 87.17

A Divided Jammu and Kashmir: Regional Administrations and Competing Sovereignty Claims

When the ceasefire took effect, the former princely state was divided between two administrations. India administered the Kashmir Valley, most of Jammu province, and Ladakh, including the districts of Leh and Kargil. Pakistan administered a western strip of territory, comprising Muzaffarabad, Mirpur, Kotli, and part of Poonch, which was organized under a provisional Azad Jammu and Kashmir government and president, as well as the northern regions of Gilgit and Baltistan, which were governed more directly from Karachi. The two parts differed greatly in size, population, and economic weight. The Valley, with its capital and its fertile basin, was the most populous and politically central region, and it remained in Indian hands, while the northern territories were large in area but sparsely populated.

The division resulted from the interplay of geography, regional politics, and wartime developments. The Valley was held because the Indian airlift reached it before the capital fell and because the approaches were defended in November. Jammu was held because of the presence of Indian-controlled roads and of a Dogra Hindu population that supported the Maharaja, though western Jammu, where the insurgency was strong, passed to Azad Kashmir. Ladakh was held after the Zoji La campaign and the efforts of local leaders. Gilgit and Baltistan passed to Pakistan because of the coup of November 1947 and the capture of Skardu in 1948. The arrangement was therefore not the product of any plan, in India or Pakistan, for a final territorial settlement. It was the outcome of campaigns, uprisings, and the places where troops happened to be when the ceasefire was ordered.

The administrative arrangements on each side evolved separately. In the area it administered, Pakistan treated Azad Jammu and Kashmir as a distinct entity and its northern regions as territory under federal control. An agreement signed in Karachi on 28 April 1949 by a representative of Pakistan, the Azad Kashmir president, and the Muslim Conference leadership—not by representatives from Gilgit or Baltistan and not to be confused with the India–Pakistan military agreement of 27 July—placed responsibility for defense, foreign affairs, and the administration of Gilgit and Baltistan with Pakistan’s central government. On the Indian side, the state’s government under Sheikh Abdullah administered the territory with substantial autonomy under the terms of accession, an arrangement discussed in §87.19.

The April agreement exposes the unevenness of political participation in the new arrangement. The signatories were a Pakistani minister, the president of the provisional Azad Kashmir government, and a Muslim Conference leader. No representative of Gilgit or Baltistan signed, although the document allocated their administration to Pakistan's side. That absence is historically significant even without assuming that inhabitants shared a single alternative preference. It shows how local authority, party representation, and the external supervision of a strategically important frontier were assembled through a small number of political intermediaries. Azad Jammu and Kashmir's elected Legislative Assembly developed much later, under the reforms of 1970; it should not be projected back onto 1949.

On the Indian-administered side, too, constitutional authority was not identical to the extent of military control. The National Conference's reform ambitions depended on the continuing existence of a state government, but wartime command, communication with Delhi, and restrictions on political opposition complicated any straightforward picture of autonomy. In the northern and western territories held by Pakistan, residents encountered other mixes of local institutions and outside officials. The ceasefire thus stabilized an armed line without creating two equally structured administrations. Comparing those structures reveals the difference between a state's assertion of legal title and its day-to-day ability to govern.

It should be stated plainly that the 1949 ceasefire line was a military demarcation and not a mutually recognized final international boundary. India maintained that the whole of the former princely state was legally part of India by virtue of the accession, and regarded the Pakistani-administered areas as occupied. Pakistan held that the state’s status was undetermined pending a plebiscite and regarded the Indian-administered areas as under disputed control. Each therefore treated the line as a practical rather than a juridical frontier. For the people of the state, including families whose land, kin, and trading relations lay on either side, the line cut through economic and social connections that had existed for generations.

Section 87.18

War, Displacement, and Political Change among the Peoples of Jammu and Kashmir

The war and the political changes of 1947–1949 affected the state’s inhabitants through violence, flight, and changes of authority. The best-documented violence occurred in late 1947 in Jammu province, where Muslims in the districts around Jammu city were attacked and killed, and many were driven across the new border into Pakistan, in a chain of events connected to the wider Punjab violence discussed in Chapter 86. The perpetrators included elements of the state’s forces, armed Hindu and Sikh bands, some from outside the state, and local communities, and the targets were Muslim villagers. Estimates of the numbers killed and displaced vary greatly. Contemporary press reports and later writers have given figures that range from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand, and these estimates are contested, because no systematic count was made and because the numbers have been used politically by both sides. Historians therefore treat any single figure as an estimate and attribute it to its source.

In the west, Hindu and Sikh minorities in towns such as Mirpur, Kotli, and Muzaffarabad were killed, abducted, or driven out in the autumn and winter of 1947 by insurgents, tribal fighters, and local mobs, and survivors fled to Jammu and to Indian-held areas. Violence against non-Muslims in the Valley, notably at Baramulla, was also reported. These events, together with those in Jammu, produced refugees moving in opposite directions across what would become the ceasefire line, and they entered the memory of each community as defining experiences. They also fed political arguments: Pakistan’s leaders pointed to the killings of Muslims in Jammu to justify support for the insurgents, while Indian leaders pointed to the killings of non-Muslims in the west and at Baramulla to justify the intervention.

Displacement was experienced on several scales at once. A household might lose its home in one town, move to a relative's village, and then cross an increasingly militarized route after a second outbreak of fighting. Public records often classify such people only when they entered a camp or claimed relief, leaving many temporary journeys undocumented. Their movement was also shaped by who controlled roads, river crossings, and mountain passes at the time. This is why an estimate drawn from one locality or relief scheme cannot be converted without qualification into a mortality or refugee total for the entire former princely state.

Political change within the state’s Indian-administered portion was rapid. Sheikh Abdullah became head of an emergency administration on 30 October 1947 and, in March 1948, Prime Minister of the state. His government organized volunteers, restored administration in the Valley, and began a program of land reform that, from 1950, transferred ownership of land to cultivators and weakened the landlord class, which had been disproportionately Hindu. Many Kashmiris, particularly in the Valley, supported the National Conference and its program. Others, including supporters of the Muslim Conference and Hindu organizations in Jammu, were hostile to the new order, and the government restricted opposition. The state’s leaders also negotiated with India over the terms of the state’s relationship, because the accession was limited to three subjects and the National Conference wished to preserve its autonomy.

The inhabitants were not passive objects of Indian and Pakistani policy. Local leaders, including Abdullah, Ibrahim, Ghulam Abbas, Kushok Bakula in Ladakh, and local figures in Gilgit and Baltistan, pursued their own programs and bargained with the external governments. Communities made choices, often under pressure, about whether to stay, flee, or fight. The evidence, however, is limited. Official demographic and casualty figures are partial and politicized, no census or referendum was conducted across the whole state, and the voices of ordinary people appear mainly in later oral histories and memoirs. Historians must therefore be cautious in claiming to know what the majority of Kashmiris wanted in 1947 or 1948.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Jammu and Kashmir Household Affected by Fighting, Displacement, or Division

This is a composite case based on documented patterns. It does not describe a named family and contains no quotations.

Consider a household in the Poonch–Mirpur region before 1947. The men may have served in the British Indian Army, and remittances from military pay supplemented a small holding. The household paid local taxes that it considered heavy, traded in the markets of both Rawalpindi and the state’s towns, and had relatives on both sides of what was soon to become a boundary. In the autumn of 1947 the household’s world changed. Women and children were often the first to be sent away, and property was left behind. A Muslim family in Jammu might cross to Sialkot. A Hindu or Sikh family in Mirpur might make its way to Jammu city and live for years in a refugee camp. After the ceasefire line was drawn, the household might find that its relatives lived in a different state, that its land was beyond reach, and that its livelihood depended on a new authority.

The composite shows how war, communal violence, and the demarcation of a line combined in individual lives, and how families’ experiences depended on religion, region, and chance. Official accounts record numbers and positions, but they rarely record such details.

Section 87.19

The Territorial Legacy of Partition: Integration, Sovereignty, and the Unresolved Kashmir Dispute

The three major contested cases had different outcomes, and the differences show how multiple variables interacted. In Junagadh, the ruler acceded to a Dominion with which the state had no land connection, the population was overwhelmingly of another religion, and the surrounding territory belonged to India; the Dewan’s inability to maintain order allowed India to assume control without a major war, and a referendum was held under Indian administration. In Hyderabad, the ruler sought independence in the middle of Indian territory, the state had a large Hindu majority, Indian military capacity was overwhelming, and Pakistan had neither the means nor, after Jinnah’s death, the leadership to intervene; India used force. In Jammu and Kashmir, the ruler hesitated, the population was mostly Muslim but politically divided, the state bordered both Dominions, tribal and Pakistani forces entered, India airlifted troops after an accession, and neither side could win decisively. The state’s territory was therefore divided and its status left open.

Principles invoked by the governments were not applied consistently across the three cases, and each government could point to the other’s inconsistency. India applied popular preference to justify its position in Junagadh and Hyderabad, where Hindu majorities were governed by Muslim rulers, but relied on the ruler’s signature in Kashmir, where the population was mostly Muslim. Pakistan relied on the ruler’s authority in Junagadh, rejected it in Kashmir, and invoked self-determination there. These contradictions did not arise from simple bad faith but from the strategic interests of each state and from the lack of any agreed principle in the Independence Act. They shaped the rhetoric of subsequent diplomacy.

The implications for India–Pakistan relations were lasting. The war left both armies deployed along a line that neither accepted as a boundary, with the shared road and rail links of the old state severed, and with trade across the Punjab border curtailed. Each government included the dispute in its military planning and its foreign policy, and each described the other’s claims as illegitimate. The Valley and Jammu became part of India’s internal political and constitutional order, while Pakistan treated its portion as part of a dispute whose resolution it sought through the United Nations. Subsequent wars, treated in Chapter 92, and the later insurgency and security confrontations discussed in Chapter 105 grew in part from this unresolved situation.

The unresolved dispute also sharpened the importance of international recognition and administrative precedent. The 1949 ceasefire line indicated where organized hostilities had stopped, but recognition of the two Dominions did not amount to acceptance of either state's maximum territorial claim. In the following years, official maps, education, diplomatic correspondence, and border administration would repeat competing descriptions of the same geography. These practices had practical consequences for movement, property, and political expression, even when they did not change the line itself. The legacy of Partition was therefore reproduced not only through armies but through the ordinary documents and institutions by which states described their territories.

Between 1949 and 1950 the relationship between Jammu and Kashmir and India took a distinct constitutional form. Because the accession had covered only defense, external affairs, and communications, Indian leaders and the National Conference negotiated an arrangement under which the state’s relations with the Union would be governed by a special provision. In October 1949 the Constituent Assembly adopted the provision that became Article 370 of the Constitution, which limited the Union’s legislative powers over the state to the subjects in the Instrument of Accession, together with additional matters on the concurrence of the state government, and it provided for the state’s own constituent assembly. The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. Chapter 88 treats the operation of the Constitution, and Chapter 105 examines how Article 370 and the state’s status later changed. For present purposes, what matters is that the arrangement reflected the compromise between the legal fact of accession and the political fact of the state’s autonomy and the international dispute.

Section 87.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Independence, Integration, War, and Sovereignty

This chapter has followed the process by which legal independence became territorial sovereignty, and it has shown that the process was longer, more varied, and more contested than the ceremonies of August 1947 suggested. The Indian Independence Act created two Dominions but left unresolved the status of the princely states, whose relationship with the Crown lapsed without any automatic successor. India’s government, using the instrument of accession and the persuasive power of Patel, Menon, and Mountbatten, secured the accession of nearly all of the states on limited terms and then, through merger agreements, unions, and privy purses, converted accession into administrative integration over the following years. Pakistan carried out a smaller parallel process on its side.

The chapter’s four analytical processes, legal accession, administrative integration, military control, and political legitimacy, help to explain the differences among the cases. In most states all four coincided or followed in sequence. In Junagadh, legal accession to Pakistan was overridden by Indian pressure and a referendum whose conditions Pakistan contested. In Hyderabad, a standstill agreement gave way to military control that preceded administrative integration, and violence afterward left a legacy that the state’s later history could not ignore. In Jammu and Kashmir, a legal accession to India, a political promise to consult the people, and a military intervention existed alongside a rebellion, an invasion, an international dispute, and a division of territory. The failure to align the four processes in Kashmir is what made it unique.

The chapter also shows why internationally recognized independence did not automatically settle territorial authority or political legitimacy. A state can be recognized and still not control all the territory it claims, and it can control territory without the consent of all its inhabitants. India’s consolidation, through institutional continuity, a strong central bureaucracy, and military superiority, and Pakistan’s territorial development, burdened by a shortage of institutions and a divided geography, followed different institutional and political paths, which Chapters 88 and 90 develop. Kashmir exposed a continuing conflict between competing legal, political, and representative claims that neither state, nor the United Nations, was able to resolve.

Students should also note the roles of people who are easily overlooked: the peasants of Telangana, the Praja Mandal activists, the insurgents of Poonch, the Gilgit Scouts, the people of Ladakh, and the refugees who crossed the lines. They were participants whose choices, as well as those of rulers and ministers, shaped outcomes. Their experiences, however, are more difficult to recover, and the documentary record is dominated by governments. This imbalance is itself a historical fact that students should bear in mind in evaluating claims about what “the people” wanted.

A comparison across the chapter offers a final test of any single explanation. Neither a ruler's signature, nor a religious majority, nor a neighboring government's military strength can independently account for all three contested accessions. Geography constrained Junagadh; negotiations and military superiority shaped Hyderabad; and internal rebellion, accession, invasion, conventional war, and United Nations diplomacy left Kashmir divided. In each case, the experiences of residents complicate the language of rulers and governments. Students should ask not only who possessed a legal claim at a particular moment, but who exercised authority, who could contest it, and what choices remained available to the population.

With the war ended and the ceasefire line fixed, the new states turned to internal consolidation. Chapter 88 turns to the Constituent Assembly and the Constitution of 1950, through which India defined itself as a sovereign democratic republic and in which the special provision for Jammu and Kashmir took its place. In doing so it opens Volume VI, which follows the histories of India, Pakistan, and the other states of South Asia after independence. The unresolved dispute over Kashmir, however, did not remain in the past. It reappears in the wars discussed in Chapter 92 and in the security questions examined in Chapter 105.

The Crisis of 1947 as Experienced by Different Groups in Jammu and Kashmir

These summaries are analytical illustrations based on documented patterns, not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not have equal power or equal responsibility.

The Maharaja and his administration: Facing the collapse of his forces and an armed invasion, the ruler sought rapid external help and secured it by accession, a decision that preserved his capital but ended his claim to independent authority.
Poonch insurgents and Azad Kashmir leaders: Many saw themselves as resisting an oppressive regime and as defending Muslim communities, and they sought support from Pakistan, while the later accession to India appeared to them as the Maharaja’s attempt to preserve his rule.
National Conference supporters in the Valley: Many welcomed an end to Dogra rule and the prospect of land reform and supported cooperation with India, though that support did not necessarily extend to a permanent settlement without consultation.
Hindu and Sikh minorities in the west: Many experienced the crisis as sudden violence and expulsion, and they looked to the Indian army and the Jammu authorities for safety.
Muslim villagers in Jammu province: Many experienced the crisis as attacks by organized bands and flight across the border, and they often had no say in the political decisions around them.
Ladakhi and northern communities: Buddhist leaders in Leh worried about domination by the Valley’s politics, while in Gilgit and Baltistan local rulers and soldiers pursued their own ends, shaped by opposition to Dogra rule.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Paramountcy to Union

What changed? The Crown’s paramountcy ended, hundreds of states lost their separate political existence, the map of South Asia was reorganized into two Dominions, and Kashmir’s status shifted from a Dogra state to a divided territory under two administrations.

What remained continuous? Many rulers retained wealth and status through privy purses; the Government of India Act of 1935 continued as the interim constitutional framework; much of the colonial administrative apparatus continued; and landholding structures, including jagirdari in Hyderabad, persisted until later reform.

Who experienced change most directly? Rulers, the displaced, the inhabitants of Hyderabad, and the people of Jammu and Kashmir.

Who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers in states that merged peacefully, whose daily lives changed less quickly than their formal status.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that formal sovereignty and lived experience changed at different speeds and that institutions often outlasted the regimes that created them.

Cause and Effect

Why Jammu and Kashmir Became a War and an International Dispute

Long-term causes

The Dogra state’s creation in 1846, its diverse and unevenly governed population, the politics of grievance against the Maharaja, and the lack of any agreed principle in 1947 for the states’ future.

Short-term causes

The Maharaja’s delay, the Poonch uprising, violence in Jammu, the tribal incursion, and the collapse of state forces in October 1947.

Event or transformation

The accession of 26–27 October, the Indian airlift, and the first India–Pakistan war.

Immediate effects

A divided state, mass displacement, the beginnings of UN involvement, and a ceasefire line.

Long-term consequences

A persistent India–Pakistan dispute, military deployments, constitutional arrangements within India, and political conflict within the state.

Historical complexity

No single actor or decision explains the outcome. Local rebellion, Pakistani support, Indian intervention, the Maharaja’s choices, and the limits of the UN all contributed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the legal independence of India and Pakistan in August 1947 was converted, through negotiation, pressure, war, and international mediation, into territorial sovereignty. Its central argument is that independence established two sovereign Dominions but left unsettled the questions of which territories each would control and on what basis, and that four processes, legal accession, administrative integration, military control, and political legitimacy, often diverged.

The Indian Independence Act created the Dominions on 15 August, with Pakistan observing 14 August as the day of its inauguration in Karachi. Both Dominions continued under the adapted Government of India Act of 1935 with the Crown as formal head of state. They inherited unequal institutions: India retained Delhi’s central bureaucracy and most military installations, while Pakistan had to build a capital, ministries, and financial institutions in Karachi across a geography divided into two wings.

Section 7 of the Act ended the Crown’s suzerainty over the princely states without transferring paramountcy to the Dominions. The states, which held about two-fifths of the subcontinent’s territory, thus faced a choice that few could exercise freely. Interpretations differed among the British, Congress, and the League, but geography and economic dependence made independence unrealistic for most.

Patel, Menon, and Mountbatten secured the accession of nearly all the states to India through standard instruments covering defense, external affairs, and communications, with assurances for rulers’ internal position. Pressure, inducements, and popular movements all contributed, and the process cannot be described as wholly voluntary or wholly coercive. Pakistan’s parallel process included the accession of Bahawalpur, Khairpur, and the northwestern states and the disputed incorporation of Kalat.

Junagadh’s Muslim Nawab acceded to Pakistan in a Hindu-majority state surrounded by Indian territory. India responded with political mobilization, economic pressure, and troops, took over administration on 9 November 1947 at the Dewan’s request, and held a referendum in February 1948 whose official result was overwhelmingly for India but whose conditions Pakistan contested. Hyderabad, the largest state, signed a standstill agreement but failed to reach a settlement, and India invaded in September 1948. The operation was followed by violence against civilians, chiefly Muslims, whose scale is estimated by the Sunderlal committee at between about 27,000 and 40,000 deaths and by others at different figures, and which demonstrates that coercion was integral to Hyderabad’s incorporation.

After accession, rulers agreed to mergers and unions in exchange for privy purses, and by about 1949 most of princely India had been absorbed into larger units. The process was thus gradual and multi-staged, extending well beyond Independence Day.

Jammu and Kashmir, a Dogra state with a majority-Muslim and regionally diverse population, was the most consequential case. After the Maharaja’s delay, the Poonch uprising, and the entry of Pashtun tribal forces from Pakistan on 22 October 1947, the state’s authority collapsed in the west. The Instrument of Accession dated 26 October and accepted on 27 October, with the stated intention of consulting the people, provided the basis for the Indian airlift. Historians have debated the exact timing and circumstances, and the legal accession, the political promise, and the military intervention must be distinguished.

The war that followed was fought in the Valley, Jammu, Poonch, Ladakh, Gilgit, and Baltistan, with a changing mix of forces that included, from 1948, Pakistan’s regular army. India took the dispute to the United Nations, which established UNCIP and, in Resolution 47, recommended steps toward withdrawal and a plebiscite. The resolutions of 1948 and 1949 laid out stages of ceasefire, truce, and plebiscite consultation. Only the ceasefire, taking effect on 1 January 1949, and the Karachi Agreement of 27 July 1949 establishing the ceasefire line were carried out. The line was a military demarcation, not an international boundary, and should not be confused with the later Line of Control.

The war and the partition of the state produced mass violence and displacement affecting Muslims in Jammu and non-Muslims in the west, as well as political change under Sheikh Abdullah’s administration. The chapter closes by comparing Junagadh, Hyderabad, and Kashmir, observing that outcomes depended on rulers’ decisions, population, geography, and force, and that the unresolved Kashmir dispute shaped India–Pakistan relations. Chapter 88 turns to India’s Constitution and the Republic, and Volume VI later returns to the dispute.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 87 shows that the end of empire was not a single moment but a sequence of acts through which new states defined, claimed, and sometimes fought over their territory. Studying it requires holding together the legal, administrative, military, and political dimensions of sovereignty, and recognizing that they can come apart. The cases of Junagadh, Hyderabad, and Kashmir demonstrate that the same principles, the ruler’s authority, the people’s preference, geographic contiguity, and strategic need, could be invoked in different ways depending on the circumstances and the interests of those invoking them.

The chapter also shows how much of the later history of South Asia was shaped in these months. The consolidation of the Indian Union gave the new state a territorial basis that Pakistan, with its divided geography and shortage of institutions, did not have in the same form. The Kashmir dispute created a lasting source of confrontation and a framework of UN involvement that has persisted. And the violence in Hyderabad and the displacement in Jammu and Kashmir joined the memories of Partition as experiences that shaped communities’ relations with the states that governed them.

Within the six-volume sequence, this chapter forms the bridge between the history of nationalism, war, and Partition and the history of postcolonial state-building. It brings Volume V to a close by showing how the end of British rule produced two functioning but territorially contested states, and it opens Volume VI’s questions about constitutions, development, and the relations among South Asian states. Students should carry forward the habit of asking, in each later chapter, whose consent a settlement rested upon, and what it took for that settlement to endure.
