Nuclear Politics, SAARC, and Regional Interdependence

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 18 May 1974, in the Rajasthan desert near the town of Pokhran, a device buried in an underground shaft detonated. India's government described it as a peaceful nuclear explosion, and for many Indians the event symbolized scientific accomplishment and strategic independence. In Pakistan it provoked alarm. In Washington, Ottawa, and other capitals, it prompted anxiety about the future of the nonproliferation regime that the 1968 Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty had tried to build. The explosion demonstrated technical capability. It did not, by itself, establish a deployed nuclear arsenal, and one task of this chapter is to explain precisely what was and was not established.

Eleven years later, in December 1985, the leaders of seven South Asian countries gathered in Dhaka. India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives signed the Charter of the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC). The signing included heads of state whose governments were military rulers, monarchs, and elected leaders, and whose countries had been at war, suspected one another of hostile intentions, or disagreed about trade, water, and borders. Their agreement is the other starting point for this chapter.

The contrast between Pokhran and Dhaka creates the chapter's central question. Why did governments that were developing increasingly sophisticated instruments of strategic competition also agree to build an institution dedicated to regional cooperation? The answer is not that one development was sincere and the other cynical. Governments pursued security through military and technological means while also seeking diplomatic arrangements that could reduce risk, widen their international options, or manage the burdens of neighborhood. Both patterns were real and they affected one another.

The chapter also widens the perspective beyond governments. During the same decades, millions of South Asians sought work abroad, especially in the oil-rich states of the Persian Gulf, and sent earnings home to villages in Kerala, Punjab, Sylhet, Sri Lanka, and elsewhere. Families rebuilt houses, paid school fees, and repaid debts. Migrants also lived under contract systems that gave them few rights. Older diasporas in Africa, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia, and newer communities in Britain, Canada, and the United States, connected South Asia to wider global networks. These movements were produced by households, recruiters, employers, and markets, not primarily by diplomatic agreements.

The chapter argues that South Asia became simultaneously more strategically divided and more economically and socially interconnected between 1974 and 1991. Formal political integration remained limited. SAARC's Charter excluded bilateral and contentious issues and required unanimity, and official intraregional trade remained small. Yet through labor markets, remittances, informal exchange, and diasporas, the region's societies were bound together and to the wider world in ways that governments only partly controlled.

Chapter 97 completes Part XVII. Earlier chapters treated the period largely through national histories: Chapter 93 on India's democratic crisis, Chapter 94 on its social and regional politics, Chapter 95 on Pakistan, and Chapter 96 on Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the smaller states. This chapter assembles those histories into a regional synthesis, examining the issues that crossed borders. It does not repeat the Bangladesh War narrative (Chapter 92), the political history of Pakistan under Bhutto and Zia (Chapter 95), or the domestic conflicts of the smaller states (Chapter 96). Pakistan's nuclear tests of 1998 belong to Chapter 101, and India's economic liberalization to Chapter 98.

The evidence for this chapter is varied and sometimes difficult. Nuclear history relies on declassified diplomatic records, legislation, memoirs, and scholarly reconstructions, and many claims about secret programs remain contested. SAARC history rests on treaty texts and summit documents. Migration and remittance history depends on balance-of-payments statistics, International Labour Organization reports, household studies, and migrant testimony. Each source type answers some questions well and others poorly, and this chapter is explicit about those limits. It also distinguishes verified milestones from attributed claims throughout.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish civilian atomic-energy development, nuclear-explosive capability, and an operational nuclear arsenal, and apply the distinctions to India and Pakistan.
  2. Explain the origins and international consequences of India's 1974 nuclear explosion.
  3. Analyze how the 1971 defeat, the Indian test, and Pakistan's institutions shaped the development of Pakistan's nuclear program.
  4. Assess the role of nonproliferation law, including the Symington and Pressler provisions, and the way Cold War priorities affected its enforcement.
  5. Evaluate the Brasstacks and 1990 crises and the limits of the evidence for claims about nuclear escalation.
  6. Explain the significance and limitations of the 1988 agreement on nuclear installations.
  7. Trace the diplomatic origins of SAARC, from Ziaur Rahman's initiative to the Dhaka summit of 1985.
  8. Analyze the SAARC Charter, including unanimity and the exclusion of bilateral issues, and assess early institutional achievements.
  9. Explain why geographic proximity did not produce extensive formal intraregional trade before 1991.
  10. Describe the structure of Gulf labor migration, including recruitment, gender, and the differences between temporary and permanent migration.
  11. Interpret remittance statistics carefully and assess competing views of remittances as development resource and source of dependence.
  12. Connect diasporas, the end of the Cold War, and the Gulf crisis to the pressures facing South Asia in 1991.

Historical Context

Chapters 88 through 96 traced the political construction of the postcolonial states of South Asia. India became a parliamentary republic under the Constitution of 1950, pursued planned development, and followed a policy of nonalignment while building close defense and trade ties with the Soviet Union. Pakistan, created in 1947 in two wings separated by Indian territory, struggled with constitutional instability and military rule, and lost its eastern wing in 1971. Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives each followed distinct paths as independent or semi-independent states. By 1971 South Asia contained seven sovereign states of very unequal size, with India dominant in population, territory, and industrial capacity.

The Cold War structured the region's external relations. Pakistan joined American-sponsored alliances in the 1950s, later turned toward China, and from 1979 became a partner of the United States in the Afghan war. India chose nonalignment, fought a border war with China in 1962, and signed a treaty of peace, friendship, and cooperation with the Soviet Union in 1971. Smaller states used balancing strategies of their own. The result was a region connected to the global rivalry yet marked by its own conflicts, above all the dispute over Kashmir and the India–Pakistan rivalry.

Economically, most governments pursued state-led development with high tariffs and licensing controls, with Sri Lanka's partial liberalization after 1977 as a notable exception. The Green Revolution, discussed in Chapter 94, changed agriculture in parts of India and Pakistan. Population growth and limited employment created pressure to find work elsewhere. Meanwhile, the 1973 oil crisis transformed the Gulf states into employers of foreign labor, and migration became one of the main ways South Asian households connected to the global economy.

This chapter should be read as a regional synthesis. It uses developments already narrated in the national chapters but asks different questions: how did security competition, diplomacy, labor migration, and diasporas interact across borders, and what did they mean for the region as a whole by 1991?

Nuclear Technology, National Security, and the Emergence of Strategic Competition

How India and Pakistan moved from civilian atomic science toward the strategic ambiguity that would shape the region for decades.

Section 97.1

India's Atomic Energy Program and the Origins of the 1974 Nuclear Test

India's nuclear history began as a project of scientific state-building rather than weapons design. Homi J. Bhabha, a Cambridge-trained physicist who had helped found the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in the mid-1940s, persuaded Jawaharlal Nehru that atomic energy could underpin an independent, industrializing India. The Atomic Energy Commission was created in 1948, and the Department of Atomic Energy followed in 1954, placing nuclear research under the prime minister's direct authority. A research reactor, Apsara, began operating at Trombay near Bombay in 1956; the Canada-India reactor known as CIRUS followed in 1960 with Canadian design and American heavy water; and the Tarapur power station, built with American assistance, entered service in 1969. Bhabha's three-stage plan, designed around India's large thorium reserves, linked the program to long-term energy self-sufficiency.

This was an internationalist enterprise. India trained physicists and engineers abroad, imported reactors and materials from Canada and the United States, and participated in the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) after its founding in 1957. Nehru simultaneously became one of the most prominent public advocates of nuclear disarmament and an end to weapons testing. These positions were not contradictory in his own account: he argued that India would use atomic energy for peaceful purposes while urging the nuclear powers to restrain themselves. Yet Bhabha's institutions also developed the plutonium-handling, reprocessing, and engineering capacities that would later make a weapons option technically feasible. The key historical point is that the same infrastructure served several possible futures.

The strategic environment changed in the 1960s. India's border war with China in 1962 exposed military weaknesses and damaged confidence in diplomatic nonalignment as a security policy. China's first nuclear test at Lop Nur in October 1964 placed a nuclear-armed state on India's northern frontier. Wars with Pakistan in 1947–48 and 1965 sustained the sense of a threatened environment on a second front. Bhabha died in a plane crash in January 1966, and Lal Bahadur Shastri, who had also died that month, was succeeded by Indira Gandhi. Debate within India's political class, scientific establishment, and press intensified over whether India should seek nuclear weapons, rely on security guarantees from the nuclear powers, or retain only the technical option.

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), opened for signature in 1968, forced the question into diplomacy. India sought credible security assurances from nuclear-armed states against nuclear threats and did not obtain them in a form it regarded as adequate. It also objected that the treaty divided the world into states that could legitimately hold nuclear weapons and states that could not. Because the treaty defined a nuclear-weapon state as one that had tested before 1 January 1967, India's later test could never place it within that category. India therefore remained outside the treaty, a decision that structured its international position for the following half-century.

Historians must distinguish three conditions that are easily blurred. A civilian atomic-energy program produces electricity, isotopes, and research. A nuclear-explosive capability means the ability to detonate a device, which requires fissile material, a design, and a test or high confidence in an untested design. An operational nuclear arsenal requires weaponization, delivery systems, command arrangements, and a doctrine of deployment. By the early 1970s India's program had moved toward the second condition. It had not entered the third. Indira Gandhi's government authorized preparations for an explosive test in the early 1970s, but historians still debate how clearly its leaders had defined the purpose beyond demonstrating technical competence, enhancing prestige, and keeping options open. Accounts by participants and later researchers differ on these points, and the chapter treats claims about intentions as interpretations rather than settled facts.

Domestic politics and institutional design also mattered. Atomic energy was organized as a closely held enterprise, answerable to the prime minister and shielded from routine parliamentary scrutiny, and this secrecy allowed a technical option to be preserved without a public decision to build weapons. Public debate nevertheless grew after 1964. Some parties and commentators, including the Jana Sangh, argued that India needed a bomb; others, particularly on the left and within the Gandhian tradition, rejected nuclear weapons on moral and economic grounds. Later accounts report that Prime Minister Shastri authorized studies of peaceful nuclear explosions in 1965, though the accounts differ on how far the work proceeded before Indira Gandhi's government gave it fresh impetus in the early 1970s. The decision was therefore cumulative, taken across several governments and institutions, and not a single moment of choice.

Section 97.2

Pokhran, May 18, 1974: India's Nuclear Explosion and Its International Consequences

On 18 May 1974, India detonated a nuclear device in an underground shaft at the Pokhran test range in the Rajasthan desert. The government announced that it had conducted a peaceful nuclear explosion, a category some states, including the United States and the Soviet Union, had explored for excavation and engineering uses. The operation was a project of the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, with Raja Ramanna prominent among the scientists, and the prime minister, Indira Gandhi, authorized it. The device used plutonium produced in the CIRUS reactor, a fact that raised immediate questions about the conditions under which Canada and the United States had supplied equipment, heavy water, and fuel. Indian officials argued that the cooperation agreements concerned peaceful uses and that the explosion was consistent with that description. Canada, which had supplied the reactor, rejected the argument and suspended nuclear cooperation.

The domestic political setting mattered. In 1974 Indira Gandhi's government faced inflation, labor unrest, a national railway strike, and the growing opposition movements discussed in Chapter 93. The test was widely popular in India and was celebrated as scientific accomplishment and strategic independence. Opposition parties across the spectrum largely welcomed it, although critics questioned its costs and some scientists and commentators questioned its purpose. Historians generally agree that it gave the government a moment of national prestige during a difficult year, while cautioning against reducing the decision to short-term domestic politics, since the technical preparations ran across several years and several institutions.

International responses were immediate and divided. The United States expressed concern, and declassified diplomatic records show officials weighing its effect on nonproliferation, South Asian stability, and relations with both India and Pakistan. Contemporary American records document both the test itself and the concern over its consequences for the nonproliferation regime. Canada ended cooperation. The explosion also helped lead major supplier states to form what became the Nuclear Suppliers Group in 1975, an arrangement to coordinate export controls on sensitive nuclear technology. In the United States, the shock contributed to later legislation such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Act of 1978. These measures altered the environment in which both India and Pakistan sought materials and equipment.

Pakistan's response was the most strategically consequential. Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto described the test as a threat to Pakistan's security and sought assurances from major powers, while also strengthening the nuclear effort he had already begun after 1971 (Section 97.3). India offered assurances that it had no intention of developing weapons, which Pakistani leaders did not regard as binding because capability can be converted into policy more quickly than intentions can be verified. This asymmetry between capability and declared intent is central to the later history of South Asian nuclear ambiguity.

A careful account must not overstate the result. India in 1974 had demonstrated that it could produce and detonate a nuclear explosive device. It had not deployed nuclear weapons, announced a nuclear-weapons doctrine, built an operational delivery system for them, or declared itself a nuclear-weapon state. Subsequent Indian governments, particularly after the Janata government took office in 1977, spoke publicly of peaceful intentions. India did not conduct further tests until 1998. In the long period between, the country maintained what scholars call a policy of nuclear ambiguity or recessed deterrence: capability without declared deployment.

The dispute over Canadian cooperation illustrates the legal ambiguity that surrounded the test. Canada's agreements with India required that the CIRUS reactor and its products be used for peaceful purposes. Indian officials argued that a peaceful nuclear explosion fell within that description, while Canada and the United States held that the assurances had never been meant to cover any explosive device. The operation was later popularly known as Smiling Buddha, a code phrase associated with the fact that the test occurred on the Buddhist festival of Buddha Jayanti, though students should treat the nickname as part of later memory and not as evidence about intent. Estimates of the explosive yield varied between Indian official figures and outside assessments, and the exact yield remains a matter on which sources differ. After the Janata government took office in 1977, India declared that it would not conduct further tests, and disputes over American fuel supplies for Tarapur continued through the late 1970s and early 1980s, showing how the 1974 decision continued to affect India's access to foreign technology.

Historical Vocabulary
Peaceful Nuclear Explosion and Nuclear Ambiguity

A peaceful nuclear explosion (PNE) was a term used by several governments in the 1960s and 1970s for nuclear detonations described as civil engineering tools, such as excavation. Technically, the explosive device used in such an explosion cannot be cleanly separated from one used in a weapon, which is why the NPT restricted the practice for non-nuclear-weapon states that joined it. Nuclear ambiguity describes a condition in which a state is widely believed to possess nuclear-weapon capability but does not formally declare weapons possession or deployment.

Students should avoid treating either phrase as an excuse or an accusation. Both are historical terms used by governments and analysts. Their value is that they help distinguish what India demonstrated in 1974 from what neither India nor Pakistan had publicly established by 1991.

Section 97.3

Pakistan's Nuclear Program: Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, Scientific Institutions, and Strategic Response

Pakistan's nuclear program did not begin in 1974. The Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission (PAEC) was created in 1956, following the American Atoms for Peace initiative, and Pakistan developed training programs, a research reactor at the Pakistan Institute of Nuclear Science and Technology (PINSTECH), and, with Canadian assistance, the Karachi Nuclear Power Plant (KANUPP), which began operating in 1972. In the 1960s Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, then foreign minister under Ayub Khan, had already argued publicly that Pakistan should keep a nuclear option open if India acquired one. His reasoning, often cited later, was that conventional inferiority and the experience of 1965 made a deterrent attractive.

The 1971 war, examined in Chapter 92, was the decisive turning point. Pakistan lost its eastern wing, tens of thousands of soldiers became prisoners of war in Indian custody, and its leaders concluded that conventional parity with India would be difficult to restore. In January 1972, soon after becoming president, Bhutto convened senior scientists in Multan and called on them to develop a nuclear-weapons capability. Munir Ahmad Khan, a reactor engineer who had worked at the IAEA, became PAEC chairman that year and led the initial effort. The Multan meeting is therefore a more important origin than the Indian explosion of 1974, although the explosion strengthened domestic and international arguments within Pakistan.

Pakistan's early strategy followed two routes. One was plutonium: acquiring reprocessing technology, including through negotiations with France that the United States opposed, and that France ended in 1978 under pressure. The other, which became dominant, was uranium enrichment, which does not require a reactor and produces material in a smaller and less conspicuous facility. Both strategies depended on foreign technology, imported components, and informal procurement networks, because Pakistan had limited domestic industrial capacity for sensitive equipment.

Historians therefore caution against reading the Pakistani program as a mechanical reaction to India. Several factors operated together: the 1971 defeat; concerns about conventional imbalance; Bhutto's personal convictions about Pakistan's place in the world; institutional competition between civilian scientific agencies and the military; the changing nuclear policies of Canada, France, and the United States; and the availability of partnerships and technology. Indian policy contributed to Pakistani threat perceptions but did not fully explain Pakistani decisions, just as Chinese capabilities contributed to Indian ones without fully determining them.

The political and ideological setting of the Pakistani program should be understood with care. Bhutto had argued during the 1960s that Pakistan might have to make great sacrifices to match India's capability, a statement often summarized with the phrase about eating grass that is frequently attributed to him, and he later presented the effort as a matter of national survival. Pakistan's partnerships with the United States through the alliances of the 1950s had not produced the support its leaders expected in 1965 or 1971, which deepened skepticism about external guarantees, and relations with China became increasingly significant. Some commentators later called the program an Islamic bomb, referring to a supposed pan-Islamic dimension and Gulf financing; this phrase was a polemical label with contested evidentiary support, and historians treat claims about foreign funding cautiously. The program also outlasted the politician who began it: Bhutto was overthrown by Zia in 1977 and executed in 1979, but the program continued under military control, showing that its institutions had become part of the Pakistani state.

External pressure on Pakistan's reprocessing plans grew during the mid-1970s. Canada ended nuclear cooperation with Pakistan's KANUPP station in 1976 after the 1974 test sharpened its concerns about safeguards, which complicated the plant's fuel and spare-parts supply. The French agreement to supply a reprocessing plant, which Pakistan had negotiated in 1976, drew sustained American objection; accounts of the pressure applied by American officials, including during Secretary of State Henry Kissinger's contacts with Bhutto, vary in their details and in how forcefully they describe the pressure. France ultimately withdrew from the project in 1978. These setbacks did not end the program; they encouraged Pakistan to place greater emphasis on enrichment, which could be pursued in smaller, less visible facilities. The sequence illustrates how supplier restrictions altered the technical path without ending the political commitment.

Section 97.4

Uranium Enrichment, A. Q. Khan, and Pakistan's Developing Nuclear Capability

Abdul Qadeer Khan, a Pakistani metallurgist trained in Europe, worked in the Netherlands for a subcontractor to the URENCO enrichment consortium in the early 1970s, where he had access to centrifuge technology and supplier information. In 1975 he approached Bhutto's government and returned to Pakistan the following year. In 1976 an enrichment-oriented organization, soon known as the Engineering Research Laboratories, was established at Kahuta near Islamabad; Zia-ul-Haq's government renamed it the Khan Research Laboratories in 1981. The history of Kahuta involves several sensitive claims about technology transfers and foreign procurement. Scholars and journalists generally agree that the program relied heavily on clandestine acquisition of equipment and components through European and other intermediaries, though the details differ by source.

Pakistan's nuclear organization was not a single institution. PAEC, under Munir Ahmad Khan, retained responsibility for reactors, plutonium work, and weapons-design activities as described in later accounts, while the Kahuta laboratories under A. Q. Khan developed enrichment. Military leaders, especially after Zia's 1977 coup, increasingly controlled strategic direction and security. Rivalries between the organizations and between their leaders have been described in later memoirs and studies, and these are sometimes self-serving. For example, public attribution of credit has itself been a political question in Pakistan, and students should treat memoir claims with caution.

The program passed through the foreign-policy turbulence of the Zia years (see Chapter 95). Before 1979, American law, including the Symington Amendment (1976) and later provisions, restricted assistance to states importing enrichment technology outside safeguards, and the United States suspended aid to Pakistan in April 1979 on that basis. After the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979, Washington reassessed its priorities, offered a large military and economic assistance package, and obtained congressional waivers. This shift illustrates how regional security needs shaped enforcement of nonproliferation law. By the mid-1980s officials in several governments publicly or privately concluded that Pakistan was approaching or had reached a threshold capability, although they differed on its timing and character.

Pakistani officials denied that the program was military. In the 1980s they described it as peaceful and argued that Pakistan was prepared to accept equal restraints if India accepted them as well. In 1987 an interview with A. Q. Khan published by the journalist Kuldip Nayar was widely read as a signal that Pakistan possessed the ability to build nuclear weapons; Khan later disputed or qualified aspects of it. This episode belongs to the era of deliberate ambiguity, in which signaling was often indirect and subject to denial.

The historian should therefore separate categories of evidence. Public claims are statements by governments and officials. Intelligence assessments are judgments, often later declassified, made with incomplete information. Scientific accomplishments are documented facilities and processes. Independently confirmed events are those supported by verifiable physical or documentary evidence. Pakistan did not conduct publicly acknowledged nuclear explosive tests until May 1998, which belong to Chapter 101. For the period before 1991, the safest conclusion is that Pakistan developed a substantial and increasingly capable enrichment and weapons-related program and that governments acted as if a Pakistani explosive capability was approaching or had been achieved, while the exact status at particular dates remains contested.

Accounts of Khan's career illustrate the problem of evidence. A Dutch court convicted Khan in absentia in 1983 on espionage-related charges concerning the removal of centrifuge information, and the conviction was overturned on a procedural ground in 1985, which meant that the underlying allegations were never fully adjudicated. Reports in later decades, including intelligence assessments and investigative studies, described reported assistance from outside suppliers, including China, in aspects of Pakistan's program; Pakistani sources have rejected or qualified some of these claims, and the evidence varies in reliability. The proliferation network associated with Khan that was exposed in the early 2000s lies beyond the chapter's chronology and should not be projected back onto every episode of the 1970s and 1980s. The responsible conclusion for this period is that the enrichment program was real, large, and heavily dependent on imported technology, while the details of foreign assistance remain contested.

The state of the program in the early and mid-1980s is documented chiefly through later assessments and statements by officials. Western intelligence assessments of the period concluded that Pakistan was producing enriched uranium at Kahuta, and American officials warned Pakistan against crossing particular thresholds of enrichment; accounts differ on what was said and on when particular milestones were passed. Claims about Pakistani tests of weapon designs without nuclear yield, sometimes called cold tests, circulated from the early 1980s, but they derive from later memoirs and press accounts of varying reliability. Pakistani officials continued to deny a weapons program, a position that preserved diplomatic room for continued American assistance. The historian can therefore document institutions, facilities, and procurement efforts with reasonable confidence, while treating the timing of specific weapons-related achievements as uncertain.

India–Pakistan Strategic Rivalry, Nuclear Diplomacy, and Confidence Building

How a rivalry sharpened by the 1971 war interacted with the diplomacy of nonproliferation, crisis management, and limited agreements.

Section 97.5

India–Pakistan Strategic Competition after 1971

The 1971 war transformed the strategic balance between India and Pakistan. The Simla Agreement of July 1972 committed both governments to resolve disputes bilaterally, converted the Kashmir ceasefire line into the Line of Control, and returned prisoners and territory after negotiation. Yet agreement did not end suspicion. For India, the war demonstrated superior conventional capability and removed the eastern-front threat that had complicated planning in 1965. For Pakistan, the same outcome reinforced the fear that a larger neighbor could use force to alter its territorial and political structure. Chapter 92 narrates the war; here the important point is how both governments interpreted its lessons.

Military expenditure, force structure, and alliance relationships reflected those interpretations. India's armed forces were larger and supported by an expanding defense industry and an arms relationship with the Soviet Union, formalized in the Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation of August 1971. Pakistan, after the collapse of its earlier alliance commitments, sought external support from China, Muslim-majority states, and, after 1979, a renewed partnership with the United States, discussed in Chapter 95. These relationships reduced but did not remove the conventional imbalance. Indian planners also had to consider China, a consideration Pakistani planners could use diplomatically, but which also strained resources.

Kashmir remained a central issue. The Line of Control became the practical boundary after 1972, but neither government accepted the other's claims, and each interpreted its own position as consistent with the agreement. From 1984 the two armies faced one another on the Siachen Glacier, where an Indian operation in April 1984 occupied the main passes in one of the world's highest battlefields. Border incidents, spy cases, accusations of support for insurgents, and disputes over river waters and trade shaped a persistent climate of mistrust. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 remained an exception: it survived the wars and continued to function as a rare instrument of technical cooperation.

Scholars distinguish three concepts that are often conflated. Conventional deterrence relies on conventional forces to discourage attack. Nuclear deterrence depends on the credible threat of nuclear retaliation. Political coercion uses military capability and signaling to extract concessions without necessarily fighting. India and Pakistan experimented with all three in the 1970s and 1980s, but nuclear capability remained undeclared, which complicated each. A deterrent that is not publicly acknowledged may be less effective at signaling, while public acknowledgment could provoke sanctions and arms racing. Governments appear to have accepted ambiguity as a compromise, though historians dispute how deliberate this choice was.

Military modernization reinforced mutual suspicion. India continued to import advanced aircraft and equipment from the Soviet Union and added Western systems such as the Jaguar and Mirage 2000 strike and fighter aircraft in the 1980s. Pakistan's agreement with the United States in 1981 for a multi-year assistance package of about $3.2 billion included F-16 fighters, delivered beginning in 1983, which Indian officials regarded as a significant change in the regional balance. Each government cited the other's purchases to justify its own. Allegations about support for insurgents added another layer. Indian officials in the 1980s accused Pakistan of aiding Sikh militancy in Punjab, and Pakistani officials denied such claims while criticizing Indian policy in Kashmir and its role in Sri Lanka. These allegations were politically charged, and historians generally treat them as contested unless supported by documentary evidence. What can be said is that the climate of the 1980s was one in which each government expected the other to exploit its internal difficulties.

The burden of defense differed in the two states. Pakistan, a considerably smaller economy, devoted a substantially higher share of its national income to defense than India did, and the military's institutional influence in domestic politics under Zia further increased the weight of security considerations in policy. India's larger economy allowed larger absolute expenditure and also supported a domestic arms industry, but India also had to plan for two possible fronts, one facing Pakistan and the other facing China. These differences shaped how each government viewed risk. For Pakistan, the central problem was preventing a decisive defeat by a larger neighbor; for India, it was maintaining sufficient forces on more than one frontier without sacrificing development priorities. The tension between military requirements and economic priorities appears in the policy debates of both states.

Section 97.6

Nonproliferation, Technology Controls, and International Diplomacy

The NPT created a legal distinction between five recognized nuclear-weapon states, defined by tests conducted before 1967, and all other parties, which agreed to forgo weapons and accept IAEA safeguards in exchange for access to peaceful nuclear technology and a commitment by the weapon states to pursue disarmament. India refused to sign because it regarded the treaty as discriminatory and as an instrument that would freeze an unequal order while leaving China outside the commitments of non-nuclear states. Pakistan declined to sign principally because India had not, arguing that it could not accept constraints that left its larger neighbor free. Both governments claimed that their positions could change if the other changed, a statement that functioned as a mutual veto.

The responses to the 1974 test strengthened technology controls. The formation of the Nuclear Suppliers Group, the American Nuclear Non-Proliferation Act of 1978, and tightened policies in Canada and Western Europe made it harder for India and Pakistan to obtain sensitive items legally. India's problem was chiefly fuel and technology for the Tarapur station and heavy-water supplies, issues that produced disputes with the United States through the 1970s and 1980s. Pakistan's problem was access to enrichment and reprocessing technology and the components they required. Both states responded with greater domestic development and clandestine or indirect acquisition where necessary, strategies that made them subjects of international suspicion.

American legislation provides the clearest examples. The Symington Amendment, added to the Foreign Assistance Act in 1976, restricted economic and military assistance to countries that delivered or received nuclear enrichment equipment or technology outside international safeguards. It was applied to Pakistan in April 1979. The Glenn Amendment of 1977 addressed reprocessing. The Pressler Amendment, adopted in 1985, required the president to certify annually that Pakistan did not possess a nuclear explosive device and that proposed assistance would significantly reduce the risk of its possessing one. Presidential certifications permitted aid for fiscal years 1986 through 1990; the last was issued in 1989, while Pakistan was cooperating in the Afghan war. When fiscal year 1991 began in October 1990, President George H. W. Bush did not issue the requisite certification; the resulting restrictions halted most bilateral economic assistance and military aid. The legislation and the decisions to certify or withhold are separate historical matters: the law created a standard, while executive decisions reflected political judgment and strategic context.

These episodes show a recurring pattern. Washington's nonproliferation objectives and its broader Cold War goals sometimes conflicted, and the result was uneven enforcement that both South Asian governments could interpret as evidence of inconsistent international rules. Indian leaders observed that nuclear weapons states did not accept equal obligations, and Pakistani leaders observed that sanctions depended on geopolitical utility. In both countries, such interpretations strengthened domestic support for autonomous capabilities.

Diplomatic proposals illustrate the competing visions of regional restraint. Pakistan proposed in 1974 that South Asia be declared a nuclear-weapon-free zone, a proposal it brought repeatedly to the United Nations, and later suggested measures such as mutual inspections and regional test bans. India rejected the zone proposal on the ground that it excluded China and that regional arrangements could not address a global nuclear order. India, for its part, promoted universal disarmament, and in June 1988 Rajiv Gandhi presented a plan at the United Nations special session on disarmament calling for the elimination of nuclear weapons in stages. Each side's proposals were consistent with its strategic interests. Pakistan sought measures that would constrain the larger Indian program; India sought measures that would bind all nuclear states, including China, and not only itself. The result was a stalemate in which both states supported nonproliferation in principle while refusing the specific arrangements the other proposed. The pattern shaped regional diplomacy until the 1998 tests changed its terms.

Export controls were refined in the late 1970s. The Nuclear Suppliers Group published guidelines in 1978 that listed sensitive items and called for safeguards on exports, and member governments gradually tightened conditions on transfers, a process that continued through the 1980s and into the 1990s when full-scope safeguards became a more general requirement. These measures made it harder for India to obtain items such as heavy water and fuel from outside suppliers, which increased its emphasis on indigenous production. They also made Pakistan's acquisition of sensitive technology dependent on clandestine channels. The international system thus pushed both states toward self-reliance and secrecy, even as it sought to slow their progress.

Section 97.7

Crisis and Nuclear Ambiguity: Brasstacks and India–Pakistan Tensions in the Late 1980s

The Brasstacks crisis arose from a large Indian military exercise in Rajasthan, conducted from late 1986 into early 1987 under the army chief, General Krishnaswamy Sundarji. It was exceptionally large, involved coordinated infantry, armor, mechanized, and air elements, and took place near the Pakistani border. Pakistani leaders interpreted the exercise as a possible cover for an attack, and Pakistan responded by moving forces and raising readiness. India, in turn, observed Pakistani movements and mobilized additional units. By January 1987 the two armies faced one another across a long frontier with reciprocal fears of surprise.

Diplomacy reduced the danger. Officials communicated through military and diplomatic channels, and talks in early 1987 produced agreements to pull back forces. In February, President Zia-ul-Haq traveled to India ostensibly to watch a cricket match in Jaipur, a form of informal diplomacy remembered in the region as cricket diplomacy. The details of what each government intended remain debated. Indian accounts commonly describe Brasstacks as a legitimate exercise whose scale was misunderstood. Pakistani accounts commonly describe it as a provocation or at least a dangerous ambiguity. Scholars have used archival and interview evidence to argue about how close either side was to initiating war.

Nuclear uncertainty was part of the setting. Pakistani and Indian leaders did not publicly acknowledge nuclear weapons, yet each had reason to suspect the other of significant capability. The Khan interview published in March 1987 was read in that context. Some later retrospective accounts, including statements by former officials and journalists, claimed that the crisis or the 1990 crisis nearly led to nuclear war. These claims vary in credibility and detail. This chapter treats them as attributed assertions and notes that historians disagree on whether decision-makers seriously approached nuclear use. A more cautious conclusion is that leaders in both states were uncertain about the other's capabilities and intentions and that their uncertainty heightened the danger of misjudgment.

The crisis of 1990 followed the intensification of the insurgency in Kashmir (examined in Chapter 105) and rhetoric in both countries. During the spring of 1990 the two governments moved military forces and exchanged threatening statements. President Bush sent Deputy National Security Adviser Robert Gates to Islamabad and New Delhi in May 1990 to urge restraint and to encourage communication. Accounts of the Gates mission differ in their emphasis, and later claims about nuclear readiness remain contested; some American analysts later argued that the crisis was dangerous, while others argued that the leaders were never close to deploying nuclear weapons. What the evidence supports is that international intervention and bilateral contact helped defuse the crisis, and that both governments learned that rapid escalation could occur in a nuclear-ambiguous environment.

The scale of the exercise helps explain why it was alarming. Estimates of the numbers involved are commonly given in the hundreds of thousands of troops, although sources differ on the exact composition of forces and on how many were actually participating in exercise phases as opposed to being placed in reserve positions. Accounts also differ on the degree of coordination between military and civilian leadership in India. Some analyses suggest that political leaders, including the prime minister and defense officials, had not fully appreciated the implications of the army's plan, while Indian military accounts emphasize that the exercise followed established planning. Pakistan's military and intelligence leaders interpreted indicators of Indian movement in the worst light, which led to counter-mobilization. Such differences in interpretation are typical of crises in which each side watches the other's movements with incomplete information. The episode shows how routine military activity can be read as preparation for war, an interpretive risk that nuclear ambiguity magnified because neither side was certain what the other could or would do.

The interpretation of Zia's visit to Jaipur illustrates the difficulty of reconstructing private diplomacy. Reports describe a brief exchange between Zia and Rajiv Gandhi at the airport in which Zia warned that war would have grave consequences, an account that has been cited as an example of implicit nuclear signaling, though participants and analysts disagree about what was said and how it was meant. The crisis also had institutional consequences. Both militaries reviewed their planning, communication procedures, and exercise practices, and the two governments agreed to improve channels for notification of large exercises. Later analysts have debated whether the crisis demonstrated the stabilizing effect of caution or the dangers of misperception. This chapter treats it as evidence for both possibilities, a view reflected in the historical debate in Section 97.8.

Section 97.8

Arms Control and Confidence Building: The 1988 Nuclear Installations Agreement

The most concrete diplomatic result of this period was the Agreement on the Prohibition of Attack against Nuclear Installations and Facilities. Foreign Secretaries Humayun Khan (Pakistan) and K. P. S. Menon (India) signed it on 31 December 1988 on the margins of the SAARC summit in Islamabad, and it entered into force on 27 January 1991 after ratification. Its principal commitment was that each government would refrain from undertaking, encouraging, or participating in any action aimed at causing the destruction of, or damage to, a nuclear installation or facility in the other country. Annual exchanges of lists of covered installations began in 1992, outside this chapter's principal chronology but an important consequence of the agreement.

The origins of the agreement lay partly in the fears of the early and mid-1980s. Both governments were aware of speculation that one might attempt a preventive attack on the other's emerging nuclear facilities, and the possibility of attacks on Kahuta or Trombay appeared in press and policy commentary. The Brasstacks crisis, the change of government in Pakistan after Zia's death in August 1988, and Rajiv Gandhi's interest in improved relations created an opening. Negotiations also reflected a belief that limited agreements could reduce the risk of one particular form of escalation without requiring either government to disclose or constrain its programs.

The agreement's significance and limitations must both be stated. It was a real commitment in a field where governments often avoid formal agreements, and it has been observed through subsequent crises, including exchanges of installation lists each year despite military confrontation. But it did not require inspections, limit the size or character of nuclear programs, define nuclear doctrine, or address Kashmir, conventional forces, or militant violence. It also did not remove suspicions about covert programs, because a prohibition on attack presupposes the existence of facilities without requiring either side to declare their purpose. In other words, it reduced one danger while leaving the structure of rivalry intact.

A major historical contradiction emerges here. The same decade in which India and Pakistan advanced nuclear capabilities also produced bilateral agreements, summit meetings, and communication channels. Nuclear competition increased insecurity by raising the stakes of any conflict and encouraging secrecy, yet it also encouraged limited forms of diplomacy because the consequences of miscalculation became more severe. Whether this combination produced stability or merely deferred conflict is the subject of the historical debate that follows.

The background of the agreement includes concern about preventive attacks on nuclear sites. Israel's 1981 attack on Iraq's Osirak reactor demonstrated that such an action was possible, and later accounts attribute to Indian and Israeli planners some discussion of options against Kahuta in the early 1980s, though these accounts are disputed and not fully documented. Pakistani leaders voiced their fear of such an attack. In December 1985, Rajiv Gandhi and Zia-ul-Haq reportedly reached an oral understanding not to attack each other's nuclear facilities, which preceded the written agreement three years later. The route from understanding to treaty shows how governments sometimes preferred informal commitments before they were willing to publish formal ones. Subsequent exchanges of lists on 1 January each year, beginning in 1992, have continued through periods of intense hostility, which suggests that a narrow agreement can survive when broader diplomacy fails, even though it does not guarantee peaceful relations.

The path to ratification was not straightforward. The agreement was signed during the first months of Benazir Bhutto's government, which operated under significant constraints from the military and the presidency, and in India political change followed Rajiv Gandhi's electoral defeat in 1989. The exchange of instruments of ratification occurred in January 1991, more than two years after signature. Domestic critics in both states raised objections: some Indian commentators argued that the agreement did not address Pakistan's program, while some Pakistani commentators doubted that India would adhere to it. The delay shows that even a limited agreement had to survive domestic political change and bureaucratic scrutiny before it became binding, and its eventual entry into force reflected continuing interest in at least this form of restraint.

Historical Debate

Did Nuclear Development Strengthen or Undermine South Asian Security?

The question: Did the acquisition of nuclear capabilities by India and Pakistan reduce the likelihood of major war, or did it increase danger, cost, and isolation?

Interpretation A: Nuclear capability increased the risks of large-scale conventional war, and the absence of full-scale war after 1971 reflects a stabilizing effect. Proponents point to the restraint shown in 1987 and 1990, the 1988 agreement, and the logic of deterrence. Some also argue that nuclear capability gave Pakistan assurance against a repeat of 1971 and gave India strategic autonomy against China.
Interpretation B: Nuclear ambiguity encouraged risk-taking, increased fear of surprise, diverted resources from development, and exposed the region to sanctions and international suspicion. Critics argue that restraint in crises reflected other factors, including diplomacy and external pressure, and that the presence of capabilities made each crisis more dangerous. Some scholars argue that nuclear capability emboldened sub-conventional conflict by providing a shield for militant or coercive strategies, an argument developed particularly for the later period.

Evidence: The evidence includes declassified diplomatic records, military-expenditure data, accounts by officials, crisis chronologies, and comparative theory. Each is incomplete. Counterfactuals, such as whether a conventional war would have occurred in 1987 or 1990 without nuclear capabilities, cannot be tested directly.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians interpret the later nuclear tests, the Kargil conflict, and the long-term pattern of crises, and how they weigh diplomatic agreements against structural insecurity.

SAARC, Regional Diplomacy, and the Limits of Economic Integration

How seven governments created a regional institution while keeping its most contentious issues outside its agenda.

Section 97.9

The Origins of Regional Cooperation: Bangladesh's Initiative and the Politics of South Asian Diplomacy

Ideas of regional cooperation in South Asia predated the 1980s. The Asian Relations Conference in New Delhi in 1947 had imagined a wider Asian solidarity, and economists, officials, and writers occasionally proposed federations or common markets. Partition, the Kashmir conflict, and the Cold War, however, made such proposals politically difficult. South Asia lacked the clear geopolitical balance that had enabled regional organizations elsewhere. India accounted for most of the region's population, territory, and industrial capacity, and nearly every other state bordered India while few bordered one another. Pakistan, the second largest state, was separated from India by a militarized frontier. This asymmetry made cooperation attractive to smaller countries but suspicious to larger ones.

The 1971 war altered the regional order. Bangladesh emerged as a seventh sovereign state, India's strategic position was strengthened, and Pakistan was reduced in size and reoriented toward the Gulf and China. Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives each sought to preserve their autonomy in a region dominated by India. Several of these states also had unresolved disputes with India over water, trade, transit, or migration. A regional framework offered them a space in which India might be discussed on more equal terms, while India's own interest in avoiding the appearance of dominance gave it reasons for caution.

The initiative that led to SAARC came from Bangladesh. President Ziaur Rahman, who had come to power after the political turmoil of the mid-1970s (see Chapter 96), advocated a framework for regional cooperation in the late 1970s and in 1980 circulated a proposal to the other governments suggesting consultations on an institutional structure. His motives combined domestic and external considerations: Bangladesh was economically fragile and dependent on trade and aid, and it saw regional cooperation as a way to broaden diplomatic options and to address shared issues such as river management and trade. Zia was assassinated in May 1981, but his successors, including Hussain Muhammad Ershad, continued the effort.

The responses were mixed. India worried that a multilateral organization might become a forum in which smaller states combined against it, or in which external powers sought influence, and preferred bilateral treatment of political problems. Pakistan feared that regional cooperation could consolidate Indian economic dominance and was reluctant to appear to accept it politically. Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives were generally more enthusiastic, though each calculated its interests differently. Sri Lanka under J. R. Jayewardene had developed a more market-oriented economy and sought regional markets, while Nepal and Bhutan, as landlocked states, saw transit and trade benefits. All governments agreed on a gradual approach starting with non-controversial subjects.

Water was one of the issues that gave smaller states reasons to seek forums beyond bilateral talks. Bangladesh's dispute with India over the Farakka Barrage, which diverted Ganges water, began in the 1970s and remained a significant grievance; interim arrangements in 1977 were temporary and contested. Nepal sought benefits from rivers that flowed into India, and Bhutan and India developed hydropower cooperation. For these states, a regional framework appeared to offer a means of raising shared issues, although the eventual Charter excluded bilateral disputes. India's initial reaction in 1980 was cautious, and accounts commonly report suspicion that the proposal might lead to a bloc of smaller states acting together. Over time, India's attitude shifted toward acceptance, partly because the proposal was framed in terms of noncontroversial cooperation and partly because refusing to participate could have isolated India within its own region. The agreement to begin with modest areas of technical cooperation reflected that bargain.

Section 97.10

The Formation of SAARC: The Dhaka Summit of December 1985

The diplomatic process proceeded through a series of meetings of foreign secretaries and ministers. Foreign secretaries met in Colombo in April 1981, in Kathmandu in November 1981, and in Islamabad in August 1982, with additional meetings of senior officials examining potential areas of cooperation. In August 1983 foreign ministers meeting in New Delhi adopted the Declaration on South Asian Regional Cooperation and launched an Integrated Programme of Action in agriculture, rural development, telecommunications, meteorology, and health and population activities, later expanded to include other fields. The subjects were selected to avoid controversy, and the early structure was deliberately modest.

The first summit took place in Dhaka on 7 and 8 December 1985. The heads of state or government of seven countries attended: Rajiv Gandhi of India, Zia-ul-Haq of Pakistan, Hussain Muhammad Ershad of Bangladesh, J. R. Jayewardene of Sri Lanka, King Birendra of Nepal, King Jigme Singye Wangchuck of Bhutan, and Maumoon Abdul Gayoom of the Maldives. The seven founding states were Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. The Charter of the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation was signed on 8 December. The meeting was notable for bringing together leaders whose countries had been at war, were engaged in disputes, or were in some cases suspicious of one another. It also coincided with Rajiv Gandhi's early period in office, when he sought improved relations with neighbors, and with the Zia government's search for diplomatic breathing space.

The Charter set out a deliberately balanced structure. Its objectives included promoting the welfare of the peoples of South Asia, accelerating economic growth, social progress, and cultural development, strengthening collective self-reliance, and cooperating with international and regional organizations. Its principles emphasized sovereign equality, territorial integrity, political independence, noninterference in internal affairs, and mutual benefit. Article X established two especially important rules: decisions at all levels would be taken by unanimity, and bilateral and contentious issues would be excluded from the organization's deliberations. These provisions allowed governments to join without fear that the organization would be used to adjudicate disputes, but they also meant that every member held an effective veto.

The governing institutions followed a layered pattern. Heads of state or government would meet in summits, normally annually. A Council of Ministers, composed of foreign ministers, would formulate policies; a Standing Committee of foreign secretaries would oversee implementation; and technical committees would coordinate work in specific areas. This pyramid permitted continuous contact and some bureaucratic socialization among officials, which may have been among SAARC's more durable contributions.

A central question for historians is whether SAARC created a meaningful regional political community or a carefully limited framework for cooperation. The evidence of the Charter supports the second reading in its formal design. The shared tone of the summit speeches, the participation of royal and military and elected rulers alike, and the early willingness to meet on neutral ground suggest a more modest but genuine achievement: the normalization of regional diplomacy as a continuing practice.

The summit's procedures set patterns that persisted. Chairmanship rotated among members in alphabetical order, the Council of Ministers was to meet at least twice each year, and the Standing Committee, composed of foreign secretaries, supervised the work of technical committees and the budgeting of programs. Financing came from contributions by member states, which kept the organization modest and dependent on consensus. The summit also provided a rare occasion for bilateral meetings: Rajiv Gandhi and Zia-ul-Haq, whose countries had a long record of hostility, met on the margins, as did other leaders. The Charter itself comprised a small number of articles, and its brevity reflected the intention to avoid binding commitments. For the smaller states, formal equality with India and Pakistan in the organization carried symbolic importance, even though real power remained unequal. This combination of formal equality and practical asymmetry explains much of SAARC's later history.

Comparison with other regional organizations clarifies SAARC's design. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, founded in 1967, also adopted a principle of noninterference and a consensus style of decision-making, and the two organizations are sometimes compared for that reason. But ASEAN's founding states shared a stronger common concern about external threats and were not dominated by a single member to the degree that India dominated South Asia, while the European Community had pursued deep economic integration through supranational institutions. SAARC's founders chose a model closer to loose intergovernmental cooperation. The choice reflects the region's history: states whose borders had been drawn by partition and war were reluctant to share sovereignty, and the largest state was wary of being constrained by a coalition of smaller ones.

Historical Vocabulary
Unanimity and the Exclusion of Bilateral Issues

Unanimity means that no SAARC decision can be adopted if any member objects. The exclusion of bilateral and contentious issues means that disputes between two members, such as Kashmir or river-water disagreements, cannot be placed on the formal agenda of the organization.

These principles were not accidents. They reflected a calculation that a regional organization could survive only if it avoided the issues that divided its most powerful members. Students should recognize that the same rule that enabled SAARC's founding later limited its capacity to address security and political disputes.

Section 97.11

SAARC's Early Institutions, Programs, and Political Limitations, 1985–1991

After the Dhaka summit, SAARC built institutions gradually. The second summit met in Bangalore in November 1986, the third in Kathmandu in November 1987, the fourth in Islamabad in December 1988, and the fifth in Male in November 1990. The Secretariat was established in Kathmandu in January 1987, with a Secretary-General appointed on a rotating basis from member states; Abul Ahsan of Bangladesh served as the first. Its role was administrative and coordinating rather than supranational: it had no authority to impose decisions or to act independently of member governments. Nepal's selection as host reflected a broader pattern in which smaller states sought institutional roles.

The early programs concentrated on technical cooperation. Technical committees dealt with agriculture, rural development, telecommunications, meteorology, health and population, transport, postal services, scientific and technological exchange, and later education, culture, and sports. SAARC established information centers and organized workshops, exchanges, and training programs. These activities were modest in scale but meaningful for specialists and for governments seeking to share experiences in areas such as crop research, disease control, and weather forecasting. They also created habits of consultation among ministries that otherwise had little direct contact.

Two concrete early agreements illustrate the pattern. At the Kathmandu summit of November 1987 the members signed an agreement establishing the SAARC Food Security Reserve, under which each member agreed to earmark a quantity of food grains that could be made available to others in emergencies. Its practical effect was limited, because releases required procedures and agreement, and because India, as the largest producer, held most of the capacity. Nevertheless it was a notable early instance of an agreed regional mechanism. The same summit also produced the SAARC Regional Convention on Suppression of Terrorism, which committed members to treat specified acts of violence as extraditable offenses. Its signing illustrated shared concern about political violence in Punjab, Sri Lanka, and elsewhere, but its implementation varied with the political relationships among governments.

SAARC's structure facilitated technical cooperation because such subjects could be handled through experts and did not require governments to concede sovereignty. It constrained political cooperation because unanimity gave each state a veto and because Article X excluded the disputes most likely to undermine regional stability. SAARC did not mediate between India and Pakistan, address Kashmir, or resolve the Sri Lankan ethnic conflict. The Indian Peace Keeping Force intervention in Sri Lanka from 1987 (see Chapters 96 and 103) occurred under bilateral arrangements, not SAARC auspices. For the same reason, observers began distinguishing the organization's declared objectives from its demonstrated accomplishments.

Some of its political significance was symbolic and procedural. Summits provided occasions for bilateral meetings, as the Gandhi–Bhutto contact at Islamabad in 1988 and the Indian and Pakistani leaders' exchanges at later summits demonstrated. Smaller states could raise issues and gain visibility. The experience of working together also encouraged expert and civil-society networks. But the practical economic benefits during the first years remained small and the organization's budget modest.

The Terrorism Convention is instructive because it shows both political agreement and its limits. It committed members to treat listed offenses as extraditable and to cooperate in prosecution, and it entered into force in 1988. Yet its operation depended on national legal systems and on political judgments about what constituted terrorism, and states disagreed over the classification of insurgents and armed movements. Other early activities included information centers, such as an agricultural information center established in Dhaka, exchange programs, and annual thematic campaigns, such as years devoted to issues like drug abuse and shelter. These programs were modest but often provided the first routine institutional contact among specialists from different countries. Observers noted that SAARC's greatest strength was the regularity of its meetings and its capacity to keep communication channels open between governments that otherwise had few forums in which to meet.

The organization's resources were modest. The Secretariat was small, its staff was drawn from member states, and its budget depended on contributions from governments, so that its capacity to initiate programs was limited. Smaller members nonetheless valued technical cooperation because it gave them access to expertise, training, and visibility that they could not easily obtain bilaterally with India, while India and Pakistan found that low-stakes cooperation in noncontroversial fields carried little political risk. This convergence of interests explains why technical programs advanced while political questions did not. It also explains why contemporary and later assessments of SAARC often described it as an organization whose achievements were real but narrow, a description that the evidence of the first years supports.

Section 97.12

Regional Trade, Economic Barriers, and the Limits of South Asian Integration

The political conditions that limited SAARC were reinforced by economic structures. During the 1970s and 1980s most South Asian governments followed development strategies that favored import substitution, state planning, licensing, tariff protection, and foreign-exchange controls. Such policies discouraged trade with all external partners, including neighbors. Industrial structures were also often competitive rather than complementary: several countries produced textiles, jute, tea, or agricultural goods and sought markets in industrial economies. Formal trade among the seven countries remained a small fraction of their total external trade, commonly estimated in the low single digits as a percentage, far lower than in the European Community or the Association of Southeast Asian Nations at the time. Figures vary according to definitions and sources.

Specific barriers included high and complex tariffs, quantitative restrictions, import licensing, nontariff barriers, limited banking and payment arrangements, inadequate road and rail links, slow border procedures, and security restrictions. India and Pakistan maintained severe limits on bilateral trade, with Pakistan permitting imports from India only for specified items. Transport routes were shaped by the colonial and Partition geographies: railways and roads often ran between ports and hinterlands and not across new borders. The partition of Bengal and Punjab had interrupted historically integrated trade networks, and the legacies of 1947 and 1971 still influenced border management.

Some trade relationships were deeply structured. India and Nepal operated under treaties of trade and transit that allowed Nepal's access to Indian ports, notably Calcutta. India and Bhutan maintained a close economic partnership with open movement of goods. Bangladesh depended on India for a range of products while also exporting jute and other goods, and its ports at Chittagong and Mongla and its river system were important to eastern South Asian trade. Sri Lanka and the Maldives were more connected to maritime networks. Karachi served as a port for Afghan transit trade, a route through which goods entered the informal economy. Disputes over transit and trade could be acute: in 1989, when India and Nepal failed to renew separate trade and transit treaties, a prolonged crisis produced hardship in Nepal and strained relations.

Official figures do not capture all exchange. Informal cross-border trade, including smuggling and small-scale commerce by local traders, was significant on the Indo-Bangladesh frontier, along the borders with Nepal, and on the western frontier of Pakistan. Goods such as cattle, textiles, consumer products, spices, and fuel moved through networks that exploited price differences created by tariffs and controls. This trade shows that geographical proximity and social ties created demand even where states restricted exchange, and that official statistics understate regional interconnection while also showing the weakness of formal integration.

An important chronological boundary applies. SAARC's preferential trading arrangement, the SAPTA, was signed in 1993 and became operational in 1995. It cannot be attributed to the organization's first years. The pre-1991 period instead explains why later trade agreements were thought necessary: governments recognized that technical cooperation had not been matched by market integration. The comparison between SAARC's institutional aspirations and the limited trade integration of its first years highlights the central distinction of this chapter between institutional cooperation, which advanced slowly, and underlying economic and social interdependence, which developed through other channels.

Several structural features of the region's economies explain the limits of formal trade. Sri Lanka's liberalization after 1977 and efforts in Bangladesh and Pakistan to expand exports tended to look toward industrial markets, not neighbors. India's large and protected domestic market offered little incentive for its producers to seek regional exports, while its neighbors feared being flooded with Indian goods if barriers fell. Pakistan's trade with India was limited by political hostility, and trade between Pakistan and Bangladesh remained small after 1971. Bhutan and India, by contrast, maintained a particularly close economic relationship under agreements that allowed free movement of goods, and India was Nepal's dominant trading partner. Different transport gauges, poorly connected roads and railways, and complex customs procedures raised costs. Economists later argued that these conditions meant the region traded far below its potential, but they also warned that estimates of potential depend on assumptions about reforms that had not yet occurred.

A later compilation published by the Reserve Bank of India, using UNCTAD data, estimates that recorded intraregional trade represented about 3.5 percent of the region’s overall trade in 1980 and 2.7 percent in 1990. These figures are retrospective measures of the countries that formed SAARC; they do not imply that SAARC itself existed in 1980. The same statistical discussion reports major differences among national economies: India’s trade with its South Asian neighbors represented a far smaller share of its overall trade than such exchanges did for landlocked Nepal. This variation reinforces the need to distinguish a region-wide average from each country’s dependence on nearby markets.

Historical Debate

Was SAARC a Meaningful Advance in Regional Cooperation or an Institution Too Constrained to Succeed?

The question: Should SAARC be judged by its limited accomplishments, by its role in normalizing regional diplomacy, or by the larger expectations stated by its founders?

Interpretation A: SAARC was a meaningful achievement. It brought seven governments into a permanent forum, built institutions, generated technical cooperation, and created channels of communication that survived periods of tension. Supporters note that its founding itself required overcoming deep mistrust and that expecting rapid political integration was unrealistic.
Interpretation B: SAARC was too constrained to address the region's real problems. Unanimity and the exclusion of contentious issues meant that the organization could not deal with security, trade barriers, or conflict. Critics argue that the budget and ambition were too limited and that the organization became a venue for ritual declarations.

Labor Migration to the Gulf and the Economic Importance of Remittances

How oil revenues, recruitment networks, and household strategies connected South Asian villages to Gulf labor markets.

Section 97.13

The Gulf Oil Boom and the Expansion of South Asian Labor Migration

The most widespread connection among South Asian societies in these years was not a treaty or a political institution but a labor market. After the oil-price increases of 1973–74 and again in 1979, the oil-exporting states of the Persian Gulf, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman, gained enormous revenues and launched large programs of construction, infrastructure, industry, and public services. Their populations were small relative to these ambitions, and their governments, firms, and households turned to foreign labor. Workers from Egypt, Yemen, Jordan, and Palestine had been important earlier; increasingly, recruiters looked to South and Southeast Asia, where wages were lower and labor was abundant.

The first large South Asian flows came from Pakistan and India. Pakistani workers, many from Punjab and the northwest, were recruited to build roads, ports, buildings, and industrial plants, and to work as drivers, mechanics, and technicians. Indian migration came from several regions, with Kerala particularly prominent and with substantial participation from Gujarat, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu; the Gulf had long been connected to the Malabar and Gujarati coasts by maritime trade, and older merchant and seafaring networks helped create channels. Bangladesh's labor migration expanded from the late 1970s, and Sri Lanka's, which included many women, grew significantly in the 1980s. Nepal's overseas labor migration in the period was smaller and was oriented more toward India and military service, with larger Gulf migration developing later.

Demand was diverse. Construction was the largest sector, but the Gulf also required engineers, accountants, nurses, teachers, secretaries, drivers, shopkeepers, cooks, and domestic workers. Some migrants were highly educated professionals, particularly Indian engineers and medical staff; many were semiskilled or unskilled. The labor market was stratified by nationality, language, skill, and gender, with employers often preferring particular groups for particular jobs, and governments concerned with controlling the balance between citizens and foreign workers. Wage differences relative to home countries were large enough to make even difficult conditions attractive for many households.

The distinction between temporary contract migration and permanent settlement is fundamental. Gulf states generally did not offer foreign workers a path to citizenship, permanent residence, or political rights. Employment was tied to contracts and sponsorship, and families were often not permitted to accompany lower-income workers. Migrants were therefore expected to return, even when many stayed for decades. This was different from migration to Britain, Canada, or the United States, where settlement was possible. It meant that Gulf migration generated large flows of labor and money without generating corresponding membership in host societies.

The scale of the change justified describing it as an economic transformation. Millions of people moved over the course of the decades, and households in many districts reorganized their economies around the expectation of overseas employment. Where migrants had little chance of permanent membership, the social consequences fell chiefly on sending communities, which absorbed both the wages and the burdens of absence.

The growth of the foreign workforce transformed the Gulf societies as well as South Asia. In several states, including Kuwait, Qatar, and the United Arab Emirates, foreign residents came to outnumber citizens, and governments adopted policies to regulate the balance and to discourage settlement. Scholars have argued that, after the mid-1970s, Gulf governments and employers shifted from reliance on Arab migrants toward Asian workers, partly because of lower costs and partly because of political concerns about the activism of Arab migrants; this interpretation is debated, but the change in the composition of the workforce is well documented. South Asian firms and contractors also won projects in the Gulf, which created demand for engineers, supervisors, and skilled workers. Migration thus involved a layered economy, in which large enterprises, small subcontractors, and informal recruiters all participated.

Section 97.14

Recruitment, Gender, Working Conditions, and Migrant Experiences

Obtaining a job abroad required navigating recruitment systems that were partly formal and partly informal. Employers in the Gulf might hire through direct contacts, foreign-based agents, or labor contractors; in South Asia, licensed recruitment agencies and unlicensed subagents connected villages to overseas jobs. Governments regulated emigration through bureaus and legislation, including India's Emigration Act of 1983 and Sri Lanka's Bureau of Foreign Employment, created in 1985, and Pakistan and Bangladesh built agencies to supervise overseas employment. Regulations intended to protect workers often coexisted with weak enforcement, and prospective migrants frequently paid substantial fees, sometimes beyond legal limits, for visas, tickets, and placement.

Financing migration meant debt. Families sold land, pledged jewelry, borrowed from relatives or moneylenders, or took informal loans to pay recruitment costs. The expectation was that wages would repay the debt and then generate savings, but delays, contract changes, or early termination could leave families worse off. Employers commonly retained control through sponsorship arrangements, often called kafala, in which a resident or firm acted as the worker's legal sponsor, and in some cases through confiscation of passports. Legal protections differed among destination countries and by occupation, and workers in sectors such as domestic service often had the weakest protection because labor laws did not cover them or were difficult to enforce.

Gender shaped migration. In the early Gulf boom most South Asian migrants were men, either leaving families behind or migrating alone. During the 1980s, however, women's migration expanded, notably Sri Lankan women employed as domestic workers in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and other states, and Indian women, particularly from Kerala, working as nurses and in other roles. For many women, migration offered income and independence unavailable at home; for some it also created exposure to isolation, unpaid wages, abuse, or restricted movement, risks documented in later research by organizations such as Human Rights Watch. These patterns varied among individuals and countries, and it would be misleading to treat all women migrants as victims or all as empowered.

It would likewise be wrong to describe all South Asian migrants as low-skilled laborers. Doctors, engineers, managers, accountants, and teachers worked in the Gulf, often alongside Western and Arab professionals, and they earned higher salaries and had more bargaining power. Differences in class, caste, region, language, and religion also affected recruitment and experience. Migrants from the same village or district often formed networks that passed on information about jobs and employers, so that chain migration concentrated flows from particular areas.

Working conditions ranged from satisfactory to harsh. Reports and later studies documented long hours, poor housing in labor camps, heat exposure, delayed wages, and limited recourse for grievances. Governments in South Asia, dependent on remittances and cautious about diplomatic relations with host states, often had limited leverage. The International Labour Organization and non-governmental groups began documenting these problems more systematically in the 1980s, though comprehensive data remained incomplete.

Regulation of recruitment illustrates the difficulty of protecting migrants. India's Emigration Act of 1983 established a system of protectors of emigrants and required clearance for workers in certain categories, often described as emigration-check-required categories, which was intended to prevent exploitation of less-educated workers. In practice, such rules could also drive migration into informal channels and could be circumvented through travel on other visas. Sending states competed with one another for access to Gulf labor markets, which reduced their leverage to demand better terms. Migrants also relied on social networks and religious or community organizations for assistance, since formal remedies were distant. Whatever the legal structure, a worker whose employer withheld wages or changed conditions had limited recourse, and this vulnerability is among the central social facts of the Gulf labor system.

Government policies toward women's migration shifted over time and reflected concerns about protection and social norms. Bangladesh reportedly imposed restrictions on women's emigration for work in the early 1980s and relaxed them later, and other states experimented with age limits or approval requirements. Such measures were justified as protective, but critics argued that they could push women into irregular channels and limit their choices. Sri Lanka, by contrast, encouraged the expansion of women's overseas domestic employment while building training and welfare institutions, even as problems of abuse and unpaid wages persisted. These contrasting policies show that gender was not only an experience of migrants but also an object of state regulation.

Historical Vocabulary
Kafala (Sponsorship) and Contract Migration

Kafala is an Arabic term for guarantee or sponsorship. In the Gulf labor system, it refers to arrangements linking a foreign worker's legal residence and employment to a specific sponsor, commonly an employer. The system gave sponsors considerable power over workers' mobility, and its details varied by country and period.

Historians use the term carefully. It describes a family of legal and customary practices and not a single uniform law. Its importance for this chapter is that it explains why labor migration could be economically large while migrants remained legally temporary and often vulnerable.

Section 97.15

Remittances, Household Economies, and Social Transformation

A remittance is money or goods sent by migrants to relatives at home. Their importance in these decades arose because they reached households directly. A Gulf worker's wages, even if modest by host-country standards, might exceed what a family could earn at home several times over. Studies of migrant-sending districts in Kerala, Punjab, Sylhet in Bangladesh, and parts of Sri Lanka show that remittances were spent first on household consumption, then on housing, education, healthcare, and debt repayment, and sometimes on land, small businesses, or agricultural inputs. The relative balance varied by region, household income, and the length of time a migrant had been abroad.

Visible changes appeared in villages and towns. New brick and concrete houses replaced mud or thatch structures, appliances and consumer goods became more available, and shops, transport services, and construction businesses expanded in migrant-sending areas. Education spending increased for many children, including girls in some localities, though results differed. In some areas, remittances contributed to rising land prices and wages, inflation of marriage expenses, and new forms of status competition. Anthropologists studying Sylheti villages and Kerala's Gulf-linked communities have described how migration changed ideas of success, family obligation, and religious practice.

Family relations were reorganized. Wives of migrants often became de facto managers of households and farms, exercising authority in the absence of husbands while remaining subject to the expectations of in-laws and communities. Parents and older relatives could gain or lose influence depending on who controlled remittances. Children experienced absent fathers, or, increasingly, absent mothers. These changes could provide economic security but also strain relationships and emotional bonds, and the long separation inherent in contract migration produced loneliness, anxiety, and sometimes breakdown of marriages.

Migration was unequally distributed. Households needed capital to finance recruitment, access to networks that supplied visas, and in some cases education or skills. Poorer families, those without contacts, and certain caste or minority communities might be unable to participate, or might do so on worse terms. Within sending regions, remittance-receiving households therefore often pulled ahead of neighbors, widening local inequality even as they raised average living standards. Other households depended on the labor opportunities that migration created locally, such as construction and services, and some experienced price increases without direct benefit.

The social costs deserve equal attention. Debts incurred for recruitment could trap families; failed or interrupted migration, unpaid wages, injury, or death abroad could produce hardship. Dependence on overseas employment also made households vulnerable to events they could not control, including changes in Gulf labor policy, shifts in oil revenue, or regional conflicts. The historian's task is to recognize both the improvement many families achieved and the vulnerability built into the arrangement.

Local variation was substantial. In Kerala, a state with high literacy and a strong tradition of overseas movement, remittances were associated with the expansion of banking, construction, and private education, and observers described a distinctive Gulf-linked economy. In the Punjab of Pakistan, remittances changed housing and landholding in some districts. In Sylhet, the earlier British connection meant that migration was tied to older networks and that remittances supported both household consumption and community institutions. Researchers have also noted that remittances financed religious buildings and reshaped practice, with debates over the influence of Gulf religious ideas on South Asian communities; evidence on this question is contested, and historians caution against assuming a simple direct causal effect. The consistent pattern across regions was that migration provided resources and also created new social tensions and dependence on distant employers.

Section 97.16

Remittances and National Economic Development: Foreign Exchange, Imports, and Dependency

At the national level, remittances changed balance-of-payments positions. For Pakistan, an International Monetary Fund study reports that workers' remittances rose from approximately $136 million in fiscal 1972–73 to nearly $2.6 billion in fiscal 1985–86, an increase that transformed the country's foreign-exchange position and helped finance a persistent trade deficit. The figures illustrate how rapidly migration reshaped economic life, although comparison with other countries requires consistent definitions and years. Pakistani flows reached high levels in the early 1980s and then moderated as Gulf construction slowed following the fall of oil prices in 1986. Bangladesh, India, and Sri Lanka also experienced substantial growth, though from different bases and with different compositions of migrants, destinations, and channels.

Several definitions matter. Workers' remittances are current transfers by migrant workers to resident households, a category measured in balance-of-payments statistics. They differ from foreign direct investment, foreign aid, portfolio investment, and the earnings of long-resident emigrants who have become citizens abroad. They also differ from transfers sent through informal channels such as the hawala system, which did not appear in official accounts and which governments sometimes encouraged workers to avoid through exchange-rate and import incentives. Because exchange-rate controls often created premiums in unofficial markets, official remittance statistics may understate the actual flows in some countries and years.

Remittances had macroeconomic effects. They financed imports, including consumer goods and capital goods, supported reserves, and helped governments sustain balance-of-payments positions that might otherwise have required adjustment. They also supplemented household savings. In some countries they increased demand for housing, transport, and services, influencing employment and price patterns. Policymakers, however, struggled to direct remittances toward long-term productive investment; much was spent on consumption and construction, which scholars debated as either rational household behavior or a missed opportunity.

Interpretations differ. One view treats remittances as a development resource that reduces poverty, finances education and health, and relaxes the foreign-exchange constraint on growth. A second emphasizes their role as a stabilizer of household incomes, especially under uncertain agricultural conditions. A third warns of dependence: reliance on external labor markets exposes economies to oil-price cycles, host-state policy, and political crises; it may encourage real-exchange-rate appreciation, discourage local employment generation, and bring brain drain. The historical evidence supports elements of each view and suggests that outcomes depended on how governments managed exchange rates, financial institutions, and investment.

The vulnerabilities were demonstrated by the oil-price decline of the mid-1980s and, dramatically, by the Gulf crisis of 1990–91. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, followed by the international military response, disrupted employment, trade, and remittances. Hundreds of thousands of South Asian workers lost jobs or fled and returned home; India conducted a large airlift of its citizens, and Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Nepal faced returnees, lost remittances, and higher fuel costs. For governments already under economic stress, the shock helped expose their reliance on the Gulf. In India, the crisis coincided with a foreign-exchange emergency that would lead to the reforms of Chapter 98.

Governments tried to capture remittances through policy. Pakistan introduced instruments in the mid-1980s, including foreign-exchange bearer certificates, that allowed holders to keep and use foreign exchange more freely, and India and Bangladesh used non-resident deposit schemes and wage-earner programs to attract savings and encourage the use of official channels. These measures reflected the fact that informal transfers, especially through hawala-type networks, offered migrants better exchange rates or convenience. Some economists warned that large inflows could raise domestic prices, appreciate real exchange rates, and weaken export competitiveness, an argument sometimes described as a version of the so-called Dutch disease; others emphasized the positive effects on household welfare and the balance of payments. For the period in question, historians should avoid generalizing about outcomes without examining specific countries and years.

Comparisons across countries must account for differences in scale. Remittances were large relative to Pakistan's and Bangladesh's foreign-exchange earnings, which made them central to macroeconomic management in those economies. For India, with a much larger economy and more diversified external sector, remittances were significant but represented a smaller share of national income. In Sri Lanka, the growth of remittances in the late 1980s made them a major source of foreign exchange alongside exports such as tea and garments. In each case the ratio of remittances to exports, imports, or gross domestic product tells a different story from the absolute dollar figure, which is why historians use several indicators and note the base year and definitions used.

Contemporaneous international statistical compilations put the expansion in perspective. A 1980 article in the IMF’s Finance & Development, drawing on a September 1978 IMF Survey tabulation, reported workers’ remittances of approximately $535 million for India, $43 million for Bangladesh and $3 million for Sri Lanka in 1975. In that same series the corresponding 1977 amounts were approximately $1.0 billion, $75 million and $12 million. These are historical published estimates of reported flows, not newly harmonized World Bank data, and their fiscal-year conventions and category coverage must be stated before comparison with later balance-of-payments series.

Section 97.17

South Asian Diasporas: Colonial Legacies and New Migration Destinations

South Asian overseas communities had a long history before the Gulf boom. Merchants and seafarers had crossed the Indian Ocean for centuries, and the colonial era created new channels. After the abolition of slavery in the British Empire, Indian indentured laborers were recruited for plantations in Mauritius, Natal, Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname, and elsewhere, a system that continued until its abolition between 1917 and 1920 (see Chapter 75). Indian merchants, clerks, artisans, and soldiers also settled in East Africa, Burma, Malaya, Singapore, and the Gulf. These communities developed distinct languages, religious practices, and political identities, and they should not be confused with later migrants.

After independence, several of the older diasporas faced upheaval. Burma's nationalization policies of the 1960s and the expulsion of Asians from Uganda in 1972 forced many people of South Asian origin to relocate, often to Britain, Canada, or India. Ceylon's citizenship laws affected Indian Tamil plantation workers, a question discussed in Chapter 91. These events show that the descendants of earlier migrations were not guaranteed security in the new nation-states of Africa and Asia.

Between the 1960s and 1980s, new destinations expanded. Postwar Britain recruited labor from Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh (then East Pakistan), and, even after restrictions in the 1962 and 1968 Commonwealth Immigrants Acts and the 1971 Immigration Act reduced new labor migration, family reunification continued to bring wives, children, and relatives. Canada's 1967 points system and the United States' Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened new pathways for professionals, students, and later family members, producing a skilled migration of physicians, engineers, scientists, and academics. Sikh and Gujarati migration to Canada and Britain, Sylheti migration to Britain, Punjabi and Kerala migration to the United States and the Gulf, and Tamil migration after 1983 formed distinct streams.

It is essential to distinguish older settled diasporas from newer temporary labor communities. Descendants of indentured laborers and long-resident merchants were often citizens of their countries with their own political histories. Postwar migrants to Britain, Canada, and the United States could often settle and become citizens. Gulf workers, by contrast, were temporary contract migrants with few settlement rights. Immigration laws, professional demand, political circumstances, colonial links, education, and family networks shaped each stream, so that the term South Asian diaspora covers a wide range of experiences and legal statuses.

Several episodes illustrate the insecurity of older diasporas. In 1972 Uganda's government expelled its Asian population, which numbered in the tens of thousands; many held British documents, and the arrival of refugees in Britain provoked intense political debate. In Fiji, descendants of indentured laborers formed a large part of the population, and military coups in 1987 removed a government that was perceived as dominated by the Indo-Fijian community, which led to further emigration. Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad, and Suriname each followed different political paths. At the same time, the United States' 1965 immigration law ended national-origins quotas and created opportunities for professionals, while Indian physicians and nurses played notable roles in health systems in Britain and North America. These examples show that the term diaspora covers communities with very different histories and legal positions.

Section 97.18

Diaspora Communities, Political Identity, and Transnational Connections

Overseas communities maintained and remade language, religion, family organization, and cultural practice. Temples, gurdwaras, mosques, churches, and community centers provided religious and social spaces. Language schools, newspapers, film distribution, music, and food businesses sustained cultural ties, while ethnic associations, professional networks, and business partnerships created economic links. Second-generation children often negotiated between the expectations of their families and the societies in which they grew up, and identities diverged by region, religion, caste, class, and generation. Britain's Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Indian, and Sri Lankan Tamil communities, for example, were not one community but several.

Diaspora political activity connected to events in South Asia. After the Bangladesh War of 1971, Bengali communities in Britain and elsewhere mobilized support for the new state through fundraising, publicity, and lobbying. After the anti-Tamil violence of July 1983 in Sri Lanka, known as Black July (see Chapter 96), thousands of Tamils fled the island, some to Tamil Nadu in India and others to Britain, Canada, Australia, and Western Europe, creating a diaspora shaped by displacement and political conflict. Sikh communities in Britain and Canada were affected by developments in Punjab in the 1980s, with some members supporting the movement for a separate Khalistan, while many others rejected it, and the bombing of Air India Flight 182 in 1985 remains a major event in the history of Canadian-Indian relations. These examples show diaspora engagement across the political spectrum.

Not all movement was equivalent. Voluntary migration for work or study, contract labor with limited rights, forced displacement caused by violence or expulsion, and asylum involving legal claims of persecution are distinct categories with different legal implications. Tamil refugees in India and the West faced uncertain status, while Gulf workers faced restrictions but could return, and skilled migrants to North America often had settlement rights. The chapter avoids merging them, because doing so would conceal differences in power, vulnerability, and opportunity.

Overseas communities contributed to debates in South Asia through remittances, investment, political donations, publications, and advocacy, and governments increasingly recognized their significance, though formal policies toward diasporas developed more fully after 1991. Yet diaspora positions were never unified. Members might support or oppose governments, nationalist movements, religious organizations, or separatist causes, and the loudest voices were not necessarily representative. Historians therefore treat diasporas as plural communities with internal disagreements, and not as extensions of national politics.

Cultural production overseas also deserves attention. Indian films circulated through video and cassette networks in Britain, the Gulf, and East Africa, and music scenes such as British bhangra developed among second-generation Punjabi communities in places like Southall. Pakistani migration to Britain included many people from the Mirpur region, where the construction of the Mangla Dam displaced communities in the 1960s and encouraged movement. Religious institutions, including mosques, temples, and gurdwaras, served as centers of social life and sometimes as sites of internal disputes over leadership and doctrine. Younger generations negotiated differences between family expectations and the surrounding society, and they developed new hybrid identities. These developments show that diasporas were not static replicas of the homeland but communities that changed through interaction with their new environments.

Section 97.19

South Asia at the End of the Cold War, 1988–1991

South Asia's international environment changed sharply at the end of the 1980s. Under the Geneva Accords of April 1988, the Soviet Union agreed to withdraw its forces from Afghanistan, and the withdrawal was completed in February 1989 (see Chapters 95 and 101). Pakistan had been a front-line state in the anti-Soviet coalition, receiving substantial American assistance and hosting millions of Afghan refugees. As Soviet withdrawal ended the war's strategic purpose, Washington's priorities shifted, the Pressler certification became harder to justify, and in October 1990 aid was suspended. Pakistan faced the prospect of sanctions, an unfinished Afghan conflict, refugees, and an expanded militancy that would later affect its security and politics.

India's position was also affected. Its relationship with the Soviet Union had been a pillar of diplomacy, defense supply, and trade, including trade arranged in rupees. As the Soviet economy weakened and its political system underwent reform and disintegration, India lost a reliable partner and market. Meanwhile, relations with the United States and China were being reassessed. Rajiv Gandhi's government and its successors in 1989–91 faced coalition instability, the Kashmir insurgency, ethnic conflict in neighboring Sri Lanka, and rising fiscal pressures. The smaller states had to adjust as well: Nepal, Bhutan, the Maldives, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh saw the end of a bipolar framework that had allowed some to balance external influences, and they sought new ways to protect autonomy.

The Gulf War of 1990–91 added another shock. The invasion of Kuwait threatened South Asian workers, raised oil prices, disrupted remittances, and forced governments to decide among competing alliances. Pakistan sent forces to Saudi Arabia but experienced domestic divisions over the conflict; India struggled with evacuations and fuel costs; Bangladesh and Sri Lanka faced losses of remittance income and returnees. The war showed how much the region's economies had come to depend on the Gulf and on global energy markets.

The end of the Cold War also changed expectations about economic policy. The collapse of centrally planned economies and the prestige of market reforms strengthened arguments in international institutions and among economists for liberalization, privatization, and export orientation, while the debt and balance-of-payments difficulties of developing countries increased the leverage of lenders. In South Asia, Sri Lanka had already liberalized from 1977, Pakistan and Bangladesh were experimenting with limited reforms, and India's policymakers were debating change. The pressures of 1991, therefore, included geopolitical uncertainty, economic vulnerability, expanded regional connectivity, and unresolved security competition.

India's balance-of-payments crisis of mid-1991 and the reforms that followed belong principally to Chapter 98 and are introduced here only as a transition. The essential point for this chapter is that by 1991 the region's governments confronted a world in which the strategic and economic assumptions of the previous two decades no longer held.

Several developments illustrate the diplomatic adjustments of the late 1980s. Mikhail Gorbachev visited India in November 1986 and signed the Delhi Declaration, which reflected both continuity in the relationship and a Soviet interest in new approaches to security. In December 1988 Rajiv Gandhi visited Beijing, the first visit by an Indian prime minister in decades, which began a process of reducing tension along the border. In November 1988 Indian forces helped to defeat an attempted coup in the Maldives at the request of its government, an episode that showed both India's capacity to act and the vulnerability of small states. Sri Lanka's experience with the Indian Peace Keeping Force from 1987 to 1990 illustrated the complexity of India's regional role and the resentments it could produce. Pakistan's elections of November 1988 returned civilian government after Zia's death, but its security policy remained heavily influenced by the military. These events show that the regional order was shifting before the formal end of the Cold War.

Section 97.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Security Competition, Regional Cooperation, and Economic Interdependence

The preceding sections described four distinct, simultaneous processes. First, nuclear programs reshaped strategic calculations. India's 1974 explosion demonstrated capability without declaring weapons; Pakistan's program, rooted in the defeat of 1971 and accelerated by the Indian test, developed enrichment capacity through the 1980s. Both governments preserved ambiguity, and international responses, including export controls and sanctions, were shaped by Cold War priorities. Second, India and Pakistan remained political and security rivals. Brasstacks and the 1990 crisis showed how quickly mistrust could produce mobilization, while the 1988 agreement on nuclear installations showed that governments could still negotiate narrowly bounded commitments.

Third, SAARC created a limited but durable institutional framework. The seven founding states built a Charter, a Secretariat, summit diplomacy, and technical programs, but unanimity and the exclusion of bilateral issues prevented the organization from addressing the disputes that most affected regional stability. Formal intraregional trade remained constrained by protectionist policies, infrastructure, and political distrust, even as informal exchange continued. The preferential trading arrangement of 1993–95 therefore lay beyond the period and arose partly from recognition of its absence.

Fourth, Gulf migration and remittances deepened South Asia's dependence on global labor markets, and diasporas expanded cultural, political, and economic networks. Millions of workers connected South Asian villages to the Gulf and, indirectly, to one another through shared recruitment systems, remittance flows, and experiences of host-state controls. Older and newer diasporas linked the region to Britain, North America, Africa, and Southeast Asia. These connections were largely produced by individuals, households, firms, and markets, not by interstate agreements, and they often moved faster than formal diplomacy.

The end of the Cold War, the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan, and the Gulf crisis created new strategic and economic pressures. Pakistan lost its privileged position with Washington; India lost a longtime partner and faced a currency crisis; and the smaller states recalculated their options. These pressures help explain why the early 1990s became a turning point, treated in the following chapters.

The essential conclusion is that South Asia's states were becoming more interconnected without necessarily becoming more politically united. The governing question of the chapter can therefore be answered as follows: nuclear rivalry, regional diplomacy, labor migration, remittances, and diaspora networks transformed South Asia by changing its strategic risks, its institutions, its household economies, and its external links. Economic and social interdependence expanded through channels that states did not fully control, while political and strategic divisions persisted because they rested on territorial disputes, mutual suspicion, asymmetries of size and power, and the domestic politics of each state. This chapter completes Part XVII. Chapter 98 turns to India's economic crisis and liberalization, with Chapters 99 and 100 following the political transformations that accompanied them.

The chapter's evidence also teaches a methodological lesson. Nuclear history depends on sources that are partial, classified, or self-serving; SAARC history depends on documents that record intentions more reliably than outcomes; and migration history depends on statistics and testimonies that capture some experiences and omit others. A responsible historian therefore keeps verified milestones separate from attributed claims, treats numerical series as evidence with definitions and limits, and resists retrospective narratives that make events seem inevitable. The three stories are connected by a common structure: in each case governments acted, but outcomes depended on forces beyond their control, whether the technical diffusion of nuclear knowledge, the veto of any member state in a regional organization, or the decisions of millions of workers and employers. Part XVII ends with that recognition, and Part XVIII begins with the question of how a region facing economic crisis and a changed international order would respond.

Chapter Summary

Between 1974 and 1991, South Asia became simultaneously more strategically divided and more economically and socially interconnected. This chapter has argued that interstate political integration remained limited while interdependence expanded through several channels: scientific and military competition, diplomatic institutions, labor markets, remittances, informal trade, and overseas communities.

India's atomic energy program began as a civilian project under Nehru and Bhabha, supported by international technical partnerships. The 1962 war with China, China's 1964 test, and conflicts with Pakistan changed the strategic context. India's refusal to join the NPT reflected objections to its discriminatory structure. On 18 May 1974 India conducted an underground nuclear explosion at Pokhran, described as peaceful. It demonstrated explosive capability but did not establish a deployed arsenal, and India followed a policy of ambiguity until 1998.

Pakistan's program originated in the institutions of the 1950s and 1960s but was driven decisively by the 1971 defeat and Bhutto's Multan meeting of January 1972. The Indian test intensified its urgency. Pakistan pursued reprocessing and, more successfully, uranium enrichment at Kahuta under A. Q. Khan, while the PAEC retained other responsibilities. Foreign procurement networks, American aid decisions, and domestic military control all shaped the program. Pakistan conducted no acknowledged test before 1998, and its status in the 1980s was a matter of assessment and signaling.

The India–Pakistan rivalry persisted after 1971, shaped by Kashmir, conventional imbalance, alliance relationships, and the Siachen conflict. Nonproliferation law, including the Symington and Pressler provisions, was enforced unevenly because of Cold War priorities. The Brasstacks crisis of 1986–87 and the 1990 crisis showed how mistrust and uncertainty could produce dangerous mobilizations, while claims of near nuclear war remain contested. The 1988 agreement on nuclear installations, signed 31 December 1988 and in force from 27 January 1991, was a limited but real confidence-building measure.

SAARC emerged from Ziaur Rahman's initiative and years of diplomatic preparation. The Dhaka summit of December 1985 founded the organization with seven members, a Charter based on sovereign equality and noninterference, unanimity in decision-making, and the exclusion of bilateral and contentious issues. The Secretariat opened in Kathmandu in 1987, and programs in agriculture, health, meteorology, and other fields advanced, including the Food Security Reserve. SAARC created durable habits of diplomacy but could not address the disputes that most threatened regional stability.

Formal intraregional trade remained small because of protectionist policies, licensing, payment constraints, poor border infrastructure, political distrust, and similar production structures. Transit relations, particularly India–Nepal, showed the political sensitivity of trade, while informal exchange continued across borders. SAARC's preferential trade arrangement belongs to 1993–95 and cannot be attributed to the pre-1991 period.

Gulf labor migration transformed households and economies. After the oil boom, millions of South Asians worked under temporary contracts as construction workers, professionals, drivers, nurses, and domestic workers. Recruitment systems, debt, sponsorship, and gender shaped experience. Remittances financed consumption, housing, education, and imports, widened local inequality, and strengthened balance-of-payments positions. Pakistan's remittances rose from about $136 million in fiscal 1972–73 to nearly $2.6 billion in 1985–86. The oil-price fall of 1986 and the Gulf crisis of 1990–91 exposed the vulnerabilities of dependence.

Older and newer diasporas linked South Asia to Britain, North America, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Caribbean. Indentured, merchant, professional, family-reunification, refugee, and contract streams must be distinguished. Overseas communities sustained cultural institutions and engaged in homeland politics, from Bangladesh in 1971 to Tamil displacement after 1983, but they were never politically unified.

The end of the Cold War, the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan, and the Gulf crisis created new pressures. Pakistan's strategic value to Washington declined, India lost its Soviet partner and faced a currency crisis, and the smaller states recalculated. By 1991 the region faced geopolitical uncertainty, economic vulnerability, greater connectivity, and unresolved security rivalry. These pressures lead directly to Chapter 98.

The chapter's central lesson is that interdependence and division are not opposites. Governments, households, firms, and communities connect societies through channels that are not controlled by diplomatic or military agreements, and political rivalry can persist even as those connections deepen.

Key Terms

Atomic energy
The use of nuclear processes for power, research, and isotopes, which in India was organized through the Atomic Energy Commission and Department of Atomic Energy.
Peaceful nuclear explosion
A term for nuclear detonations described as civil engineering tools; India used it for the 1974 test.
Nuclear ambiguity
A condition in which a state is believed to have nuclear capability without formally declaring weapons possession.
Deterrence
A strategy that seeks to prevent attack by threatening unacceptable retaliation.
Nonproliferation
Policies and agreements intended to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons.
Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty
A treaty opened in 1968 that recognizes five nuclear-weapon states, defined by tests before 1967, and commits others to forgo weapons.
Safeguards
Inspection and accounting arrangements, administered by the IAEA, to verify peaceful use of nuclear materials.
Uranium enrichment
A process that raises the proportion of fissile uranium-235, used in Pakistan's Kahuta program through centrifuges.
Reprocessing
Chemical separation of plutonium from spent reactor fuel.
CIRUS
A research reactor built with Canadian design and American heavy water, whose plutonium was used in the 1974 device.
Symington Amendment
A 1976 US law restricting assistance to states obtaining unsafeguarded enrichment technology.
Pressler Amendment
A 1985 US law requiring annual presidential certification that Pakistan did not possess a nuclear explosive device.
Nuclear Suppliers Group
A group of supplier states formed after 1974 to coordinate nuclear export controls.
Simla Agreement
The 1972 India–Pakistan agreement committing both to bilateral dispute resolution.
Line of Control
The de facto line dividing Indian- and Pakistani-administered Kashmir after 1972.
Siachen
A glacier in Kashmir where Indian and Pakistani forces confronted one another from 1984.
Brasstacks
A large Indian army exercise of 1986–87 that produced a military crisis.
Confidence-building measure
A procedure or agreement that reduces misunderstanding and the risk of conflict.
Nuclear installations agreement
The 1988 India–Pakistan agreement, in force from 1991, prohibiting attacks on each other's nuclear facilities.
SAARC
The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation, founded in 1985 by seven states.
Dhaka Declaration
The statement adopted with the SAARC Charter at the 1985 summit.
Article X
The Charter provision requiring unanimity and excluding bilateral and contentious issues.
Integrated Programme of Action
The 1983 program of cooperation in agriculture, rural development, telecommunications, meteorology, and health.
Food Security Reserve
The 1987 SAARC arrangement for emergency food stocks.
SAARC Secretariat
The coordinating body based in Kathmandu from 1987.
SAPTA
The SAARC preferential trading arrangement signed in 1993 and operational in 1995, after this chapter's period.
Transit trade
Movement of goods through one country to or from another, important for landlocked Nepal and Bhutan.
Informal trade
Cross-border commerce outside official records, including smuggling and small-scale exchange.
Kafala
Sponsorship arrangements tying foreign workers' residence and employment to a sponsor.
Contract migration
Temporary overseas employment governed by labor contracts, without settlement rights.
Workers' remittances
Transfers by migrant workers to resident households, measured in balance-of-payments statistics.
Balance of payments
An account of a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world.
Diaspora
A community dispersed from a homeland that maintains connections to it.
Indenture
A system of contract labor used in the colonial period, ended between 1917 and 1920.
Gulf crisis
The 1990–91 conflict that followed Iraq's invasion of Kuwait and disrupted South Asian migrant employment.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a region can be both divided and connected. Nuclear programs, border mistrust, and the limits on SAARC describe one South Asia; migrant remittances, kinship networks, informal trade, and diasporas describe another. Neither picture is complete without the other.

It also shows how history often unfolds through multiple scales. National leaders decided on test sites and treaties, but households decided where to send sons and daughters to work, and recruiters, employers, and markets shaped lives in ways that no summit declaration addressed. The chapter's evidence therefore ranges from treaties to balance-of-payments tables.

The developments of 1974–1991 shaped what followed. The nuclear ambiguity of these years led to the tests of 1998 (Chapter 101) and to a more visible nuclear dimension in Kashmir and regional security (Chapter 105). SAARC's institutional pattern defined later regional diplomacy, and Gulf migration became central to the economies discussed in Chapters 102, 103, 104, and 106. The pressures of 1991 opened the era of liberalization examined in Chapter 98.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter marks a shift from national histories to regional synthesis. It reminds students that the study of South Asia requires attention to borders and to the movements that crossed them, and that political division and social connection can coexist for decades without resolving one another.
