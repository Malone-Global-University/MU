Part I — The Land and the Indus World
4

Society, Belief, and Transformation in the Indus World

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

A small stone seal, barely three centimeters square, shows a horned animal standing before what appears to be a ritual stand, above a short row of signs that no living reader can pronounce. Thousands of similar seals survive from the cities of the Mature Harappan world, each one a compressed record of belief, administration, and craft that historians can see clearly but cannot yet fully understand. This chapter asks what can, and cannot, be known about the people who carved them.

Chapter 3 traced the emergence of the Mature Harappan urban transformation: the great planned cities of Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Dholavira, their water management, their standardized weights and measures, and their far-reaching trade in raw materials and finished craft goods. That chapter necessarily concentrated on the physical and economic architecture of Indus civilization, the features most directly visible in excavated brick, drains, and storerooms. This chapter turns to the harder and more contested questions that lie behind that architecture: how Harappan households and communities were organized, what their sign or script system may have recorded, what their seals and symbols meant, what their treatment of the dead suggests about belief and status, how they connected with distant Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf, how rural life outside the great cities differed from urban life within them, and, finally, why this remarkable urban system gradually lost its coherence during the centuries after 1900 BCE.

These are, by any honest accounting, the most difficult questions this textbook has yet posed. Chapter 1 warned that the Indus script remains undeciphered, and that warning governs almost everything in this chapter. Historians can describe with confidence what a seal looks like, where it was found, and what materials it is made from. They cannot state with confidence what language its inscription records, what name or title it names, or what specific belief its imagery expresses. This chapter treats that uncertainty not as an embarrassment to be minimized but as the central intellectual challenge of studying Harappan civilization, a challenge this chapter meets by distinguishing sharply between what the evidence supports and what remains, however tempting, informed speculation.

At the same time, uncertainty about specific meanings should not be mistaken for an absence of evidence altogether. Household architecture across many Harappan sites reveals patterns of household organization and craft production. Burial practices, though documented for only a small proportion of the population expected from Harappan settlements, reveal patterns in grave goods that invite cautious inference about social difference. Seals, weights, and inscribed sherds document sustained contact with Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf, and Central Asia, corroborated from the Mesopotamian side by cuneiform references to a distant trading partner Mesopotamian texts called Meluhha. Rural settlements across the wider Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra zone, vastly more numerous than the handful of great cities, show that most Harappans probably never lived in a city at all. And the archaeological record of the centuries after 1900 BCE shows cities losing their distinctive uniformity gradually, unevenly, and for reasons that were more environmental and economic than sudden or catastrophic.

This chapter therefore asks students to hold two truths together. The Indus Civilization was one of the most materially sophisticated societies of the ancient world, urban, and using a sophisticated sign system that many scholars interpret as linguistic writing, although that interpretation remains debated, and connected by trade across a vast Afro-Eurasian network reaching from Central Asia to Mesopotamia. And yet, because its sign system cannot be read, its religion, its politics, and the internal meaning of its own symbols remain more opaque to historians than those of contemporary Egypt or Mesopotamia, where cuneiform and hieroglyphic texts allow historians to hear something closer to the civilization's own voice. Learning to reason carefully within that opacity, distinguishing solid archaeological inference from speculative reconstruction, is itself one of this chapter's central lessons.

By its end, this chapter will have carried the Indus story from the height of Mature Harappan urbanism through its gradual transformation into the regional cultures of the later second millennium BCE, setting the stage for Chapter 5's turn to the Indo-Aryan-speaking communities of the early Vedic world, a very different society, attested by different kinds of evidence, that would eventually come to dominate the historical narrative of northern South Asia.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the evidence for household organization and family life in Mature Harappan cities.
  2. Explain why the Indus script remains undeciphered and what kinds of claims about it can and cannot be responsibly made.
  3. Analyze Indus seals as evidence for administration, craft identity, and possible religious or symbolic meaning.
  4. Evaluate the limits of archaeological evidence for reconstructing Harappan religious belief.
  5. Interpret burial evidence as a cautious indicator of social organization and difference.
  6. Trace documented contact between the Indus world, Mesopotamia, and the Persian Gulf, including the Mesopotamian references to Meluhha.
  7. Describe rural and small-town Harappan settlement and explain why most Harappans did not live in the great cities.
  8. Explain the environmental and economic factors associated with the gradual transformation of Mature Harappan urbanism after approximately 1900 BCE.
  9. Distinguish "decline" from "collapse" and "disappearance," and explain why historians increasingly favor the language of regionalization and transformation.
  10. Identify continuities between Harappan material culture and later South Asian technologies and practices, while avoiding overstated claims of direct religious or cultural inheritance.

Historical Context

Chapter 3 established the physical scale of Mature Harappan civilization: cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, each housing tens of thousands of residents, organized around planned street grids, sophisticated drainage, and standardized weights and measures, and connected to smaller regional centers such as Dholavira across a cultural zone stretching from the northwest borderlands through the Ghaggar-Hakra system into Gujarat. That chapter also introduced the great puzzle that governs this one: despite this material sophistication, no Harappan ruler's name, no dynastic history, and no religious text survives, because the thousands of short inscriptions on seals, pottery, and small tablets remain undeciphered.

This absence of readable texts places the Indus Civilization in a genuinely unusual position among the great early urban civilizations. Contemporary Mesopotamia, writing in cuneiform, and Egypt, writing in hieroglyphic and later hieratic scripts, both left substantial administrative, religious, and literary records that allow historians to reconstruct king lists, legal codes, myths, and everyday transactions in considerable detail. The Indus Civilization offers no comparable textual record. Historians must therefore rely far more heavily on material evidence, supplemented, for questions of external contact, by texts written about the Indus world by outsiders, chiefly Mesopotamian cuneiform references to a trading partner called Meluhha, discussed in Section 4.6.

This chapter also depends on evidence recovered from beyond the handful of famous cities. Regional archaeological surveys across Sindh, Punjab, Gujarat, and the Ghaggar-Hakra zone have documented many hundreds of smaller Harappan-period settlements, ranging from substantial towns to small villages, and this evidence, discussed in Section 4.7, has increasingly reshaped scholarly understanding of Harappan society away from a city-centered picture toward one that recognizes the great cities as the visible peaks of a much larger and more varied settlement system.

Section 4.1

Households and Social Organization

At Mohenjo-daro and other major sites, excavated houses commonly contained multiple rooms organized around internal courts or central spaces and were constructed from varying combinations of mudbrick and baked brick; bathing areas and wells are especially well documented at Mohenjo-daro, though construction materials and access to private water supply varied considerably from city to city and neighborhood to neighborhood. This broad, if uneven, consistency across widely separated cities has led many archaeologists to infer a shared set of domestic norms, if not necessarily a shared political authority enforcing them.

House size varies considerably within individual neighborhoods, from small single-room dwellings to substantial multi-courtyard residences, a pattern generally interpreted as evidence of differences in wealth or status among households, though the precise basis for that differentiation, whether occupational, hereditary, or otherwise, cannot be recovered from architecture alone. Some houses show clear evidence of craft production, including bead-making debris, kiln remains, or shell-working waste, suggesting that many households combined residence with workshop activity rather than separating domestic and productive space as later urban societies sometimes would.

Evidence for family structure remains limited. Archaeologists cannot determine with confidence whether Harappan households were organized around nuclear families, extended kin groups, or some other arrangement, and gendered divisions of labor, discussed cautiously in the Everyday Life feature below, must be inferred indirectly from associated artifacts rather than stated directly by any surviving source. What is reasonably clear is that households functioned as basic units of both residence and production, embedded within neighborhoods that shared drainage infrastructure and, very likely, some degree of communal identity, even in the absence of any text that could confirm how residents understood that identity themselves.

Section 4.2

The Indus Script

As Chapter 1 established, the Indus script remains undeciphered. This section examines that fact more closely, since it shapes nearly every subsequent topic in this chapter. Several thousand short inscriptions survive, chiefly on stone and terracotta seals, but also on copper tablets, pottery, and other objects, typically consisting of only a handful of signs, rarely more than a dozen or so in a single inscription. Approximately four hundred distinct signs have been catalogued, though scholars disagree about how many of these represent genuinely distinct signs versus variant forms of the same sign. Most inscriptions appear to read predominantly right to left, though a small number of multi-line cases have been interpreted by some researchers as running in alternating directions line by line, a pattern sometimes called boustrophedon; this directional evidence, however, settles nothing about whether the underlying system encoded language.

Despite more than a century of attempted decipherments, no reading commands broad scholarly consensus. Proposed identifications of the underlying language have included early Dravidian, an Indo-Aryan language, a language unrelated to either surviving family, and, more controversially, no language at all in the linguistic sense, a possibility examined in the Historical Debate feature below. The brevity of surviving inscriptions is itself a major obstacle: unlike Egyptian hieroglyphics or Mesopotamian cuneiform, which survive in lengthy administrative and literary texts that allowed nineteenth-century scholars to test proposed decipherments against internal consistency, Indus inscriptions are too short to provide the same kind of corroborating evidence for any single proposed reading.

This textbook does not endorse any specific decipherment. It presents the Indus script as an important, actively studied body of evidence whose content remains unknown, and it treats with particular caution any popular or scholarly claim to have identified specific Harappan rulers, deities, or political titles through script analysis. Where such claims appear in other sources students may encounter, this textbook's position, consistent with the methodological caution established in Chapter 1, is that they should be treated as hypotheses rather than established fact.

Section 4.3

Seals and Symbolic Communication

Indus seals, most commonly carved from steatite, a soft stone that hardens when fired, and typically bearing a short inscription above a carved animal motif, represent some of the most evocative and most studied objects surviving from Harappan civilization. The most frequently depicted animal, appearing on a majority of surviving seals, is a horned quadruped that most researchers describe as a "unicorn," generally interpreted as a stylized or composite bovine figure rather than a literal depiction of a one-horned animal known to Harappan observers, though the precise significance of this predominance remains debated. Other seals depict elephants, tigers, rhinoceroses, water buffalo, and, less frequently, more complex scenes involving multiple figures, including a widely discussed seal impression sometimes described as showing a seated figure surrounded by animals, an image some researchers have connected, speculatively, to later South Asian religious iconography, a connection this textbook treats with considerable caution given the immense chronological gap and the absence of any text confirming continuity.

Indus stamp seals were used to make impressions in clay. Sealings bearing impressions of cords, woven cloth, containers, doors, or other closures support administrative uses involving control of stored goods, access, accountability, and possibly trade, though their precise institutional functions remain debated. The consistent association of specific animal motifs with specific inscriptions on multiple seals has led some researchers to propose that seals identified individuals, families, clans, or occupational groups, though, as with script decipherment, no single interpretation has achieved consensus. What can be said with more confidence is that seals and sealings support the existence of structured administrative practices and widely shared symbolic conventions across an enormous geographic area, itself a remarkable indicator of cultural standardization even without a translated text to confirm what that communication specifically conveyed.

Section 4.4

Religion and the Limits of Interpretation

No topic in this chapter requires more sustained caution than Harappan religion. Popular and even some scholarly accounts have proposed identifications of specific Harappan deities, sometimes drawing direct lines to later Hindu religious figures such as a proto-Shiva or a mother goddess, based on seal imagery, terracotta figurines, and comparative analogy with much later South Asian religious tradition. This textbook treats such identifications as speculative rather than established. The evidence, consisting of images without accompanying readable text, permits description but not confident religious interpretation.

What the material evidence does support is more modest but still significant. Recurring tree motifs, horned figures, complex animal and human scenes, and numerous small terracotta figurines of standing female forms with elaborate headdresses suggest that Harappan communities engaged in symbolic or ritual practices involving these images, though their specific meaning, whether connected to fertility, ancestor reverence, seasonal cycles, or something else entirely, cannot be recovered with confidence. No structure yet excavated at a Mature Harappan site has been confidently identified by the broader archaeological community as a temple in the sense familiar from Mesopotamian or Egyptian architecture, a notable absence that itself invites caution against assuming Harappan religious life resembled that of contemporary Bronze Age societies elsewhere.

Historians must resist two opposite errors here: dismissing the rich iconographic evidence as meaningless simply because it cannot be fully interpreted, and overconfidently projecting later, well-documented South Asian religious concepts backward onto images separated from them by well over a thousand years and by the absence of any confirmed textual link. The honest position, consistent with this textbook's approach throughout, is that Harappan communities very likely possessed structured religious or ritual life, expressed through recurring symbols whose specific content remains substantially unknown.

Section 4.5

Burial Practices and Social Difference

Formal cemeteries are documented at a number of Mature Harappan sites, including Harappa itself and, more recently and extensively, at Rakhigarhi and Farmana in the Ghaggar-Hakra zone. Formal cemetery burial, however, represents only a small proportion of the population expected from the size of Harappan settlements, suggesting that other mortuary practices probably existed but remain poorly documented archaeologically. Typical burials involve an extended body placed in a pit, sometimes lined with mudbrick, accompanied by pottery vessels and, less frequently, personal ornaments such as shell or carnelian bead jewelry; excavators at Rakhigarhi have emphasized that most burial structures and grave assemblages there were generally modest, though a small number of unusual brick-lined or otherwise atypical graves also occur.

Differences in grave construction and grave goods may reflect distinctions of status, identity, age, gender, ritual treatment, or other social factors, but the generally modest cemetery assemblages documented so far do not map cleanly onto clearly defined social classes, and sample sizes at most sites remain small enough that broad claims about a rigid or hereditary social hierarchy go beyond what the evidence can firmly support. A 2019 study successfully recovered genome-wide data from one Mature Harappan individual excavated at Rakhigarhi. That individual lacked detectable Steppe-pastoralist ancestry and showed no evidence of ancestry deriving from western Iranian farming populations; instead, the individual's West Eurasian-related ancestry came from an ancient Iranian-related lineage that had diverged before the lineages represented by later Iranian farmers and herders. A single genome, however, cannot establish the genetic composition of Harappan society as a whole or resolve larger questions about its relationship to later South Asian populations, a topic revisited in later chapters.

As with religion, historians must be cautious about inferring specific social institutions, such as caste in any later sense, from burial evidence alone. What the evidence supports is a general pattern of variation among individuals in mortuary treatment, consistent with the differentiated house sizes discussed in Section 4.1, rather than confirmation of any particular institutional structure.

Section 4.6

Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf

The clearest textual evidence bearing on the Indus Civilization comes not from the Indus world itself but from Mesopotamia, whose cuneiform administrative and literary texts, spanning broadly from the late third into the early second millennium BCE, refer repeatedly to trade with a distant land called Meluhha, alongside two other named trading regions, Dilmun and Magan. Dilmun, centered on Bahrain and nearby Gulf areas, became an important entrepôt and intermediary in Gulf trade, particularly in later periods as direct references to Meluhha became less frequent; Magan, generally associated with Oman and adjacent areas, was another important trading region and a source of commodities such as copper. Meluhha itself is described in Mesopotamian texts as a source of valuable materials including carnelian, certain woods, and other goods; the royal tradition associated with Sargon of Agade recorded that ships of Meluhha, Magan, and Dilmun moored at his capital, and other inscriptions refer to Meluhhan interpreters and to individuals identified as being of Meluhhan origin resident in Mesopotamian cities.

Most scholars identify Meluhha with the Indus Civilization or with the trading networks it controlled, based on the correspondence between goods named in the Mesopotamian texts and materials, particularly carnelian beads of a distinctive type manufactured using techniques documented at Harappan sites such as Chanhu-daro, that have been recovered archaeologically at Mesopotamian sites. This identification is widely, though not universally, accepted, and it provides historians with a rare external textual perspective on the Indus world, a valuable corrective to the near-total absence of readable internal texts.

Archaeological evidence corroborates this documented contact. Indus-style seals, weights, and etched carnelian beads have been recovered from sites in Mesopotamia and along the Persian Gulf, while Indus sites themselves have yielded imported materials, including lapis lazuli from Afghanistan and various Gulf-region goods, consistent with a genuine two-way exchange network rather than one-directional trade. This evidence situates the Indus Civilization firmly within a broader Bronze Age exchange network connecting South Asia, the Persian Gulf, Mesopotamia, and, through further connections discussed in Chapter 2, Central Asia and Afghanistan.

Section 4.7

Rural Communities beyond the Major Cities

The great cities of Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Dholavira, however impressive, represent only the largest nodes within a much more extensive Harappan settlement system. Regional archaeological surveys have documented several hundred Harappan-period sites across Sindh, Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the Ghaggar-Hakra zone, ranging from substantial secondary towns of considerable size down to small agricultural villages, indicating a genuine settlement hierarchy of the kind first suggested by the Early Harappan evidence discussed in Chapter 2.

Many smaller settlements relied substantially on farming and herding, often combined with local craft production and exchange. They participated in broader regional economic networks, but the extent to which individual villages provisioned major cities through markets, tribute, taxation, or other arrangements cannot be determined securely from the archaeological evidence alone. Many smaller sites nonetheless show clear participation in the broader Harappan cultural system, including standardized pottery styles, weights, and, at some sites, seals and script fragments, suggesting that Harappan cultural and economic integration extended well beyond the handful of great cities that dominate popular accounts of this civilization.

Recognizing this wider settlement pattern matters for how students should understand the civilization as a whole. A picture drawn only from Mohenjo-daro and Harappa risks implying that Harappan civilization was primarily urban in character; in fact, most people probably lived in smaller towns and villages, engaged directly in agriculture and craft production rather than the more visible administrative and commercial activity concentrated in the largest centers.

Section 4.8

Environmental and Economic Change

Beginning around 1900 BCE, the archaeological record across the greater Indus zone shows a gradual but unmistakable loosening of the standardization that had characterized the Mature Harappan period: weights become less uniform, the distinctive script appears with declining frequency, and long-distance exchange with Mesopotamia, documented in Section 4.6, tapers off. Several converging factors have been proposed to explain this transformation, and most current scholarship treats them as operating together rather than as competing single-cause explanations.

Palaeoclimate and palaeohydrological evidence indicates repeated episodes of weakened rainfall, increasing aridity, and severe river drought across parts of the Indus domain during the later third and early second millennia BCE. Recent reconstructions have identified several severe, prolonged droughts, some lasting more than a century, within roughly the twenty-fifth through fourteenth centuries BCE, and researchers have argued that reduced rainfall and river flow probably contributed to population dispersal, alongside social and economic factors, as part of a genuinely complex explanation rather than a single environmental trigger. These stresses varied considerably by region and likely affected agricultural reliability and freshwater availability unevenly across the Indus zone.

In the Ghaggar-Hakra region specifically, students should be cautious about one once-common but now outdated explanation. Current geomorphological evidence indicates that the major diversions of the Sutlej and Yamuna rivers away from the Ghaggar-Hakra palaeochannel occurred thousands of years before Mature Harappan urbanism, the Sutlej shift shortly after roughly 8000 years ago and the Yamuna shift on the order of 18,000 years ago, rather than during the period of Harappan decline itself. Contemporaneous Himalayan river diversion should not, therefore, be treated as a direct cause of Harappan deurbanization in this region. Harappan settlements along the Ghaggar-Hakra instead occupied an older palaeovalley whose water supply, by the time of Mature Harappan settlement, depended principally on reduced monsoon-fed and seasonal flows rather than a perennial glacier-fed river, making these settlements especially vulnerable to the monsoon weakening and drought episodes described above.

Economic factors also changed during this period, though their causal relationship to urban decline is less certain than once assumed. Long-distance exchange through the Persian Gulf changed and diminished as the Mature Harappan system regionalized, while intermediaries such as Dilmun became increasingly important relative to direct references to Meluhha in Mesopotamian sources. These commercial changes may have reduced support for some specialized urban crafts, but because shifts in Gulf trade patterns are documented across a broad span of time rather than at one precise moment clearly following Harappan regionalization, this textbook presents environmental stress as the better-supported pressure on Harappan urbanism, with changing long-distance exchange as a contributing but less precisely dated factor, rather than asserting a fixed hierarchy or exact sequence of cause and effect between the two.

Section 4.9

Urban Decline, Regionalization, and Harappan Legacies

Archaeologists generally describe the period following approximately 1900 BCE as the Late Harappan or Localization Era, terms chosen deliberately to avoid implying a sudden catastrophic "collapse." Excavated evidence shows population persisting but redistributing in different ways in different regions rather than following one common migration trajectory: many areas saw movement toward smaller settlements, parts of the eastern Harappan domain show increased settlement toward Haryana and the western Ganga-Yamuna interfluve, and Gujarat followed its own distinct regional trajectory. Distinctive regional cultures emerged from this process, retaining some elements of Mature Harappan material culture, such as certain pottery forms and craft techniques, while losing the broader standardization, monumental urban planning, and widespread use of the Indus sign/script system and long-distance seals that had defined the preceding period.

This textbook resists describing this transformation as the "disappearance" of the Indus Civilization. Population did not vanish, and many craft, agricultural, and architectural techniques persisted, in modified form, among the descendant regional cultures of the second millennium BCE, cultures that would eventually interact with the Indo-Aryan-speaking communities examined in Chapter 5. At the same time, this textbook does not claim a simple, unbroken line of direct religious or political inheritance from Harappan civilization into later, textually documented South Asian traditions; any such claim would outrun what the undeciphered script and largely silent material record can support. The most defensible position, and the one this textbook adopts, is that Harappan civilization underwent a genuine transformation, gradual, regionally uneven, and driven by a combination of environmental and economic pressures, rather than a sudden end, and that its technological, agricultural, and craft legacies persisted into the successor cultures examined in the following chapters, even as its urban political and administrative system did not survive intact.

Section 4.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined the social, religious, and external dimensions of Mature Harappan civilization that lie behind the physical infrastructure described in Chapter 3, and it has traced the gradual transformation of that civilization across the centuries following 1900 BCE. Its central and recurring lesson concerns the limits of evidence: because the Indus script remains undeciphered, historians can describe households, seals, burial practices, and trade connections in considerable material detail, but they cannot confidently reconstruct Harappan religion, political organization, or specific social institutions in the way that cuneiform and hieroglyphic texts allow for contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt.

The household and settlement evidence examined in Sections 4.1 and 4.7 shows a society organized around household or domestic production units within standardized urban neighborhoods, embedded within a much larger rural settlement system that most historians now recognize as essential to understanding Harappan civilization as a whole, rather than treating the great cities as representative of the entire population. The sign-system, seal, and religious evidence examined in Sections 4.2 through 4.4 demonstrates a society with sophisticated, widely shared symbolic communication whose specific content remains substantially unrecoverable, a caution this chapter has applied consistently against more confident popular claims. The burial evidence of Section 4.5, drawn from only a small proportion of the expected population, offers cautious support for social differentiation without confirming any specific institutional structure. The Mesopotamian evidence of Section 4.6 provides the clearest external corroboration available for the Indus world's participation in a wider Bronze Age exchange network, while the environmental evidence of Sections 4.8 and 4.9 explains the civilization's gradual transformation as shaped by recurring drought and monsoon weakening alongside economic, settlement, and other regional changes, rather than a single sudden catastrophe or a Himalayan river shift contemporary with the decline itself.

Chapter 5 now turns to a very differently documented world: the Indo-Aryan-speaking communities of the early Vedic period, whose evidence, an orally transmitted religious corpus rather than archaeological material and undeciphered inscriptions, poses its own distinct interpretive challenges, discussed at length in that chapter's opening sections.

Primary Evidence Window

Indus Seals as Material Evidence

Evidence type: Carved steatite seals and clay sealings · Date range: Mature Harappan period, c. 2600–1900 BCE · Recovered through: Excavation at Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal, and related sites

Context: Thousands of seals survive, most bearing a short inscription above a carved animal, most commonly the so-called "unicorn" motif. Some clay sealings preserve impressions of woven cloth or cord, suggesting their use in securing bundled trade goods.

A typical seal measures roughly two to three centimeters square, carved in reverse so that its impression reads correctly, combining a short sign sequence with a finely detailed animal image, evidence of considerable craft skill invested in an object whose full meaning remains unknown.

Analysis: Seals and sealings support the existence of structured administrative practices, standardized symbolic communication across a vast area, and skilled craft production, but their specific content, whether names, titles, clan affiliations, or something else, cannot be read. Historians must therefore describe seals' form and probable function while resisting confident claims about their precise meaning.

Questions for students:

Historical Debate

What Was the Indus Script?

The question: Does the Indus sign system represent a full writing system recording a specific language, or does it represent a non-linguistic symbolic system, such as clan emblems or religious or political symbols, that did not encode spoken language in the way cuneiform or hieroglyphics did?

Interpretation A — A genuine writing system: Some statistical analyses of sign ordering, positional restrictions, and repetition patterns across the surviving corpus have found structure compatible with linguistic writing, and researchers advancing this view argue that these properties favor the interpretation that the system encoded a specific, currently unidentified language, while also acknowledging that further evidence would be needed to confirm it.
Interpretation B — A non-linguistic symbol system: Other researchers have argued that the extreme brevity of the surviving corpus, the absence of any long connected text, and specific properties of the sign inventory are more consistent with a system of emblems or symbols conveying limited categorical information, such as identity or ownership, than with a full language-encoding script, and they have directly disputed whether the statistical measures used to support Interpretation A can reliably distinguish linguistic writing from nonlinguistic symbol systems at all.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same corpus of roughly four thousand short inscriptions and rely on statistical and comparative analysis rather than any confirmed translation; the absence of a bilingual inscription comparable to the Rosetta Stone explains why decipherment has proven so difficult, but it does not by itself demonstrate that the underlying system was nonlinguistic.

Why it matters: The outcome of this debate directly shapes how much historians can ever hope to know about Harappan language, ethnicity, and political organization.

Student task: Explain why neither the statistical structure of the signs nor the absence of a bilingual inscription has, on its own, resolved this question, and identify what kind of additional evidence might help settle it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cities That Did Not Simply Vanish

What Changed

  • Urban standardization in weights, bricks, and script weakened after c. 1900 BCE
  • Long-distance trade with Mesopotamia declined sharply
  • Population shifted away from the largest cities toward smaller regional settlements

What Stayed the Same

  • Agricultural and craft techniques persisted among successor regional cultures
  • Population did not disappear but relocated and adapted
Cause and Effect

Why Urbanism Declined

Long-term causes

Gradual weakening of monsoon rainfall patterns across northwestern South Asia during the later third millennium BCE, compounded by the Ghaggar-Hakra zone's dependence on monsoon-fed rather than perennial glacier-fed flow.

Recurrent or acute pressures

Repeated multi-decadal to century-scale drought episodes, alongside changing patterns of Gulf and Mesopotamian exchange.

Effect

Reduced agricultural reliability and freshwater availability, and diminishing long-distance commercial connectivity, together undermined the capacity of cities to sustain large populations and standardized administration.

This textbook's response

Treats decline as multi-causal regionalization shaped by recurring drought and monsoon weakening alongside economic, settlement, and other regional changes, not a Himalayan river shift contemporary with Harappan decline, and not a single catastrophic collapse.
Historical Vocabulary
Script versus Sign System

A script in the strict linguistic sense encodes a specific spoken language. A sign system may convey meaning, categories, or identity without necessarily encoding language in this way. Because the Indus material's status remains debated, as discussed in Section 4.2, this textbook generally uses the more neutral term "sign system" alongside "script."

Historical Vocabulary
Meluhha and the Boundary Problem

Meluhha is a Mesopotamian name, not a Harappan self-designation; no evidence indicates what the Indus peoples called themselves or their land. Historians must therefore treat "Meluhha" as an outside label useful for tracing trade contact, not as evidence of Harappan political or ethnic self-identity.

Everyday Life and Social History

Craft, Household, and the Limits of Evidence

Workshop debris found within ordinary houses suggests that craft production, bead-making, shell-working, and pottery, was frequently a household activity rather than confined to separate specialized zones. Evidence for gendered labor divisions remains too indirect to support confident claims, and this textbook cautions against projecting later, better-documented South Asian gender norms onto a society whose own voice remains unreadable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the social, religious, and external dimensions of Mature Harappan civilization and its gradual transformation after c. 1900 BCE. It traced household organization and craft production, the undeciphered status of the Indus script and its implications, the probable administrative and symbolic functions of seals, the deep uncertainty surrounding Harappan religion, the cautious inferences available from burial evidence, the documented Mesopotamian trade connection evidenced by references to Meluhha, the essential but often overlooked rural settlement system beyond the great cities, and the environmental and economic factors behind urban decline.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled a disciplined approach to genuinely uncertain evidence: describing confidently what the material record supports, while resisting the temptation to fill the silence of the undeciphered script with unsupported claims about religion, ethnicity, or political structure. Chapter 5 now turns to a differently documented world, the early Vedic period, attested through an orally transmitted textual tradition rather than archaeological remains alone.

Grave goods
Objects placed with the deceased, used cautiously as evidence for mortuary treatment and possible social differentiation.
Localization Era
The post-1900 BCE period of regional cultural transformation following Mature Harappan decline.
Settlement hierarchy
Differences in size and function among Harappan-period settlements, from great cities to small villages.
Aridification
Increasing dryness of climate, implicated in the weakening of Harappan agricultural systems.
Ghaggar-Hakra system
The largely dry historical river system occupied by many Harappan settlements; its major Himalayan-fed tributaries diverted away long before Harappan urbanism, leaving a palaeovalley dependent on monsoon-fed flow that was especially vulnerable to later drought.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter asked students to sit with genuine uncertainty: a civilization materially rich enough to leave seals, cities, and trade records, yet silent in the one way that would let historians hear it speak directly. That silence is not a failure of scholarship but a feature of the evidence itself, and learning to reason carefully within it is one of the most valuable skills this textbook can offer.

Chapter 5 turns to a different kind of silence and a different kind of voice: the orally composed Vedic corpus, which speaks at length but only imperfectly anchors that speech to verifiable historical fact. Carrying forward this chapter's habits of caution will serve students well there too.
