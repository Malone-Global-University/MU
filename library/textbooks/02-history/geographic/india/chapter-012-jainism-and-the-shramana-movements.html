Jainism and the Shramana Movements

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the towns and forest groves of the eastern Gangetic plain during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, a traveler moving from one settlement to the next might, within the space of a single day's walk, encounter a Brahmin priest preparing a fire sacrifice, a wandering ascetic who had renounced fire and household altogether, a follower of Gautama teaching the Four Noble Truths beneath a tree, a follower of Mahavira observing meticulous care not to harm the smallest crawling insect underfoot, and a teacher who insisted that no exertion of will could alter a destiny already fixed by cosmic necessity. None of these figures spoke for the whole of Gangetic religious life. Each represented one thread in an unusually dense and competitive weave of renunciant and priestly traditions, all responding to a shared set of questions and arriving, often, at strikingly different answers.

Chapter 11 examined one of the most historically influential of these answers in close detail: the life, teaching, and early community of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha. That chapter necessarily narrowed its focus, following early Buddhism's doctrines and institutions with the same disciplined attention to evidence that this textbook has applied to every major historical subject. But early Buddhism did not develop in isolation. This chapter widens the lens that Chapter 11 deliberately narrowed. It turns to Jainism, the second of the major shramana traditions to survive as a continuous living religion into the present day, and to the broader company of wandering ascetics, debaters, and rival teachers—among them the Ajivikas—who together made the sixth and fifth centuries BCE one of the most intellectually fertile and competitive periods in South Asian religious history. Early Buddhism was one among several significant renunciant traditions of this period. The surviving evidence does not permit reliable comparison of their relative numbers or institutional prominence, but Jain and Ajivika teachers clearly represented serious rival traditions of their own.

The term historians and scholars of religion use for this broader world is shramana, from a Sanskrit and Prakrit root meaning "one who strives" or "one who exerts effort." It is important to understand from the outset that shramana does not name a single religion, a unified doctrine, or an organized institution. It names a shared social type and a shared field of inquiry: renunciants who left settled household life, typically adopted celibacy, mendicancy, and some form of ascetic discipline, and who devoted themselves to resolving urgent questions about action, consequence, suffering, rebirth, selfhood, and release from the cycle of existence. Buddhist monks were shramanas. Jain monks were shramanas. Ajivika ascetics were shramanas. So, in the broadest sense, were numerous other wandering teachers whose names and doctrines survive only in fragments, usually preserved because a Buddhist or Jain text mentioned them, favorably or, more often, as a foil to be refuted.

At the center of this chapter stands Vardhamana, known to later tradition by the honorific title Mahavira, "great hero," and also called Jina, "conqueror"—a title from which the very name "Jain" derives. Jain tradition does not present Mahavira as the founder of a wholly new religion springing from nothing. It presents him as the twenty-fourth in a lineage of enlightened teachers, or Tirthankaras, "ford-makers," who periodically renew a teaching understood by the tradition to be eternal. This textbook treats that claim of eternal antiquity as a matter of religious conviction rather than a proposition historians can verify, while treating Mahavira himself, and the community that formed and grew around his teaching, as a substantial and well-attested historical subject deserving the same careful, source-conscious treatment this textbook has given to the Buddha, to Vedic religion, and to every other subject examined so far.

This chapter also introduces a figure who rarely receives sustained attention in general histories: Makkhali Gosala, traditionally associated with the Ajivika movement, whose teaching of niyati, cosmic necessity or fate, placed him and his followers in direct and often bitter competition with both Buddhist and Jain communities for followers, patronage, and intellectual authority. Recovering Ajivika thought requires particular care, because nearly everything historians know about it comes from the hostile testimony of its rivals—a methodological problem this chapter addresses directly rather than passing over in silence.

Running through all of this is a single, recurring historical argument. The eastern Gangetic world of the sixth and fifth centuries BCE was not a two-sided contest between a unified "Brahmanical orthodoxy" and a unified "shramana reform movement." It was a genuinely plural intellectual marketplace, in which priestly and renunciant thinkers alike were still actively developing their positions, borrowing vocabulary from one another, disputing publicly, and competing for the patronage of kings, merchants, and householders who, on the evidence of later textual tradition, were not necessarily bound exclusively to any single teacher or community. Understanding Jainism on its own terms, and understanding the wider shramana world in which it took shape, requires setting aside the tempting shorthand of a simple contest between "orthodoxy" and "reform" in favor of a more accurate, more complicated, and ultimately more interesting picture.

This chapter does not attempt a full comparative treatment of Upanishadic and Brahmanical thought; that transformation, occurring alongside and in dialogue with the developments traced here, is the subject of Chapter 13. Nor does this chapter revisit the Buddhist doctrines, institutions, or biography already examined in Chapter 11 in detail. Instead, it asks a question that only makes sense once Chapter 11's groundwork is in place: given that the Buddha's teaching arose from this same broad milieu of questions about karma, rebirth, suffering, and liberation, why did other teachers working within the very same milieu, addressing the very same questions, arrive at such different conclusions? Answering that question is the task of the chapter that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "shramana" as a broad social and intellectual category rather than a single organized religion, and identify the major traditions it encompassed.
  2. Describe Mahavira's traditional biography and explain why historians distinguish between his historical significance and the doctrinal claim that he was the twenty-fourth Tirthankara in an ancient lineage.
  3. Explain the Jain concepts of jiva and ajiva and compare them carefully with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta examined in Chapter 11.
  4. Analyze the centrality of ahimsa in Jain ethical and ascetic practice, while distinguishing rigorous monastic observance from modified lay practice.
  5. Describe the Jain understanding of karma as a quasi-material substance binding the soul, and contrast it with the causal model of karma presented in Chapter 11.
  6. Explain the Three Jewels of Jain practice and their relationship to the path toward liberation.
  7. Describe the structure of Jain monastic and lay communities and assess the role of lay support in sustaining renunciant institutions.
  8. Evaluate the evidentiary problems involved in reconstructing Ajivika teaching, given that most surviving evidence originates from hostile Buddhist and Jain sources.
  9. Compare shramana critiques of Vedic ritual authority with the internal transformations then underway within Brahmanical thought, avoiding a simplified "orthodoxy versus reform" framework.
  10. Assess the significance of religious and philosophical plurality in the sixth- and fifth-century-BCE Gangetic world, including patterns of shared vocabulary, mutual borrowing, and sharp disagreement.
  11. Distinguish between contemporary evidence and later canonical or hagiographical tradition when evaluating claims about early Jain and Ajivika history.
  12. Contextualize Jainism and the wider shramana movements within the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9 and the broader religious history developed across Chapters 9 through 13.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does it mean to describe Buddhism, Jainism, and the Ajivika movement as belonging to a common "shramana" milieu, given how differently they answered similar questions?
  2. How should historians treat Mahavira's traditional status as the twenty-fourth Tirthankara, and what can be said with more confidence about his historical role?
  3. Why does Jain teaching on the soul (jiva) differ so sharply from the Buddhist doctrine of non-self, and what does that difference reveal about the diversity of shramana thought?
  4. Why did ahimsa become so central and so systematically elaborated within Jain ethical and ascetic life?
  5. How does the Jain understanding of karma as something that binds and can be shed differ from the causal, intention-centered Buddhist model examined in Chapter 11?
  6. How did Jain monastic and lay communities sustain one another, and what does this reveal about the practical conditions renunciant movements needed in order to survive?
  7. Why is it especially difficult for historians to reconstruct Ajivika teaching on its own terms, and how should that difficulty shape the confidence of historical claims about the movement?
  8. In what sense did Brahmanical thought change during this period rather than standing still as a fixed target for shramana criticism?
  9. Why might historians describe the sixth- and fifth-century-BCE Gangetic world as religiously and philosophically plural, and what evidence supports that description?

Historical Context

Chapter 9 established the Second Urbanization as the economic and social transformation underlying this entire cluster of chapters: renewed city growth, expanding agricultural surplus, the rise of merchant communities and coinage, and the political consolidation of the mahajanapadas across the Ganges valley. Chapter 10 traced Magadha's emergence as the dominant regional power from among these competing states. Chapter 11 then examined the life and early community of the Buddha within this changing social, economic, and political environment. These transformations formed part of the historical context in which new and changing religious traditions became especially visible, but they should not be treated as sufficient causes of Buddhism, Jainism, or the wider shramana movements. This chapter now returns to that wider religious ferment directly, examining it not as background to Buddhism but as a historical subject in its own right.

The eastern Gangetic region in which Mahavira, Gautama, Makkhali Gosala, and numerous other teachers were active shared a common set of social conditions: growing cities and market towns; a class of merchants and householders with resources to support renunciant communities through alms and patronage; competing monarchies and oligarchic republics whose rulers, on the evidence of later textual traditions, are depicted as sometimes engaging with or supporting more than one religious tradition; and an intellectual environment in which questioning inherited Vedic ritual authority had become a widespread and socially visible activity. Buddhist and Jain texts alike describe a crowded field of wandering teachers, debaters, and ascetic orders active in cities such as Rajagriha, Vaishali, Shravasti, and Kaushambi, circulating along the same trade routes and, at times, competing directly for the same audiences and the same patrons.

Reconstructing this world poses distinctive evidentiary problems, extending those already introduced in Chapter 11. Early Buddhist and Jain communities preserved their respective founders' teachings orally for generations before those teachings were committed to writing in anything resembling their surviving canonical forms, and each tradition's textual corpus was shaped, edited, and to some degree systematized by later generations of monks working within established institutional and doctrinal frameworks. This means that texts describing Mahavira's life and teaching, like the Pali texts describing the Buddha's life and teaching examined in Chapter 11, cannot be read as contemporary transcripts. They must be read as later religious tradition, valuable for reconstructing what Jain communities came to believe and practice, but requiring independent corroboration, where it exists, before particular biographical or chronological claims can be treated as secure history.

A further complication, specific to this chapter, concerns sources for movements outside the two traditions—Buddhism and Jainism—that eventually produced their own surviving canonical literatures. The Ajivikas, once a renunciant community with royal patrons, left no surviving Ajivika canonical corpus securely identifiable as such. Nearly everything known about Ajivika teaching survives because Buddhist and Jain authors, writing as rivals and often as polemicists, chose to describe, and typically to refute, Ajivika positions within their own texts. This chapter treats such hostile testimony as a valuable but methodologically fraught source, one that preserves real information about a rival tradition's positions while inevitably filtering those positions through the interpretive and rhetorical purposes of the reporting tradition.

Finally, this chapter's treatment of Brahmanical thought requires a caution introduced already in Chapter 6 and developed further in Chapter 13: the Vedic and Brahmanical tradition against which shramana movements defined themselves was not a fixed, unchanging target. It was itself undergoing significant internal transformation during precisely this period, a transformation examined in its own right in the next chapter. Readers should therefore understand the developments traced in this chapter as one part of a larger, multi-sided intellectual transformation unfolding simultaneously across renunciant and priestly traditions alike, not as a simple story of new challengers confronting an unchanging old order.

Section 12.1

The Shramana Milieu

Chapter 11 introduced, briefly, the wandering ascetics and renunciant teachers active in the eastern Gangetic region during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, before narrowing its focus to a single figure and a single tradition. This section reopens that wider world on its own terms. The term historians use to describe it, shramana, derives from a root meaning "to strive" or "to exert effort," and it names a social type rather than a specific doctrine: a person who had renounced household life, family obligation, and, typically, participation in Vedic ritual, in favor of a mendicant existence devoted to disciplined striving toward some form of liberation from suffering and rebirth.

Shramanas were not, by this description alone, members of a single religion. Buddhist monks were shramanas. Jain monks were shramanas. Ajivika ascetics were shramanas. So too, in a broader sense, were various other wandering teachers, some named in Buddhist and Jain texts, whose specific doctrines have not survived in any detail, and whose communities did not persist as continuous, identifiable traditions into later history. What united these figures was less a shared conclusion than a shared vocation and a shared set of urgent questions: What is the nature of the self, if any self exists? What binds a being to the cycle of rebirth? What role, if any, does intentional action play in shaping future existence? Is liberation from this cycle possible, and if so, by what means? What obligations, if any, does a renunciant retain toward family, society, and living beings generally?

Shramana communities depended on settled populations—villages, towns, and cities alike—for alms and survival, a dependency this chapter will return to in Section 12.6, while growing urban centers provided especially visible concentrations of patrons, audiences, and rival teachers. Mendicants relied on alms freely offered by householders, merchants, and sometimes rulers, and this reliance placed renunciant communities in constant, visible interaction with settled society even as their teaching often urged withdrawal from it. Buddhist and Jain texts alike describe crowded scenes of public debate in which rival teachers, their students, and interested householders gathered to hear competing positions defended and challenged, sometimes before an audience that included kings or wealthy patrons whose support could substantially affect a community's fortunes. This competitive, public, and socially embedded character of shramana religious life is essential to keep in view throughout this chapter: these were not isolated hermits pursuing private enlightenment in total seclusion from the world, but active participants in a lively and often contentious religious marketplace centered on the growing cities of the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9.

It is equally important to state plainly, at the outset of this chapter, what the shramana milieu was not. It was not a single organized movement with shared leadership, a shared canon, or even broad doctrinal agreement. Buddhist, Jain, and Ajivika teachers disagreed sharply and explicitly with one another—disagreements this chapter examines in detail in the sections that follow—and each tradition's surviving texts devote considerable energy to refuting the positions of its shramana rivals, not merely those of Brahmanical priests. Treating "shramana" as a synonym for a unified reform movement opposed to a unified Brahmanical orthodoxy would flatten a genuinely plural and often internally contentious intellectual world into a simplified binary that the evidence does not support. This chapter's task, across the sections that follow, is to recover some of that plurality by examining Jainism in careful, dedicated detail and then turning to the Ajivikas and the wider field of shramana competition.

Section 12.2

Mahavira

Jain tradition identifies Vardhamana, later honored with the titles Mahavira ("great hero") and Jina ("conqueror," from which "Jain" derives), as the historical teacher around whom the continuous Jain religious community formed. As with the Buddha's biography examined in Chapter 11, the surviving accounts of Mahavira's life were composed and compiled by his religious community generations after his death, drawing on oral tradition that had already been shaped by devotional and doctrinal purposes before being fixed in writing. This textbook therefore treats the traditional biography that follows as religiously significant testimony about how the early Jain community remembered and understood its founding teacher, rather than as a neutral contemporary record subject to independent verification in every detail.

Jain tradition places Mahavira's birth in the region of Vaishali, in the eastern Gangetic world discussed in Section 12.1, into a family of the ruling or warrior class. Śvetāmbara tradition commonly gives his birth and death as 599 and 527 BCE respectively, though Jain traditional chronologies are not entirely uniform even within the tradition itself. As with the traditional dates long assigned to the Buddha, examined in Chapter 11, modern scholarship has raised significant questions about this chronology, chiefly on comparative grounds relating to the internal chronology of early Buddhist texts, which mention Mahavira's death occurring during the Buddha's own lifetime. Because the Buddha's own dates have themselves been revised by much current scholarship toward a range centered on the fifth century BCE, as discussed in Chapter 11, modern reconstructions frequently shift Mahavira's dates later as well, often into the fifth century BCE—proposals include, among others, a range centered on roughly 540 to 468 BCE—but no single revised chronology commands scholarly consensus. This textbook presents the traditional Jain chronology and the range of revised scholarly proposals side by side, without treating any one revised dating as settled, in the same spirit of open acknowledgment of unresolved chronological debate that Chapter 11 applied to the Buddha's dates.

According to Jain tradition, Vardhamana renounced his household at around the age of thirty. Traditional biographies diverge on his life before renunciation: Śvetāmbara tradition describes a period of marriage and fatherhood, while Digambara tradition holds that Mahavira never married. This divergence is one more instance of the broader pattern, discussed further in Section 12.6, in which later Jain traditions preserve differing biographical and institutional details rather than a single uniform account. Following his renunciation, tradition holds that Vardhamana undertook approximately twelve years of severe ascetic practice, including extended fasting, exposure to physical hardship, and rigorous non-attachment to bodily comfort, before attaining kevala jnana, complete or omniscient knowledge. Having attained this state as a kevalin—an omniscient being who remains embodied and continues to teach—he taught for several decades, gathering a substantial community of monastic and lay followers before his death at Pavapuri, at which point Jain tradition holds that he achieved final moksha, the soul's complete and permanent release from embodied existence. Jain doctrine distinguishes these two moments carefully: kevala jnana is omniscience attained while still living and teaching, while moksha proper occurs only when embodied existence itself ends.

A point of particular importance for this textbook, and one that a careless reader might easily overlook, is that Jain tradition does not present Mahavira as the founder of an entirely new religion emerging without precedent. Jain teaching holds that Mahavira was the twenty-fourth in a succession of Tirthankaras, "ford-makers" or "crossing-makers," enlightened teachers who periodically appear across vast cycles of cosmic time to renew and restate a teaching the tradition regards as eternal rather than newly invented. This means that, within Jain self-understanding, Mahavira is best described not as the inventor of Jainism but as its most recent, and for historical purposes its most historically accessible, renewer. This textbook can treat Mahavira as a substantial historical figure and the effective founder, in a historical sense, of the continuous Jain religious community without thereby affirming or denying the tradition's claim regarding the deep antiquity of the full twenty-four-Tirthankara lineage, a claim that lies outside what historical evidence can independently establish.

One figure within that lineage, however, deserves particular historical attention: Parshvanatha, revered as the twenty-third Tirthankara whom Jain traditional chronology commonly places around 877–777 BCE. Historians who accept Parshvanatha as a historical figure do not regard those precise traditional dates as secure, and some place a possible historical teacher considerably later, perhaps around the seventh century BCE; the traditional dating and any scholarly reconstruction of Parshvanatha's historicity should therefore be kept clearly distinct. Unlike most of the Tirthankaras preceding him in Jain tradition, whose historicity lies far beyond what evidence can address, Parshvanatha has attracted serious scholarly interest as a possible historical figure, on the strength of references within early Jain literature suggesting that communities associated with Parshvanatha's teaching existed independently before, and in some tension with, Mahavira's own movement, and that some of Mahavira's early followers may have come from within an already-existing Parshvanatha tradition. This possibility, together with its genuine evidentiary limits, is examined further in the Historical Debate feature below. A historically significant possibility, rather than an established conclusion, is that Mahavira's community built upon, reformed, or absorbed an earlier renunciant tradition associated with Parshvanatha's name and memory, rather than representing an entirely unprecedented religious innovation appearing from nothing in the sixth century BCE; the evidence, discussed further in the Historical Debate feature below, does not permit certainty on this point.

Section 12.3

Jain Teachings

At the foundation of Jain thought lies a fundamental distinction between two categories of reality: jiva, the living or sentient soul, and ajiva, everything that is nonliving. This distinction organizes Jain cosmology, ethics, and soteriology alike, and it marks one of the most significant points of contrast between Jain teaching and the Buddhist teaching examined in Chapter 11.

Jain philosophy holds that jivas are genuinely plural and, in their essential nature, eternal: each living being possesses an individual soul that persists, in principle, unchanged in its fundamental nature across countless rebirths, even as it becomes entangled, through action, with the material substance Jain tradition calls karma, discussed fully in Section 12.5. This affirmation of an enduring, individual soul stands in direct contrast to the early Buddhist doctrine of anatta, or non-self, examined in Chapter 11, according to which no permanent, unchanging self can be identified within the ever-shifting aggregates that constitute a person. Students should resist collapsing this contrast into a simplistic formula such as "Jainism believes in a soul, Buddhism does not," because the philosophical stakes run deeper than that shorthand suggests. For Jain thought, the reality of an enduring jiva is precisely what makes liberation intelligible: it is this same soul, purified of accumulated karmic matter, that attains liberation and thereafter exists eternally in a state of omniscient bliss. For early Buddhist thought, by contrast, the very notion of a fixed self to be liberated is itself identified as a primary source of clinging and suffering, and liberation is understood very differently, as the cessation of craving rather than the purification and eternal continuation of an unchanging soul. These are not minor variations on a shared theme; they represent two of the shramana milieu's most fundamentally different answers to the question of what a person is.

Jain teaching further holds that jivas are pervasively present throughout the natural world—not only in humans and animals in the ordinary sense, but, according to traditional Jain cosmology, in plants, in what Jain classification describes as earth-bodied, water-bodied, fire-bodied, and air-bodied beings—one-sensed forms of life associated with these elements—and in countless minute organisms invisible to ordinary perception. This expansive understanding of where sentient life exists has direct and far-reaching ethical consequences, examined in detail in Section 12.4, because it means that an extraordinarily wide range of ordinary activity—walking, eating, farming, even breathing—carries the potential to harm living beings, whether one intends such harm or not.

Beyond the jiva-ajiva distinction, Jain teaching envisions liberation, or moksha, as the soul's complete freedom from karmic bondage and its consequent return to its natural state of infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and power—a state the tradition associates with the accomplishment of kevala jnana discussed in Section 12.2. The Tirthankara ideal, examined already in connection with Mahavira, represents the tradition's highest religious exemplar: a soul that has attained kevala jnana, omniscient knowledge, while still embodied, and that teaches the path by which others may follow before its own eventual, and final, moksha at the end of embodied existence. Jain philosophy also developed a distinctive epistemological principle known as anekantavada, "the doctrine of manifold aspects," which holds that reality is genuinely complex and that any single perspective on it, however true within its own terms, necessarily remains partial; later Jain thinkers developed this principle into an elaborate logical system for evaluating the scope and limits of any given assertion. While a full treatment of anekantavada's later philosophical elaboration lies beyond this chapter's scope, its presence within the broader Jain intellectual tradition reflects the same careful, systematic cast of mind visible in Jain teaching on karma and ethics throughout this chapter.

Section 12.4

Ahimsa and Ascetic Discipline

No single ethical principle occupies a more central place in Jain teaching and practice than ahimsa, nonviolence or non-harming. It would be a serious historical error to suggest that Jainism invented the concept of nonviolence; ideas about restraint from harming living beings appear, in varying forms and with varying degrees of emphasis, across Vedic, Buddhist, and other South Asian religious traditions of this general era, and the broader ethical and ascetic value of restraint from violence was very much a live concern across the shramana milieu described in Section 12.1. What is genuinely distinctive about Jain tradition is not the presence of ahimsa as a value but the extraordinary scope, systematic elaboration, and practical centrality it received within Jain ethical and ascetic life, extending, as Section 12.3 explained, to a vast range of living beings that most other traditions of the period did not recognize as sentient at all.

For Jain monks and nuns, ahimsa translated into an unusually demanding regime of daily practice. Traditional accounts describe monastics sweeping the path before them to avoid stepping on insects, straining drinking water to avoid harming minute organisms, restricting travel during certain seasons to avoid trampling emerging plant and insect life, and observing careful restraint in speech, thought, and action generally, on the understanding that harm could be committed not only through direct physical violence but through careless words or even hostile intentions. This comprehensive discipline connects directly to the five vows around which formal Jain monastic life is organized: ahimsa (nonviolence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (celibacy), and aparigraha (non-attachment or non-possession). Together these constitute what later Jain tradition terms the Five Great Vows, undertaken by monks and nuns in their most rigorous form as a comprehensive framework for ascetic life.

Dietary discipline followed directly from these commitments. Jain dietary practice increasingly developed toward the strict vegetarian norms characteristic of later Jain tradition, including in many communities restrictions on root vegetables, on the understanding that uprooting a plant destroys it entirely and potentially harms additional organisms living in the surrounding soil, whereas eating fruit that has already fallen or been given in alms does not require destroying the plant itself. The earliest canonical material, however, preserves more complicated food rules than this later mature code, including passages whose interpretation remains debated among scholars, so students should not simply project the fully developed later dietary system unchanged onto Mahavira's own lifetime. Fasting formed another significant component of ascetic discipline, ranging from routine periodic fasts to, in some traditional accounts, extended terminal fasting undertaken voluntarily by advanced practitioners as a final act of non-attachment, a practice examined further, with appropriate historical caution regarding its frequency and social meaning across different periods, in later chapters where Jain devotional and ascetic literature receives fuller treatment.

It is essential, however, that students not assume every Jain layperson practiced ascetic discipline at anything approaching the level expected of monks and nuns. Jain tradition developed a distinct and considerably less demanding framework of lay observance, examined further in Section 12.6, involving modified and partial versions of the Five Great Vows—sometimes described as the lesser or "small vows"—appropriate to householders who continued to work, marry, raise families, and participate in economic life. A lay follower observing this ethical logic might, in principle, take considerable care in selecting an occupation that minimized harm to living beings, favoring trade over occupations understood to involve more direct or unavoidable violence, such as certain forms of agriculture—an illustration of how the ethical reasoning of ahimsa could bear on occupational choice, though this should not be read as evidence that early lay Jain communities were already characteristically drawn toward commerce, since the strong historical association between Jain communities and mercantile occupations developed and solidified over many subsequent centuries. Recognizing this graduated structure—rigorous monastic observance at one end, variably demanding lay practice at the other—prevents the serious historical distortion of treating every historical Jain, in every period, as though they lived under identical and maximally demanding ascetic constraints.

Section 12.5

Karma and Liberation

Chapter 11 introduced karma, within the Buddhist context, chiefly as a matter of intentional moral action generating consequences that shape future experience—a fundamentally ethical and causal framework centered on the quality of a person's volitions. Jain teaching, while sharing the broader shramana concern with karma, rebirth, and the possibility of liberation, developed a substantially different and more elaborately systematized understanding of what karma actually is and how it operates.

In Jain philosophy, karma is conventionally described not merely as an abstract moral principle but, in terms distinctive to the tradition, as a subtle form of matter that adheres to and binds the soul as a direct consequence of action, and especially as a consequence of action accompanied by passions such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed. Every action, in this framework, carries the potential to draw karmic matter toward the soul, with more intensely passionate or harmful actions drawing correspondingly heavier and more binding karmic accretion. This accumulated karmic matter, Jain teaching holds, obscures the soul's natural qualities of infinite knowledge, perception, and bliss, much as dust settling upon a mirror obscures the clarity of its reflection without altering the mirror's underlying nature. Liberation, on this understanding, is fundamentally a process of purification: preventing the further influx of new karmic matter through disciplined restraint, of the kind examined in Section 12.4, while simultaneously working to shed karmic matter already accumulated through ascetic practice, particularly through austerities such as fasting, which Jain tradition understands as an especially effective means of burning away existing karmic bondage.

This comparison illuminates something important about the shramana milieu as a whole. Buddhism and Jainism, despite sharing a broad concern with karma, rebirth, and liberation, and despite sharing some overlapping vocabulary, developed genuinely distinct causal models of what binds a being to suffering and what liberation actually consists of. For early Buddhist teaching, bondage arises chiefly from craving and ignorance, and liberation consists in the cessation of that craving through insight and disciplined practice, without positing a substance-like karmic matter accumulating upon an enduring soul, since Buddhist teaching denies the existence of any such permanent soul in the first place. For Jain teaching, by contrast, bondage is understood in more explicitly quasi-material terms, as an actual accretion binding an eternally existing soul, and liberation consists in the soul's purification and eventual return to its own true, unencumbered nature. Neither tradition's model can be reduced to the other, and students should resist any temptation to treat "karma" as though it named one single, uniformly understood concept shared identically across every shramana tradition.

The Jain path toward liberation is traditionally organized around what later tradition terms the Three Jewels: samyak darshana, right faith or right view, an initial and necessary orientation toward the truth of Jain teaching; samyak jnana, right knowledge, a genuine and increasingly refined understanding of reality as Jain philosophy describes it, including the jiva-ajiva distinction examined in Section 12.3; and samyak charitra, right conduct, the disciplined ethical and ascetic practice examined in Section 12.4, understood not merely as external behavior but as the practical embodiment of right faith and right knowledge. Jain tradition holds that these three, pursued together rather than in isolation from one another, constitute the path by which a soul progressively sheds karmic bondage, first attaining kevala jnana, omniscience while still embodied, and ultimately achieving moksha, final liberation from embodied existence itself, as discussed in connection with the Tirthankara ideal in Sections 12.2 and 12.3.

Section 12.6

Jain Monastic and Lay Communities

Jain tradition describes the community that formed around Mahavira's teaching as a fourfold order, encompassing monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen—in Jain terminology, sadhus, sadhvis, shravakas, and shravikas respectively. This fourfold structure is significant for understanding how Jainism, like early Buddhism examined in Chapter 11, managed to sustain itself institutionally across centuries rather than remaining a small and ephemeral circle of ascetic practitioners.

Monks and nuns undertook the full and rigorous observance of the Five Great Vows discussed in Section 12.4, adopted an itinerant, largely homeless existence, depended entirely on alms freely offered by lay supporters for their food, and generally avoided prolonged settlement in any single location, though seasonal residence during the monsoon months—when travel risked greater harm to emerging plant and insect life, consistent with the ahimsa principle examined above—became an important and enduring feature of Jain monastic practice. Renunciation of household life, property, and family ties was understood as a necessary precondition for the kind of comprehensive ethical and ascetic discipline the monastic vows demanded, a discipline this textbook has already suggested was not realistically expected of the wider lay community.

Lay Jains, described in Jain sources by the term shravaka ("hearer," referring to one who hears and accepts the teaching without adopting full monastic renunciation), occupied an equally essential though differently structured role within the tradition. Lay practice involved modified versions of the monastic vows, careful attention to occupation and diet consistent with ahimsa, regular alms-giving to support monks and nuns, periodic fasting and religious observance, and, in many cases, active participation in building and maintaining religious institutions. This reciprocal relationship—monastics providing spiritual instruction and a living exemplar of the ascetic path, laypeople providing the material support that made monastic life materially possible—was not incidental to Jainism's survival but constitutive of it. A religious tradition demanding total renunciation of household life from its most advanced practitioners could not have sustained itself across generations without a parallel and substantial lay community willing to provide ongoing material support, precisely the pattern already examined for early Buddhism in Chapter 11.

Evidence from early Jain literature suggests that women participated actively in this fourfold community from an early period, with traditions describing substantial communities of nuns alongside monks, though, as with early Buddhist evidence examined in Chapter 11, historians should exercise caution before assuming that gender relations within the earliest Jain community precisely mirrored the more fully developed institutional arrangements described in considerably later texts.

Students should also exercise particular chronological caution regarding one further point. Later Jain history came to be organized around a significant institutional division between two major traditions, the Digambara ("sky-clad," associated with monastic nudity as an expression of complete non-attachment) and the Shvetambara ("white-clad," associated with monastics wearing simple white robes), whose disagreements extended to matters including monastic practice, the content and transmission of canonical texts, and questions concerning women's capacity for liberation. This division, however, reflects a considerably later stage of Jain institutional history. Scholars generally describe it as a gradual process of differentiation beginning around the start of the Common Era and continuing over subsequent centuries, rather than a single event, and well removed in time from Mahavira's own lifetime. This textbook is careful not to project the fully differentiated Digambara-Shvetambara division backward onto the sixth and fifth centuries BCE as though Mahavira's own community had already been organized along these later lines. The existence of this later division is noted here chiefly because it helps explain why different Jain textual traditions, including differing versions and selections of canonical literature, have survived into the present—a point relevant to the Primary Source Window later in this chapter—rather than because the division itself belongs to the historical scope of this chapter.

Section 12.7

Ajivikas and Other Movements

Jainism and Buddhism were the most historically durable of the shramana traditions examined in this textbook, but they were not the only significant renunciant communities of the sixth and fifth centuries BCE. Among the most historically significant of the movements that did not survive as continuous, independently attested traditions into the present were the Ajivikas, associated chiefly with the teacher Makkhali Gosala, whose community rivaled both Buddhist and Jain movements for followers and royal patronage across the eastern Gangetic world and, on the evidence of later inscriptions examined in subsequent chapters, retained significant patronage and visibility for some centuries afterward.

The central and most historically distinctive Ajivika teaching, as reported chiefly by hostile Buddhist and Jain sources, concerned niyati, generally translated as fate, necessity, or destiny. According to this teaching, as later Buddhist and Jain texts describe it, the course of every being's existence, across the entire span of its transmigration through countless rebirths, is fixed by an impersonal cosmic determinism rather than shaped by the intentional moral quality of individual actions in the manner Buddhist and Jain teaching, in their very different ways, both maintained. If niyati genuinely governed existence as thoroughly as hostile sources suggest Ajivika teaching held, this would carry the striking implication that ascetic discipline, ethical restraint, and religious striving could not, strictly speaking, alter or accelerate a being's progress toward liberation, since that progress was already predetermined by cosmic necessity independent of individual effort.

This chapter must insert a firm methodological caution at precisely this point. Virtually all surviving evidence concerning Ajivika doctrine comes from Buddhist and Jain texts composed by members of rival traditions actively engaged in refuting Ajivika teaching, not from any surviving body of Ajivika self-description. This is a source problem of a different order than the ones already encountered in this chapter regarding Mahavira's biography or the Buddha's biography in Chapter 11, where at least the tradition's own later community produced and preserved its own account, however removed in time from the events it describes. For the Ajivikas, historians possess no comparable body of self-authored literature at all. What survives is testimony from opponents with an obvious interpretive and rhetorical stake in presenting Ajivika teaching in the least favorable and most easily refuted light—commonly, as this chapter has already suggested, as a doctrine of passive fatalism that rendered ethical striving pointless and that could therefore be dismissed as spiritually and practically bankrupt by contrast with the disciplined effort Buddhist and Jain teaching each demanded of their own followers.

Responsible historical treatment of the Ajivikas therefore requires treating hostile testimony as evidence of what Buddhist and Jain communities wished to say about a genuine and evidently formidable rival, rather than as a neutral or complete account of what Ajivika teachers actually taught and how their own followers understood that teaching. It is possible, though not demonstrable from the surviving evidence, that Ajivika teaching on niyati involved considerably more nuance, and perhaps a more developed account of the relationship between cosmic determinism and disciplined ascetic practice, than the simplified caricature preserved in rival sources suggests. Historians can state with reasonable confidence that the Ajivikas existed as an organized shramana community with a distinct and recognized identity; that their central distinguishing teaching concerned some form of niyati, or fate; that they practiced their own forms of rigorous asceticism, since even hostile sources acknowledge Ajivika ascetic rigor even while disputing its ultimate spiritual value; and that they attracted sustained attention from Buddhist and Jain rivals, and eventually significant royal patronage, discussed briefly in the Chronology Preview above and treated more fully in the Mauryan-era chapters that follow. Beyond this, confident historical reconstruction of Ajivika thought on its own terms remains genuinely limited by the character of the surviving evidence.

Buddhist and Jain texts of this period name other renunciant teachers as well, describing a genuinely crowded field of wandering ascetics, materialists, skeptics, and debaters active alongside Mahavira, Gautama, and Makkhali Gosala. This chapter does not attempt biographies of every such figure, both because the surviving evidence for most of them is exceedingly thin and because a chapter-length treatment of every named rival teacher would obscure rather than clarify this chapter's central argument. What matters historically is the pattern these figures collectively illustrate: a genuinely diverse and competitive shramana marketplace in which Buddhism and Jainism, however historically significant they would eventually prove to be, represented only two among several serious and actively contending answers to the shared questions of karma, rebirth, and liberation.

Section 12.8

Debate with Brahmanical Traditions

Jain and other shramana traditions defined themselves, in significant part, through explicit critique of Vedic sacrificial religion and Brahmanical ritual authority, and this section examines the substance of that critique while resisting an oversimplified narrative in which a unified body of shramana "reformers" confronted a static and unified Brahmanical "orthodoxy."

The genuine points of contention were substantial. Buddhist and Jain traditions, along with other renunciant critiques known from the surviving sources, rejected the authority of Vedic ritual sacrifice as a path to liberation or even, in the case of animal sacrifice specifically, as ethically defensible at all—a rejection that followed directly, for Jain tradition, from the comprehensive commitment to ahimsa examined in Section 12.4, since Vedic sacrificial ritual in this period could involve the ritual killing of animals. These traditions similarly rejected the hereditary ritual privilege claimed by Brahmin priests as a class, holding instead, at least in principle, that liberation was accessible to any person, regardless of birth, who undertook the appropriate ascetic and ethical discipline, though the degree to which this principle translated into genuinely open access to monastic ordination and religious authority across different castes and social groups in actual historical practice remains a matter deserving careful, evidence-based examination rather than idealized assumption. They further rejected the notion that ritual correctness, precisely performed sacrifice, or inherited priestly knowledge could by themselves secure a favorable rebirth or liberation, insisting instead, in their differing ways, on the primacy of intentional ethical action, ascetic discipline, or, in the distinctive Jain case, the purification of karmic matter accumulated through the soul's own conduct.

Yet it would seriously misrepresent this period's intellectual history to describe it as a simple contest between shramana reformers and an unchanging Brahmanical establishment. As Chapter 13 examines in detail, Brahmanical thought during precisely this same period was undergoing its own profound internal transformation, developing, through the early Upanishadic tradition, sophisticated new understandings of atman, Brahman, karma, and moksha that in some respects paralleled, and in other respects diverged sharply from, the positions shramana teachers were simultaneously developing. Brahmanical thinkers were not passive targets absorbing shramana criticism without response; they were active participants in the same broad intellectual ferment, reworking inherited Vedic concepts to address many of the same urgent questions about action, consequence, selfhood, and liberation that animated the shramana movements examined in this chapter.

Nor should students imagine that either "side" of this supposed contest was internally unified. Shramana traditions disagreed among themselves as sharply as they disagreed with Brahmanical positions, as Sections 12.3, 12.5, and 12.7 have already demonstrated in detail regarding the self, karma, and cosmic determinism. Brahmanical thought, similarly, encompassed considerable internal diversity across different schools, regions, and individual teachers, a diversity Chapter 13 explores directly. The genuinely accurate picture, developed further in Section 12.9, is not a two-sided debate but a genuinely multi-sided intellectual field in which renunciant and priestly thinkers alike were actively reworking inherited categories, borrowing productively from one another's vocabulary even while sharply disputing one another's conclusions, in response to the shared social and economic transformations of the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9.

Section 12.9

Religious Pluralism in Ancient India

This section draws together the threads developed across the preceding eight sections into a single interpretive synthesis. The term "pluralism" requires a preliminary caution: this chapter does not use it to suggest that the sixth- and fifth-century-BCE Gangetic world had developed a consciously articulated modern doctrine of religious tolerance, in the sense that phrase might carry in later periods examined elsewhere in this textbook. Rather, the term describes an observable historical condition: the coexistence, competition, and mutual interaction of multiple, genuinely distinct religious and philosophical traditions within a shared social and economic environment, without any single tradition possessing, on the evidence available, the institutional power to eliminate or fully subordinate its rivals during this formative period.

Several patterns support this description. Multiple renunciant teachers—Mahavira, Gautama, Makkhali Gosala, and others whose names survive more faintly in the sources—were active contemporaneously within overlapping geographic regions, drawing followers from among the same urban and rural populations. Later textual traditions depict rulers and wealthy patrons engaging with, and sometimes supporting, more than one teacher or community, a pattern of multiple patronage that recurs, on firmer evidentiary footing, throughout later South Asian history. Ordinary householders, similarly, do not appear, on the evidence of these traditions, to have been necessarily bound to a single teacher or community in the exclusive sense familiar from some later religious contexts; a household might plausibly have given alms to Jain monks, attended a Buddhist teaching, and continued to observe inherited Brahmanical domestic ritual within the same generation. This textbook offers such a household as a reconstructed illustration of what nonexclusive patronage could have looked like in practice, not as a documented case, reflecting a religious landscape in which boundaries between traditions, while real and meaningful, were not always experienced as mutually exclusive by the wider population in the way formal monastic vows were experienced as exclusive by ordained renunciants themselves. Public debate, as Section 12.1 described, was a recognized and socially significant form of religious contest, in which rival teachers directly engaged one another's positions before audiences capable of judging, supporting, or rejecting what they heard.

This shared environment also produced genuine overlap in vocabulary and conceptual structure across traditions that nonetheless reached sharply different conclusions. Karma, rebirth, dharma, moksha or nirvana, ahimsa, and renunciation itself functioned as terms and concepts in active circulation across Jain, Buddhist, Ajivika, and Brahmanical discourse alike, even though, as this chapter has shown repeatedly regarding karma and the self in particular, each tradition frequently attached substantially different meanings to shared vocabulary. This observation carries an important interpretive consequence that students should carry forward: the presence of shared terminology across traditions does not, by itself, demonstrate that one tradition simply borrowed or copied its teaching wholesale from another. It demonstrates, more accurately, that these traditions developed within a common intellectual environment, addressing a common set of urgently felt questions, using a partly shared vocabulary inherited from the wider culture of the Second Urbanization's Gangetic world, while nonetheless arriving, through some combination of inherited concepts, debate, interaction, parallel development, and borrowing whose precise directions cannot always be reconstructed, at genuinely distinct and often incompatible conclusions.

Understanding this plural, competitive, and conceptually interconnected religious environment is essential preparation for the chapters that follow. Chapter 13 turns directly to the internal transformation of Brahmanical thought during this same period, a transformation this chapter has deliberately treated only in outline, in order to give it the dedicated attention its historical importance demands. Later chapters, beginning with the Mauryan Empire in Chapter 15, will show how subsequent rulers navigated, patronized, and sometimes attempted to regulate this plural religious landscape, most famously in the case of Ashoka's dhamma, examined in Chapter 16, which explicitly addressed the challenge of governing a society encompassing multiple, mutually competing religious traditions of precisely the kind this chapter has described.

Section 12.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter opened with a question left implicit at the close of Chapter 11: given that the Buddha's teaching arose from a broad shramana milieu shared with other renunciant traditions, why did those other traditions, addressing substantially the same questions, arrive at such different conclusions? The chapter's central historical argument, developed across the preceding nine sections, is that the sixth- and fifth-century-BCE Gangetic world did not produce a single reform movement with one coherent set of answers. It produced an interconnected but genuinely plural field of competing religious and philosophical experiments, of which Buddhism, examined closely in Chapter 11, and Jainism, examined closely in this chapter, were the two most historically durable, but by no means the only serious participants.

Sections 12.1 and 12.2 established the shared social type—the shramana, or striving renunciant—within which Mahavira's historical career took shape, while insisting on the important distinction between Mahavira's substantial historical significance as the founder, in a historical sense, of the continuous Jain community, and the separate doctrinal claim, belonging properly to religious tradition rather than to historical verification, that he was the twenty-fourth in an ancient lineage of Tirthankaras. The possible historicity of Parshvanatha, examined further in the Historical Debate feature below, adds a further layer of genuine, if limited, historical complexity to this picture.

Sections 12.3 through 12.5 developed the chapter's central comparative argument in detail, showing that Jain teaching on the soul (jiva, understood as eternal and individually plural, in sharp contrast to Buddhist anatta), on karma (understood in quasi-material terms as an accretion binding the soul, in contrast to the Buddhist model centered on intentional causation without reference to an underlying soul), and on the practical path toward liberation (organized around the Three Jewels and the comprehensive ethical discipline of ahimsa) constituted a genuinely distinct system of thought, not a variant restatement of Buddhist teaching under different vocabulary. The chapter's treatment of ahimsa, in particular, demonstrated that Jainism's distinctiveness lay not in inventing nonviolence as a value, already present in varying forms across the wider shramana and even Brahmanical worlds, but in the extraordinary systematic scope it gave that value, extending ethical concern to an unusually wide category of living beings and translating that concern into a demanding, comprehensively structured monastic discipline.

Section 12.6 shifted from doctrine to institution, showing that Jainism's survival across centuries depended on the same reciprocal structure already observed for early Buddhism in Chapter 11: a rigorously disciplined monastic community supported materially by a broader and less demanding lay community, together forming the fourfold Jain sangha. This section also introduced an important chronological caution, warning against projecting the later, fully differentiated Digambara and Shvetambara traditions backward onto Mahavira's own lifetime.

Section 12.7 widened the chapter's scope beyond Jainism to examine the Ajivikas, whose teaching of niyati, cosmic necessity, placed them in direct doctrinal competition with both Buddhist and Jain positions on action, consequence, and liberation. This section's central methodological lesson—that virtually all surviving evidence about Ajivika teaching comes from hostile Buddhist and Jain testimony, requiring historians to read that testimony critically rather than as neutral description—illustrates a source-critical problem of particular severity, extending the evidentiary cautions this textbook has applied consistently since Chapter 1's treatment of the undeciphered Indus script and Chapter 11's treatment of the Pali canon.

Sections 12.8 and 12.9 completed the chapter's argument by dissolving the tempting but inaccurate shorthand of a simple contest between shramana reformers and Brahmanical orthodoxy. Genuine points of doctrinal and ethical disagreement between shramana and Brahmanical traditions were real and substantial, but Brahmanical thought was itself changing during this period, a transformation reserved for full treatment in Chapter 13, and neither shramana nor Brahmanical traditions were internally unified. The accurate picture is one of genuine religious and philosophical plurality: multiple traditions competing for followers and patronage, sharing an overlapping vocabulary and a common set of urgent questions, while reaching frequently incompatible conclusions through some combination of inherited concepts, debate, interaction, parallel development, and borrowing whose precise directions cannot always be reconstructed.

Chapter 13 now turns to complete this cluster of chapters on Second Urbanization religious transformation by examining the internal development of Brahmanical thought itself, tracing the movement from the Brahmanas through the early Upanishads and the emergence of sophisticated new understandings of atman, Brahman, karma, and moksha. Students should carry forward from this chapter a central lesson that will recur throughout this textbook's later treatment of South Asian religious history: apparent doctrinal unity, whether attributed to "Hinduism," "Buddhism," "Jainism," or any other broad religious label, typically conceals a much more varied, contested, and historically developing reality beneath the surface of a convenient name.

The Ācārāṅga Sūtra, Early Jain Canonical Tradition on Ascetic Conduct

Text: Ācārāṅga Sūtra ("Book of Conduct"), Book II, First Part, Lectures 1–4 (on begging for food, begging for a couch, walking, and modes of speech)—the composite paraphrase below draws on these specific lectures · Tradition: Early Jain canonical literature, preserved chiefly within the Shvetambara canon · Transmission: Orally transmitted within the Jain monastic community for a considerable period before being committed to writing in a form resembling its surviving text · Type: Instructional material concerning ascetic discipline, ahimsa, and the conduct expected of Jain monks

Context: Like the Pali canon examined in Chapter 11's treatment of early Buddhism, the Ācārāṅga Sūtra is not a contemporary transcript of Mahavira's own words captured at the moment of utterance. It is a later transmitted tradition, shaped by generations of oral recitation and communal memory within the early Jain monastic community before achieving anything resembling its surviving written form. Historians therefore treat it as valuable evidence for early Jain normative ideals, teaching, and prescriptions concerning ascetic conduct and ahimsa; the actual, lived practice of ordinary monks and nuns across different periods must be reconstructed separately, using other kinds of evidence where available, and cannot simply be read off a prescriptive text of this kind.

Paraphrase: Drawing together instructions from these lectures on begging for food, begging for a couch, walking, and modes of speech, the text instructs the Jain monk to exercise the most careful and deliberate attention in walking, in seeking food and lodging, and in speaking, so as to avoid causing harm to any living being, however small, and describes this comprehensive vigilance as essential to the ascetic path Mahavira himself is said to have exemplified through years of rigorous self-discipline.

Analysis: This material illustrates, in concrete practical terms, the systematic elaboration of ahimsa examined in Section 12.4: nonviolence, within this textual tradition, is presented not as an abstract ideal but as a comprehensive prescriptive discipline extending into the smallest details of daily monastic conduct. The text also illustrates how Jain tradition used Mahavira's own remembered example as a model for prescribing monastic behavior, a pattern of using a founding teacher's exemplary conduct to ground later community norms that recurs across many religious traditions examined in this textbook, including early Buddhism in Chapter 11.

Historical Debate

Can Historians Reconstruct Pre-Mahavira Jain Tradition?

The question: Did an organized renunciant tradition associated with Parshvanatha exist independently before Mahavira's own teaching career, and if so, what was Mahavira's relationship to it—did he found an entirely new movement, reform an existing one, or absorb an already-established community into his own?

Interpretation A — Mahavira as the effective founder of a new movement: On this view, while Jain tradition's claim of twenty-four Tirthankaras extending across vast cosmic time belongs to religious cosmology rather than historical evidence, Mahavira himself should be understood as the effective historical founder of the Jain community in any meaningful historical sense, since he is the figure around whom the earliest verifiable monastic and lay institutions, teachings, and textual traditions actually took shape. References to Parshvanatha and earlier Tirthankaras, on this view, may represent later Jain tradition retrospectively organizing its own history around a lineage structure, a common pattern in religious historiography, rather than reliable evidence of Parshvanatha's independent historical existence and organized following.
Interpretation B — Mahavira as reformer or successor within an existing tradition: On this view, early Jain textual references suggesting tension, dialogue, or absorption between followers of Parshvanatha and Mahavira's own emerging community are historically significant and should not be dismissed merely because Parshvanatha's fuller biography, like that of the earlier Tirthankaras, cannot be independently verified. If an organized ascetic tradition associated with Parshvanatha's name and memory genuinely predated Mahavira, even by a substantial interval, then Mahavira's historical role would be better described as a reformer, consolidator, or successor working within an existing renunciant lineage rather than as the outright founder of a wholly new religious movement.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of early Jain canonical references to Parshvanatha and to interactions between his followers and Mahavira's community, but they weigh the reliability and interpretive significance of these later-transmitted references differently, much as historians weigh similarly transmitted evidence differently elsewhere in this textbook's treatment of orally preserved religious tradition.

Why it matters: This textbook treats Mahavira as the central, historically substantial figure around whom the continuous Jain community took shape, while remaining genuinely open to the possibility, supported by textual evidence internal to the Jain tradition itself, that he worked within, reformed, or absorbed an earlier renunciant tradition rather than creating Jain religious life from nothing. This is a more historically cautious position than either flatly affirming the full twenty-four-Tirthankara lineage as verified history or flatly dismissing Parshvanatha as a later invention.

Student task: Using the evidence and interpretive framework described above, explain why the question of Parshvanatha's historicity matters for how historians understand the origins of the Jain community, and identify what kind of additional evidence, if it existed, might help resolve the debate more decisively.

Historical Vocabulary
Jiva and Ajiva

Jiva refers, in Jain philosophy, to the living or sentient soul, understood as genuinely plural—each being possessing its own individual soul—and as eternal in its essential nature, persisting across countless rebirths until it achieves liberation. Ajiva refers to everything nonliving: matter, space, time, and other categories of nonsentient reality within Jain cosmology. This fundamental distinction underlies Jain ethics, since the comprehensive Jain commitment to ahimsa follows directly from the tradition's expansive recognition of where jiva, sentient life, is understood to exist—not only in humans and animals but, according to Jain teaching, in plants and in what Jain classification describes as earth-bodied, water-bodied, fire-bodied, and air-bodied beings—one-sensed forms of life associated with these elements.

Students should take particular care to distinguish the Jain jiva from the Buddhist doctrine of anatta, or non-self, examined in Chapter 11. Jain teaching affirms an enduring individual soul as the very foundation of its account of karmic bondage and liberation; early Buddhist teaching denies that any such permanent, unchanging self can be identified at all. These represent two of the shramana milieu's most fundamentally different philosophical starting points, not minor variations on a single shared idea.

Historical Vocabulary
Tirthankara and Jina

A Tirthankara, literally "ford-maker" or "crossing-maker," is, within Jain religious teaching, an enlightened being who attains kevala jnana, omniscient knowledge, while still embodied, and who, before achieving final moksha at the end of embodied existence, teaches the path by which others may cross the metaphorical river of suffering and rebirth toward liberation themselves. Jain tradition holds that twenty-four such Tirthankaras have appeared within the current cosmic era, of whom Mahavira, examined in Section 12.2, is understood as the twenty-fourth and most recent.

Jina, "conqueror," is a title applied to a Tirthankara in recognition of that figure's conquest over the passions and karmic bondage that otherwise perpetuate the cycle of rebirth. The English term "Jain" and "Jainism" derive directly from this title, meaning, in effect, "follower of the conqueror" or "the tradition of the conquerors." This textbook uses "Tirthankara" and "Jina" as they appear within Jain religious tradition, while maintaining, as Section 12.2 explained, appropriate historical caution regarding which specific claims about this lineage can be independently verified by historical evidence and which belong properly to religious conviction.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Karma Across Traditions

What Changed

  • Karma shifted, across different shramana and Brahmanical traditions, from a broadly shared concern with action and consequence into sharply distinct causal models—Buddhist intentional causation, Jain quasi-material karmic accretion, and, as Chapter 13 will show, evolving Brahmanical Upanishadic formulations
  • The soul or self became a genuine point of philosophical division rather than a settled premise, with Jain teaching affirming an eternal individual jiva against the Buddhist denial of any permanent self
  • Renunciant institutions grew from individual teachers and small circles of followers into organized, durable monastic and lay communities capable of sustaining themselves across generations

What Stayed the Same

  • Karma remained a central problem across Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical traditions, connecting action in some manner to future consequence and rebirth even as the specific mechanism of that connection differed sharply, while the Ajivikas, as represented in surviving rival accounts, challenged the causal efficacy of intentional action altogether through their doctrine of niyati
  • Liberation from the cycle of rebirth remained the shared ultimate goal across Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical traditions alike, however differently each understood what liberation actually consisted of
  • Renunciant communities continued to depend on lay material support throughout the periods this textbook examines, a pattern that persists into much later South Asian religious history

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the shared vocabulary and the genuine underlying differences prevents students from assuming that "karma" names one single, uniformly understood concept across South Asian religious history, a caution this textbook will apply again whenever karma reappears in later chapters.

Cause and Effect

Sustaining Renunciant Traditions Through Lay Support

Long-term causes

The Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9 produced growing cities, agricultural surplus, and a prosperous merchant and householder class with resources to support religious communities beyond their own immediate subsistence needs.

Short-term causes

Individual teachers such as Mahavira attracted committed followers willing to adopt comprehensive ascetic renunciation, while nearby householders and merchants chose to provide alms and material support, while also receiving teaching and opportunities for merit.

Effect

A stable, reciprocal fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen developed, capable of transmitting teaching, maintaining institutions, and surviving across generations well beyond the lifetime of any single founding teacher.

This textbook's response

This same reciprocal structure recurs across early Buddhism, examined in Chapter 11, and Jainism, examined in this chapter, and will reappear, in modified forms, throughout this textbook's later treatment of South Asian religious institutions.

Historical complexity: Not every renunciant tradition of this period is equally visible to historians today; the eventual disappearance of independently attested Ajivika communities from the historical record, discussed in Section 12.7, cannot be confidently explained on present evidence, and this textbook resists speculating about its causes beyond what the sources support.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ahimsa, Occupation, and Daily Practice Among Lay Jains

For lay Jain households, the comprehensive ethical commitment to ahimsa examined in Section 12.4 had direct and practical consequences for daily life that extended well beyond formal religious observance. Occupational choice became, for many lay Jains, a matter of genuine ethical significance: occupations understood to involve extensive, direct, or unavoidable harm to living beings—certain forms of agriculture involving plowing and the consequent disturbance of soil-dwelling organisms, for instance—could, in principle, be regarded as less consistent with strict ahimsa than trade, commerce, or other occupations understood to involve comparatively less direct harm. This should be read only as an illustration of how the ethical logic of ahimsa could bear on occupational reasoning, not as evidence that early lay Jain communities were already characteristically drawn toward commerce; the strong historical association between Jain communities and mercantile occupations is a pattern that developed and solidified over many subsequent centuries and should not be projected back onto the community's earliest period.

Diet, too, carried this same practical weight. Careful dietary restraint governed by ahimsa, attentive food preparation to avoid harming minute organisms, and periodic fasting connected the household's daily routines to the broader ascetic ideal exemplified by monks and nuns, even though, as this chapter has emphasized, lay practice remained considerably less demanding than full monastic observance and the strict vegetarian code later characteristic of Jain life developed more fully over subsequent centuries. Alms-giving to wandering monks and nuns provided another recurring point of contact between household and monastic life, embedding the reciprocal structure examined in Section 12.6 into the practical rhythms of daily domestic and commercial activity across the towns and cities of the eastern Gangetic world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has widened the lens Chapter 11 deliberately narrowed, moving from a focused study of early Buddhism to a broader examination of the shramana milieu from which it emerged, with particular attention to Jainism as the second major renunciant tradition to survive as a continuous living religion into the present, and to the Ajivika movement as an instructive example of a once-significant shramana community whose teaching survives chiefly through the testimony of its rivals.

The chapter began by defining "shramana" carefully as a broad social and vocational category—wandering, mendicant renunciants devoted to disciplined striving toward liberation—rather than as a single organized religion or shared doctrine. It then turned to Vardhamana Mahavira, examining his traditional biography with the same source-critical caution this textbook applied to the Buddha's biography in Chapter 11, distinguishing Mahavira's substantial historical role as the effective founder of the continuous Jain community from the separate doctrinal claim that he was the twenty-fourth in an ancient lineage of Tirthankaras, a claim belonging to religious tradition rather than historical verification. The chapter gave particular attention to Parshvanatha, the twenty-third Tirthankara in Jain tradition, as a genuinely debated possible historical predecessor whose relationship to Mahavira's own movement remains a live scholarly question, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

The chapter's central comparative argument unfolded across its treatment of Jain teaching, ahimsa, and karma. Jain philosophy's foundational distinction between jiva, the eternal and individually plural soul, and ajiva, the nonliving, stands in direct and instructive contrast to the Buddhist doctrine of anatta examined in Chapter 11, illustrating that the shramana milieu produced genuinely different, not merely differently worded, answers to the question of what a person fundamentally is. The chapter examined ahimsa as Jainism's most distinctive ethical achievement—not the invention of nonviolence as a value, which appears in varying forms across the wider period, but its extraordinarily systematic elaboration into a comprehensive ascetic discipline governing monastic conduct in the smallest particulars, tempered by a considerably less demanding structure of lay observance that this chapter took care not to conflate with full monastic practice. The chapter's treatment of karma showed that Jain teaching, describing karma in quasi-material terms as an accretion binding the soul, developed a causal model of bondage and liberation substantially different from the Buddhist model centered on intentional action without reference to an enduring soul, reinforcing the chapter's broader argument that shared vocabulary across shramana traditions frequently concealed genuinely distinct underlying philosophies.

The chapter then examined the institutional structure that allowed Jainism to survive and grow: a fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, bound together by a reciprocal relationship in which rigorous monastic renunciation depended on sustained lay material support, and lay religious life drew its structure and meaning from the monastic ideal it supported but did not fully replicate. This section introduced an important chronological caution against projecting the later, fully differentiated Digambara and Shvetambara traditions backward onto the earliest Jain community.

Turning beyond Jainism, the chapter examined the Ajivika movement and its teaching of niyati, cosmic necessity, as a further significant and historically serious shramana tradition. This section's central methodological achievement was to demonstrate, concretely, why historians must read hostile source testimony critically: because virtually all surviving evidence about Ajivika teaching comes from Buddhist and Jain texts written by rivals with an evident interpretive stake in presenting Ajivika doctrine unfavorably, confident historical reconstruction of Ajivika thought on its own terms remains genuinely limited, even though the movement's historical existence, general doctrinal orientation, and evident competitive significance are well established.

The chapter's final sections dissolved the oversimplified narrative of a two-sided contest between shramana reformers and a static Brahmanical orthodoxy. Genuine doctrinal disagreement between shramana and Brahmanical traditions was real, but Brahmanical thought was itself undergoing significant internal transformation during this same period, a transformation reserved for Chapter 13's full treatment, and neither shramana nor Brahmanical traditions were internally unified. The chapter's concluding argument described the sixth- and fifth-century-BCE Gangetic world as a genuinely plural religious and intellectual environment: multiple traditions coexisting, competing for followers and patronage, sharing overlapping vocabulary and common questions, and yet arriving at frequently incompatible conclusions through some combination of inherited concepts, debate, interaction, parallel development, and borrowing whose precise directions cannot always be reconstructed.

These arguments prepare students directly for Chapter 13, which completes this textbook's three-chapter treatment of Second Urbanization religious transformation by turning to the Brahmanical tradition's own internal development during this same era, and they anticipate later chapters, particularly Chapter 16's treatment of Ashoka's dhamma, in which this textbook will examine how South Asian rulers navigated, patronized, and sought to accommodate the plural religious landscape this chapter has described.

Key Terms

Shramana
A broad social and vocational category naming wandering, mendicant renunciants devoted to disciplined striving toward liberation; encompasses Buddhist, Jain, Ajivika, and other renunciant traditions without implying shared doctrine or organization.
Mahavira
Vardhamana Mahavira, "great hero," the central historical teacher around whom the continuous Jain religious community formed, revered in Jain tradition as the twenty-fourth Tirthankara.
Jina
"Conqueror," an honorific title applied to a Tirthankara, from which "Jain" and "Jainism" derive.
Tirthankara
"Ford-maker," an enlightened teacher within Jain tradition who achieves liberation and teaches the path by which others may cross the cycle of rebirth; Jain tradition recognizes twenty-four across the current cosmic era.
Parshvanatha
Revered as the twenty-third Tirthankara in Jain tradition and a possible earlier historical teacher whose relationship to Mahavira's community remains historically debated.
Jiva
In Jain philosophy, the living or sentient soul, understood as eternal in essential nature and individually plural, in contrast to the Buddhist denial of a permanent self.
Ajiva
In Jain philosophy, everything nonliving, including matter, space, and time.
Ahimsa
Nonviolence or non-harming, the central ethical principle of Jain teaching, extended systematically to an unusually broad category of living beings and structured comprehensively into monastic discipline.
Five Great Vows
The comprehensive monastic vows of Jain ascetic life: ahimsa (nonviolence), satya (truthfulness), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (celibacy), and aparigraha (non-attachment).
Karma (Jain conception)
In Jain philosophy, a subtle form of matter that binds the soul as a consequence of action, particularly action accompanied by passion, obscuring the soul's natural qualities until purified through disciplined practice.
Three Jewels
The Jain path to liberation, comprising samyak darshana (right faith), samyak jnana (right knowledge), and samyak charitra (right conduct).
Kevala jnana
Complete or omniscient knowledge, the state Jain tradition holds Mahavira achieved following years of ascetic practice, preceding his subsequent teaching career.
Moksha (Jain usage)
Liberation, understood in Jain philosophy as the soul's complete freedom from karmic bondage and its return to its natural state of infinite knowledge, perception, and bliss.
Ajivika
A shramana community associated chiefly with Makkhali Gosala and the doctrine of niyati, competing with Buddhist and Jain communities for followers and patronage.
Niyati
Fate, necessity, or cosmic determinism, the central Ajivika teaching as reported by hostile Buddhist and Jain sources.
Makkhali Gosala
Teacher traditionally associated with the Ajivika movement, known chiefly through the testimony of rival Buddhist and Jain texts.
Digambara
"Sky-clad," one of the two major later Jain traditions, associated with monastic nudity; its full institutional differentiation postdates Mahavira's own lifetime.
Shvetambara
"White-clad," the other major later Jain tradition, associated with monastics wearing simple white robes; its full institutional differentiation postdates Mahavira's own lifetime.
Ācārāṅga Sūtra
An early Jain canonical text preserving teaching on ascetic conduct and ahimsa, transmitted orally before being fixed in something resembling its surviving written form.
Sangha (Jain usage)
The fourfold Jain religious community of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen.
Śravaka / Shravika
A lay follower of Jain teaching, male (shravaka) or female (shravika), who observes modified ethical vows without adopting full monastic renunciation.
Anekantavada
"The doctrine of manifold aspects," a Jain epistemological principle holding that reality is complex and that any single perspective on it remains necessarily partial.
Renunciation
The deliberate abandonment of household life, family ties, and property in favor of a mendicant existence devoted to religious discipline, foundational to the shramana vocation examined throughout this chapter.
Religious pluralism (as used in this chapter)
The historical condition of multiple, genuinely distinct religious and philosophical traditions coexisting, competing, and interacting within a shared social environment, without implying a consciously articulated modern doctrine of tolerance.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to resist a tempting simplification: the idea that South Asia's sixth- and fifth-century-BCE religious ferment produced a single "shramana movement" standing in simple opposition to a single "Brahmanical orthodoxy." The actual historical picture, recovered through careful attention to Jainism's distinctive teachings, the Ajivikas' contested legacy, and the genuinely plural competitive environment in which both operated alongside early Buddhism, is considerably more interesting and considerably more historically accurate than that convenient shorthand.

Jainism's comprehensive elaboration of ahimsa, its affirmation of an eternal and individually plural soul, and its quasi-material understanding of karmic bondage together constitute a genuinely distinctive philosophical and ethical system, not a variant of Buddhism under a different name. The Ajivikas, despite leaving no surviving texts of their own, remind students that historical reconstruction depends entirely on what evidence happens to survive, and that a tradition's absence from the historical record in its own voice is not evidence of its historical insignificance. And the wider shramana milieu, considered alongside the internal transformation of Brahmanical thought that Chapter 13 examines next, demonstrates that South Asia's ancient religious history is best understood as a genuinely plural field of competing, interacting, and mutually influencing traditions rather than a simple story of reform displacing orthodoxy.

Students who understand Jainism on its own terms, who recognize the evidentiary caution required to discuss the Ajivikas responsibly, and who carry forward this chapter's central lesson—that shared religious vocabulary frequently conceals genuinely distinct underlying philosophies—are well prepared to encounter the internal transformation of Brahmanical thought in Chapter 13, and, further ahead, the complex religious landscape that later rulers, beginning with the Mauryas in Chapter 15, would need to navigate, patronize, and, in Ashoka's case, attempt to hold together within a single, religiously diverse empire.

Chapter 13 now turns from the renunciant traditions examined across Chapters 11 and 12 to the Brahmanical tradition's own contemporaneous transformation, tracing the movement from the Brahmanas through the early Upanishads and the emergence of new understandings of atman, Brahman, karma, and moksha that developed within, rather than merely against, the inherited Vedic tradition.
