South Asia and the Second World War

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the afternoon of 3 September 1939, within hours of Britain's declaration of war on Germany, the Viceroy of India announced that India was also at war. The announcement did not require a vote in any Indian legislature or the consent of any elected Indian minister. In eight provinces Congress ministries, elected only two years earlier, were governing millions of people; in others, ministries of the Punjab Unionists, the Bengal coalition, and local groupings administered their provinces; across the subcontinent hundreds of princes ruled under treaty. All of them, and close to four hundred million people, were now belligerents. This chapter asks what that fact, and the six years of global war that followed, did to the foundations of British authority in South Asia.

Chapter 82 ended with the political landscape of 1939: the provincial elections of 1937, the Congress ministries, the reorganized Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the socialist and communist currents, and the competing visions of federation and nationhood that had emerged under the Government of India Act of 1935. This chapter begins when a global conflict collided with those arrangements. It traces the unilateral decision to enter the war, the resignation of the Congress ministries, the widening of the Muslim League's constitutional claims, the Lahore Resolution of March 1940, the August Offer, the limited civil resistance of 1940 and 1941, the expansion of the war into Asia, and the failed Cripps Mission of March and April 1942.

The chapter's second line of argument concerns mobilization. The British Empire needed South Asian men, goods, money, and ports on a scale unmatched in its history. The prewar Indian Army numbered roughly two hundred thousand, while approximately 2.5 million men volunteered for Indian armed service over the war as a whole. Cumulative enlistment and personnel serving on a particular date are different measures. Factories, railways, docks, and mines expanded to meet military demand. A financial system tied to London generated enormous sterling credits and powerful inflation. Wartime shortages and controls reshaped the lives of soldiers' families, factory workers, farmers, and urban consumers. The story of the war in South Asia is therefore not only a story of constitutional negotiation but also of a society reorganized for a global conflict.

The chapter distinguishes carefully between two kinds of chronology. The political narrative runs from 1939 to April 1942 and is told in the first three divisions. The structural history of military and economic transformation runs across the whole war, 1939 to 1945, and is told in the last two. This arrangement allows the chapter to explain the scale of wartime change without absorbing the specialized narratives reserved for Chapter 84, which covers the Quit India uprising, the Indian National Army, the eastern front, and the Bengal famine. Chapter 85 then follows the negotiations that ended British rule.

The chapter also insists on the plurality of South Asian responses. Congress, the League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Sikhs, Ambedkar and Scheduled Caste organizations, the Communists, princes, provincial ministries, soldiers, workers, and peasants each experienced the war differently. The geographic frame is also wider than British India: Ceylon, Nepal, the princely states, Burma, and the Indian Ocean connections of the region are part of the story. The chapter does not project the borders of 1947 onto the map of 1940, and it treats the Lahore Resolution as a document with ambiguities that later events resolved in particular ways.

The evidence is as varied as the subject. Viceregal statements and party resolutions, the text of the Lahore Resolution, the Draft Declaration of 1942 published as a British command paper, memoirs, censored soldiers' letters, official histories of the armed forces, Reserve Bank reports, and price statistics all contribute. Each has limits. Official documents reveal the colonial government's reasoning but not the experience of ordinary subjects; party records reflect partisan aims; statistics describe aggregates but conceal local conditions. The feature boxes in the chapter train students to ask who produced each source, why, and what it can and cannot establish.

By the close of the chapter, students should understand why the Second World War did not simply interrupt India's independence struggle but transformed its terms: Britain needed Indian cooperation yet resisted meaningful sovereignty, the principal Indian movements moved toward different constitutional strategies, and the economic and military consequences of the war reshaped colonial society. That combination of imperial dependence, constitutional deadlock, divergent national movements, and economic disruption is the connecting argument of all twenty sections.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the constitutional basis on which India was declared at war in September 1939 and why the absence of Indian consent became a political issue.
  2. Trace the Congress Working Committee's demands, the Viceroy's responses, and the resignation of the Congress ministries in October and November 1939.
  3. Compare the responses of the Muslim League, Hindu Mahasabha, Sikh organizations, Scheduled Caste organizations, princely rulers, and left parties to the outbreak of war.
  4. Analyze the Muslim League's move from minority safeguards to claims of Muslim nationhood, including the roles of Jinnah and provincial leaders.
  5. Interpret the language, ambiguities, and later uses of the Lahore Resolution of March 1940 without projecting the borders of 1947 onto 1940.
  6. Evaluate the terms of the August Offer of 1940 and explain why Congress rejected it and the League withheld unconditional acceptance.
  7. Distinguish Gandhi's individual satyagraha of 1940–41 from the Quit India movement of 1942.
  8. Explain how the entry of the Soviet Union and Japan into the war changed South Asia's strategic position and Indian political calculations.
  9. Assess the terms of the Cripps proposals and the reasons for the mission's failure, distinguishing documentary evidence from later interpretation.
  10. Describe the expansion and transformation of the Indian armed forces and the global deployment of Indian soldiers.
  11. Evaluate the wartime economy, including industrial expansion, sterling balances, inflation, and unequal burdens.
  12. Assess how imperial dependence, emergency powers, and constitutional deadlock weakened British political authority without ending its coercive capacity.

Historical Context

Three earlier developments frame this chapter. First, the Government of India Act of 1935, examined in Chapter 81, had created provincial autonomy but not federal responsibility. Provinces gained elected ministries from 1937, but at the centre the Governor-General retained wide authority over defence and external affairs, and the federation that would have integrated the princely states never came into being. When war broke out, the constitution gave India's elected leaders power in the provinces and none over the decision to fight.

Second, the elections of 1937 and the ministries of 1937 to 1939, covered in Chapter 82, had reshaped party relations. Congress won majorities in most provinces and formed ministries in eight. The Muslim League did poorly in the elections but reorganized under Jinnah, and the rivalry between the two grew sharper through disputes about coalition-building, symbols, education, and representation. Regional parties, the Hindu Mahasabha, Scheduled Caste organizations, socialists, and communists complicated the picture. The war reached a polity that was already fragmented and competitive.

Third, the memory of the First World War shaped expectations on both sides. As Chapter 78 showed, India had contributed soldiers and resources to that conflict, and constitutional promises in 1917 had been followed by the limited Montagu-Chelmsford reforms, the Rowlatt Acts, and Jallianwala Bagh. Indian leaders in 1939 were determined not to repeat a cycle in which sacrifice was met with disappointment, and British officials remembered the wartime upheavals of 1914 to 1918 and anticipated difficulty.

The period also falls within the long economic history of the colonial state. The Great Depression of the 1930s had damaged agricultural prices and rural incomes, and the colonial economy was already marked by uneven industrial growth, heavy military expenditure, and dependence on sterling. The wartime financial arrangements therefore built on existing structures. Geography mattered too: India's position between the Middle East and Southeast Asia, its long coastline, and its eastern frontier with Burma made it vulnerable and indispensable once Japan entered the war.

India Enters the War: Imperial Authority and Political Rupture

How a decision taken in London and Delhi committed a subcontinent to war, and how Congress, the League, and others responded.

Section 83.1

India Enters the Second World War, September 1939

On 1 September 1939 German forces crossed the Polish frontier, and on 3 September, after Berlin ignored a British ultimatum, Britain and France declared war on Germany. Within hours the Viceroy and Governor-General of India, Victor Hope, Marquess of Linlithgow, announced that India too was at war. No vote was taken in the Central Legislative Assembly, no provincial ministry was asked for an opinion, and no Indian political party was consulted beforehand. The announcement made some three hundred million people in British India, and by extension the subjects of hundreds of princely states whose rulers were bound to the Crown, belligerents in a conflict that had begun on another continent. Understanding why this was legally possible, and why it nevertheless became politically explosive, is the starting point for the whole chapter.

The legal explanation lay in the unfinished constitution that Chapter 81 traced. The Government of India Act of 1935 had introduced provincial autonomy in 1937, and the elections and ministries examined in Chapter 82 showed how seriously Indian parties took the new provincial governments. But the federal half of the Act, which would have given Indian representatives some responsibility at the centre, had never come into force because too few princely states acceded to it. At the centre, therefore, the older arrangements survived. The Governor-General appointed an Executive Council, retained reserved authority over defence and external affairs, and answered to the Secretary of State for India and the British Cabinet rather than to any Indian electorate. The Central Legislative Assembly contained elected members, but it could not remove the Executive. When the Crown went to war, the constitution treated India's belligerency as following automatically from that decision.

The contrast with the self-governing Dominions is instructive. Canada's Parliament debated the question and declared war a week after Britain did, on 10 September. In South Africa the government divided over entry, and the question was settled in Parliament by a narrow vote that brought General Smuts to power. Ireland remained neutral. India was not a Dominion, and its representative institutions had no such power. For Indian nationalists this was precisely the point. Many Congress leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru, had condemned fascism through the 1930s, supported Republican Spain and China, and felt no sympathy for Hitler. The objection was therefore not that India should remain neutral between democracy and Nazism, but that a country denied self-government could not be committed to war by an authority it had not chosen. Nehru, who was returning from China when the war began, was among those who stated the position in these terms.

The announcement also exposed the distinction between imperial military obligation and Indian consent. The Indian Army had long been used as an imperial reserve, as Chapter 78 showed for the First World War, and its costs had historically fallen heavily on Indian revenues. In 1939 plans already existed for Indian units to garrison positions in Egypt, Aden, the Persian Gulf, Burma, and Malaya. A 1939 defence review and the financial arrangements that followed shifted a larger share of the cost of forces used beyond India's own defence to the British Exchequer, an arrangement whose long-term monetary consequences are examined in Section 83.17. But the underlying principle was unchanged. The Government of India could deploy Indian resources, and the Governor-General could mobilize them, without securing the agreement of India's elected leaders.

The Viceroy followed the declaration with emergency legislation. An amendment to the 1935 Act, passed in London in the first days of the war, enabled the central government to give directions to provincial governments on matters that would otherwise have lain within the provincial sphere, and a Defence of India Ordinance, later enacted as the Defence of India Act, empowered the authorities to make rules on public order, censorship, detention, and economic control. The federal scheme was formally suspended for the duration. Linlithgow, a conservative administrator who had chaired the parliamentary committee that examined the 1935 reforms, believed that Indian cooperation would follow from the logic of the situation. He consulted Gandhi, Jinnah, Rajendra Prasad, Subhas Chandra Bose, and other leaders in the weeks after the declaration. These meetings were consultations, not negotiations; none altered the fact that the central decision had already been taken.

Historians therefore often describe the events of September 1939 as the opening of a legitimacy problem rather than a strategic one. Britain's stated war aim was the defence of liberty against aggression, and Indian parties could hardly be expected to ignore the discrepancy between that aim and the manner in which India itself had been brought into the war. The sources for this moment, including the Viceroy's statements, press reports, and the correspondence collected in Gwyer and Appadorai's standard compilation, show both the speed of the colonial decision and the immediacy of Indian questions about what the war was for. Those questions, taken up first by the Congress Working Committee, determined the politics of the next two and a half years.

Section 83.2

Congress Demands, Failed Negotiations, and the Resignation of Provincial Ministries

The Congress Working Committee met at Wardha in the second week of September 1939 to consider its response. Its statement of 14 September condemned Nazi and fascist aggression in unequivocal terms and expressed sympathy for its victims, while making clear that India could not be a party to a war said to be fought for democratic freedom when freedom was denied to India itself. The Committee asked the British Government to declare its war aims, and in particular to state whether those aims would apply to India and whether the principles of democracy and self-determination would be applied in practice to the subcontinent. The text was largely shaped by Nehru, whose anti-fascism and anti-imperialism were equally strongly held, and it was worded to keep two possibilities open: cooperation in the war on terms of Indian freedom, or non-cooperation if those terms were refused.

Within Congress there was no single view. Gandhi, who met the Viceroy at the outset, was personally sympathetic to Britain and France and unwilling to embarrass them at a moment of extreme danger. He held to nonviolence as a creed and could not endorse a war effort, but he was reluctant to launch mass agitation. C. Rajagopalachari, who had headed the Madras ministry, favoured an arrangement that would trade cooperation for a real transfer of responsibility. Nehru wanted a clear declaration of independence as the price of support. Subhas Chandra Bose, who had left the Congress presidency earlier in 1939 and formed the Forward Bloc, argued that the war presented a moment to press Britain to the limit. The Working Committee's position was a compromise among these tendencies, and Chapter 82 has already shown how divided Congress had become after the Tripuri session of 1939.

The British reply came in a Viceregal statement on 17 October 1939. Linlithgow explained that Britain's objective was the defence of freedom against aggression, restated that the 1935 Act's goal was Dominion status, and promised that after the war the Government would be willing to consult representatives of several communities, parties, and the princes about possible modifications of the Act. For the present he proposed a consultative group representing the main political parties and the states. This was far short of what Congress had asked. It declined to define war aims in a way that applied to India, and it deferred any constitutional change until the war had ended. Nehru's reaction was that the statement reasserted the old imperial position; Gandhi, though restrained, did not regard it as a satisfactory basis for cooperation.

On 22 October the Working Committee rejected the Viceroy's statement and called on the Congress ministries to resign. The ministries were by then operating in eight provinces: Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, the North-West Frontier Province, and Assam, where Congress led a coalition. Their resignations followed in the last days of October and in November 1939. The decision was not casual. It meant surrendering provincial administrations that had controlled police, revenue, education, local government, and a vast patronage network for nearly three years. It was also consistent with Congress's earlier commitment, repeated in resolutions before 1939, that the party would not support an imperialist war. The ministers resigned as a public protest rather than because they had been defeated in their legislatures, and they emphasized that the withdrawal was a refusal of the colonial terms of participation, not an act of hostility to the Allied cause.

The administrative consequences were immediate. In seven of those provinces—Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces and Berar, Bihar, Orissa, and the North-West Frontier Province—the governors invoked Section 93 of the 1935 Act and ruled with advisers drawn largely from the Indian Civil Service. Assam instead acquired an alternative non-Congress ministry, allowing responsible provincial government to continue there. Legislatures were suspended rather than dissolved. Many of the reforms the Congress governments had begun, such as tenancy legislation, prison releases, and educational and public-health initiatives, were interrupted. Provincial officials who had worked under Congress ministers now answered once more to British governors, and the wartime regulations discussed in Section 83.19 could be enforced by a bureaucracy no longer restrained by Indian ministers.

Historians disagree about whether the resignations were wise. Critics, including some contemporary Congress figures, argued that the party handed power in the provinces back to the colonial authorities, abandoned an arena in which it had built experience of governing, and left the field to rivals who were willing to cooperate. Defenders argued that remaining in office while the Viceroy denied Indian consent would have compromised the party's anti-imperial credentials and exposed ministers to implementing wartime measures they could not control. Both positions have force. What is clear is that the resignations altered the political environment and prepared the ground for the League's initiatives and for the divergent responses of other parties, the subject of the next two sections.

Historical Vocabulary
Section 93 and Provincial Autonomy

Section 93 of the Government of India Act of 1935 allowed a provincial governor, when constitutional government could not be carried on, to assume powers normally exercised by ministers and legislature, with the approval of the Governor-General. After the eight Congress ministries resigned in 1939, governors invoked it in seven provinces; Assam formed an alternative non-Congress ministry. Provincial autonomy meant that elected ministers controlled most provincial subjects from 1937, though governors retained special responsibilities.

The terms matter because they show the limits of the 1935 scheme: ministers governed by the constitution's permission and could be replaced by direct bureaucratic rule when they withdrew. The modern phrase "president's rule" in independent India derives from this colonial precedent in its structure, though the legal context is different.

Section 83.3

Provincial Governments, Princely States, and Divergent Responses to War

When the Congress ministries withdrew, India did not present a united front of either support or opposition. The political landscape fragmented, and the options facing parties and rulers were more varied than a simple choice between backing Britain and resisting it. The provinces themselves illustrated the variety. In seven provinces governors now ruled directly under Section 93. In Punjab, Bengal, and Sindh, non-Congress ministries continued; in Assam, a different ministry took office after the Congress-led government resigned. The four provinces thus retained ministerial government, though their political bases and relationships with colonial officials varied. These ministries differed from one another in composition and aim, and the point deserves emphasis because it shows that provincial politics in 1939 was not a binary struggle between Congress and the League.

The Punjab was governed by the Unionist Party under Sir Sikandar Hayat Khan, a coalition of Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh landed and rural interests whose political strength rested on a defence of agrarian society and on the province's close link to the army. The province supplied a disproportionate share of Indian Army recruits, and its leaders had strong incentives to support the war effort, which protected recruitment, pensions, and rural prosperity. In Bengal, the ministry led by A. K. Fazlul Huq depended on a coalition in which the Krishak Praja Party and the Muslim League shared power, while Sindh's ministry fluctuated among local groupings. These provincial executives could cooperate with the Viceroy without conceding anything on India's constitutional future, and in several cases they used wartime opportunities to extend their administrative reach.

The princely states formed another large and differently placed group. Some five hundred and sixty rulers governed roughly a quarter of the subcontinent's population and about two-fifths of its area, under treaties and understandings of paramountcy that Chapter 71 described. Their reaction to the war was generally one of loyalty, expressed in offers of troops, money, aircraft, and facilities. The State Forces expanded, and princes such as the Nizam of Hyderabad and the rulers of Mysore, Gwalior, and many smaller states made contributions. At the same time the war highlighted the question of responsible government in the states. The All-India States' People's Conference, with Nehru as president, pressed for representative institutions, and the suspension of the federal scheme removed the framework within which rulers might otherwise have negotiated a place in an Indian polity.

Non-Congress political organizations took positions that reflected their own social bases. The Hindu Mahasabha, led by V. D. Savarkar, supported cooperation with the war effort in order to expand military training and recruitment among Hindus, arguing that the militarization of Hindu society would strengthen its bargaining power. It opposed the Congress resignations and criticized any constitutional concession to the League. Among the Sikhs, the Akali Dal and other organizations were divided; leaders such as Master Tara Singh were concerned with the position of the community in the Punjab and with securing representation in the army and in any constitutional settlement, and some Sikh politicians saw cooperation as a means of bargaining. For the Sikhs the army was both an economic mainstay and a symbol of status, and the war therefore opened questions about recruitment and community weight that continued through the decade.

B. R. Ambedkar, whose political activity in this period is treated more fully in Chapter 80, led the Independent Labour Party and spoke for Scheduled Castes who had long been excluded from many forms of public employment, including the army. He supported the opening of military service to groups previously marked as non-martial and criticized Congress for treating the Poona Pact settlement of 1932 as a final answer to Dalit political claims. The formation of the Mahar Regiment in 1941 is one example of the way wartime recruitment widened access, although it did not dismantle prejudice. Periyar and the non-Brahmin movement in the south, meanwhile, criticized Congress politics as the instrument of upper-caste interests, a view that influenced attitudes to the resignations.

Left parties added further variety. The Congress Socialist Party, the Communist Party of India, and the Forward Bloc all denounced the war as an imperialist contest, though for different reasons and with different strategies, and the Communists would reverse their position in 1941 as Section 83.10 explains. To these must be added the distinctions between all-India and provincial interests: a political organization with a national platform could judge the war as a constitutional question, while a provincial ministry usually judged it by what the war meant for recruitment, revenue, and local patronage. Viewed together, these responses show a society negotiating its own interests in the shadow of a decision made elsewhere. Indian opinion cannot be reduced to enthusiasm for the Allied cause or to nationalist refusal. It comprised cooperation for bargaining, opposition on principle, loyalty from social interest, and ambivalence, often held by the same individuals at different moments.

Section 83.4

The Muslim League, Jinnah, and the Political Opportunity of Wartime Realignment

Muhammad Ali Jinnah stood at the centre of a changed political configuration after the Congress ministries resigned. The Muslim League had performed modestly in the provincial elections of 1937, winning only a minority of the seats reserved for Muslims, and Chapter 82 described the reorganization Jinnah then undertook to widen the League's base. In 1939 the League was still far from being the acknowledged spokesman of Muslim opinion. Powerful regional parties such as the Punjab Unionists and the Krishak Praja Party operated outside its discipline, and many religious and political organizations of Muslims, notably the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind and the Majlis-i-Ahrar, favoured cooperation with Congress and the idea of a composite Indian nationalism. The League's position in 1939 was one of increasing but incomplete influence.

Jinnah's first response to the outbreak of war was to bargain. The League's Working Committee in September 1939 stated that Muslim support for the war effort would depend on assurances of justice to Muslims and of consultation with the League before any constitutional change was made. Jinnah held meetings with the Viceroy and insisted that the Congress had no right to speak for all communities. He pressed the Government to recognize the League as the authoritative organization of Muslims. The Viceroy had no intention of conceding that point formally, but he valued a counterweight to Congress, and Jinnah's willingness to negotiate gave him leverage that the League had not previously enjoyed.

The resignations of the Congress ministries gave Jinnah an opening. On 2 December 1939 he appealed to Muslims and to others who had suffered under what he described as Congress misrule to observe 22 December as a Day of Deliverance and thanksgiving. The grounds were a series of allegations about the treatment of minorities in Congress-governed provinces, assembled in reports commissioned by Muslim organizations, such as the Pirpur and Sharif reports and a pamphlet by Fazlul Huq. Historians treat these documents with care. They reflected real grievances in some provinces, including disputes over the Vande Mataram song, the Wardha education scheme, and local administrative decisions, but they were also partisan compilations whose claims were contested by Congress and by independent observers. The Day of Deliverance was important less because its allegations were uniformly verified than because it demonstrated the League's capacity to mobilize and publicize a collective grievance.

Observance of the day was not confined to the League. Ambedkar's organization and Periyar's followers joined in, marking their own criticisms of Congress, and the event brought together groups that disagreed on much else. Congress leaders responded that the League was manufacturing division and diverting attention from the struggle against British rule. This exchange was a pattern that would recur. Congress claimed to represent all Indians and often resisted the idea that religious community was the basic political unit. The League claimed that a party presenting itself as national but predominantly Hindu in leadership and symbolism could not represent Muslims. Neither claim was wholly accurate, but each was politically effective.

Historians dispute how far the Congress withdrawal created the League's opportunity and how far the League would have grown in any case. Some, including Ayesha Jalal in her influential account of Jinnah's strategy, emphasize the extent to which the Viceroy's need for allies and Jinnah's tactical skill elevated the League's status at a time when its provincial base was weak. Others stress the longer social and ideological processes through which many Muslims came to identify politically with the League during the 1940s. The point to preserve here is a limited one. Congress's withdrawal from provincial office altered the political environment and gave the League both an argument and a platform. It did not mean that Jinnah by the end of 1939 held universal Muslim support; that support was built in subsequent years through by-elections, organization, and the pressures of wartime politics.

The League's developing constitutional ideas, which moved decisively after December 1939, are treated in Division II. Before turning to them, it is useful to step back from the immediate controversies and consider how a single development, the war's outbreak, appeared differently to those who experienced it from different positions. The following feature sets out several of those perspectives.

Section 83.5

From Minority Safeguards to Muslim Nationhood: Debates within the Muslim League

The Lahore Resolution of March 1940 did not appear out of nothing. It crystallized ideas that had circulated in Muslim political and intellectual life for a decade or more, and its sources were plural. Sir Muhammad Iqbal's presidential address to the League at Allahabad in 1930 had envisaged a consolidated Muslim state within or alongside an Indian federation in the north-west. In 1933 a group of Cambridge students led by Choudhry Rahmat Ali circulated a pamphlet using the name "Pakistan" for a proposed north-western homeland, though their scheme attracted little contemporary support from established League leaders. During the later 1930s academics at Aligarh, including Zafar ul-Hasan and Afzal Husain Qadri, produced schemes of regional division, and Jinnah himself, who in the 1920s had defended joint electorates and constitutional safeguards, moved steadily toward the language of Muslim nationhood.

The League's earlier constitutional demands were minority demands. Jinnah's Fourteen Points of 1929 asked for federal structures with residual powers in the provinces, reserved representation for Muslims, and protection of religious and educational rights. These claims assumed that Muslims were a minority within a single Indian constitutional order. What changed in 1939 and 1940 was the argument that Muslims constituted a nation, and that the relevant constitutional unit was therefore not a protected minority but a collectivity entitled to self-determination in the areas where it was a majority. That argument did not command unanimous agreement even within the League. It required a reconciliation between the League's all-India leadership, whose base lay in Muslim minority provinces such as the United Provinces and Bombay, and the Muslim-majority provinces whose politicians stood to gain or lose most from any territorial scheme.

Provincial leaders therefore mattered enormously. Sir Sikandar Hayat Khan, the Unionist premier of the Punjab, favoured a loose confederal structure and drafted a scheme in which regional zones would have wide autonomy and the centre only a limited role. His position was shaped by the interests of a mixed Punjab electorate and a commitment to provincial self-rule, and he later insisted that what the League adopted at Lahore was not a plan for partitioning the Punjab and creating a sovereign Muslim state. A. K. Fazlul Huq, the Bengal premier, had risen as a champion of the Bengali peasantry against zamindars and moneylenders, and he brought to the League an agrarian and Bengali politics that did not fit comfortably with its north Indian leadership. Both men moved in and out of Jinnah's orbit, and each would later clash with him over the League's discipline.

Muslim positions beyond the League were varied and sometimes strongly opposed to partition. The Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, under Husain Ahmad Madani, argued for a composite nationalism in which Muslims and non-Muslims could jointly pursue independence. Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, the Congress president from 1940, was the most prominent Muslim leader within Congress and argued that the Muslim stake in India lay in its shared political life. Allah Bakhsh Soomro of Sindh convened the Azad Muslim Conference, which claimed to represent Muslims opposed to the League's programme. Further, the Khaksar movement, led by Allama Mashriqi, had its own militant approach, and its confrontation with police in Lahore on 19 March 1940, days before the League session opened, left casualties and cast a shadow over the proceedings. These differences matter because they prevent retrospective narratives from treating the Muslim public of 1940 as a single political actor.

Jinnah's own position evolved by stages. By early 1940 he had told journalists and colleagues that Hindus and Muslims were not merely communities but distinct civilizations whose coexistence in a single state would produce either tyranny or conflict. The League's Working Committee appointed a subcommittee in February 1940 to examine constitutional schemes. Yet Jinnah did not issue a detailed blueprint. He preferred a general principle that could be adapted, a method that preserved flexibility and kept Muslim-majority provincial leaders on board. The ambiguity was deliberate, and it left room for varied interpretations, as the next section shows.

Section 83.6

The Lahore Resolution of March 1940: Sovereignty, Federation, and Pakistan

The twenty-seventh session of the All-India Muslim League met at Lahore from 22 to 24 March 1940, in Minto Park, a large public ground in the city. Attendance was substantial, with delegates and visitors from across the subcontinent. The session began under the shadow of the Khaksar shooting of 19 March, and Jinnah visited the injured before the proceedings. His presidential address on 22 March, delivered largely in English, ranged over the history of Hindu-Muslim relations, rejected the idea that Muslims were a minority in the ordinary sense, and argued that Hindus and Muslims belonged to two different religious philosophies, social customs, and literatures, and so constituted two nations. Whatever one thinks of that claim as history, it was an argument for treating the League's goal as a political settlement between nations rather than a bargain over safeguards.

The resolution itself was moved on 23 March by A. K. Fazlul Huq and seconded by Choudhry Khaliquzzaman, with support from delegates from Sindh, Baluchistan, the North-West Frontier, and the Punjab, and it was adopted on 24 March. Its drafting history is itself revealing. Elements of Sikandar Hayat Khan's earlier scheme were reworked by the League's subjects committee, which also removed or altered formulations that might have implied a fixed set of institutions. The text declared that no constitutional plan would be workable or acceptable to Muslims unless it followed the principle that geographically contiguous units be demarcated into regions, with such territorial readjustments as might be necessary, and that the areas in which Muslims were numerically in a majority, as in the north-western and eastern zones of India, should be grouped to constitute "Independent States" in which the constituent units would be "autonomous and sovereign." It added that adequate safeguards should be provided for minorities in these units and regions, and for Muslim minorities elsewhere.

The wording is ambiguous in ways that historians have long noted. The resolution says "States" in the plural, and it speaks of zones and of constituent units rather than of one country. It does not name the zones' boundaries, it does not mention the partition of the Punjab or Bengal, and it does not use the word "Pakistan," which was attached to the resolution afterward, largely by the Hindu press, and then adopted by supporters. It refers to "territorial readjustments" without defining them. It leaves unclear whether the constituent units were provinces, groups of provinces, or newly delimited territories, and whether the eastern and north-western zones would form a single state or two. It does not state what relationship, if any, the independent states would have with each other or with a residual India. Reading the 1947 Pakistan, with its specific borders and its two wings, back into this text would be a serious anachronism.

In the months that followed, the League's leaders and press treated the resolution as the basis of a demand for Pakistan. At its Madras session in April 1941 the League incorporated the objective of the Lahore Resolution into its constitution. Jinnah's speeches increasingly presented the demand as non-negotiable in principle while leaving details for later settlement. The Delhi convention of League legislators in April 1946, covered in Chapter 85, would amend the language to refer to a single sovereign state, a development that illustrates how much interpretive work the 1940 text had left to the future. Many contemporaries also read the resolution differently from one another: Sikandar Hayat Khan emphasized provincial autonomy within a loose union, Bengal's leaders emphasized the eastern zone's separate identity, and the League's all-India leadership tended to emphasize sovereignty.

The social base of support for the resolution varied by region. In the Muslim-minority provinces, where the new state would not include many Muslims, the appeal rested on a sense of political security and a claim to represent Muslim identity as such. In the north-west and the east, the issue was more directly one of power: whether Muslim majorities would govern their provinces or share power under a Hindu-majority centre. Peasant grievances against landlords, economic competition between Hindu and Muslim professional classes, and the experience of the Congress ministries all played their parts, but none of these factors by itself explains the appeal of the resolution. Its attraction lay partly in its capacity to mean different things to different audiences.

The original text, with its qualifying phrases and unresolved terms, should therefore be read as a document of political positioning, not as a map of partition. It shows a movement toward the idea of separate sovereignty without settling who would hold sovereignty, over what territory, and by what procedures. That ambiguity is the reason the document remains a subject of historical argument, as the debate feature below explains.

Historical Debate

What Did the Lahore Resolution Mean in 1940?

The question: Was the Lahore Resolution a blueprint for a sovereign Muslim state, a bargaining position to secure strong safeguards within a united India, or a deliberately open formula that could serve both purposes?

Interpretation A: Historians such as Ayesha Jalal, in The Sole Spokesman (1985), have argued that Jinnah used the demand for Pakistan as a bargaining counter to secure parity with Congress at the centre and that the ambiguity of the 1940 text reflected a political strategy rather than a fixed goal. On this view, the plural "States," the lack of boundaries, and Jinnah's later flexibility show that the settlement of 1947 was not the aim of 1940.
Interpretation B: Other historians, including Venkat Dhulipala in Creating a New Medina (2015), have argued that the idea of Pakistan as a sovereign state was widely and seriously debated in the early 1940s, that many Muslims understood it as a real constitutional goal, and that the League's popular mobilization cannot be reduced to a bargaining tactic.

Evidence: Both rely on the text of the resolution, Jinnah's speeches, League proceedings, provincial politics, press debate, and the later record of negotiations. They weigh differently the significance of ambiguity, of Jinnah's private strategy, and of grassroots enthusiasm.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians understand the origins of Partition, the contingency of events between 1940 and 1947, and the responsibility of different actors. It also bears on contemporary political memory, in which the Lahore Resolution is variously presented as the founding document of Pakistan or as a moment in a longer negotiation.

Section 83.7

Responses to Lahore: Congress, Muslim Critics, Sikhs, and Alternative Constitutional Visions

The Lahore Resolution drew immediate and varied responses. Congress leaders, including Gandhi, regarded the proposal with dismay. Gandhi wrote that the division of India into separate states would be a vivisection and argued that the idea ran counter to the unity of Indian life. At the same time Congress leaders were cautious in public about the League's weakness in some provinces and unsure how to respond to the claim of two nations. Maulana Azad, who had presided over the Congress session at Ramgarh a few days earlier, had given a presidential address that rejected the premise of an inevitable division of the Muslim and Hindu populations and appealed to shared historical experience and Muslim participation in Indian life. Congress's position in 1940 combined opposition to the principle of partition with reluctance to address the grievances through which the League had gained support.

Among Muslims outside the League, the reaction was sharper. The Azad Muslim Conference met in Delhi in April 1940 with participation from the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, the Majlis-i-Ahrar, the Momin Conference, and others, and it declared its commitment to a free, united India with protections for religious and cultural rights. Its supporters came from artisans, weavers, small farmers, and religious scholars in many regions. The Conference did not have the League's funding or its access to the Viceroy, but its existence documents that Muslim opinion remained divided. The League's later electoral successes in the 1945–46 elections, covered in Chapter 85, should not be read back into 1940 as though the debate had already been decided.

Sikh responses combined alarm and bargaining. The Punjab's Sikhs, a minority of roughly one-eighth of the province's population but concentrated geographically and heavily represented in the army, feared that any arrangement placing the entire province in a Muslim-majority state would subordinate them. The Akali Dal and other Sikh organizations denounced the resolution, and Sikh leaders began to articulate counter-demands, including ideas of an autonomous or "Azad" Punjab. The Sikh position was thus not a simple endorsement of Congress's all-India unity, but a defence of community interests in a province whose future had become contested. The problem of the Punjab would become central in 1946 and 1947, and Chapter 86 considers the partition of that province in detail.

Hindu nationalist responses were categorical. Savarkar and the Hindu Mahasabha rejected the division of India and affirmed a claim to an undivided territory, linking the nation to the geography of the subcontinent. Their reaction fed the League's own arguments that a Hindu-dominated majority would resist even moderate Muslim claims. B. R. Ambedkar treated the Pakistan idea with seriousness and analytical detachment. His book Thoughts on Pakistan, published in December 1940, examined the demand, argued that the Muslim claim could not be dismissed as a mere bargaining position, and discussed the possibilities of provincial boundary revision and population exchange. Ambedkar's concerns, which centred on the position of Scheduled Castes within any new constitutional order, show that discussion of partition involved communities other than Hindus and Muslims.

Other constitutional visions persisted. Federalists continued to propose arrangements in which the provinces or regional zones would hold extensive powers within a loose union. Sikandar Hayat Khan's scheme was one such proposal, and some Congress leaders were willing, in principle, to accept wide provincial autonomy. Princes had their own interests in maintaining their treaty status. Socialists and Communists, although divided on the war, viewed the communal question as largely a distraction from class struggle, while Muslim peasant movements in Bengal and the Punjab were more concerned with debt and tenancy than with constitutional design. The Lahore Resolution thus entered a political field already crowded with proposals, and it did not displace them.

This variety explains why partition was not inevitable in 1940. The resolution articulated a demand that a growing number of Muslims, especially in the League, came to support, but its territorial implications were undefined, many Muslim leaders opposed it or interpreted it loosely, and the British Government had not yet committed to any particular scheme. Partition emerged from a sequence of choices, negotiations, failures, and violence over the following seven years, and the wartime constitutional deadlock traced in this chapter was an important part of that sequence. To treat 1940 as the moment when the outcome was settled would be to read the end of the story into its beginning.

Section 83.8

The August Offer of 1940: British Constitutional Promises and Indian Responses

The spring and summer of 1940 transformed Britain's strategic position. Germany overran Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France between April and June; the British Expeditionary Force was evacuated from Dunkirk, an evacuation in which Indian mule companies also took part; Italy entered the war on Germany's side; and Winston Churchill replaced Neville Chamberlain as Prime Minister on 10 May. Britain now faced the possibility of invasion and depended on its imperial resources more heavily than before. In India, the Secretary of State, Leopold Amery, and the Viceroy discussed ways to secure wider Indian cooperation without conceding the central issue of sovereignty. Churchill's own convictions about the empire made major concessions unlikely, but the war cabinet recognized that some declaration was necessary.

Congress itself made an unusual move that summer. In July 1940 the Congress Working Committee, meeting in Delhi and then Poona, passed a resolution offering cooperation in the war effort if Britain declared India's independence and allowed the formation of a provisional national government at the centre. The offer, associated with Rajagopalachari and Azad and tolerated reluctantly by Gandhi, represented a willingness to join the war on a nationalist basis. The British government did not accept it. Gandhi, who could not subscribe to a war effort in principle, regarded the move as a deviation from the party's nonviolent character, and in September the All-India Congress Committee at Bombay endorsed his leadership and a more restricted position, returning to the limited civil resistance discussed in Section 83.9.

On 8 August 1940 the Viceroy issued the declaration known as the August Offer. It restated that Dominion status was the objective of British policy. It proposed to enlarge the Viceroy's Executive Council by adding representative Indians and to establish a War Advisory Council that would include representatives of the princely states. It promised that, after the war, a representative Indian body would be set up to frame a new constitution, and that the Government would give full weight to the opinions of minorities. A crucial qualification said that the British Government could not contemplate transferring its responsibilities for India's peace and welfare to any system of government whose authority was denied by large and powerful elements in India's national life, nor could it coerce such elements into submission. Defence would remain under British control for the time being.

Congress rejected the August Offer. Its leaders objected that it did not promise independence, that it deferred all substantive change until after the war, and that its language about minorities gave any significant group, in effect, a veto over constitutional advance. Nehru and Azad both criticized the proposals as restating the old imperial position in new language. For Congress the central problem was not that minorities should be protected, which it accepted in principle, but that the formula allowed a party that claimed to speak for a minority to prevent change by withholding agreement. Gandhi's own attitude was more concerned with the question of the war than with the constitutional text, and his decision to launch individual satyagraha followed from the offer's rejection.

The League received the offer differently. Jinnah and the Working Committee, meeting in Bombay at the end of August and beginning of September, welcomed the assurance that no future constitution would be adopted without the consent of the minorities, and they took it as a recognition of the League's claim. At the same time they withheld full acceptance. They sought a categorical assurance that no constitutional arrangement would be framed or imposed without the League's approval and that the principle of the Lahore Resolution would be respected. The offer therefore gave the League an advantage in the negotiation without requiring it to join the Executive Council. The Viceroy's policy gave the League a form of constitutional recognition that increased its leverage, though historians continue to debate whether Linlithgow intended this result or simply sought to keep both major parties from abandoning the war effort.

The August Offer shows how closely constitutional politics and military circumstances were linked. Britain was fighting alone against Germany and Italy, India supplied troops and materials, and the Viceroy sought cooperation without surrendering the power to decide on the war's conduct. The enlarged Executive Council did come into being in July 1941, with a majority of Indian members for the first time, but its members were appointed by the Viceroy and were not responsible to any legislature or party. This distinction mattered to Congress. The council offered Indian participation in administration, not Indian authority over the state. The difference between those two things would remain at the centre of the negotiations until the Cripps Mission and beyond.

Historical Vocabulary
Dominion Status

Dominion status referred to the position of self-governing members of the British Commonwealth, such as Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa, which were internally and externally autonomous but united with Britain by allegiance to the Crown. British statements from the 1929 Irwin declaration onward described Dominion status as the goal of Indian constitutional development, without specifying when.

In the wartime negotiations, the term carried contested meanings. For Britain it implied a postwar settlement with safeguards for minorities, princes, and defence. For Congress, which had declared purna swaraj (complete independence) as its goal in 1929, it implied delay and continued imperial links. For the League and others it could be consistent with a divided or federal structure. Understanding the term prevents the mistake of reading it as simply "independence."

Section 83.9

Individual Satyagraha, Antiwar Protest, and the Limits of Congress Cooperation

After the August Offer Gandhi had to decide how Congress should express its opposition to the war without paralyzing a country facing a global emergency. He rejected mass civil disobedience as likely to embarrass Britain at a moment of extreme peril and to produce disorder he could not control, and he did not wish to obstruct a struggle against Nazism in a way that served Hitler's purposes. Yet he also held that Congress could not remain silent about a war into which India had been taken without consent. The compromise he devised, individual satyagraha, was a limited campaign in which carefully selected individuals would publicly voice opposition to the war effort and court arrest. Its object was less to defeat the government than to assert the right of Indians to speak freely against participation, a claim of political principle.

The campaign opened on 17 October 1940 at Paunar, near Wardha, where Vinoba Bhave, a close associate of Gandhi known for his work on constructive programmes, was named the first satyagrahi. He delivered a speech urging that it was wrong to help the British war effort with men or money, and he was arrested a few days later and sentenced to imprisonment. Jawaharlal Nehru was named second, and his arrest on 31 October 1940 led to a sentence of four years, a severe punishment that revealed the government's determination. Brahma Datt followed, and the list widened in later phases to include Congress legislators and former ministers, and eventually, from early 1941, a larger number of ordinary members. By many estimates, individuals imprisoned in connection with the movement numbered on the order of twenty thousand or more, though counts vary and depend on how one defines participation.

The campaign was disciplined and symbolic. Participants announced their intention in advance, so that the authorities could arrest them. They were forbidden to use violence or to interfere with recruitment or war production by force, and the slogan they used was an anti-war statement rather than a call for rebellion. The government responded with the Defence of India Rules, which allowed prosecution for speeches deemed prejudicial to the war effort, and with press censorship that restricted reports of the campaign. Newspapers faced restrictions, and some, including Gandhi's own journals, suffered interruptions. The campaign therefore tested not Britain's military capacity but its willingness to tolerate peaceful dissent while claiming to defend freedom.

Within Congress the campaign produced criticism from several sides. Rajagopalachari argued that Congress should cooperate with the war on suitable terms and that sacrificing leaders to imprisonment for symbolic protest was counterproductive; he moved increasingly away from the party leadership and resigned from Congress in 1942. Socialists and younger activists complained that the campaign was too gentle for the occasion. Subhas Chandra Bose, who had been imprisoned in 1940, escaped from India in January 1941, a journey that belongs to Chapter 84. Nehru, though he supported the principle of resistance, was uneasy about timing and the possibility of Japanese expansion, and he accepted Gandhi's approach with reservations. The campaign shows how Congress's unity relied heavily on Gandhi's authority and how much that authority depended on the party's ability to avoid decisive choices.

The balance of the campaign changed as the war itself changed. In December 1941 the government released many of the imprisoned satyagrahis, including Nehru and Azad, in the days surrounding the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor and Malaya. At the end of that month the Congress Working Committee, meeting at Bardoli, adopted a resolution indicating that the party would be prepared to cooperate in defending India if Britain declared its independence, and it relieved Gandhi of responsibility for the party's war policy so that the party could adopt a more flexible stance. Gandhi's leadership was restored in early 1942, but the episode revealed the division between his pacifism and the pressures of a looming invasion.

Individual satyagraha should be distinguished from the Quit India movement of August 1942. It was limited in scope, controlled by Gandhi through selection of participants, and aimed at demonstrating the right to protest, not at paralyzing the administration or ending British rule. Quit India, discussed in Chapter 84, involved a nationwide mass movement, the arrest of the entire Congress leadership, popular rebellion, and severe repression. The earlier campaign nevertheless contributed to that later outcome by keeping Congress in a posture of principled opposition, by filling jails with experienced organizers, and by showing the government that constitutional offers short of real power would not secure the party's cooperation.

Section 83.10

The War Becomes Global: The Soviet Union, Japan, and the Crisis in Asia

Between 1940 and early 1942 the war ceased to be a mainly European conflict and became a worldwide one, and the strategic position of India changed with it. In the spring and early summer of 1940 Germany's victories in the west left Britain isolated. In June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union, an event that transformed the diplomacy of the war and, as it happened, the policy of Indian communists. The Communist Party of India, banned since 1934 and operating underground or from jail, had previously treated the war as an imperialist struggle between rival capitalist powers. After Germany attacked the Soviet Union, the party's leadership came to argue that the war had become a people's war against fascism and that cooperation with the Allied effort was required. The change isolated the party from Congress, which was then engaged in civil resistance, and eventually led to the lifting of the ban on the party in July 1942, a development that Chapter 84 places in context.

The Atlantic Charter of August 1941, a joint declaration by Churchill and Roosevelt, spoke of the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they would live. Indian leaders immediately asked whether it applied to India. In September 1941 Churchill told the House of Commons that the Charter's principles referred to the restoration of sovereignty to nations under Nazi occupation and did not apply to India and Burma, which were governed by separate constitutional arrangements. The statement was widely noted in India and gave nationalists grounds for arguing that the democratic language of the Allies did not extend to the colonial world. It also reinforced the American public's awareness of the Indian question, which would grow as the United States joined the war.

Japan's entry transformed the situation. On 7 and 8 December 1941, Japanese forces attacked Pearl Harbor, landed in northern Malaya at Kota Bharu, and bombed or invaded other points in Southeast Asia. Within days the Royal Navy lost the battleships Prince of Wales and Repulse off the Malayan coast. The Malayan campaign, in which Indian divisions formed a large part of the defending force, ended in the surrender of Singapore on 15 February 1942, with tens of thousands of Indian troops among the captured. Hong Kong had fallen at Christmas 1941. Rangoon fell on 8 March 1942, and Japanese forces pressed north through Burma toward the Indian frontier, while the Andaman Islands were occupied in March. In a matter of weeks the British position in Asia collapsed, and the belief that the Raj was invincible was weakened for Indian civilians and soldiers alike.

The effects on Indian opinion and administration were substantial. For the first time since the eighteenth century the subcontinent faced a credible prospect of invasion from the east. Civilians in Madras and Calcutta feared air raids, and in April 1942 Japanese carrier aircraft attacked Colombo and Trincomalee in Ceylon and struck coastal targets on India's east coast, including Visakhapatnam and Kakinada. Refugees, many of them Indians who had lived and worked in Burma or Malaya, began to cross into Assam and Bengal, and their accounts of the retreat, including racial discrimination in evacuation arrangements, circulated widely. The government considered measures for the defence of eastern India, some of which, such as the removal of boats and stocks of rice, had consequences that Chapter 84 examines in connection with the Bengal famine.

Allied pressure on Britain also rose. In February 1942 Chiang Kai-shek visited India, met Gandhi and Nehru, and urged London to concede self-government in order to secure Indian support against Japan. China had a strategic interest in India as the base for supplies after the closure of the Burma Road. President Roosevelt, who regarded colonial empires with suspicion and wished to see Indian resources mobilized effectively, made known to Churchill that he favoured a settlement. Within Britain, the Labour members of the coalition, notably Clement Attlee and Stafford Cripps, argued that a constitutional initiative was needed. Churchill remained sceptical but yielded to the combined pressure, and the decision to send a mission, discussed in the next section, was the result.

Ceylon deserves special mention here. Its harbour at Trincomalee and its position astride the sea lanes of the Indian Ocean made it a key naval base, and Japanese attacks on Colombo and Trincomalee in April 1942 demonstrated its vulnerability. British naval forces relocated some of their strength to safer anchorages on the East African coast and at the Maldives' Addu Atoll. Ceylon is treated in more detail in Section 83.16, and its later wartime role as the site of the Allied South East Asia Command is part of Chapter 84's account of the eastern front. What matters here is that the strategic geography of the war now placed South Asia in the front line, not at its rear.

Section 83.11

The Cripps Mission, March–April 1942: Proposals for Postwar Self-Government

The decision to send Sir Stafford Cripps to India was taken by the War Cabinet in early March 1942, after Singapore's fall and as Rangoon was about to be lost. Cripps, who held the posts of Lord Privy Seal and Leader of the House of Commons, had recently returned from serving as British ambassador in Moscow. A left-wing barrister, he had long been sympathetic to Indian self-government, had known Nehru personally since the 1930s, and was thought to be acceptable to Congress. A Cabinet committee, including Attlee, had prepared a Draft Declaration between late February and 9 March, and Churchill, who was reluctant but constrained by Allied and domestic pressure, agreed that the proposals would be taken to India for discussion. The Draft Declaration was published on 30 March 1942, as a British command paper (Cmd. 6350), after Cripps arrived in Delhi on 22 March.

The Draft Declaration contained two sets of provisions. The first concerned the postwar settlement. After the end of hostilities, a constitution-making body would be convened to frame a new constitution for an Indian Union, which would be a Dominion associated with the United Kingdom and other Dominions through common allegiance to the Crown but equal in every respect, with the freedom to leave the Commonwealth. The body would be elected by the lower houses of the provincial legislatures, by proportional representation, with the princely states invited to appoint representatives in proportion to their population. The British Government undertook to accept and implement the constitution, subject to two conditions: that any province unwilling to accept the new constitution could retain its existing constitutional position, with the possibility of acceding later, and that a treaty would be negotiated between the Government and the constitution-making body to cover matters arising from the transfer of power, including the protection of racial and religious minorities.

The second part concerned the war. The Declaration said that during the critical period His Majesty's Government must retain control and direction of the defence of India as part of its world war effort, while inviting the leaders of the principal sections of the Indian people to participate in the counsels of their country, the Commonwealth, and the United Nations. The language was general. It did not specify how the Viceroy's Executive Council would be reconstituted, what portfolios Indians would hold, or whether the Executive would function as a cabinet. In oral discussions Cripps went further, suggesting that the Executive might be reconstituted as a kind of national government, with the Viceroy acting as a constitutional head. This difference between the published text and Cripps's verbal assurances later became a source of controversy.

Cripps met Congress leaders, including Azad as president and Nehru, with whom he had long conversations, and also Gandhi, who came to Delhi, listened, and returned to Wardha. He met Jinnah and the League leaders, Sikh representatives, the princes' representatives, Ambedkar and other leaders of the Scheduled Castes, and representatives of other groups. Ambedkar and M. C. Rajah wrote to Cripps on 1 April expressing concern that the proposals treated Congress and the League as the only bodies that mattered and that the interests of the Depressed Classes were insufficiently protected. The princes were ambivalent. They welcomed the assurance that treaty rights would be respected, but some were uneasy about participation in a constituent body. The mission therefore ran across a wide field of interests, each of which saw different dangers in the Draft Declaration.

Responses were predominantly negative. Congress objected to the provision for provincial non-accession, which it feared could lead to the fragmentation of the country; to the nomination of state representatives by rulers rather than election by their peoples; and to the retention of British control over defence. Gandhi's reaction, according to a widely repeated remark attributed to him, was that the offer was a post-dated cheque on a bank that was failing. The authenticity and exact wording of this remark have been debated, but it has become shorthand for the view that a promise of future power could not substitute for authority at a moment of crisis. The League objected that the plan did not explicitly concede Pakistan, though it noted that the non-accession provision recognized in principle that provinces might remain outside a union. Sikh leaders opposed the possibility that the Punjab might be separated from the Union, and the Hindu Mahasabha rejected the scheme.

Negotiations then concentrated on defence and the character of the interim government. Nehru and Cripps explored the possibility that an Indian member would hold the Defence portfolio, with the Commander-in-Chief, General Wavell, retaining control of military operations. The Viceroy, the Commander-in-Chief, and the British cabinet resisted any arrangement that would give Indians real authority over the army. President Roosevelt's personal representative, Colonel Louis Johnson, arrived in Delhi in April and became involved in the discussions. By the second week of April the negotiations had collapsed. Congress's formal rejection and Cripps's final exchanges with Azad and Nehru were followed by Cripps's departure on 12 April 1942 and by his broadcast defending the proposals. The Mansergh and Lumby volume of The Transfer of Power documents the exchanges in detail.

Section 83.12

Why the Cripps Mission Failed: Sovereignty, Defence, and Political Distrust

The collapse of the Cripps Mission had several overlapping causes, and the sources, which include official papers, memoirs, and later scholarship, support more than one emphasis. The first and most basic was the dispute about timing and authority. Britain was offering a postwar settlement and a reorganized wartime government in which Indians would play a larger role. Congress wanted a transfer of real authority at once, with an Indian cabinet responsible for the conduct of the war and answerable to public opinion, not to the Viceroy. Britain regarded such a transfer in the middle of a military emergency, with Japanese forces advancing toward Bengal, as both administratively dangerous and politically unacceptable. The gap between a promise and a power transferred in the present explains much of the mistrust.

Defence was the sharpest point of contention. The Commander-in-Chief and the Viceroy insisted that the conduct of the war could not be divided between a British military command and an Indian civil minister, and Churchill's government supported them. Congress's leaders, including Nehru, were prepared to accept that the Commander-in-Chief should retain operational control but wanted an Indian Defence Member with genuine responsibilities for supply, organization, and relations with the public. Discussions on this point came close to agreement at moments in early April, according to several accounts, before the British side drew back. Whether the gap was bridgeable remains a matter of dispute.

A second set of disagreements concerned the postwar provisions. Non-accession meant different things to different parties. For Congress it threatened the unity of the country and set a precedent for fragmentation, since provinces such as the Punjab and Bengal, themselves divided by religion, might be asked to decide as wholes. For the League it fell short of the recognition of separate sovereignty it sought, since provinces could opt out individually but there was no assurance of a Muslim-majority state and no clarification of how the zones in the Lahore Resolution would be formed. For Sikhs it risked their incorporation into a Muslim-majority Punjab outside the Union. The nomination of state representatives, the procedures of the constituent body, and the treaty to protect minorities each added further complications.

Political constraints in Britain also mattered. Churchill's attitude to Indian self-government was well known, and his cabinet's willingness to move beyond the Draft Declaration was limited. Linlithgow, who had been uncertain about the mission from the start, was reluctant to see the Executive Council turned into a cabinet that would diminish his own authority, and he did not conceal his scepticism. Cripps, constrained by the Draft Declaration and by his cabinet instructions, had little scope to offer more. Some scholars, including R. J. Moore in his study of Churchill, Cripps, and India, have emphasized how much of the outcome depended on British cabinet politics and on the unwillingness of key figures to give ground. Others stress the intransigence or timing of Indian leaders.

A final factor was distrust. Congress leaders remembered that British promises in the First World War, discussed in Chapter 78, had been followed by the Rowlatt Acts rather than self-government. Gandhi, committed to nonviolence and sceptical of a war effort in any case, was not an enthusiastic negotiating partner and left Delhi early. British officials, for their part, doubted that Congress would cooperate honestly in defence and suspected that its leaders would use any power they were given to obstruct the war. Each side interpreted the other's stated positions through the experience of earlier failures, and the short timetable intensified the mistrust.

The failure did not make Quit India inevitable, but it made confrontation more likely. Congress emerged with a strengthened conviction that Britain would concede nothing until forced to; the League, which had not accepted the proposals but had avoided being identified as the obstacle, had enhanced its position; and the British government's credibility had been damaged in India, in China, and in the United States, where Roosevelt's displeasure was clear. At the same time, there were still options. The Congress Working Committee in the following months debated alternatives, including cooperation on new terms and civil disobedience, and Gandhi's own position moved through several stages in May and June before hardening in July. The constitutional crisis as it stood at the close of the mission on 12 April 1942 was unresolved. Japan was in Burma, the Executive Council remained unreformed, and the principal Indian parties held irreconcilable positions. What followed, in Chapter 84, was the product of that stalemate and of the pressures that developed through the summer.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Cripps Mission Fail?

The question: Did the mission fail because Britain never intended to concede real power, because Indian leaders, especially Congress, demanded more than a wartime government could safely grant, or because the structural conditions of the war made agreement almost impossible?

Interpretation A: Some historians, with R. J. Moore's work on Churchill, Cripps, and India as an important example, stress British constraints: Churchill's reluctance, Linlithgow's resistance to altering the Executive Council, and the military establishment's refusal to share control of defence. On this view, the offer was too limited and was undermined by those who should have implemented it.
Interpretation B: Others emphasize Indian choices: Gandhi's pacifism and distrust, Congress's rejection of a postwar settlement that allowed partition by non-accession, and the League's refusal to accept anything short of explicit recognition of its demand. On this view, the gap between the parties was as wide as the gap between Britain and Congress.

Evidence: Evidence includes the published Draft Declaration, the Mansergh and Lumby papers, correspondence between Cripps, Churchill, and the Viceroy, memoirs of Nehru, Azad, and others, and the records of American mediation. Interpretations differ over Cripps's oral assurances, the near-agreement on defence, and the role of timing.

Why it matters: The question bears on whether a constitutional settlement might have been possible in 1942 and on how responsibility for the later crisis is assigned. Most scholars now see multiple causes, with the structural pressure of war and mutual distrust operating alongside particular decisions.

Section 83.13

Recruitment, Expansion, and Transformation of the British Indian Armed Forces

The Indian Army that entered the war in 1939 was small, equipped for frontier policing and limited imperial tasks, and organized around British officers commanding Indian soldiers. Different prewar strength estimates range from roughly 194,000 to 240,000, depending on who was counted. Across the entire war, approximately 2.5 million men volunteered for service in the Indian armed forces—a cumulative recruitment figure associated with the largest volunteer military mobilization of the period. It should not be misread as a precisely dated headcount of the Indian Army in August 1945 or projected backward to the time of the Cripps Mission. The expansion was gradual, constrained by shortages of equipment, instructors, and officers, and by the unresolved political questions of 1939 to 1942. At the time of the Cripps Mission the force was already several times its prewar size, and reconstructions commonly place it at roughly a million, but the greatest expansion came afterward, as the demands of the Burma campaign and other theatres grew.

Recruitment remained voluntary. India did not adopt conscription, in part because the political risks of compulsion in the face of Congress opposition were obvious. The state relied on pay, rations, allowances, pensions, and the prestige of military service, supplemented by the work of provincial recruiting boards, village officials, and local notables. In rural areas the army offered a reliable wage at a time of rising prices, and during the economic pressures of 1942 and 1943 recruitment rose in several regions. Historians such as Yasmin Khan and Srinath Raghavan have stressed that for many recruits enlistment was an individual economic decision as well as an act of loyalty, shaped by family needs, debt, local tradition, and the options available to peasants and landless labourers.

Regional recruitment patterns changed but did not disappear. Before the war the army had drawn disproportionately from the Punjab, the North-West Frontier, Nepal, and certain communities in the United Provinces and Rajputana, justified by the colonial doctrine of martial races examined in Chapter 73. During the war the army's needs forced it to widen its base. Units were formed from communities previously excluded or underrepresented, including Madrasis, Bengalis, and Scheduled Castes such as the Mahars. The Punjab nonetheless remained the largest single source, and its Muslim, Sikh, and Hindu recruits kept their prominence. The expansion thus altered the social composition of the army without dismantling the structures that had shaped it, and it left questions about the distribution of the army between the provinces that would matter at Partition.

Training and organization changed too. The army created new divisions, armoured and airborne units, signals, engineers, and technical services. Soldiers learned trades as mechanics, radio operators, drivers, and clerks that had civilian value after the war. Women's auxiliary services were established, including the Women's Auxiliary Corps (India) in 1942, and nursing and medical services expanded. The Royal Indian Navy expanded from roughly 1,800 people on the eve of war to about 30,000 by its close, and the Indian Air Force likewise grew from a small prewar organization to tens of thousands of personnel. These service estimates use distinct personnel classifications and must not be aggregated with cumulative Army recruitment figures. These services became new fields for Indian professional participation, even though senior command remained largely British.

The officer corps was the most politically sensitive element. The Indian Military Academy at Dehra Dun had been opened in 1932 to train Indian officers, but before the war the number of Indian commissioned officers was small. Wartime expansion required many more, and emergency commissions were granted to thousands of Indians, many from the new urban middle classes. Indian officers nevertheless faced discrimination in pay, promotion, and social treatment, and the pace of Indianization remained a source of grievance. Their experience affected the careers of those who later led the armed forces of India and Pakistan, and it altered the relationship between the army and Indian society.

Service should therefore be understood from several viewpoints. For the colonial state, the army was an imperial instrument and the chief guarantee of its ability to wage war and keep order. For recruits it was a job, a source of status, and a possible route to new skills. For nationalist politicians it was an object of suspicion and, increasingly, of ambition, since the loyalty and composition of the army would be crucial to any future state. Each of these perspectives is documented in the official histories, the recruitment records, and the censored letters that soldiers sent home, and none is sufficient alone.

Section 83.14

Indian Soldiers and the Global War: Africa, the Middle East, Europe, and Asia

Indian soldiers fought in more theatres than any previous generation of the army, and their service gave the war a global dimension in South Asia that no wartime political narrative can capture. Indian mule companies were among the forces evacuated at Dunkirk in 1940. In East Africa, the 4th and 5th Indian Divisions took part in the campaigns against Italian forces in Eritrea and Ethiopia in 1940 and 1941, including the hard-fought battle for Keren. In the Western Desert, the 4th Indian Division fought in Operation Compass in December 1940, and Indian formations remained prominent in the desert campaigns of 1941 and 1942. In 1941 Indian troops operated in Iraq, Syria, and Iran, where British and Soviet forces occupied the country in August and September, securing oil supplies and a supply route to the Soviet Union.

The Far East brought the army's most painful experience. Indian divisions formed a large part of the force that defended Malaya and Singapore in 1941 and 1942, and Indian battalions fought at Hong Kong. The campaigns ended in defeat and mass captivity. Tens of thousands of Indian soldiers became prisoners of the Japanese, and their treatment, and the political pressures placed upon them, lie behind the formation of the Indian National Army discussed in Chapter 84. Others took part in the long retreat from Burma in 1942. From 1943 Indian divisions moved to Italy, where they fought in the Sangro and Cassino battles and the Gothic Line offensive, while the larger part of the army prepared for the reconquest of Burma, which Chapter 84 narrates.

Casualty figures are uneven and should be read with care. Official-history and later commemorative statistics do not always count the same population or event. Figures for men killed in action cannot simply be set against totals that also include deaths from disease and wounds, personnel missing or presumed dead, other services, or different geographic jurisdictions. Where definitions differ, a single reconciled death figure should not be inferred from the apparent discrepancy. Disease was a major killer in tropical theatres, and malaria and other illnesses caused far more suffering than combat in some campaigns until medical practice improved. Indian soldiers also won many gallantry awards, including a considerable number of Victoria Crosses.

Service in the same army did not mean equal treatment. Indian soldiers were commanded by British officers, with the intermediate rank of Viceroy's Commissioned Officer available to Indians but ranking below the youngest British lieutenant. Pay, rations, and allowances differed by race and unit. Medical care for Indian troops was often inferior, and evacuation arrangements in the 1942 retreats from Malaya and Burma were widely resented because Europeans were given priority. Religion and caste also affected daily life: army messing, ritual requirements, and the composition of units reflected the colonial classification of communities and shaped the way the army managed difference.

Soldiers' own voices come to historians mainly through censored letters, memoirs, interviews, and unit records. The letters reveal worry about families, wages, remittances, and food, as well as pride, fear, and bewilderment. They show that many soldiers had little interest in the political debates on the home front but followed them with attention, and that the sight of other countries, of European society, and of a defeated Britain affected their attitude to the empire. Some servicemen later joined nationalist movements; most did not. The experience of foreign service widened their horizons and gave many of them new standards by which to judge colonial rule, a development with long-term consequences for the postwar politics examined in Chapter 85.

The global reach of Indian service is also evidence of how heavily the imperial war effort leaned on South Asia. It shows that the questions Congress and the League raised about consent, representation, and war aims were not abstract constitutional quarrels. They concerned the daily use of Indian lives and resources in distant theatres and the proportion of the costs Indians would be asked to bear.

Section 83.15

Wartime Industry, Railways, Labor, and the Expansion of State Direction

The war transformed the Indian economy by creating enormous demand for military supplies. A Department of Supply was established at the outset of the war to coordinate purchases, and in October and November 1940 a conference at Delhi led to the creation of the Eastern Group Supply Council, which aimed to make India and neighbouring territories a supply base for the Middle East and the Far East. Orders flowed to factories making uniforms, boots, blankets, and tents, to engineering workshops producing ammunition and equipment, and to mills producing jute sandbags and cotton textiles. The government expanded ordnance factories and encouraged private firms to undertake defence contracts. India thereby became a major arsenal for the Allied war effort in Asia and the Middle East.

Heavy industry saw opportunities and constraints. The Tata Iron and Steel Company at Jamshedpur raised output, and new ventures began, including the Hindustan Aircraft Company at Bangalore, founded in December 1940 by Walchand Hirachand with the support of the Mysore government and later the central government, and a shipyard at Visakhapatnam. But India lacked machine tools, and wartime shipping shortages made imports of equipment difficult. The result was uneven development: sectors favoured by military demand expanded rapidly, while others, especially those producing consumer goods for civilians, were starved of materials. Industrialists such as the Tatas and Birlas earned large profits and grew in political and economic weight, a development reflected in the Bombay Plan of 1944, which anticipated an independent India's economic planning.

Railways, ports, and docks carried the heaviest burdens. Railway traffic increased as troops, supplies, and food moved across the country, and the government diverted locomotives, wagons, and rails to the Middle East and to military needs, leaving the network overstretched and poorly maintained. Docks at Bombay, Calcutta, Karachi, Madras, and Visakhapatnam handled vast cargoes. Military construction included airfields, roads, camps, and hospitals, particularly in Assam and eastern Bengal, which would become the staging area for Burma. These projects employed large numbers of labourers, many recruited from distant villages, and changed local economies.

Labour conditions were complex. Employment in factories, mines, and military construction grew, and demand for workers gave some groups greater bargaining power. Yet prices rose faster than wages in many sectors, and workers struck for dearness allowances in textile centres such as Bombay and Kanpur. The Defence of India Rules gave the government powers to restrict strikes and enforce discipline, and officials treated stoppages in war industries as threats to national security. Women's employment expanded in some places, in factories, offices, and auxiliary services, and wartime exigencies led the authorities to relax restrictions on women's underground work in coal mines. These changes did not alter the broader gender hierarchy, but they opened some new roles.

The state's role in the economy grew accordingly. It controlled prices, allocated scarce materials, directed transport, and managed procurement, using the emergency rules that the war made available. Planning and control became a normal part of administration, a precedent that postwar governments would inherit. At the same time these measures often failed to prevent shortages and profiteering. Their administration depended on a stretched bureaucracy and on local intermediaries, and contractors and traders found ways to profit.

War accelerated some forms of industrial growth while subjecting civilian production and consumption to severe constraints. This combination of opportunity and shortage helps explain why the war produced both prosperity and bitterness. Those who held contracts or sold to the army often did well; those with fixed incomes, those who relied on imported goods, and those who lacked access to controlled supplies bore the costs. The financial mechanisms behind this unevenness are examined in Division V.

Section 83.16

South Asia beyond British India: Ceylon, Nepal, Princely States, and Regional Connections

Treating British India as the whole of South Asia would distort the wartime picture. The region comprised distinct constitutional territories, each with its own relationship to Britain and to the war, and the history of the conflict across them was varied. Ceylon, a Crown colony with a State Council in which Sinhalese, Tamil, and other leaders participated, was a vital strategic base. Its ports, particularly Colombo and Trincomalee, served the Royal Navy, and its airfields supported air operations. The island's political leaders, including D. S. Senanayake and Sir Baron Jayatilaka, generally cooperated with the war effort while pressing for constitutional advance, a process that led to the Soulbury inquiry and the later path to independence described in Chapter 91. The Japanese raids of April 1942 and the establishment of the Allied South East Asia Command at Kandy in 1944 gave Ceylon a central place in the campaign against Japan.

Nepal was not part of British India and was formally independent, though bound to Britain by treaty and by the long tradition of Gurkha recruitment. The Rana prime minister, Juddha Shamsher, committed Nepal's support to Britain at the war's outset, offering troops, resources, and facilities. Gurkha regiments of the Indian Army expanded greatly, with the recruitment of Nepalese men in much larger numbers than before; commonly cited figures speak of well over a hundred thousand Gurkha servicemen. The Ranas valued the alliance as a guarantee of their rule and as a source of income, and the consequences for Nepali society, including remittances and military pensions, lasted beyond the war. Bhutan, in its different relation with the Government of India, was less directly involved.

The princely states contributed in ways that varied with their size and resources. Large states such as Hyderabad, Mysore, Gwalior, Kashmir, and Patiala raised and equipped State Forces, donated money and aircraft, and provided supplies. Their contributions reinforced the princes' claim to loyalty and their case for the preservation of their treaty status, even as nationalist and popular movements within the states pressed for representation. The war thus strengthened the princes' immediate standing with the British while deepening the long-term tension between their rule and political change, a tension examined in Chapters 71 and 87.

Burma had been separated from British India in April 1937 under the Government of Burma Act and was governed as a distinct colony with its own ministry, in which U Saw and Ba Maw held office at different moments. Burma's separation did not end its connections with India. A large Indian population, probably around a million, lived and worked there as labourers, clerks, traders, and moneylenders, including the Chettiar banking community of south India. Japanese conquest in early 1942 caused a mass exodus. Hundreds of thousands of Indian civilians fled toward Assam and Bengal, often on foot over difficult mountain routes, and many died from disease and exhaustion. The treatment of different groups in evacuation, with Europeans favoured, became a lasting grievance. Burma also had been a major source of rice imports for parts of India, and its loss would have economic consequences.

The Indian Ocean connected these territories. The Maldives, a British protectorate, hosted a naval anchorage at Addu Atoll. Indian merchants, lascars, and workers moved among East Africa, the Gulf, Ceylon, Malaya, and Burma, and Indian troops served at many of these points. The war cut these networks in some places and expanded them in others. Chettiar and other Indian business communities in Southeast Asia suffered losses, while the traffic in military supplies and personnel across the ocean grew.

Keeping these territories distinct matters for analysis. Each had its own constitutional status, political leaders, and economy, and each encountered the war differently. Their histories converge in the Allied logistical and strategic system centred on India, but they do not constitute a single British Indian administrative unit. Recognizing their differences also anticipates the divergent postwar paths that Volume VI examines.

Section 83.17

Financing the War: Inflation, Sterling Balances, and Colonial Economic Policy

The war's costs were paid through a financial system that tied India's economy to Britain's. The Government of India's defence expenditure rose steeply, financed by higher taxation, borrowing, and the expansion of the money supply. New taxes, including an excess profits tax introduced in 1940 and increases in income tax and customs duties, raised revenue, while the government sold war loans and savings certificates. The Finance Member, Sir Jeremy Raisman, managed a budget that had to meet military, civil, and supply obligations while avoiding collapse of public confidence. The pattern of financing, however, relied heavily on creating money, and its effects spread through the whole economy.

The arrangements between London and Delhi were central. Under the financial settlement negotiated in 1939 and 1940, India paid for its own peacetime defence needs, while Britain bore the additional costs of using Indian forces outside India and of supplies bought for imperial purposes. In practice the British government purchased goods and services in India and paid for them not in gold or dollars but by crediting sterling to the Reserve Bank of India's account in London. The Reserve Bank, in turn, issued rupees to the Indian government to cover its payments. The result was that India accumulated large sterling credits, known as sterling balances, which by the end of the war stood at well over a billion pounds in widely cited figures. India changed from a net debtor to a major creditor of Britain.

This creditor status was ambiguous. On paper India was acquiring assets, and some Indian economists and officials, including Reserve Bank leaders, hoped the balances could finance postwar development. Yet the balances were held in London, could not be spent freely, and depended on British goodwill and economic recovery. Nationalists argued that India was in effect financing its own occupation and the war effort by lending to its rulers while its people faced shortages. Britain's own officials worried about the size of the debt and about how it would be settled after the war, a negotiation that continued into the independence period.

Monetary expansion fuelled inflation. The Reserve Bank of India’s historical Issue Department series reports notes in circulation at Rs 236.6 crore at end-December 1939 and Rs 1,137.5 crore at end-June 1945; it gives Rs 1,236.9 crore at end-June 1946. The series is not a measure of coin-plus-note currency, and the Rs 171.8 crore sometimes misattributed to 1939 actually belongs to end-December 1935. Wholesale prices rose in tandem, and an article in the International Labour Review in 1944, drawing on official indices, reported that the general wholesale price index had more than doubled from its August 1939 base by 1943. Prices of manufactured goods and foodgrains rose sharply because of military demand, reduced imports, and shortages. The exact path differed by commodity and region, but the broad trend was clear, and it persisted until the end of the war.

Nominal economic expansion should not be confused with improved living standards. Government expenditure, corporate profits, and industrial output rose in nominal terms, and some producers and contractors prospered. But money wages in many occupations lagged behind prices, and real incomes for large sections of the population fell. Wartime inflation redistributed resources from those with fixed or slowly adjusting incomes, including labourers, small salaried workers, and many peasants who bought food in markets, to those who held goods, land, or contracts. The experience of inflation, and the sense that it had been imposed by wartime policy, informed the political mood on which both Congress and Communists drew.

Colonial economic policy during the war was thus contradictory. It promoted production for the military, expanded state direction of the economy, and fostered some industrial growth, but it did so within a financial framework that favoured British needs and created mounting inflationary pressure. The structure of sterling credits would shape economic bargaining between India and Britain after 1945, and the inflation of these years left a legacy that Chapter 84, on the famine of 1943, shows in its most severe form.

Historical Vocabulary
Sterling Balances

Sterling balances were credits held in London by the Reserve Bank of India and the Government of India, accumulated because Britain paid for supplies and military expenditure in India by crediting sterling rather than paying in goods, gold, or convertible currency. By the end of the war they exceeded a billion pounds, turning India from a debtor to a creditor of Britain.

The term matters because the balances were a financial asset on paper but a constrained one in practice. They could not be freely spent, and they were tied to British economic recovery. They also linked wartime inflation to colonial finance, since the rupees issued against them circulated in India while the goods they paid for left the country. Their settlement became part of postwar bargaining between India and Britain.

Economic Dislocation: Food Prices, Shortages, Labor, and Unequal Wartime Burdens

The financial and military pressures described above reached households most directly through the markets for food and consumer goods. As prices rose and supplies tightened, ordinary people encountered the war economy at the grain shop, the cloth market, the railway station, and the wage office. Shortages affected foodgrains, sugar, kerosene, cloth, and other necessities, and the combination of military procurement, transport disruption, and reduced imports created difficulties in many regions. The pressures were not uniform. They varied by region, by crop, and by social position, and a full explanation requires attention to differences among rural and urban households as well as between those with land or goods and those without.

The loss of Burma in early 1942 deprived India of a significant source of imported rice, which had been important for some regions and cities, especially in eastern and southern India, even though imports formed a modest share of total national consumption. Shipping was diverted to military purposes, so that the movement of food by sea became less reliable, and coastal trade suffered. Within India the railways were crowded with military traffic, and civilian goods often waited or moved slowly. The government also purchased large quantities of grain and other supplies for the army and for export to other theatres, adding to demand. These factors meant that local shortages could develop even where national stocks seemed adequate.

Government responses developed unevenly. Price control measures and procurement systems were introduced in stages and were often poorly coordinated between the centre and the provinces. Provinces imposed restrictions on the movement of grain, which could trap supplies within regions and aggravate scarcity elsewhere. Rationing schemes began to appear in major cities in 1942 and 1943, giving some urban populations a degree of protection that rural populations lacked. Campaigns to grow more food were announced. Hoarding and profiteering were widely reported, and the officials responsible for enforcement, drawn from the same stretched bureaucracy that handled other wartime tasks, struggled to control them.

The burdens fell unequally. Agricultural labourers, who were paid in cash or in kind at rates that did not keep pace with prices, were among the most exposed. Small peasants who sold their crops to pay debts and later bought grain at higher prices suffered, whereas farmers with surplus to sell could gain. Factory workers received dearness allowances in some industries, but wage increases often lagged behind the cost of living, and housing in overcrowded wartime cities was expensive. The families of soldiers received allotments from pay, which gave them a regular income and protection against some of the worst effects, though inflation eroded its value. Merchants and traders with stocks often profited, and consumers in poorer households bore the heaviest costs.

Bengal was especially affected by these pressures. It was a densely populated province, dependent on rice, and its position as the eastern military base for the Burma campaign meant heavy demands on its resources and transport. The movement of troops, the construction of airfields, the influx of workers, and the proximity of the Japanese advance all raised prices and strained the food system there. The province's political setting, with its divided ministry, added to the difficulties. It is important not to read the later famine of 1943 backward as the sole outcome of these pressures, and this chapter does not narrate or adjudicate its causes. Chapter 84 treats the famine in its own right, including the debate about responsibility. The point here is that the economic dislocations of 1942 and earlier created conditions of vulnerability.

The effect on the legitimacy of colonial administration was cumulative. People who experienced rising prices, shortages, and official controls tended to hold the government responsible, whether fairly or not. The war economy therefore combined material hardship with political resentment, and these feelings informed the willingness of many people to support protest in 1942.

Section 83.19

The Weakening of Imperial Authority: Emergency Powers, Dependence, and Political Legitimacy

The Raj in 1942 was in some respects stronger than before the war and in others more vulnerable. It possessed wider emergency powers, a larger army, a bigger bureaucracy for economic control, and the capacity to detain opponents. Yet it depended more than ever on the cooperation of Indian soldiers, officials, producers, taxpayers, and princes. This tension between increased coercive capacity and increased dependence is the heart of the argument that the war weakened imperial authority.

Emergency legislation conferred extensive powers. The Defence of India Act and its Rules allowed detention without trial, censorship of the press and mail, control over movement and assembly, and regulation of economic activity. Section 93 rule in seven provinces meant that governors and their Indian Civil Service advisers governed without responsible ministers for long periods; Assam retained ministerial government under another party alignment. Newspapers faced restrictions on what they could print about the war and about political protest. The government used these powers against revolutionaries, Communists, and Congress satyagrahis, and administrative practice in the provinces became more centralized and more security-minded. The result was an increase in the government's formal powers.

Yet the administration's reliance on Indians deepened. By the early 1940s Indians made up roughly half of the Indian Civil Service, and they filled most of the police, revenue, and subordinate administrative posts. The army depended on Indian soldiers and increasingly Indian officers. Industrial production required Indian entrepreneurs and workers, revenue depended on Indian taxpayers, and the Executive Council, expanded in 1941, included a majority of Indian members. Each of these groups had interests of its own, and officials worried about their reliability, particularly after the Japanese successes. A government that relied on persuasion and cooperation as well as force could not easily operate if these groups withdrew their support.

International scrutiny added pressure. The United States, China, and the Soviet Union all had stakes in India's contribution to the war, and Roosevelt and Chiang Kai-shek made their views known. American troops, journalists, and officials arrived in India in growing numbers from 1942, and the Atlantic Charter and Allied propaganda about freedom focused attention on the colonial question. British leaders, who needed American support, could not ignore criticism, even though they resisted proposals they thought would weaken their hold. The international setting thus altered the context in which the Raj operated.

The political parties' demands meanwhile hardened. Congress insisted on independence and national government, the League on recognition of Muslim nationhood and a veto on constitutional arrangements, the Sikhs and Scheduled Castes on protection against domination, and the princes on preservation of treaty rights. The Viceroy could balance these claims for a time, and the war gave him a reason to defer decisions. But the delay also confirmed the impression that Britain would not transfer authority and encouraged parties to prepare for the postwar settlement by consolidating their bases. The legitimacy of the British position thus eroded not through a single event but through accumulating contradictions between stated war aims and actual policy.

It would be an error to conclude that colonial coercive capacity had collapsed. The police, the army, and the administration remained effective, as the suppression of the 1942 uprising would show. The weakening was political and moral: the Raj retained the ability to arrest, detain, and repress but had lost much of its capacity to command consent. That distinction is crucial for understanding why the events of 1942 to 1945, reviewed in Chapter 84, damaged British rule so seriously without destroying it at once.

Section 83.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: The War and the Transformation of India’s Political Future

The history of South Asia in the Second World War, as traced to April 1942, supports an argument that goes beyond the proposition that the war interrupted the struggle for independence. It transformed the struggle's institutional and material foundations. Britain needed Indian soldiers, supplies, revenue, and cooperation to sustain a global war, yet it was unwilling to concede sovereignty in return. The tension between these two facts shaped each episode of the period from September 1939 to April 1942, from the unilateral declaration of war to the failure of the Cripps Mission.

Four processes interacted. First, constitutional deadlock hardened. The declaration of war without consultation provoked the Congress ministries' resignations, the Viceroy's statements of 1939 and 1940 offered only postponed and conditional change, and the Cripps proposals, though more substantial than anything before, foundered on defence, timing, and distrust. Second, nationalist and communal movements diverged. Congress pursued a strategy of principled opposition under Gandhi's leadership, while the League used the political space created by Congress's withdrawal to articulate a constitutional demand, the Lahore Resolution, whose ambiguity enabled a growing coalition. Other groups, Sikhs, Scheduled Castes, Hindu nationalists, Muslim opponents of the League, princes, and left parties, defined their own responses.

Third, mobilization broadened participation in the war. The expansion of the army to a size that would exceed two and a half million, the opening of new communities to military service, the growth of technical and officer cadres, the industrial and transport effort, and the global deployment of Indian soldiers made South Asia central to Allied strategy. Fourth, the economy was reshaped by wartime finance. Sterling balances, inflation, shortages, and controls created both opportunities and hardships, with burdens falling unevenly across regions and classes. These developments formed an interconnected system rather than separate stories.

The political result was a combination of imperial dependence and constitutional immobility. The colonial state became more coercive and more dependent at once, and the rhetoric of freedom used by its allies underscored the contradiction. Congress and the League increasingly pursued different constitutional strategies, and the contest between them became more significant to British policy. It would be too simple to say that Partition was determined by these years. The Lahore Resolution left its meaning open, many Muslims and others rejected it, and the British government and Indian parties retained choices. But the wartime deadlock narrowed the options and shaped the conditions in which later negotiations took place.

The chapter's chronology should also be read in two layers. The political narrative runs from the war declaration and the resignations of 1939, through Lahore, the August Offer, and individual satyagraha in 1940 and 1941, to the Japanese advance and the Cripps Mission in 1942. The structural story of military and economic transformation runs through the whole war, to 1945, and continues to affect the postwar period through the size and composition of the armed forces, the sterling balances, industrial capacity, price levels, and administrative controls. Separating these layers allows the chapter to explain the scale of mobilization without absorbing the specialized narratives reserved for the next chapter.

The chapter closes at the political threshold of Quit India. In April 1942 the Cripps Mission had left, Japanese forces were in Burma, the constitutional crisis was unresolved, and the parties were preparing for confrontation, uncertainty, or both. Chapter 84 examines what followed: the August 1942 rising and its repression, Subhas Chandra Bose's wartime career and the Indian National Army, the campaigns on the eastern front, and the Bengal famine of 1943. Together those events deepened the crisis of the Raj that this chapter has traced to its beginning. Chapter 85 then turns to the negotiations through which Britain, Congress, the League, and others sought a way out.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Second World War did not merely interrupt the Indian independence struggle but transformed its institutional and material foundations. Britain needed Indian soldiers, supplies, revenue, and cooperation to fight a global war, yet it was unwilling to concede real sovereignty in return. That contradiction ran through every episode from September 1939 to April 1942 and shaped the economic and military history of the whole war.

India entered the war on 3 September 1939 by the Viceroy's announcement, a step that was legally possible because the Government of India Act of 1935 had created provincial autonomy without federal responsibility. The absence of consultation, in contrast with the procedures followed in the self-governing Dominions, made consent the central political issue. Congress, which condemned fascism but objected to the manner of India's entry, asked Britain to state its war aims and, after an unsatisfactory reply on 17 October, directed its ministries to resign.

The resignations ended Congress rule in eight provinces, bringing Section 93 government in seven and a successor ministry in Assam. They also created a political opening. The Muslim League, which had performed modestly in 1937, used the Day of Deliverance of December 1939 and its negotiations with the Viceroy to raise its profile, though its support was not universal. Other groups, among them the Hindu Mahasabha, Sikh organizations, Ambedkar's followers, princely rulers, and left parties, responded according to their own interests, showing that Indian opinion cannot be reduced to support for or opposition to the war.

The Lahore Resolution of March 1940 marked a decisive step in the League's constitutional thinking. Moved by Fazlul Huq and adopted after Jinnah's address, it demanded that Muslim-majority areas in the north-west and east be grouped as "Independent States" with autonomous and sovereign constituent units. Its plural, geographically unspecific wording left open what would be created, and many Muslims, Sikhs, Hindus, and others contested or interpreted it differently. Partition was not settled in 1940.

The August Offer of 1940 promised a postwar constituent body and an enlarged Executive Council, but linked change to the agreement of minorities. Congress rejected it as inadequate, and the League welcomed the assurance while withholding acceptance. Gandhi's individual satyagraha, launched in October 1940, asserted the right to oppose the war without paralyzing the administration and must be distinguished from the mass movement of 1942.

The war widened with Germany's attack on the Soviet Union and Japan's attacks in December 1941. The fall of Singapore and Rangoon, Japanese raids on Ceylon and India's east coast, and pressure from the United States and China produced the Cripps Mission of March and April 1942. Its Draft Declaration offered Dominion status after the war, a constituent assembly, and provincial non-accession, but kept defence in British hands. Congress and the League rejected it for different reasons, and historians continue to debate the weight of British, Indian, and structural causes.

The second half of the chapter turned to mobilization. From a prewar army of roughly two hundred thousand, Indian military mobilization produced about 2.5 million cumulative volunteers across the war; recruitment increasingly extended to communities previously excluded by colonial classifications. Indian soldiers served in East Africa, North Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and Italy, enduring a hierarchy that discriminated by race and rank. Industry, railways, and ports expanded under state direction, producing profits for some and strain for others.

War finance rested on sterling credits and monetary expansion. The sterling balances turned India into a creditor, but currency circulation and prices rose sharply, eroding real incomes and creating shortages. Burdens fell unevenly across agricultural labourers, soldiers' families, industrial workers, and merchants. The chapter described these pressures, including those that affected Bengal, without narrating the famine of 1943, which Chapter 84 examines.

The colonial state became more coercive and more dependent at the same time. Emergency powers, censorship, and detention enlarged its formal capacity, while its reliance on Indian personnel, producers, soldiers, and taxpayers deepened, and international scrutiny increased. Its political legitimacy weakened even though its coercive capacity had not collapsed.

The chapter closes at the threshold of Quit India. In April 1942 the constitutional crisis was unresolved, the principal movements pursued divergent strategies, and Japanese forces were on India's frontier. Chapter 84 follows the confrontation that resulted, and Chapter 85 the negotiations that ended British rule.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a global war altered the conditions of a national struggle. Before 1939 the question was how and when India might gain self-government. After 1942 it was also whether the British could govern without Indian consent, how far Indian movements could agree on the shape of a future state, and what a mobilized society, with a vastly larger army, a bigger industrial base, and a damaged economy, would demand.

The chapter also reveals the interdependence of political and material history. The failure of the Cripps Mission cannot be understood without the strategic crisis that produced it, and the economic pressures of the war cannot be understood without the political contest over who spoke for India. Considering Ceylon, Nepal, Burma, the princely states, and the Indian Ocean reminds students that South Asia was not a single administrative unit even when it was a single war theatre.

Many consequences of these years lasted beyond them: the League's new status, the Congress leadership's imprisonment and resolve, the size and composition of the armed forces, the sterling balances, and the habits of state control. Their significance emerges in later chapters, beginning with the uprising and crises of Chapter 84 and the negotiations of Chapter 85, and, in Volume VI, in the institutions of the independent states.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter belongs to the closing phase of Volume V. It links the nationalism and constitutional politics of Chapters 76 to 82 with the crisis of the Raj in Chapters 84 to 87, and it prepares for the postcolonial histories that follow. Its central lesson is that the end of empire in South Asia was shaped by war, finance, and negotiation as much as by ideology, and that no single outcome was fixed in 1939.
