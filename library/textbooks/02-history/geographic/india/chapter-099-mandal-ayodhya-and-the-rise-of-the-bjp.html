Mandal, Ayodhya, and the Rise of the BJP

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 7 August 1990 the prime minister of India rose in Parliament to announce that a report written ten years earlier, and left in government files for almost the whole of that decade, would at last be put into effect. Within weeks, students were on the streets of Delhi; within a few more, a politician was riding through the western and northern states in a vehicle styled as a chariot, on his way to a town on the Ghaghara river. The two events, the decision on government jobs and the journey toward Ayodhya, belong to different histories. Together they reorganized Indian politics.

Chapter 98 traced the economic crisis of 1991 and the era of coalition government in which liberalization occurred. This chapter turns to the social and religious politics that made that coalition era possible: the mobilization of caste communities that demanded a share of state power, the Hindu nationalist campaign over a disputed mosque at Ayodhya, and the growth of the Bharatiya Janata Party from a two-seat parliamentary group in 1984 into the leader of a governing alliance by 1998. It covers principally the years from 1980 to 2004, with constitutional and organizational background reaching back to 1947 and to the nineteen-twenties.

The chapter begins from a puzzle. By the late 1980s the Congress party, which had governed India for most of the years since independence, appeared to be the natural party of government, though weakened. Within fifteen years it had lost its position as the single dominant party, and the political system had come to be dominated by coalitions of regional, caste-based, and national parties. This change was not produced by a single cause or a single movement. It resulted from the interaction of social change, judicial decisions, party strategy, and violent events, and it cannot be summarized as a contest between caste and religion.

Three transformations organize the narrative. The first concerns the Mandal Commission and the system of reservation for Other Backward Classes, from its constitutional foundations through the Supreme Court’s decision in Indra Sawhney (1992). The second concerns Hindu nationalism and the Ayodhya movement, from the ideological formation of Hindutva through the demolition of the Babri Masjid on 6 December 1992. The third concerns the rise of the BJP and the fragmentation of the Congress system, through the National Democratic Alliance government and the election of 2004. The chapter’s twenty sections, in five divisions, take these in turn and then examine their interaction.

The chapter requires the careful handling of evidence. Official reports, such as the Mandal and Srikrishna reports, are indispensable but were produced by commissions with specific mandates. Court judgments establish legal facts, not necessarily historical ones. Party resolutions and speeches show intention and rhetoric. Archaeological claims about Ayodhya have been contested among specialists. Reports of numbers killed in riots vary. The chapter therefore distinguishes religious belief, organized political campaigns, legal findings, and acts of violence, and it attributes responsibility to specific actors and institutions rather than to whole communities.

The geography of the story is also unevenly distributed. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, with their large populations and rich political traditions, were central; Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Madhya Pradesh were important for the BJP; the Dravidian parties of Tamil Nadu, the Left Front of West Bengal and Kerala, and the Telugu Desam Party show that large parts of India followed different paths. Neighbouring countries appear when they were affected, as Pakistan and Bangladesh were by the aftermath of 6 December 1992.

For the series as a whole, this chapter marks a transition within Volume VI. The constitutional order described in Chapters 88 and 89 was designed to accommodate social and religious diversity within a democratic framework; the crises of the 1990s tested that design. Chapter 100 will follow the party system described here into the twenty-first century.

Historical Context

The Constitution of 1950 set out an ambitious combination of principles: equal citizenship, universal adult franchise, protection of minorities, and special measures for disadvantaged groups. Chapter 88 examined its drafting, and Chapter 89 described the early development of affirmative action, the abolition of intermediary landholding, and the reorganization of states along linguistic lines. In the decades that followed, the Congress governed through a coalition that, in much of north India, joined upper-caste elites, Dalits, and Muslims, and that accommodated regional leaders within a single organization.

That arrangement weakened from the 1960s. Opposition parties, including socialists and the Jana Sangh, made inroads in the states in 1967. Indira Gandhi’s centralization of the party, the Emergency of 1975–1977 (Chapter 93), and the breakup of the Janata Party gave rise to new alignments. The Green Revolution created a class of prosperous peasants, many of them from OBC communities (Chapter 94), and regional movements in Punjab, Assam, and Kashmir (Chapters 94 and 105) tested the Union. By the late 1980s, the Bofors controversy and economic strain had weakened the Rajiv Gandhi government.

Religion entered national politics in new ways. The conversions at Meenakshipuram in 1981, the Shah Bano controversy, and the opening of the Ayodhya locks in 1986 were events in which the state, parties, courts, and religious organizations interacted. The organizations of Hindu nationalism, which had been marginal electorally before the Emergency, found in this environment an opportunity.

The economic context was also changing. The liberalization of the early 1990s (Chapter 98) took place while these political processes were under way, and the two should be read together: the decentralization of power to states, the weakening of the Congress, and the expansion of the middle classes all shaped the political space in which Mandal and Ayodhya had effect. The reader should keep in mind that this chapter and Chapter 98 cover overlapping years from different perspectives.

§§99.1–99.4 · Caste, constitutional equality, reservations, and the transformation of political representation

Section 99.1

Caste, Social Inequality, and the Origins of the Mandal Question

The Mandal controversy of 1990 did not begin with a single commission or a single prime minister. It grew from a constitutional problem that the framers of independent India had confronted in 1946–1950: how a republic committed to equal citizenship should respond to inequalities that had been organized, over many centuries and in many regional forms, around hereditary status. Chapter 88 examined the Constituent Assembly and the provisions on fundamental rights; Chapter 89 examined the first generation of affirmative-action policy. This section recalls only what students need in order to understand why, four decades after independence, a decision about government jobs could transform national politics.

The Constitution of 1950 combined a guarantee of equality before the law and equal opportunity in public employment (Articles 14–16) with enabling provisions for special measures. Article 16(4) permitted the state to reserve appointments for any “backward class of citizens” not adequately represented in state services. Reserved seats in legislatures and positions in government service were created for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, groups listed in schedules attached to the Constitution. Article 340 empowered the President to appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of other socially and educationally backward classes and recommend remedies. After the Supreme Court’s 1951 decision in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan struck down a Madras communal order on admissions, Parliament adopted the First Amendment, which added Article 15(4) and made special provision for backward classes in education explicitly constitutional. The text deliberately used the language of social and educational backwardness; it did not define “caste” as the sole criterion, and the question of whether caste could serve as a proxy for backwardness became a recurring subject of litigation.

The category “Other Backward Classes” (OBCs) must therefore be understood as an administrative and political construction rather than a timeless social order. It referred to a very large and internally unequal set of communities that were neither Scheduled Castes (formerly “untouchable” groups, now often described collectively as Dalits) nor Scheduled Tribes, nor the groups conventionally regarded as dominant upper castes. Some OBC communities, such as landowning cultivators in particular districts, held substantial local power; others, including many artisan, service, and pastoral communities, possessed little land and limited access to education. Scholars therefore describe the OBC label as covering more variation in wealth and ritual standing than either the Scheduled Castes or the upper castes. Castes were also differently placed in different regions: a community listed as backward in one state might be treated as socially dominant in another.

The first national attempt to map this terrain was the First Backward Classes Commission, appointed in January 1953 under the Gandhian scholar Kaka Kalelkar. Its 1955 report identified a long list of backward communities and urged consideration of caste as a criterion, but its own chairman expressed misgivings about the recommendations, and the Union government did not implement the report. For the next two decades the central government left most OBC policy to the states.

The result was a pattern of uneven development. Southern states, where non-Brahmin movements had challenged Brahmin predominance in education and administration since the early twentieth century (Chapter 80), had the longest institutional history of reservation. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Kerala operated systems of quotas, and the Havanur Commission in Karnataka (1975) and the Karpoori Thakur formula in Bihar (1978) extended the principle to other states. In much of northern India, by contrast, OBC communities remained thinly represented in the central bureaucracy, the universities, and the leadership of the Congress party, even as some of them gained economically from land reform and the Green Revolution (Chapter 94). That mismatch between rising rural prosperity or numerical weight and limited access to the institutions of the state is a central background to the political mobilization examined in Division II.

A final caution concerns historical language. Caste is sometimes described as an unchanged ancient system, but the categories used by the state in 1950 were shaped by colonial censuses and ethnographic classification (Chapter 73), by reform movements, and by the competitive politics of the twentieth century. The Mandal Commission would draw on these earlier classifications while attempting to give them a more systematic administrative basis. Students should keep in mind that the debate over reservation was not a debate over whether ancient hierarchy existed in the abstract but over which present-day disadvantages the state should measure and how.

Historical Vocabulary
Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, and “Dalit”

Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) are groups named in schedules under the Constitution (the Presidential orders of 1950 for the first lists), entitled to reserved legislative seats and public-employment quotas. Other Backward Classes (OBC) is a looser administrative category, drawn up by central and state commissions, for socially and educationally backward communities outside the SC and ST schedules. Dalit is a term of self-description adopted in the twentieth century, especially in Maharashtra, for communities who experienced untouchability; it overlaps heavily, but not perfectly, with the Scheduled Castes.

The distinction matters because Mandal politics concerned the OBCs, while Dalit politics had a different history and different institutional priorities. Treating them as one bloc obscures both the cooperation and the conflict between them.

Section 99.2

The Second Backward Classes Commission and the Mandal Report

The Janata government that took office after the Emergency (Chapter 93) promised to do what the Congress had not: appoint a new national commission on backward classes. On 20 December 1978 Prime Minister Morarji Desai announced the decision in Parliament, and the commission was constituted in January 1979 under B. P. (Bindeshwari Prasad) Mandal, a Yadav politician from Bihar and former chief minister of that state. The commission, formally the Second Backward Classes Commission, was asked to determine criteria for identifying socially and educationally backward classes, to recommend steps for their advancement, and to examine reservation in government posts. It began its work in a political environment shaped by the Janata Party’s own social base, which included Charan Singh’s peasant-proprietor constituency and socialist leaders who had long advocated preferential policies for backward castes.

The commission worked by a combination of field visits, questionnaires to state governments, a sample survey of villages in each state and union territory, and the study of earlier state commission reports and census materials. Because the decennial census had not enumerated castes other than SC and ST since 1931, it used the 1931 census as a base and extrapolated. On that basis the commission estimated that groups other than the SC, ST, and non-Hindu communities that it did not classify as backward accounted for about 52 percent of the population, spread over a list of several thousand castes and communities. This figure was immediately and persistently contested, and it remains a point of dispute: later national sample surveys produced lower estimates in some rounds, and the absence of a full caste enumeration until the twenty-first century kept the question politically alive.

To decide which communities were backward, the commission devised eleven indicators grouped under social, educational, and economic headings, weighted so that social criteria counted most heavily. Social indicators included how a community was regarded by others as socially backward, dependence on manual labour for livelihood, and the incidence of early marriage among women; educational indicators looked at children who had never attended school and rates of dropout; economic indicators examined average family assets, housing conditions, and indebtedness. The method was widely praised for attempting a quantitative approach and widely criticized for relying on weights and data of uneven quality. Importantly, the commission applied the criteria to non-Hindu communities as well, so that its backward-class list included some Muslim, Christian, and Sikh groups.

The report was submitted on 31 December 1980 to the new Congress government of Indira Gandhi. Its central recommendation was that 27 percent of posts in central government services and public-sector undertakings be reserved for OBCs, a figure chosen so that total reservations, including the existing 22.5 percent for Scheduled Castes and Tribes, would remain just under 50 percent, in keeping with a ceiling the Supreme Court had indicated in earlier decisions. The commission also recommended reservation in promotions, in admissions to centrally run educational institutions, and in scholarships; urged land reform and measures for vocational training; and called for periodic revision of the lists.

It is important to separate the report’s status from what followed. The report did not automatically become policy. It was tabled in Parliament only in 1982, after discussion within government, and for several years it was neither implemented nor formally rejected. Congress governments under Indira and Rajiv Gandhi regarded it as a complex and politically costly document. Meanwhile, the issue of backward-class quotas was not confined to the national stage: in Gujarat, violent anti-reservation agitations in 1981 and again in 1985 followed state decisions to expand quotas, and in these episodes the conflicts spread beyond the original question to communal violence. The Mandal Report thus lay on the table of a political system that already knew reservation could provoke intense conflict.

The report also had a lasting intellectual significance beyond its recommendations. It made the OBCs an object of national statistical argument, supplied a language of “backwardness” defined through measurable indicators, and encouraged regional leaders to claim that their communities constituted a majority of the population that remained underrepresented at the top. Those arguments would, from the late 1980s onward, be taken up by parties seeking to translate numbers into votes.

Quantitative Study

Mandal, Reservations, and Representation: Three Different Numbers

Three quantities are easily confused in discussions of the Mandal Report and should be kept distinct: an estimate of how many people belonged to the category, a proposed or legal reservation percentage, and the actual share of positions held by members of the category.

QuantityWhat it measuresFigure and caveat
Population estimateShare of the population the commission classified as OBCAbout 52% (1980), extrapolated from the 1931 census; contested, with later national sample surveys giving lower estimates
Reservation percentageShare of vacancies in direct recruitment set aside for eligible OBC candidates27% recommended in 1980; adopted in the 1990 order and implemented with exclusions from 1993
Actual representationShare of serving central government employees drawn from the categoryRose gradually over later decades; it cannot be inferred from the 27% figure, because reservation applies only to new vacancies, only to eligible candidates, and only if enough qualified applicants apply and are selected
Total ceilingCombined reservations (SC 15%, ST 7.5%, OBC 27%)49.5%, just below the 50% general limit later articulated in Indra Sawhney (1992)

A reservation percentage describes a rule about vacancies, not an outcome. Because posts were filled slowly, because the rule did not apply to existing employees, and because the “creamy layer” exclusion (Section 99.4) removed some candidates, the OBC share of the central bureaucracy grew only over a long period. The commission’s own report documented very low OBC representation in the higher grades of central service at the time; students should consult the report’s tables directly rather than rely on secondary summaries.

Section 99.3

V. P. Singh, the 1990 Reservation Decision, and National Protest

For nearly a decade the Mandal Report remained a political option rather than a policy. Its revival in 1990 resulted from a particular configuration of power. The 1989 general election (Chapter 98) denied the Congress a majority and produced a National Front government led by V. P. Singh, a former Congress minister who had broken with Rajiv Gandhi over allegations of corruption in defence purchases. The government depended on outside support from two ideologically opposed groups, the BJP and the Left Front, and it was internally divided. The Janata Dal, the principal constituent, included Devi Lal, Haryana’s influential peasant leader who served as deputy prime minister; Chandra Shekhar, a senior socialist with his own ambitions; and politicians such as Sharad Yadav, Lalu Prasad Yadav, and Mulayam Singh Yadav, who drew support from OBC communities and wanted the commission’s recommendations implemented.

The decision came in the context of a looming confrontation. In August 1990 Devi Lal planned a large rally in Delhi that was widely seen as a challenge to the prime minister, and relations between the two men had broken down. On 7 August V. P. Singh announced in Parliament that the government would implement the Mandal recommendation on reservation in central government employment, and on 13 August 1990 the Department of Personnel issued an Office Memorandum providing 27 percent reservation for socially and educationally backward classes in vacancies in civil posts and services under the Government of India. Historians have debated how far the decision reflected the prime minister’s long-standing conviction and how far it was a defensive political move; the evidence supports both a genuine commitment to the OBC agenda within the Janata Dal and a calculation about securing a social base against Devi Lal and the Congress.

The announcement unleashed protest on a national scale. In north India, particularly in Delhi, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, and Gujarat, students and young job-seekers from non-reserved communities demonstrated against the order, blocked roads, and clashed with police. On 19 September 1990 a Delhi University student, Rajiv Goswami, attempted to set himself on fire; he survived, but several other young people died in similar acts in the following weeks, with accounts of the numbers varying. Supporters of the decision organized rallies of their own, and in many places violence took the form of caste confrontation. The protests were shaped by high levels of educational unemployment, by the shrinking of public-sector hiring, and by anxieties about competition for scarce government jobs. These were not purely caste-based concerns, since many OBC and Dalit families faced the same scarcity, but the symbolic politics of the movement placed caste at the centre.

The arguments on each side drew on different understandings of fairness. Supporters pointed to the near-absence of OBC members in the senior ranks of the bureaucracy, the Constitution’s explicit enabling language, and the long history of exclusion; for them the order finally extended to the majority of the population the principle that the Constitution had applied to the SC and ST. Opponents argued that reservation based on caste would entrench the very categories it sought to dissolve, harm merit-based selection, reward better-off members of backward castes, and neglect the economic deprivation of other communities. The debate was not neatly divided by caste or class. Some upper-caste intellectuals supported reservations, and many OBC and Dalit commentators criticized the creation of a hierarchy of benefits within the backward categories.

Legal challenges followed at once, and on 1 October 1990 the Supreme Court stayed the implementation of the order, taking up writ petitions that would become Indra Sawhney v. Union of India. The political consequences were immediate. The announcement of reservations, followed within weeks by the BJP’s Rath Yatra (Section 99.10), turned the government’s social-justice initiative and the Hindu nationalist campaign into overlapping crises. Many contemporaries and later historians have described the BJP’s Ayodhya mobilization as in part a response to the new salience of caste divisions, an argument examined in Section 99.19. Others emphasize that the temple campaign had its own longer history. The evidence suggests the two processes were intertwined, but not in a simple cause-and-effect pattern.

V. P. Singh’s government fell on 7 November 1990, defeated in a confidence vote after the BJP withdrew support. A faction led by Chandra Shekhar split from the Janata Dal and formed a short-lived government with Congress backing. The dissolution of the National Front did not end Mandal politics. The reservation issue had been politically legitimized for the OBCs, and leaders who built their careers on it would soon control major state governments.

Section 99.4

Indra Sawhney, the Supreme Court, and the Institutionalization of OBC Reservations

The legal challenge to the 1990 order was heard by a bench of nine judges of the Supreme Court, a size reflecting the constitutional significance of the questions. In the interim, the Narasimha Rao government that took office in June 1991 issued a modified memorandum on 25 September 1991. It added a preference for poorer sections within the backward classes and a separate 10 percent reservation for economically weaker sections of groups not covered by any existing reservation. The government’s intention was to answer the charge that caste-based quotas ignored poverty, but the second provision proved legally vulnerable.

The judgment was delivered on 16 November 1992, three weeks before the destruction of the Babri Masjid. By a majority, the Court upheld the constitutional validity of reservation for OBCs in appointments to central government service, ruling that Article 16(4) permitted reservation based on backwardness that included caste as a relevant but not exclusive factor. It also set conditions. First, the “creamy layer,” the socially advanced members of backward classes, had to be excluded, so that benefits reached those who needed them. Second, the Court held that the total reservation should ordinarily not exceed 50 percent, a rule it described as a general limit subject to extraordinary circumstances. Third, it held that reservation under Article 16(4) applied to initial appointments and could not be extended to promotions. Fourth, it struck down the 10 percent economic-reservation provision of the 1991 memorandum as inconsistent with the Constitution as it then stood, and directed the government to establish a permanent expert body to examine inclusions and exclusions in the backward-class lists.

Parliament and the executive responded within months. The National Commission for Backward Classes was constituted under statute in 1993, and on 8 September 1993 the Department of Personnel issued a new memorandum implementing 27 percent reservation in direct recruitment, with the creamy-layer criteria based on parental position, income, and property that the government specified. In 1995 Parliament enacted the Seventy-Seventh Amendment, inserting Article 16(4A) to permit reservation in promotions for Scheduled Castes and Tribes, a direct constitutional response to the Court’s ruling on promotions. Litigation over the scope of this provision continued for years.

Several legal questions must be kept separate. Reservation in initial government employment, the subject of the 1990 order, was not the same as reservation in promotion, in university admissions, or in the private sector. OBC reservation in centrally funded higher education was introduced only in the following decade, after a constitutional amendment in 2005 and a Supreme Court decision in 2008, matters belonging to Chapter 100. The 50 percent limit was a judicial rule rather than a constitutional text, which is why it remained contested. And the creamy-layer concept, which applied to OBCs but not to SC and ST, became one of the most debated features of the system.

The significance of the judgment lay in more than the arithmetic. By upholding the principle and imposing conditions, the Court gave OBC reservation constitutional legitimacy while embedding in it a measure of judicial supervision. It moved the center of the debate from whether reservation was permissible to how it should be designed, and it made the judiciary a continuing arena for questions of social justice. For parties competing for votes, the judgment settled that a politics focused on OBC representation could not be dismissed as unconstitutional; it was now a legitimate basis for national policy.

Politically, the Mandal controversy had altered the incentives of every national party. For the Congress, it complicated an inherited coalition of upper castes, Dalits, and Muslims; for the BJP, it presented both a threat to a Hindu unity that cut across caste and an opportunity to recruit OBC leaders; for regional parties, it offered a language and an electorate. The following division traces how these incentives played out.

Historical Debate

Did the Mandal Decision Deepen or Dissolve Caste Identities?

The question: Did reservation for OBCs in the 1990s primarily broaden democratic inclusion, or did it entrench caste as the basis of public life?

Interpretation A: Scholars who emphasize inclusion argue that Mandal gave millions of previously marginalized people a stake in state institutions, brought new leaders into politics, and shifted power from a narrow upper-caste elite; caste was already a basis of public life, and reservation made inequality visible and addressable.
Interpretation B: Critics argue that it hardened caste categories, encouraged competition among communities for backward status, benefited a limited group of relatively advantaged OBC communities while neglecting the poorest, and diverted attention from economic reform and universal public services.

Evidence: Evidence includes the composition of central and state legislatures, studies of OBC representation in bureaucracy and education, the creamy-layer litigation, patterns of voting by caste, and the later demands by dominant agrarian communities for inclusion in backward lists.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians interpret the later reconfiguration of party politics: as a democratizing broadening of access or as a mobilization that increased communal competition. The evidence supports both more strongly than either alone, because outcomes varied by state and community.

Section 99.5

OBC, Dalit, and Regional Political Mobilization

The political consequences of Mandal were most dramatic in the Hindi-speaking states of north India, above all Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, which together sent about one-fifth of the members of the Lok Sabha. In these states a generation of leaders from backward-caste backgrounds had risen through the socialist movement, the Bharatiya Kranti Dal and Lok Dal of Charan Singh, and the Janata Party. Their numbers in legislatures had grown since the 1970s, and the Mandal controversy gave them a unifying program: representation for the backward majority in the institutions of the state.

Two figures symbolized the change. Lalu Prasad Yadav, who became chief minister of Bihar in March 1990 at the head of a Janata Dal government, built his support on an alliance of Yadavs and Muslims, often called the “M-Y” combination, supplemented by other backward and Dalit communities. His government’s rhetoric emphasized dignity and voice for the poor and for the previously voiceless, and it combined genuine symbolic change with poor administration and growing allegations of corruption; he would be implicated in the fodder-scam cases in the mid-1990s. Mulayam Singh Yadav, chief minister of Uttar Pradesh in 1989–91, drew on a similar base among Yadavs and Muslims, and in October 1992 founded the Samajwadi Party. In 1994 Nitish Kumar and George Fernandes formed the Samata Party in Bihar, a reminder that backward-caste politics did not have a single organizational form. The Rashtriya Janata Dal was established by Lalu Prasad in July 1997 after he broke from the Janata Dal.

Dalit politics followed a different path. B. R. Ambedkar’s Scheduled Castes Federation and later Republican Party (Chapter 80) had given Dalit politics a national symbol, but by the 1970s the Republican Party had fractured, and many Dalit voters supported the Congress. Kanshi Ram, a former government scientist from Punjab, built a new organization through the All-India Backward and Minority Communities Employees Federation (BAMCEF, 1978), the Dalit Shoshit Samaj Sangharsh Samiti (1981), and finally the Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP), founded on 14 April 1984, Ambedkar’s birthday. The word bahujan, “the majority,” expressed an aspiration to unite SCs, STs, OBCs, and religious minorities against upper-caste domination; in practice the BSP’s base was initially concentrated among Dalits, particularly the Jatavs of Uttar Pradesh. Kanshi Ram’s protégée Mayawati became chief minister of Uttar Pradesh in June 1995, the first Dalit woman to head a state government, with BJP outside support; her government was short-lived, and she returned later in the decade.

These movements did not form a single front. OBC and Dalit communities often competed over land, wages, and local power, and in many villages, the principal agents of caste violence against Dalits were from middle-ranking landowning castes that were also classified as OBC. Alliances were therefore contingent. The most famous was the alliance between the Samajwadi Party and the BSP, concluded after the demolition of the Babri Masjid, which won the Uttar Pradesh assembly election of November–December 1993 against the BJP, but it collapsed in June 1995 amid acrimony. Such alliances showed both the potential and the fragility of a coalition of the backward and the oppressed.

The Janata Dal itself disintegrated in the decade after 1989, splitting into regional parties led by individual politicians. The reasons were organizational rivalry, personal ambition, differing regional bases, and the absence of a strong national organization, but also the very success of the social-justice program: once a party’s leaders had secured state governments, they preferred to retain autonomy rather than subordinate their constituencies to a national leadership. The upshot was a multiplication of parties each representing a particular caste coalition in a specific state, a pattern that Division V examines.

Mobilization had effects beyond party formation. Studies of voting behaviour by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies and others recorded that participation among lower-caste and poorer voters rose during the 1990s while the upper-caste share of turnout stagnated, a trend the political scientist Yogendra Yadav characterized as a “second democratic upsurge.” Whether this meant that the poor gained substantively in material terms is a different question. Representation in legislatures and the bureaucracy increased, but land inequality, low rural wages, and weak public services changed more slowly.

Everyday Life and Social History

Political Participation in Rural North India: What the Evidence Shows

Documented evidence of ordinary political life in this period comes from survey data, scholarly ethnographies, and organizational records rather than from invented testimony. National and state election surveys found that the lower-caste and Muslim electorates in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar voted in large and increasingly distinct blocs in the 1990s, and that turnout among poorer voters equalled or exceeded that of the better-off in many states. Kanshi Ram’s organization built its early support through a deliberate method of face-to-face organizing, including cycle marches that converged on Delhi in the 1980s, a strategy that relied on low-cost mobilization of government employees from Dalit backgrounds who contributed funds.

Scholars also record the significance of symbolic change at the local level: the election of a chief minister from a backward caste altered who could expect a hearing from district officials and the police, even where material conditions changed little. At the same time, studies of rural Bihar and Uttar Pradesh document violent conflicts between landowning castes and landless labourers, including private caste armies such as the Ranvir Sena in Bihar in the mid-1990s. These accounts show that political representation and everyday power did not move at the same pace.

Section 99.6

Hindu Nationalism: Organizations, Ideology, and Historical Development

Hindu nationalism must be distinguished from Hinduism as a religious tradition. The religious traditions grouped under the term Hindu include many sects, philosophies, devotional practices, and regional customs (Chapters 28 and 48), with no single organization and no centralized authority. Hindutva, by contrast, is a political ideology that claims to define India as a nation of Hindus. It was given its classic formulation by Vinayak Damodar Savarkar in his 1923 pamphlet Essentials of Hindutva, which defined a Hindu as a person who regards the land between the Indus and the seas as both fatherland and holy land. The definition made cultural belonging, shared history, and territory the basis of nationhood and treated Muslims and Christians, whose holy lands lay elsewhere, as ambiguous members of the nation. Many Hindus, including many who were devout, rejected this definition then and later; Gandhi, himself a Hindu, opposed it, and Gandhi’s assassin, Nathuram Godse, had links to Hindu nationalist circles (Chapter 87).

The institutional landscape of Hindu nationalism developed in several organizational forms. The Hindu Mahasabha, founded in 1915 and led by Savarkar in the late 1930s, was a political party that never became a mass electoral force. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS), founded in Nagpur in 1925 by K. B. Hedgewar, was a volunteer organization that focused on character formation, discipline, and the creation of a network of local branches (shakhas) rather than electoral politics. Under M. S. Golwalkar, its leader from 1940 to 1973, the RSS articulated a cultural nationalism that regarded minorities as having to accept Hindu cultural primacy. The RSS was banned after Gandhi’s assassination in 1948, though the ban was lifted in 1949 on conditions, and was banned again during the Emergency.

In 1951 Syama Prasad Mookerjee, a former Hindu Mahasabha leader, founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh with the support of RSS workers, creating a party that contested elections on a platform emphasizing national unity, Hindi, a uniform civil code, and opposition to concessions to Pakistan. The Jana Sangh’s electoral base lay mainly in the Hindi-speaking states and among urban traders and professionals; it never won more than 35 seats in the Lok Sabha (in 1967). In 1964 RSS leaders and Hindu religious figures founded the Vishva Hindu Parishad (VHP), a body intended to unite Hindu sects and organize Hindu religious activity, including among Hindus overseas.

These organizations are often grouped as the “Sangh Parivar,” the family of organizations associated with the RSS, but the term can obscure both their differences and the contested relations among them. A political party seeks votes and must negotiate with other parties and electoral constraints. A volunteer organization such as the RSS aims at long-term social and ideological formation and typically avoids direct electoral competition. The VHP mobilizes religious leaders and lay supporters for campaigns that are religious in language but political in consequence. The Bajrang Dal, a youth organization founded in 1984 in Uttar Pradesh, specialized in street mobilization. Their personnel overlapped, but their goals, methods, and legal liabilities did not coincide.

The Emergency proved decisive for the institutional position of Hindu nationalism. RSS cadres were jailed in large numbers alongside other opposition activists, and the Jana Sangh merged into the Janata Party in 1977. This brought the movement into a governing coalition for the first time and gave its leaders, such as Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L. K. Advani, who held cabinet portfolios, a wider legitimacy. The relationship was fragile, as Janata partners questioned whether former Jana Sangh members could continue as members of the RSS, an issue that precipitated the coalition’s end.

Hindu nationalist mobilization in the early 1980s also drew on episodes outside north India. The conversion of Dalit villagers to Islam at Meenakshipuram in Tamil Nadu in 1981 attracted national attention and was used by the VHP to argue that Hindu society was endangered. In 1983 the VHP organized the Ekatmata Yatra, a set of processions across the country carrying Ganga water and symbols of unity. These events show a movement experimenting with religious symbols as instruments of mass communication.

It is essential not to equate all Hindu participation with Hindu nationalist politics. Millions of Hindus took part in festivals, pilgrimages, and temple worship without endorsing any political organization, and voters who supported the BJP did so for diverse reasons. Equally, it is a mistake to treat the movement as merely a reaction to Muslim assertiveness or to government policy; its organizational and ideological roots predate independence and reflect internal debates about nation, culture, and the place of caste in Hindu society, including the movement’s efforts to bridge the gap between upper and lower castes.

Section 99.7

The Formation of the BJP and Its Early Political Development, 1980–1989

The BJP was born from the collapse of the Janata experiment. After the Janata Party’s governments fell in 1979–1980 and Indira Gandhi returned to power in the January 1980 election, the question of dual membership resurfaced: in April 1980 the party’s leadership decided that members could not belong to the RSS as well. Former Jana Sangh members left, and on 6 April 1980 in Delhi they founded the Bharatiya Janata Party under Vajpayee as its first president. The name and early program were designed to distance the new party from its Jana Sangh image and to retain the broader Janata heritage. The party adopted a commitment to “Gandhian socialism” and a notion of “positive secularism,” language that reflected both an effort to build a larger coalition and the continuing influence of Vajpayee’s moderate image.

The strategy failed at the polls. In the December 1984 general election, held after Indira Gandhi’s assassination (Chapter 94) and dominated by a sympathy wave for Rajiv Gandhi, the BJP won two seats in the Lok Sabha with 7.74 percent of the vote; Vajpayee himself lost his Gwalior seat. The Congress won more than 400 seats. The results led the BJP’s leaders to rethink their strategy. Advani replaced Vajpayee as president in 1986 and moved the party toward a more assertive Hindu nationalist posture and closer coordination with the RSS and the VHP.

This reorientation was not only ideological. The BJP’s leaders recognized that their organization needed to build local networks in the states where the Congress was weak. They also drew lessons from the Janata Party experience: that alliances with other parties could produce electoral gains, and that the party’s core supporters would remain loyal only if the party defined a distinct identity. In the 1989 election the BJP entered seat-adjustment arrangements with the Janata Dal and other opposition parties, which reduced the number of multi-cornered contests, and it endorsed the VHP’s Ayodhya campaign in a resolution adopted at its Palampur session in June 1989.

The 1989 results show the effect: the BJP won 85 seats with 11.36 percent of the vote, contesting a little over 220 seats. It did best in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Bihar, and it began to build a base in Maharashtra in alliance with the Shiv Sena, a regional party with its own Hindu nationalist and Marathi-regional politics. Support came disproportionately from urban middle classes, traders, and upper castes in these regions, but the party also began to attract OBC voters in places where its local leaders came from backward communities.

The BJP’s rise cannot be attributed to religious mobilization alone. Dissatisfaction with Congress was widespread by 1989, driven by the Bofors controversy, inflation, and the perceived failure of the government to deal with regional conflicts in Punjab, Assam, and Sri Lanka (Chapters 94 and 96). Local factors mattered as well: in Uttar Pradesh the party built on the dense network of RSS shakhas and on the collapse of Congress organization; in Gujarat on the memory of the anti-reservation agitations; in Maharashtra on the Shiv Sena alliance. In Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh the party’s success was limited, and in the south and east it remained marginal.

Historians therefore stress that the party’s growth was a product of the interaction between ideological mobilization, organizational work, opportunism in coalition-making, and the weakness of its competitors. The BJP’s early history shows a party testing several political identities, a moderate and Gandhian one, a coalition-oriented one, and an assertively Hindu one, and settling on a combination in which the third was prominent but not exclusive.

Section 99.8

Religion, Secularism, and Political Competition in the 1980s

The 1980s also saw changes in how the Congress, the dominant party, handled questions of religion and personal law. Indira Gandhi’s Congress had in the early 1980s increasingly used religious and regional appeals, including to Hindu sentiment in Punjab and Jammu, and Rajiv Gandhi inherited a political landscape in which religion had acquired new electoral potency. Two decisions of his government shaped the context in which the Ayodhya movement expanded.

The first concerned Shah Bano Begum, a 62-year-old Muslim woman from Indore whose husband had divorced her after decades of marriage and who sued for maintenance under Section 125 of the Criminal Procedure Code, a secular provision applicable to all citizens. On 23 April 1985 the Supreme Court, in Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum, ruled that the provision applied to Muslim women and observed that the country had no uniform civil code, as the Constitution’s directive principles envisaged. The decision provoked protest from Muslim religious organizations, including the All India Muslim Personal Law Board, which argued that it interfered with personal law derived from religious sources. Rajiv Gandhi’s government first defended the judgment, with the minister Arif Mohammad Khan speaking in its favor, then yielded: Parliament passed the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act in May 1986, which limited a divorced woman’s claim on her former husband to the period of iddat and placed responsibility for longer support on her relatives or on Waqf boards. Arif Mohammad Khan resigned from the government.

The second decision concerned Ayodhya. On 1 February 1986 a district judge in Faizabad ordered that the locks on the gates of the disputed structure be opened, allowing Hindu worshippers access to the idols that had been installed in 1949 (Section 99.9). The order followed an application by a local lawyer, and the Uttar Pradesh government did not oppose it. Many observers saw the sequence of the 1986 Act and the opening of the locks as a political bargain: after conceding to Muslim religious leaders, the government compensated Hindus. Historians continue to debate whether it was a deliberate plan by the Prime Minister’s office or a series of independent decisions; the evidence for centralized coordination remains incomplete, but the political effect was to give both Hindu nationalist and Muslim organizations grievances against the Congress.

These episodes shaped different understandings of secularism. In the Indian constitutional tradition, secularism has not meant the exclusion of religion from public life, as in the French model, but the state’s equal distance from, or equal respect for, all religions, together with protection for minorities and state intervention in religious institutions when needed for social reform. The 1950 Constitution banned untouchability, regulated temples, and allowed for personal laws based on religion; the Forty-second Amendment in 1976 added the word “secular” to the Preamble. Within this framework, different actors drew different lessons from Shah Bano. Muslim leaders defended minority rights and community control over personal law; liberal and feminist critics argued that gender equality should override such claims; Hindu nationalists charged the Congress with “appeasement” of minorities and demanded a uniform civil code and “equal treatment”; defenders of the Congress argued that the party was protecting a vulnerable minority from majority imposition.

These interpretations differed not only in values but in empirical premises: about whether Muslim women themselves wanted reform, about whether the Act benefited Muslim communities, and about whether the Congress was acting from principle or electoral calculation. Women’s organizations from several religious backgrounds opposed the 1986 Act, while many Muslim organizations supported it. In 2001, in Danial Latifi v. Union of India, the Supreme Court interpreted the Act in a manner that gave divorced Muslim women reasonable provision beyond the iddat period, narrowing the practical conflict with Shah Bano.

Section 99.9

Ayodhya, the Babri Masjid, and the Historical Dispute

Ayodhya, a town on the Ghaghara (Sarayu) river in eastern Uttar Pradesh, is a pilgrimage centre for several traditions. For many Hindus it is the city of Rama, the hero of the Ramayana (Chapter 56 discusses the epic’s later role in Mughal and regional culture), and the movement examined in this chapter focused on a particular site that devotees identify as Rama’s birthplace, Ram Janmabhoomi. Jain and Buddhist traditions also associate Ayodhya with sacred figures, and the town had been an administrative and pilgrimage centre under the Awadh nawabs in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (Chapter 60). The dispute concerned a mosque in the town, the Babri Masjid, that the historical tradition attributes to Mir Baqi, an officer of the first Mughal emperor Babur, in the early 1520s–1530s.

The historical questions fall into distinct categories, and the sources support different levels of confidence. That a mosque stood at the site by the eighteenth century is not in doubt. That Hindu devotees worshipped at and around the site, including at an outer platform known as the Ram Chabutra, is documented in nineteenth-century records, and travellers’ accounts from the late eighteenth century suggest that Hindu veneration of the area was established. Whether a temple stood on the exact site before the mosque, and whether it was demolished to build it, is a historical and archaeological question on which scholars have disagreed; the early Mughal sources do not clearly describe such an act, and the narratives of demolition appear in later texts whose reliability is contested. This chapter treats the question as unresolved in the documentary record and does not rest its narrative on either answer.

The colonial period transformed a local contest into a legal one. Disputes between Hindu and Muslim groups over the site produced violence in 1855, after which the Faizabad administration erected a railing dividing the inner courtyard, where Muslims worshipped, from the outer courtyard, where Hindus worshipped. In 1885 the mahant of a Hindu monastic order, Raghubar Das, filed a suit to build a temple on the Chabutra; the courts rejected the request, preserving the status quo. This arrangement lasted, with incidents, until independence.

The decisive event occurred on the night of 22–23 December 1949. A group of Hindu activists placed idols of Ram inside the mosque, after which a crowd gathered. Jawaharlal Nehru and the Uttar Pradesh chief minister, Govind Ballabh Pant, pressed local officials to remove the idols, but the Faizabad district magistrate, K. K. K. Nayar, declined to do so, citing the risk of violence. Instead the authorities declared the premises disputed property, attached them under Section 145 of the Criminal Procedure Code, locked the inner gates, and allowed priests to continue puja within. Civil suits followed from 1950 onward: Hindu individuals and, from 1959, the Nirmohi Akhara claimed rights of worship or management; in 1961 the Sunni Central Waqf Board sued for a declaration that the property was a mosque. All were consolidated in the Allahabad High Court.

For more than three decades the dispute lay mostly dormant in the courts. It was reactivated in 1984–1986 by the VHP campaign described in Section 99.10 and by the order of 1 February 1986, mentioned in Section 99.8, directing the opening of the locks. Muslim organizations responded by forming the Babri Masjid Action Committee and the Babri Masjid Movement Coordination Committee, which demanded protection of the mosque and denied the claim that a temple had been demolished. The dispute thus acquired two organized sides, each with leaders and constituencies, and ordinary residents of Ayodhya, among them a mixed population of Hindu priests and Muslim artisans, increasingly found themselves represented by outsiders.

Archaeological claims entered the dispute alongside political ones. Excavations by B. B. Lal at Ayodhya in the 1970s and later reinterpretation of his finds, including claims about pillar bases, were cited by supporters of the temple claim, while other archaeologists and historians disputed the interpretation. In 2003 the Allahabad High Court directed the Archaeological Survey of India to conduct a further excavation; its report said that a structure of the pre-mosque period had existed beneath the mosque, with features it described as consistent with a temple of the north Indian type, a conclusion contested by some archaeologists. The Supreme Court in 2019 treated the ASI’s findings as evidence of an underlying non-Islamic structure but noted that it did not establish that a temple had been demolished to build the mosque.

Historians must therefore distinguish four things: religious belief that this was Rama’s birthplace, which is a matter of faith rather than historical proof; competing claims about property and rights of worship, which are legal; archaeological interpretation of what lay beneath the site; and political assertion. A claim of faith may be sincere and historically significant without functioning as evidence of what happened in 1528, and a legal ruling, however authoritative for resolving title, does not settle historians’ questions about the past.

Section 99.10

The Ram Janmabhoomi Campaign, 1984–1990

The modern mass campaign began with the VHP’s first Dharma Sansad (religious parliament) in Delhi in April 1984, which called for the “liberation” of Hindu sites at Ayodhya, Mathura, and Varanasi. A Ram Janmabhoomi Mukti Yajna Samiti was formed, and in October 1984 a procession set out from Sitamarhi in Bihar toward Ayodhya; it was disrupted by the assassination of Indira Gandhi and the national mourning that followed. After the opening of the locks in 1986, the campaign gained momentum, and in 1987–1988 the television serialization of the Ramayana, broadcast on the state-owned Doordarshan network, became a nationwide phenomenon that some scholars see as creating a shared visual and devotional vocabulary on which the movement drew. The causal link is debated, because the serial’s viewers had varied responses and the epic’s significance was not political in itself.

The VHP’s methods were organizational. In 1989 it launched the Ram shila pujan program, in which consecrated bricks were carried in processions from villages across India and sent to Ayodhya, a technique that linked local religious life to the central campaign and generated widespread participation and funds, including from overseas Hindu communities. On 9 November 1989, with permission from the Congress governments at the Centre and in Uttar Pradesh, a foundation-stone ceremony was held at a site adjacent to the disputed structure. The first brick was laid by Kameshwar Chaupal, a Dalit activist from Bihar, a choice widely read as an effort to demonstrate Hindu unity across caste, in the year before Mandal.

The roles of the principal organizations should be distinguished. The VHP and its associated saints (sadhus) provided religious authority and organization; the RSS supplied volunteers and local infrastructure; the Bajrang Dal furnished crowds for confrontations; the BJP provided electoral channels and political leadership, though it did not formally lead the campaign until it adopted it at Palampur in 1989. Within this coalition there were tensions, as VHP leaders such as Ashok Singhal often favoured immediate action, while the BJP had to consider electoral and governing implications.

The Rath Yatra of 1990 was the campaign’s most visible political event. L. K. Advani, then BJP president, began a journey from the Somnath temple in Gujarat on 25 September 1990, travelling in a vehicle styled as a chariot, intending to reach Ayodhya on 30 October when VHP volunteers (kar sevaks) planned a ceremony. The route crossed several states, and reports of its planned length run to around ten thousand kilometres; details of the stops differ in the sources. At each stop Advani made speeches that presented the temple as a symbol of national self-respect and criticized what he called pseudo-secularism. Local organizers, many drawn from the RSS and the Bajrang Dal, made the procession a major mobilizing event. The yatra coincided with the Mandal protests, and observers have often connected the two, with the BJP seeking to unify Hindus across caste.

The yatra also left a trail of communal tension. Riots broke out in several cities along and near the route, including in Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh, and in the weeks following the procession more than two hundred people were reported killed in communal violence across the country, according to press and later scholarly accounts, although totals vary. On 23 October 1990 Lalu Prasad Yadav’s government in Bihar arrested Advani at Samastipur, a move that reflected both the state government’s concern about public order and Lalu Prasad’s political interest in presenting himself as a defender of secularism and of Muslim voters. The BJP withdrew support from the V. P. Singh government.

Meanwhile, tens of thousands of kar sevaks converged on Ayodhya. Mulayam Singh Yadav’s government in Uttar Pradesh deployed security forces to protect the mosque, and on 30 October 1990 some kar sevaks climbed on the structure and hoisted saffron flags. Police fired on crowds on 30 October and again on 2 November; counts vary by reporting source: a contemporaneous account relayed to the United Nations recorded sixteen deaths including a security-force member, while later press accounts frequently give seventeen protesters killed; movement organizations claimed higher numbers. The deaths of the kar sevaks became a martyrdom narrative for the movement, while for Muslims and secular critics, the episode demonstrated that the government had resisted an assault on a place of worship.

The campaign changed party relations. The Janata Dal government lost a confidence vote on 7 November 1990. The BJP gained a dramatic increase in visibility, and its opponents divided between those who sought to isolate it and those who judged it a potential partner. The campaign’s documentary record is substantial: Parliament’s debates of November 1990, state government reports, and the later Liberhan Commission, which reconstructed the chronology of the movement in some detail.

Section 99.11

Ayodhya, the BJP Government in Uttar Pradesh, and the Crisis of 1991–1992

The Mandal and Mandir crises transformed the electoral scene. In the elections of May–June 1991, held after Rajiv Gandhi’s assassination on 21 May (Chapter 98), the BJP won 120 seats in the Lok Sabha and, in simultaneous assembly elections in Uttar Pradesh, won 221 of 425 seats, forming its first government in the country’s most populous state. Kalyan Singh, a backward-caste (Lodh) politician, was sworn in as chief minister on 24 June 1991. His selection reflected the party’s effort to tie OBC leaders to Hindu nationalism. The BJP also formed governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh in the early 1990s, so that the temple movement now had allies in state office.

The new government took actions that linked the state to the campaign. In October 1991 the Uttar Pradesh government acquired about 2.77 acres of land around the disputed structure, on the stated ground of developing tourism facilities at Ayodhya; Muslim organizations and the opposition challenged the acquisition in court. The Allahabad High Court and the Supreme Court issued orders restricting permanent construction on the acquired land, while the state government gave assurances that the disputed structure would be protected. The VHP and its allies held that the movement’s goal required construction of a temple, and religious leaders announced successive dates for symbolic or actual kar seva, ceremonial service at the site.

The central government under P. V. Narasimha Rao (Chapter 98), leading a minority Congress government, faced an uncomfortable situation. It tried negotiation. In 1992 it held talks between the VHP and the Babri Masjid Action Committee and proposed that the dispute be referred to the courts or settled by evidence, a process that failed, as neither side agreed to accept an adverse determination. In July 1992 VHP workers began constructing a concrete platform near the structure, and the work was halted after intervention. By autumn, the VHP announced that kar seva would resume on 6 December 1992, and on 23 November the National Integration Council, which included party and community leaders, met and received assurances from Kalyan Singh and others.

The Supreme Court was drawn into the process. On 28 November 1992 it permitted symbolic kar seva without construction on the acquired land, relying on the Uttar Pradesh government’s undertaking to preserve the disputed structure, and appointed a judicial officer to observe the site. These assurances, it later emerged, were not honoured. The Court’s later judgments stated that the state government’s promises had been breached and that the assurance to the Court was of consequence. The question of how far courts could prevent a mass movement through undertakings from a state government’s leader would be debated afterwards.

Responsibility for maintaining order was divided. Law and order is a state subject under the Indian Constitution, so the Uttar Pradesh police and administration, led by a government sympathetic to the movement, were the primary authority. The Union government could deploy central paramilitary forces and, under Article 356, dismiss the state government, but it hesitated to impose President’s Rule in anticipation, on the ground that there was no evidence of a failure of constitutional machinery and fearing the political consequences. Central forces were positioned in the vicinity of Ayodhya, but were reportedly not used without the state government’s consent. Later inquiries, notably the Liberhan Commission, examined these decisions, and critics of the Rao government have argued that it should have acted earlier.

A responsible narrative must distinguish different kinds of evidence. Established administrative decisions include the acquisition of land, the deployment of forces, and the assurances given to courts. Public statements by leaders of the BJP, VHP, and RSS before 6 December contained both reassurances and signs of impatience. Later investigators, particularly the Liberhan Commission, drew conclusions about prior planning that critics of the commission dispute; and claims about the private intentions of individuals depend on testimony that is contested. The evidence is strongest for the fact that a large, organized mobilization was permitted to assemble at the site, that the state’s security arrangements were not sufficient to prevent what followed, and that leaders of the movement were present on 6 December.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Crisis of December 1992 Occur?

Long-term causes

A century of competing claims to the site; the 1949 installation of idols and the legal freeze; the growth of Hindu nationalist organizations; Congress governments’ handling of religion in the 1980s.

Short-term causes

The VHP–BJP campaign of 1989–1990; the 1991 BJP victory in Uttar Pradesh; the acquisition of land; the failure of negotiations; the announcement of kar seva for 6 December.

Event or transformation

The assembly at Ayodhya of a very large crowd (estimates of tens or hundreds of thousands vary) and the demolition of the Babri Masjid on 6 December 1992.

Immediate effects

Resignation of the Uttar Pradesh government; President’s Rule; riots across India; bans on several organizations; criminal investigations.

Long-term consequences

Reconfiguration of party competition; constitutional litigation; lasting distrust between communities; the civil land dispute that continued until 2019.

Historical complexity: The demolition was not inevitable. It resulted from decisions by political leaders, the incapacity or unwillingness of state authorities to enforce assurances, a central government’s hesitation, and the activism of organized groups, within a longer history of contest over the site.

Section 99.12

December 6, 1992: The Demolition of the Babri Masjid

On the morning of 6 December 1992, a Sunday, a large crowd of kar sevaks gathered at Ayodhya. Estimates of the number range widely, from about 150,000 to several hundred thousand, and no definitive figure exists. A platform near the structure held leaders of the BJP, VHP, and RSS, including L. K. Advani, Murli Manohar Joshi, Uma Bharti, and Ashok Singhal, who addressed the assembly. According to reporting and testimony later examined by the Liberhan Commission, the planned activity was a symbolic ceremony, but around midday a group of kar sevaks broke through police lines, climbed the structure, and began to attack it with tools. Efforts by some leaders to restrain the crowd were ineffective or half-hearted according to different accounts, and the police who were present did not stop the demolition.

By late afternoon, the three domes of the mosque had fallen, and by evening most of the structure was rubble. Journalists covering the event, including from foreign and Indian press, were attacked, and several cameras were destroyed. During the night kar sevaks built a makeshift temple on the site. Kalyan Singh resigned as chief minister that evening, stating that he took responsibility but had not ordered firing on kar sevaks. The Narasimha Rao government dismissed the Uttar Pradesh government and imposed President’s Rule, and on 15 December it dismissed the BJP governments of Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh, which led to the constitutional litigation discussed in Section 99.14. The central government also banned several organizations, including the RSS, the VHP, and the Bajrang Dal, and Muslim groups such as the Jamaat-e-Islami Hind; the bans were lifted or struck down by tribunals within a short time.

Prime Minister Rao denounced the destruction and promised to rebuild the mosque, a promise that was not fulfilled; instead, in January 1993, Parliament passed an act to acquire about 67 acres around the site, and the President referred to the Supreme Court a question about the legality of such acquisition. In its 1994 decision in M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India, the Court upheld the acquisition and declined to answer the reference.

The constitutional meaning of the destruction was immediate. A mosque, a protected place of worship under litigation, had been destroyed in the presence of a state government that had undertaken to protect it. This violated the rule of law and raised questions about state responsibility, and it prompted Indian secular opinion to see it as an attack on the Constitution. For supporters of the movement, the destruction was an act of popular assertion that corrected an old injury; some leaders expressed regret on the day itself, and others celebrated. Historians should record both reactions while noting that no claim of religious grievance transforms an unlawful act into a lawful one.

The legal record is complex and should not be confused. Two kinds of proceedings must be distinguished. The first, criminal, concerned who was responsible for the demolition. Two first information reports were registered on 6 December, one against unnamed kar sevaks and one against eight leaders for inciting the crowd; the cases were taken over by the Central Bureau of Investigation, consolidated, and tried after many years. In September 2020 a special CBI court in Lucknow acquitted all thirty-two surviving accused, finding that the evidence did not establish a prior conspiracy and that anti-social elements had caused the demolition. The ruling was criticized by many commentators and defended by others. The second kind, civil, concerned title to the land. The Allahabad High Court in 2010 divided the disputed land in three parts, and the Supreme Court in November 2019 gave the site to a trust for the construction of a temple and directed that an alternative five acres be allotted to the Sunni Central Waqf Board.

In that 2019 judgment, the Supreme Court characterized the placing of idols in 1949 and the destruction of the mosque in 1992 as violations of law, while basing its decision on the weighing of evidence of possession and worship. This retrospective legal finding helps clarify the record, but the final disposition of the land belongs to the later history of the dispute and is noted here only as an epilogue; the later legal history is therefore noted here as a retrospective epilogue and not as a continuation of this chapter’s narrative.

A separate parallel inquiry was the Liberhan Commission, appointed on 16 December 1992 under Justice M. S. Liberhan and expected to report within three months. It took seventeen years and submitted its report in June 2009. It held a number of leaders of the Sangh Parivar responsible, and also criticized officials and Kalyan Singh. It remains an important, though politically contested, source.

Section 99.13

Communal Violence, Social Consequences, and the Mumbai Riots of 1992–1993

The demolition of the mosque was followed within hours by violence in many parts of India and in neighbouring countries. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, crowds attacked Hindu temples and properties in reprisal, a reminder that communal violence in the subcontinent crossed borders (Chapters 101 and 102). Inside India, riots occurred in Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Assam, Delhi, Hyderabad, Kolkata, and elsewhere in December 1992, with the heaviest and best documented violence in Mumbai (then usually called Bombay). Precise national totals are uncertain; press and official estimates run to well over a thousand deaths across the country in the weeks after 6 December, and the numbers depend on the cities and dates included.

Mumbai experienced two waves. The first, in the first half of December 1992, began with protests by Muslims against the demolition and with police firing; the second, in January 1993, was far more severe. Violence intensified after attacks on Hindu mathadi workers and other incidents in the first days of January; the arson attack on the Bane family at Radhabai (Gandhi) Chawl in Jogeshwari occurred in the early hours of 8 January, after the new wave was already under way, and it involved organized attacks on Muslim neighbourhoods and retaliation. The Justice B. N. Srikrishna Commission, appointed by the Maharashtra government on 25 January 1993 after public pressure, investigated the riots and delivered its report in 1998. It recorded approximately nine hundred deaths during the period, roughly 575 Muslims and 275 Hindus, with remaining victims of other or unknown identity, as well as extensive displacement, destruction of homes and shops, and fear that led many Muslims to leave mixed neighbourhoods and Hindu residents to move out of others.

The Commission’s findings on organization are important evidence. It found that the violence of January 1993 had been, in significant part, systematic, with attackers using voters’ lists to identify homes and with members and leaders of the Shiv Sena, a Marathi-Hindu regional party led by Bal Thackeray, involved in organizing attacks through its local branches (shakha pramukhs), and it named Thackeray as having led the Sena in the manner of a general commanding a campaign. It also found that Muslim groups had committed violence and that some in the minority had acted with extremist or criminal intent. It criticized the police for bias, indiscipline, and, in some units, active participation, and it recommended reforms of police recruitment and training. The Maharashtra government accepted the report in a modified form, and few prosecutions of prominent figures followed, a record that critics have cited as evidence of impunity.

The riots must be separated from the series of bomb explosions that struck Mumbai on 12 March 1993, killing about 257 people and injuring many more. Investigators attributed the bombings to a conspiracy involving organized-crime figures based in Mumbai and Dubai, with support from Pakistani intelligence, and in later trials the courts convicted numerous accused and sentenced several to death. Some commentators and the Srikrishna Commission’s narrative treated the bombings as in part a response to the riots, while others stressed their separate criminal and international dimensions. The two sets of events, riots in which most victims were Muslims and bombings in which most victims were from all communities, produced distinct kinds of grievance, memory, and policy response.

In the long run, the violence changed the social geography of Mumbai and other cities. Studies of segregation after 1993 show increased residential concentration by religious community, and in several cities the creation of Muslim neighbourhoods, sometimes called ghettoes, reflected insecurity as well as limited access to housing elsewhere. The state’s response was uneven: compensation to victims was delayed, and the demand for justice by survivors’ organizations continued for decades. The violence also reinforced political alliances, strengthening the Shiv Sena and its BJP partnership in Maharashtra, which took power in the 1995 state election.

Historians should be careful in assigning responsibility. The commissions and courts identify specific individuals, organizations, and failures of institutions; they do not support the idea that entire religious communities were collectively responsible. Most residents of affected neighbourhoods experienced the violence as victims, witnesses, or in some cases as protectors of neighbours of another faith, and surviving testimonies include many accounts of rescue across community lines.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through the Riots: Documented Experiences

The Srikrishna Commission took evidence from hundreds of witnesses, and its report, together with studies by civil-liberties groups and scholars, preserves the experiences of ordinary residents. These sources record that many families in mixed lanes and chawls left their homes in the days after attacks began; that markets and public transport stopped; that relief camps and mosques and temples sheltered displaced persons; and that in some localities, local committees of residents, including mohalla peace committees, were formed to prevent violence. The report also documents that police patrols and responses varied sharply by locality and by the officers in charge.

This material comes from official inquiry and from organized research, and it does not allow the reconstruction of private thoughts or dialogue. It does show that everyday life after the riots involved practical decisions about housing, work, and children’s schooling that had lasting effects on neighbourhood composition, and that survivors’ memories of the period remain disputed in public life.

Section 99.14

Secularism, Constitutional Law, and the State’s Response

The Ayodhya crisis became a test of the Constitution in two respects: the legal status of places of worship, and the relationship between the Union and the states. Both were addressed in legislation and litigation.

In September 1991, while the Ayodhya campaign was at its height, the Narasimha Rao government secured passage of the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991. The Act provides that the religious character of a place of worship as it existed on 15 August 1947 shall be maintained, bars conversion of such a place from one religious denomination to another, and abates pending suits that sought to change the character of a site. It explicitly exempted the Ayodhya site, then the subject of litigation, as well as ancient monuments protected under archaeological law. The government’s stated purpose was to prevent new disputes of the Ayodhya type; the BJP opposed the bill, arguing that it ignored Hindu claims to other sites. In later decades, challenges to the Act’s validity and attempts to use litigation to contest the character of other sites would show how an interpretation of the statute could itself become politically divisive.

The second set of issues concerned Article 356, which allows the President to take over a state government when the constitutional machinery has failed. The article had been used often in the 1970s and 1980s, frequently to dismiss opposition governments for political reasons (Chapters 89 and 93). After the demolition, the central government dismissed four BJP-led state governments, and these dismissals were challenged. The most important decision was S. R. Bommai v. Union of India, delivered by a nine-judge bench on 11 March 1994. The case arose from the 1989 dismissal of the Karnataka government of S. R. Bommai, but the bench also considered the 1992 dismissals. It held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 is subject to judicial review; that a government’s majority must be tested on the floor of the assembly rather than assessed by the governor; and that secularism is a basic feature of the Constitution, so that a state government acting against secular principles could be considered to have failed in its constitutional duty. In applying these principles, the Court upheld the dismissal of the BJP governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh, while holding that the Karnataka dismissal was invalid.

The decision had two effects. It restrained political abuse of Article 356: the number of dismissals declined sharply thereafter, and the era of frequent central takeovers of state governments ended. And it made secularism a doctrine of constitutional law, giving courts a role in defining its meaning. Critics from both directions responded. Some argued that treating secularism as a basic feature gave judges excessive power over democratic politics and allowed the dismissal of elected governments on grounds of ideology; others argued that it provided necessary protection for minorities. The BJP accepted the judgment formally while contesting the Court’s reasoning on specific issues.

The decision also affected how political parties conceived of coalition government. By making it harder for the Centre to dismiss a state ministry, it strengthened the autonomy of regional parties, a development with significance for Division V. It also placed constitutional interpretation at the centre of public debate over the nature of the Indian state, so that questions of secularism, federalism, and minority rights were fought through litigation as well as at the ballot box, a pattern that would continue through the 1990s and 2000s.

The Indian understanding of secularism, finally, was not the same as separation of church and state in the American sense. The Constitution allows state support for religious education in some forms, regulates Hindu temples, and recognizes personal laws. Secularism has therefore been understood as principled distance, equal respect for religions, and a duty to protect minorities and reform practices that violate equality. Debates in the 1990s centred on whether this meant strict neutrality or the active promotion of equality among citizens, and these debates continue.

Historical Vocabulary
Article 356, “Basic Structure,” and Judicial Review

Article 356 allows the President, on a governor’s report or otherwise, to assume the functions of a state government when the constitutional machinery of the state has failed; the resulting period is called President’s Rule. The basic structure doctrine, articulated by the Supreme Court in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), holds that Parliament may amend the Constitution but may not destroy its essential features. Judicial review is the power of courts to test government actions and laws against the Constitution.

Bommai combined these ideas: by treating secularism as a basic feature, it allowed courts to examine whether a state government had violated the Constitution’s foundations, while limiting the Centre’s discretion to dismiss governments.

Section 99.15

The BJP’s Electoral Expansion, 1984–1996

The BJP’s growth after 1984 was among the most significant changes in Indian electoral history, but the numbers need careful reading. In 1984 the party won two seats with 7.74 percent of votes. In 1989 it won 85 seats with 11.36 percent; in 1991, 120 seats with 20.11 percent; in 1996, 161 seats with 20.29 percent, making it the largest party in the Lok Sabha for the first time. Its vote share rose more slowly than its seat tally because the Indian electoral system, in which the candidate with the most votes in each constituency wins, rewards parties with concentrated support and penalizes fragmentation among competitors. The BJP’s gains in seats owed as much to the division of the non-BJP vote and to seat-adjustment alliances as to the growth of its own support.

The geography of the BJP’s success was strongly regional. In 1991 and 1996 it won most of its seats in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Delhi, the Hindi belt and the western states, and it held power in several of these states for part of the period. In 1991 it won 51 of Uttar Pradesh’s then 85 seats. In southern India and the east it remained weak, with the principal exceptions of Karnataka and, through alliances, Maharashtra, and in later years Orissa and West Bengal. Where the Congress or strong regional parties, such as the DMK and AIADMK in Tamil Nadu, the Telugu Desam Party in Andhra Pradesh, and the Left Front in West Bengal and Kerala, held the field, the BJP made little progress.

Historians identify several causes of the expansion. The Ayodhya mobilization gave the party an issue and a network of activists. The collapse of Congress organization in the Hindi belt left a political space. The Mandal controversy fractured the opposition, and the BJP’s ability to recruit OBC leaders such as Kalyan Singh, Uma Bharti, and Gopinath Munde expanded its base beyond upper castes. Alliances such as that with the Shiv Sena in Maharashtra and, from the mid-1990s, with the Akali Dal in Punjab broadened its reach. And in the 1996 election, the Jain hawala diary scandal, in which prominent politicians including Advani were named in an investigation into illegal payments, interrupted the party’s image, though the party’s corruption controversies were less prominent than those of rivals in that year.

Equally important is what the numbers do not show. Increasing seat totals did not mean a majority; in 1996 the party’s 161 seats left it well short of the 272 needed, and in all elections up to 2004 its vote share remained below 26 percent. Support was uneven: studies of voting behaviour show that BJP support among Muslims, Christians, Dalits, and some tribal communities remained low in the 1990s, though in some states and constituencies the party made inroads. And the 1993 Uttar Pradesh assembly results, in which the BJP lost power to an SP–BSP coalition despite winning a similar share of votes as in 1991, demonstrated that caste alliances could neutralize a Hindu-nationalist appeal.

Section 99.16

Vajpayee’s Governments and the Emergence of Coalition Hindu Nationalism

After the 1996 election, the President, Shankar Dayal Sharma, invited Atal Bihari Vajpayee, as leader of the largest party, to form the government. He was sworn in on 16 May 1996 and, unable to find allies willing to support a BJP government, resigned on 28 May, thirteen days later, before facing a vote. The episode illustrated a limit on the BJP’s expansion: most other parties regarded it as politically untouchable because of Ayodhya and refused to join its government. The United Front, a coalition of regional and centre-left parties, took office with Congress outside support under H. D. Deve Gowda, followed by I. K. Gujral, and both governments fell by 1998 because of Congress’s withdrawals and internal quarrels (Chapter 98 treats the governance and economic policies of these administrations).

The 1998 election returned the BJP with 182 seats and produced a different outcome. During the campaign, the party had built an alliance of regional partners, including the AIADMK in Tamil Nadu, the Akali Dal in Punjab, the Samata Party and later the Biju Janata Dal in Orissa, and the Shiv Sena in Maharashtra, and after the election the Telugu Desam Party supported the government from outside. The partners were not Hindu nationalists; they joined the BJP for reasons of regional politics, such as opposition to the Congress, and they demanded that the BJP set aside its more divisive agenda. That condition shaped the political identity of the BJP-led government, which began on 19 March 1998.

The result was the coalition’s National Agenda for Governance, discussed in Section 99.17, which excluded the BJP’s core demands of building a temple at Ayodhya, abrogating Article 370 of the Constitution on Jammu and Kashmir, and enacting a uniform civil code. For the BJP, accepting this meant a trade: power in exchange for the postponement of its ideological program. For the allies, it meant that they could remain in government without endorsing Hindutva.

The Vajpayee government’s legitimacy depended on contrasting factors. Vajpayee himself was widely regarded as a moderate, a skilled orator and parliamentarian, with respect across party lines, and his leadership allowed the BJP to appear acceptable to voters and partners who were wary of its associates. At the same time, the party remained tied to the RSS and VHP; the Prime Minister had to manage them. The nuclear tests at Pokhran in May 1998 (Chapter 105; Chapter 97 ends in 1991) strengthened the government’s nationalist credentials, and the Lahore bus journey of February 1999 emphasized its diplomatic initiative (Chapter 105), but its fragile parliamentary position remained.

That fragility ended the second government. In April 1999 the AIADMK’s leader, J. Jayalalithaa, withdrew support, and on 17 April the government lost a confidence motion by a single vote. The Congress declined to form a government, and a new election was called. The government’s caretaker administration led the response to the Kargil conflict of May–July 1999, which boosted its support.

The significance of this period for the history of the BJP is that the party’s growth in the 1990s was inseparable from coalition politics. It could not win a majority alone, and needed partners; those partners imposed constraints that shaped its public program. Chapter 98’s treatment of coalition governance should be read alongside this account.

Section 99.17

The National Democratic Alliance, 1999–2004

The thirteenth Lok Sabha election, held in September–October 1999 in the aftermath of the Kargil conflict, produced a more stable result. The BJP again won 182 seats, but with a smaller vote share than in 1998 (23.75 percent), while the alliance it led, the National Democratic Alliance (NDA), won roughly 300 seats with about two dozen partners. Vajpayee was sworn in on 13 October 1999 and, unlike the 1996 and 1998 governments, his third ministry completed a full term. The Congress, with 114 seats, had its lowest tally to that point, though its vote share (28.3 percent) remained higher than the BJP’s.

The NDA was a negotiated coalition rather than a party. Its larger members, such as the Telugu Desam Party (which supported the government from outside), the Samata Party and its successor the Janata Dal (United), the Shiv Sena, the Akali Dal, the Trinamool Congress for a time, and the DMK, which switched sides in 1999, each had its own program and electorate. They enjoyed ministries and policy influence, and several withdrew when their interests required it. A convenor, the defence minister George Fernandes, coordinated the alliance, and the Prime Minister negotiated disputes personally. This structure meant that national policy was shaped by bargaining among parties with different regional constituencies, an arrangement that political scientists describe as a form of “coalition federalism.”

The alliance’s relationship with the Hindu nationalist agenda was managed through postponement. The BJP’s manifesto commitments on Ayodhya, Article 370, and a uniform civil code were left aside in the coalition’s program, and Vajpayee said that the government would address the Ayodhya dispute through dialogue or court judgment. The government appointed the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution in 2000 under Justice M. N. Venkatachaliah, a step critics suspected of preparing structural change and defenders described as a normal review. The VHP and some RSS leaders pressed for temple construction, and the government held back, with occasional friction. In 2002 the VHP set a deadline for the start of construction at Ayodhya, producing a new crisis.

That crisis connects to the most serious violence of the NDA years. On 27 February 2002 a coach of the Sabarmati Express, carrying kar sevaks returning from Ayodhya, was set on fire at Godhra station in Gujarat, killing fifty-nine people. In the following days and weeks, large-scale violence against Muslims occurred across Gujarat, then governed by the BJP under Chief Minister Narendra Modi. The government told Parliament in 2005 that 1,044 people had died, about 790 of them Muslims and 254 Hindus, with many others missing and injured; independent estimates were higher. The National Human Rights Commission criticized the state government’s response, the Supreme Court transferred several major trials out of Gujarat and monitored investigations, and many cases ended in acquittals, while others produced convictions. The cause of the Godhra fire was itself disputed: a special court later held that it was a conspiracy, while other inquiries offered alternative explanations. Vajpayee publicly criticized the failure to protect people, but the NDA, including its allies, stayed together; the Telugu Desam Party’s support continued, and some partners left over time. Chapter 100 examines the later political career of Narendra Modi.

The government’s other major issues, including the economy, foreign relations, and the Kargil war, are the subject of Chapters 98, 105, and 110, and are addressed here only insofar as they bear on coalition politics. In 2003, a series of state election victories in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Chhattisgarh gave the BJP confidence, and the government advanced the parliamentary election from autumn to April–May 2004, running a campaign, “India Shining,” that emphasized economic growth.

The result surprised most observers. The NDA won about 185 seats, the BJP 138, and the Congress and its allies won enough to form a United Progressive Alliance government with Left parties’ support. The BJP’s vote share fell to 22.16 percent, and the Congress’s was about 26.5 percent. Explanations emphasize several factors: the Congress’s success in building alliances in states such as Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, and Maharashtra, where the NDA lost partners or votes; discontent in rural areas where the benefits of growth were limited; the reaction to the 2002 violence in some constituencies; and anti-incumbency in state governments. Studies caution against a single explanation, since the swing varied across states. The result closed the first stage of BJP-led coalition politics; Chapter 100 begins with the incoming government.

Section 99.18

Regional Parties, Caste Alliances, and the Fragmentation of the Congress System

The BJP’s rise was only one of the changes in the party system. The broader transformation was the decline of the single-party-dominant system that had prevailed since independence, in which the Congress served as a broad coalition that absorbed rivals. By the 1990s that system had fragmented. Studies of parliamentary composition show that the average number of parties represented in the Lok Sabha rose from about nineteen in the elections before 1989 to about thirty-three after, and that no party won a majority between 1989 and 2009.

The causes of fragmentation were cumulative. The Congress had lost its Dalit, Muslim, and upper-caste coalition to rivals over the 1980s and 1990s. The Mandal decision legitimized OBC politics. The Ayodhya movement polarized Muslim voters toward parties that appeared able to defeat the BJP, such as the SP and RJD. Economic liberalization (Chapter 98) shifted resources to the states, where governments controlled licensing, land, and infrastructure, and strengthened regional leaders. And the rise of linguistic and regional identities, long present in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Punjab, and the northeast, found expression in parties with state-level strongholds.

These parties were not alike. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (founded 1949) and the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (1972) evolved from the Dravidian movement and alternated in power in Tamil Nadu, joining national coalitions on terms that advanced state interests. The Telugu Desam Party, founded in 1982 by the film actor N. T. Rama Rao, appealed to Telugu regional pride and later became a coalition partner under N. Chandrababu Naidu, who emphasized technology and economic reform. The Samajwadi Party and the Rashtriya Janata Dal represented the OBC-Muslim combination, the BSP the Dalit-bahujan constituency. In the east, the Left Front sustained a long period of power in West Bengal, and in the northeast numerous smaller parties represented ethnic and regional interests. Many of these parties dominated a single state, and some, like the BSP, tried to expand beyond one.

The consequences were large. Government formation became a process of bargaining among parties that could demand ministries, policy concessions, and funding for their states. National politics became a combination of state politics, and governments gained or lost power as alliances shifted. Coalition rule also changed policy making: economic reform, for instance, continued under governments of different composition because leaders, including regional ones, converged on a pro-growth approach, while social policy and federal relations were more contested. The weakening of Article 356 after Bommai reinforced this federal shift.

The new system could be called more representative, because more communities had their own parties, and more competitive, because power was contested among many actors. But representation had costs and limits. Parties built on caste or community could entrench dynastic leadership, depend on patronage, and compete for the same limited resources. Dalit and OBC parties sometimes allied with the BJP, as the BSP did in Uttar Pradesh in 1995, 1997, and 2002, and sometimes with the Congress, reflecting pragmatic bargaining rather than fixed ideological alignment.

The fragmentation also revealed the limits of caste as an analytical category. Many voters chose on the basis of local issues, candidate quality, class, and state development. Surveys found that caste and religion were important predictors of voting but did not determine it, and that the extent of caste voting varied from state to state. Students should therefore treat caste blocs as tendencies rather than certainties.

Section 99.19

Democracy, Social Justice, Secularism, and National Identity

Historians and political scientists have offered rival assessments of the Mandal and Ayodhya period. One account emphasizes democratization. On this view, caste mobilization opened the institutions of government to people who had been excluded; turnout among the poor and lower castes increased; state legislatures and the national parliament came to include more members of OBC and Dalit backgrounds; and politics became more responsive to rural voters. Mandal in particular is seen as the political arrival of the numerical majority.

A second account emphasizes polarization. It holds that the Ayodhya movement redefined national identity around a majority religion, that the violence of 1992–1993 and later episodes damaged relations between communities, and that Muslims in particular experienced rising insecurity and a decline in political representation. Some scholars have argued that the BJP’s mobilization was a deliberate reaction to Mandal, designed to rebuild Hindu unity across caste lines; others hold that the two processes had different origins and that their interaction was contingent.

A third account stresses institutional resilience. It points out that the crises of the 1990s were handled through courts, elections, and coalition bargaining rather than through authoritarian rule: the Supreme Court limited the use of Article 356, the Election Commission under T. N. Seshan (1990–1996) enforced electoral rules more vigorously, and a pluralistic party system emerged in which no single group could govern alone. Coalition government forced the BJP to moderate its program.

No one of these accounts is sufficient. The evidence shows that participation widened for some groups while insecurity and exclusion deepened for others, and that the same period saw both the strengthening of institutions that limited executive power and the occurrence of mass violence that institutions failed to prevent. A historian assessing the period must therefore ask for whom, in which regions, and on which dimension it expanded or narrowed democratic life. The pattern in Uttar Pradesh, where the Dalit and OBC parties came to power and the BJP also gained strength, differed from that in Tamil Nadu, where caste politics had a longer history and the BJP remained marginal, and from that in West Bengal, where the Left Front held power.

The period left several unresolved questions to the twenty-first century. How should the state define and measure backwardness, given the absence of a caste census until the 2010s and the demand by dominant communities for reservation? Could the Constitution’s principle of equal citizenship accommodate both group-based remedies and a common civil law? How could the state prevent communal violence and ensure accountability when its own agencies were implicated? And would a party that had grown through religious mobilization continue to accept the constraints of coalition government? Chapters 100 and 107 address these questions as they developed after 2004.

Historians also face an interpretive challenge in writing about movements that remain politically salient. The Ayodhya dispute, in particular, is remembered differently by supporters and opponents of the movement, and later legal and political developments have shaped how it is described. The standard advised here is to separate documented facts from contested interpretation, avoid projecting later outcomes onto earlier decisions, and attend to the experiences of those who lived through both the new opportunities and the new fears.

The relationship between wider participation and constitutional protection also varied across institutions. In a state election, numerical strength could allow a previously excluded group to help select ministers, but cabinet appointments alone did not guarantee access to land, schooling, policing, or impartial administration. A court judgment could protect formal rights even as enforcement remained uneven on the ground; conversely, an electoral victory could be achieved while minority citizens still feared coercion or exclusion. Historians therefore need to examine several measures together: who voted and held office, which groups entered public employment, how courts and officials responded to violence, and whether vulnerable citizens could exercise their rights safely. These measures are not interchangeable, and none by itself establishes that democracy either advanced or retreated. The central evidence of this chapter is a political order that admitted new actors while repeatedly testing whether electoral competition could coexist with equal citizenship and the restraints of constitutional government.

Section 99.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

Three transformations structure this chapter. The first was the institutional and electoral consequence of Mandal. A report written in 1980, left largely unimplemented for a decade, was activated in 1990 by a government facing a crisis of its own, and after the Supreme Court’s ruling in 1992 and the executive’s orders of 1993 it became part of the permanent machinery of the state. Its effects extended beyond jobs: it validated a language of backwardness and numerical majority, brought new leaders to office, and created a politics in which caste communities bargained for representation.

The second was Hindu nationalist mobilization and the Ayodhya controversy. Rooted in ideologies articulated before independence, an organizational network built over decades, and the political choices of the 1980s, the movement used a local dispute of a religious site to define national identity. Its high point, the demolition of the mosque on 6 December 1992, was an unlawful act by a crowd that state governments and officials had failed or declined to restrain, and it was followed by riots whose victims were mostly Muslims and whose organizers, according to official inquiries, included political organizations. The legal and historical consequences extended for decades, through the criminal and civil proceedings and the contested memory of the event.

The third was the emergence of the BJP as a national governing party. Moving from two seats in 1984 to 182 in 1998, the party combined organization, alliances, an ideological message, and the weakness of its rivals; yet it governed only as the leader of a coalition whose members demanded concessions. The BJP’s rise and the growth of caste-based and regional parties were parallel developments within a common transformation of the party system.

These transformations interacted but did not form a simple binary of caste against religion. Mandal divided Hindu society along caste lines and prompted attempts to unify it; Hindu nationalism recruited OBC and Dalit leaders even as backward-caste parties built alliances with Muslims; some Dalit and OBC leaders allied with the BJP when it was electorally advantageous. Class, region, and local power relations mattered at least as much as ideology. Understanding the period requires holding together these cross-cutting alignments.

The period also changed the relationship between constitutional institutions and mass movements. Courts became central arenas for contested questions of social justice and secularism, as shown by Indra Sawhney, Bommai, and the later Ayodhya litigation; the Union’s power over states diminished; and the Election Commission, the press, and civil-society groups gained prominence. At the same time, institutions proved unable to prevent the demolition or to deliver swift justice for the victims of violence.

The Congress, which had dominated national politics, lost its position, not because a single rival replaced it but because its coalition dissolved into many parts. Coalition government, with its bargaining and compromises, became the pattern of the era, and it moderated, without eliminating, the programmes of the largest parties. This change in the structure of political competition is the main legacy of the period for Chapter 100, which examines how the UPA government of 2004 and later BJP-led governments operated in the party system that this chapter has described.

The chapter ends, therefore, at a point of transition. By May 2004 the BJP had shown that Hindu nationalism could govern through coalition, the Congress had shown that it could still organize an alliance, and regional parties had shown that national power now ran through the states. The questions of reservation, religious equality, and the accountability of the state for communal violence remained open, and each would be argued again, in new circumstances and with new evidence, in the chapters that follow. Students moving to Chapter 100 should carry with them the central lesson of this one: that Indian democracy changed because very different groups used its institutions for their own purposes, and that the results were neither simply an expansion of democracy nor simply its erosion.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 99 has examined how caste mobilization, Hindu nationalism, and the rise of the Bharatiya Janata Party restructured Indian democracy between 1980 and 2004. Its central argument is that these processes were distinct in origin but interacted in practice, and that together with the fragmentation of the Congress system they produced a more competitive and more contested political order.

The chapter began with the constitutional foundations of affirmative action and the uneven development of policy for Other Backward Classes. The First Backward Classes Commission produced a report that was not implemented; southern states built reservation systems earlier than the Centre; and in the north, OBC communities remained underrepresented in the institutions of government despite their numbers.

The Mandal Commission, appointed in 1979 and reporting in December 1980, estimated the OBC population at about 52 percent using 1931 data, devised eleven indicators of backwardness, and recommended 27 percent reservation in central employment. The report was not implemented for a decade. V. P. Singh’s order of August 1990, issued in a political crisis within the Janata Dal, produced nationwide protest, and the Supreme Court’s Indra Sawhney judgment of November 1992 upheld the policy while excluding the creamy layer, applying a 50 percent general limit, and disallowing reservation in promotions.

Caste-based political mobilization generated new parties. OBC leaders such as Lalu Prasad Yadav and Mulayam Singh Yadav built coalitions in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, while Kanshi Ram and Mayawati created the BSP as a Dalit-bahujan party. The two movements overlapped but had different constituencies, and their alliances were fragile.

Hindu nationalism had a different lineage. Savarkar’s Hindutva, the RSS, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Jana Sangh, and the VHP formed a family of organizations with distinct functions. The BJP, founded in 1980 after the Janata Party’s collapse, initially pursued a moderate strategy, won two seats in 1984, and then moved toward a more assertive posture, adopting the Ayodhya demand in 1989.

The Ayodhya dispute had a long history, from competing claims in the nineteenth century to the placing of idols in the mosque in 1949 and the opening of the locks in 1986. The campaign of 1984–1990, culminating in Advani’s Rath Yatra, linked the dispute to national politics. After the BJP won Uttar Pradesh in 1991, the state government’s assurances to the courts did not prevent the demolition of the Babri Masjid on 6 December 1992.

The destruction was followed by riots in many cities. The Srikrishna Commission documented the Mumbai riots, with roughly nine hundred deaths and evidence of organized violence, and separated them from the bombings of March 1993. The state responded with the Places of Worship Act, with President’s Rule, and, through the Supreme Court, with Bommai, which made secularism a basic feature of the Constitution and restricted Article 356. The criminal case over the demolition and the civil land dispute were separate proceedings.

Electorally, the BJP grew from 2 seats in 1984 to 182 in 1998, but never exceeded 26 percent of the national vote in this period, and it governed through coalitions. The National Agenda for Governance excluded its most divisive demands. The NDA ruled from 1998 to 2004 but was defeated in 2004.

Parallel to the BJP’s growth was the rise of regional and caste-based parties and the end of single-party dominance. Coalition government forced bargaining and strengthened federalism.

Assessments of the period differ. Some historians stress democratization, others polarization, others institutional resilience. The evidence supports elements of each. The period left unresolved questions about reservation, personal law, communal violence, and the character of the state, which Chapter 100 and later chapters address.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a democracy with a written commitment to equality was reshaped by its own mass politics. The decisions of the 1990s were not the products of foreign pressure or a single leader; they emerged from long-standing social inequalities, from institutions that allowed demands to be expressed, and from the choices of parties and governments.

The processes examined here shaped what followed. The reservation system became a permanent feature of Indian public life, the party system established in these years governed the politics of the following decades, and the memory of 6 December 1992 shaped relations between communities and debates over history, secularism, and justice. Chapter 100 turns to the twenty-first century, when these questions reappeared in new forms.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of careful historical method for politically charged subjects. By separating belief from evidence, legal findings from historical conclusions, and organizations from communities, historians can describe violent and divisive events without assigning collective blame. That discipline is part of the larger project of this series, which has treated South Asian history as the history of many communities and regions, not a single national story.

Within Volume VI, the chapter completes a movement from the constitutional settlement of 1950, through the crises of the 1970s and 1980s, to a new equilibrium in which social and regional diversity was expressed through a plural party system. Whether that equilibrium was stable is a question for the chapters that follow.
