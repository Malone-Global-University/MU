Part XII — Company Rule and Colonial Conquest
65

Punjab, Sindh, and the Final Expansion of Company Rule

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 29 March 1849, in the Lahore Darbar, the ten-year-old Maharaja Duleep Singh and the sardars who still surrounded him accepted terms that ended the independent Sikh kingdom. The terms provided that the Maharaja would cease to rule, that the state's property would pass to the East India Company, and that the great diamond known as the Koh-i-Noor would be surrendered to the Queen of England. A state that had been ruled from Lahore for half a century, whose army had fought the Company to near-standstill on more than one field, and whose capital had been described by European visitors as one of the great courts of Asia, became a province administered by Company officers.

That ceremony was the end of a long process, and this chapter explains how it came about. Chapter 64 traced the wars through which the East India Company defeated or subordinated Mysore and the Maratha states, so that by 1818 it was the dominant power across southern and central India. That dominance did not yet extend to the northwest. Beyond the Sutlej, a Sikh state under Maharaja Ranjit Singh had built one of the largest and best-organized armies under an Indian ruler and one of the most extensive territorial states on the subcontinent. Along the lower Indus, the Talpur amirs governed Sindh from several capitals, controlling the river, the ports, and the overland routes toward Afghanistan and the Arabian Sea. For roughly three decades after the Maratha defeat these two states remained outside the Company's territorial system. By 1849 neither survived in independent form, and the Company's frontier stood at the passes of the Hindu Kush foothills.

The chapter's governing argument is that this expansion was not simply the inevitable advance of superior British armies. It depended on a sequence of specific developments: Company anxieties about Russian and Persian influence in Afghanistan; the First Anglo-Afghan War of 1839–1842, which made the Indus a military supply line; the death of Ranjit Singh in 1839 and the succession crises that followed; the changing relationship between the Lahore court and its own army; treaty-making that converted diplomatic influence into political control; and the choices of Indian actors, among them Talpur amirs, Sikh commanders, Dogra courtiers, provincial governors, and the soldiers of both the Khalsa and the Company's armies. Each of these shaped the timing and the form of the outcome. Several of them might have gone differently.

The Sikh state that the Company confronted was not a simple religious polity. Ranjit Singh was a Sikh and the Lahore government patronized Sikh institutions, above all the Harmandir Sahib at Amritsar, but most of the people he ruled were Muslims and Hindus. His court included Muslim ministers, Hindu financiers and governors, Dogra Rajput nobles, European and Indian military specialists, and Sikh sardars from many lineages. His army, the Khalsa army, drew on this diversity while developing a strong collective identity of its own. This composite character is essential to understanding both the strength of the state and the political stresses that followed its founder's death.

Sindh must be treated as a parallel story and not as a prelude to Punjab. The Talpur amirs were Baloch rulers who governed a Muslim-majority society with a significant Hindu mercantile population and a banking network, centred on Shikarpur, that reached deep into Central Asia. The Company's interest in Sindh began with commerce and the navigation of the Indus, hardened during the Afghan war, and ended in 1843 in a conquest that British contemporaries themselves, including officials on the spot, openly disputed. The conquest of Sindh preceded the Anglo-Sikh Wars, shaped the Company's strategic position on the Punjab's southern flank, and demonstrated a style of intervention and annexation that Lahore's rulers had reason to fear.

The two Anglo-Sikh Wars of 1845–1846 and 1848–1849 were hard-fought. The Khalsa army was no ill-equipped enemy; its artillery, entrenchments, and discipline inflicted heavy casualties on Company forces at Ferozeshah, Sobraon, Chillianwala, and elsewhere. Its eventual defeat resulted from a combination of military, political, and institutional factors, including divided leadership at Lahore, allegations of treachery by some senior figures that remain historically contested, Company reinforcement and supply, and the diplomacy that turned the territories and dependents of the Lahore state into pieces in a larger settlement. The settlements of 1846, in particular, produced three distinct treaties (Lahore, Amritsar, and Bhairowal) that students must carefully distinguish, because they established the transfer of Kashmir to Gulab Singh of Jammu and the effective control of Lahore by a British Resident.

The evidence for these events is extensive but uneven in perspective. Company records, officers' letters and memoirs, and published treaty collections are abundant. Persian-language court records and chronicles, such as the diaries of the Lahore court historian Sohan Lal Suri, Punjabi traditions, Sikh narratives, and Sindhi and Baloch sources preserve other points of view, and modern scholarship in Punjabi, Sindhi, Urdu, and English has done much to rebalance the picture. Much British military reporting was written to justify decisions, secure credit, or defend commanders in the press, and it frequently attributed Sikh or Sindhi actions to motives that those actors never stated. This chapter therefore distinguishes documented events from the claims of those who reported them.

The chapter ends on the eve of the 1857 uprising, describing the Company's territorial reach and its tensions. By the 1850s, Sindh and Punjab were governed by new non-regulation administrations; Khairpur, Bahawalpur, and Jammu and Kashmir remained under British paramountcy; and Punjab had become a major recruiting ground for the Company's army. Chapter 66 turns to the land, agriculture, and the economy of Company rule; Chapter 69 examines the Rebellion of 1857 itself. The present chapter keeps to the northwest and to the political, military, and administrative history that explains why Punjab and the Bengal Army would face 1857 in such different circumstances.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  3. Evaluate the roles of the Khalsa army, the Lahore Darbar, and regional elites in the empire's administration and in the crisis after 1839.
  4. Describe the political structure of Talpur Sindh and the commercial importance of the Indus and the Shikarpur banking networks.
  5. Trace the stages of British intervention in Sindh from the treaties of 1832 to the conquest of 1843, and distinguish the successive agreements.

Historical Context

The northwestern part of South Asia is defined by the Indus river system. The Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Chenab, and Jhelum, the five rivers that give Punjab its name, flow southwest to join the Indus, which continues through Sindh to the Arabian Sea. These rivers supported irrigated agriculture, riverine trade, and powerful regional states, and they sat astride the routes by which armies, merchants, and migrants had long entered the subcontinent from Afghanistan and Central Asia. The Bolan and Khyber passes, and the crossings of the Indus near Attock, made this a strategic frontier. As Chapter 57 showed, the invasions of Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Durrani in the eighteenth century weakened Mughal authority here, and Chapter 59 traced how Sikh misls, the armed confederacies of the Khalsa, filled much of the resulting political space.

By 1799 the Company's position had been transformed from the situation described in Chapters 62 and 63. In Bengal it exercised territorial and fiscal government. In the south it had destroyed the power of Mysore and, by 1818, much of the Maratha confederacy. Its method relied on subsidiary alliances, the maintenance of a large army of Indian soldiers under European officers, a revenue system that could finance that army, and a diplomatic service of Residents and political agents. The same instruments were now carried northwest. They met two states that were not Mysore and not the Marathas: a Sikh monarchy with a modern-style infantry and artillery, and a river-based Baloch regime in Sindh whose ruling houses disagreed among themselves.

Strategic anxiety was part of the Company's calculus. After 1807 the Company feared French, and later Russian and Persian, influence in Afghanistan and Iran as a potential threat to India. This anxiety, often exaggerated by contemporaries, made the Indus corridor and Afghanistan matters of imperial strategy. It was also tied to commerce: British officials and merchants hoped to open the Indus to steam navigation and to open markets to Central Asia. These motives, security and trade, appear throughout the chapter and often reinforced each other, which complicates any attempt to separate them.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. First, the terms used by officials, such as "the Punjab," "Scinde," and "Affghanistan," described political units that no longer match modern boundaries, and the North-West Frontier Province did not exist until 1901. Second, treaty language was often formal while the underlying relationships changed; the same text could signify partnership at one moment and subordination at another, depending on the balance of power. Third, much of the surviving documentation comes from the conquering power. Sources written in Persian, Punjabi, and Sindhi, and the work of modern historians who read them, provide essential correctives, but the record remains uneven, and this chapter identifies where particular claims rest on a single perspective.

Section 65.1

Ranjit Singh and the Consolidation of Sikh Political Power, 1799–1809

The Sikh state that Ranjit Singh created grew out of the political fragmentation described in Chapter 59. After the Mughal withdrawal from Punjab and the repeated invasions of Ahmad Shah Durrani, authority in the Punjab plains had passed to a number of misls, armed bands whose leaders held territory by conquest and by collecting protection payments and revenue. The misls shared a Sikh identity and gathered at Amritsar on important occasions, but they competed with one another and had no single ruler. By the 1790s Durrani authority from Kabul was weakening further as the Afghan court itself was divided by succession disputes, and Punjab's cities and fertile districts were open to any chief who could raise the force to hold them.

Ranjit Singh was born in 1780 into the Sukerchakia misl, based at Gujranwala. His father, Mahan Singh, died when Ranjit was a boy, and he grew up with family advisers amid rivalries among the misls. In 1799 he occupied Lahore, the former Mughal provincial capital, with the support of local notables who preferred a decisive ruler to the divided control of several Sikh chiefs. Lahore gave him a commercially important city, a revenue base, and access to central Punjab’s resources. In April 1801, at Baisakhi, he was installed as maharaja, a step that gave formal expression to a sovereignty he was already exercising. The state he proclaimed would be spoken of as the Sarkar-i-Khalsa, the government of the Khalsa, even though its ruling practice rested largely on a personal monarchy.

Consolidation was the central political task of these early years. Ranjit Singh could not simply subordinate the other misls, for many were led by chiefs of comparable status. He used a combination of marriage alliance, military pressure, and negotiation, allowing some chiefs to keep their estates as jagirs in exchange for military service and acceptance of Lahore's overlordship while taking over the territories of others outright. In 1802 he took Amritsar, the spiritual centre of the Sikh community and a major commercial city, from the Bhangi chiefs. Control of Amritsar mattered for revenue and for legitimacy: the ruler who patronized the Harmandir Sahib, and who could convene Sikh leaders there, strengthened his claim to speak for the Panth. At the same time he drew into his service administrators and financiers from the towns, many of them Hindus and Muslims who brought experience of revenue and accounts.

His ambitions soon extended beyond the Punjab proper, and the Sutlej became the line along which these ambitions met the Company's. Between 1806 and 1808, Ranjit Singh led expeditions across the river into the Cis-Sutlej region, the Sikh and other chiefdoms between the Sutlej and the Yamuna that had long looked to the Marathas for protection and now, after the Company's defeat of Daulat Rao Sindhia in 1803, were within the Company's military orbit. Some Cis-Sutlej chiefs appealed to the Company for protection. This coincided with a strategic concern: after the Treaty of Tilsit in 1807 British officials feared a Franco-Russian approach through Persia and Afghanistan, and the Company sought assurances from the powers of the northwest. In 1808 Charles Metcalfe was sent to Lahore to negotiate, and Ranjit Singh was initially reluctant to give up his claims south of the river. The British moved troops to the Sutlej under David Ochterlony and issued a proclamation of protection for the Cis-Sutlej chiefs in early 1809. Accounts also record that a Muharram procession incident at Amritsar in February 1809, in which Metcalfe's escort of Company sepoys held off a crowd of Akali Sikhs, impressed Ranjit Singh with the discipline of the Company's troops, though students should treat such anecdotes as part of a British narrative.

The result was the Treaty of Amritsar, signed on 25 April 1809 by Ranjit Singh and Metcalfe on behalf of the Governor-General, Lord Minto; the British Library's catalogue records a ratification date of 30 May, a distinction students should keep in view. The treaty recognized Ranjit Singh's sovereignty north of the Sutlej, committed the Company not to interfere with his territories there, and restricted his forces south of the river to those required for internal order. It placed the Cis-Sutlej states under British protection. For Ranjit Singh the agreement prevented expansion toward the Yamuna and made the Company a permanent neighbour. For the Company it secured a friendly buffer on the northwestern flank while its attention was directed elsewhere. Ranjit Singh observed the treaty for the rest of his life, which allowed him to redirect his ambitions northwest and west, and the Company respected the northern limit for three decades. Both facts are important to the question that dominates this chapter: the treaty preserved Sikh independence by accepting a limit, and the period of peace it created allowed the Lahore state to grow, while also leaving the Company a long frontier at the Sutlej.

Primary Source Window

The Treaty of Amritsar, 25 April 1809

Source type: Bilateral treaty · Parties: Maharaja Ranjit Singh and the East India Company, negotiated by Charles Metcalfe · Signed: 25 April 1809; ratification by the Governor-General is recorded in the British Library catalogue as 30 May 1809 · Archival reference: a treaty copy is catalogued in British Library IOR/H/638, pp. 25–27; published treaty collections reproduce the text

Context: The treaty was negotiated at the moment when Ranjit Singh's expeditions south of the Sutlej met the Company's strategic concern about French and Russian influence and its recent commitments to the Cis-Sutlej chiefs. It was drafted in English and Persian, the language of diplomacy, and survives in several copies and published versions.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim translation): The treaty declares perpetual friendship between the two governments and states that the Company will treat the Maharaja's state as one of the most favoured powers. The Maharaja agrees not to maintain on the left, or southern, bank of the Sutlej more troops than are needed for the internal duties of the territories he holds there, and not to encroach on the rights of the chiefs on that bank. The Company, for its part, agrees not to interfere with the Maharaja's territories and subjects north of the river. Provision is made for action by the Company if the terms are violated.

This paraphrase summarizes the treaty's general sense for study and has not been collated word by word against the British Library copy or a published text. Verify against the original before quotation.

Analysis: The treaty reads as a mutual agreement but its effects were asymmetrical. It recognized Ranjit Singh's authority north of the Sutlej, a real gain, and it was observed by both parties for decades. But it settled a boundary that closed the Punjab's southeastern expansion and created a legal basis for the Company to act as protector of the Cis-Sutlej states. Its language about friendship does not reveal the alternatives that both parties considered, including military conflict.

Limitations: The treaty is an official text and does not show the negotiation. The accounts of Metcalfe's mission come largely from British participants. The Lahore side is preserved in Persian court records, which are less often consulted in older English-language histories. A treaty also cannot show how contemporaries understood its terms, for example whether "protection" implied a duty for the Company to defend the chiefs or a right to supervise them.

Questions for students:

  1. What limits does the treaty place on each party, and which party's freedom of action is more constrained?
  2. Who created the surviving treaty texts, and for what audience?
  3. What can historians learn from the treaty about the Company's strategic concerns in 1809?
  4. Why must historians distinguish between the date of signature and the date of ratification, and what can the text not tell us about Ranjit Singh's own reasoning?
Section 65.2

The Sikh Empire: Expansion, Military Reform, Revenue, and Political Integration

With the southeastern limit fixed, Ranjit Singh turned north and west. The campaigns of the following two decades created an empire that stretched from the Sutlej to the Khyber and from the Himalayan foothills to the edge of Sindh. In June 1818 Lahore forces took Multan after a siege that cost the life of its Afghan governor, Muzaffar Khan, and brought the southern Punjab into the empire. In 1819 Kashmir, a valuable shawl-producing province long held by Durrani governors, was conquered. Toward the northwest, Sikh armies pressed beyond the Indus: Sikh forces extended tributary influence to Peshawar from about 1818, defeated Afghan opponents at Naushera in 1823, and established direct control over the city in 1834; in a battle in which the Akali leader Phula Singh was killed, and Peshawar was made a firmer part of the empire in the 1830s, under the governorship of Hari Singh Nalwa, who died at the battle of Jamrud in 1837. Sikh power also extended into the hill states, and the Dogra commander Zorawar Singh, acting under Gulab Singh of Jammu, campaigned in Ladakh in the 1830s and 1840s.

Expansion rested on military reorganization. The core of Ranjit Singh's earlier armies had been Sikh horsemen in the misl tradition, effective in mobile warfare but not well adapted to sieges or to fighting disciplined infantry. After observing the Company's sepoys and the armies of Afghanistan and Europe, Ranjit Singh built a regular army, the Fauj-i-Ain, with infantry battalions drilled in the European style, an artillery branch, and regular cavalry, alongside an irregular force of jagirdar horsemen and the Sikh irregulars known as Akalis. Early training of the new battalions drew on deserters from the Company's army and other Hindustani soldiers, and from 1822 he employed European officers who had served in Napoleonic and other armies, including Jean-François Allard and Jean-Baptiste Ventura, and later the Italian Paolo Avitabile, who served as governor in Peshawar and Wazirabad, and Americans and Britons such as Alexander Gardner in the artillery. These foreign specialists mattered, but they cannot be said to explain the army's quality. Indian officers such as Misr Diwan Chand, Hari Singh Nalwa, and others commanded campaigns; Muslim gunners and Punjabi infantry made up its rank and file; and Lahore maintained arsenals, foundries, and gun factories that produced much of its own equipment, with cannon cast under the supervision of Muslim and Sikh artisans such as Lehna Singh Majithia, who was a noted engineer.

Financing such a force required a revenue system adapted to Punjab. The empire drew its income principally from land revenue, supplemented by customs, tolls, the profits of mints, and tribute from dependent chiefs. Much of the collection was farmed out to contractors, who advanced money to the treasury in return for the right to collect, while other lands were assigned as jagirs to commanders and nobles in return for service. Some jagirs were hereditary in practice. Ranjit Singh relied on the services of Hindu and Muslim revenue officials and financiers; the Diwans of Multan, such as Sawan Mal, built the province's agriculture and canals and are credited with substantially expanding its revenues. The system worked as long as the ruler could shift officials, and replace jagirdars and contractors, and could ensure that soldiers were paid. These demands would become severe after his death.

Political integration was never complete, and it is essential to describe the empire accurately. It was ruled by a Sikh maharaja, and the army's identity was strongly Sikh, but the empire's subjects were mostly Muslims and Hindus. In the western Punjab, Kashmir, Multan, and the Peshawar valley, a large majority of the population was Muslim; Hindu merchants, bankers, and administrators were prominent in the cities. Ranjit Singh's court included Muslim ministers such as the Fakir brothers of Lahore, Hindu officials such as Raja Dina Nath, and Dogra Rajput nobles. The empire's integration was therefore achieved less by religious uniformity than by the ruler's patronage, by alliance with regional elites, and by the army's central role. Historians disagree about how far Lahore's rule represented a distinctly Sikh political project and how far a regional monarchy in the Mughal and Persianate administrative tradition that happened to be led by a Sikh. Both elements were present, and the ambiguity was a source of strength and later of stress.

Historical Vocabulary
Khalsa and Sarkar-i-Khalsa

Khalsa originally refers to the community of initiated Sikhs established by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699, as discussed in Chapter 59. In the Lahore state it acquired several overlapping meanings. Sarkar-i-Khalsa, "the government of the Khalsa," was a name for the state itself, even though it was headed by a maharaja and not governed by the community collectively. The Khalsa army was the state's military establishment, composed of men of many backgrounds, not only initiated Sikhs, but increasingly conscious of a collective Khalsa identity.

The term matters because after 1839 the army claimed to speak for the Khalsa in political decisions, through regimental panchayats. Students should not read "Khalsa" as a synonym for "the Sikh population" or assume that its religious meaning was unchanging. The word's range of use is part of the history of the period.

Section 65.3

Lahore, Imperial Administration, Religious Institutions, and Diplomacy

Lahore under Ranjit Singh was the capital of a multilingual and multi-religious order. Persian remained the language of the court and of official correspondence, Punjabi was the language of the countryside and the Sikh scriptural tradition, and Urdu, Pashto, Kashmiri, and other languages were spoken in the provinces. The royal Darbar was the centre of patronage and decision-making. The Maharaja held court, received envoys, and distributed titles and jagirs, but day-to-day government was carried on by a small group of ministers and secretaries. Fakir Azizuddin, a Muslim physician from Lahore, served as chief diplomatic officer, receiving and negotiating with British and Afghan envoys. Dhian Singh, a Dogra Rajput of Jammu, rose from the royal household to become the chief minister, while his brothers Gulab Singh and Suchet Singh acquired jagirs and commands. The Dogras' rise represented the Maharaja's effort to bind a loyal northern hill elite to his service, but it also created a powerful faction that would affect succession politics after 1839.

Provincial administration relied on governors, often military men, who collected revenue, kept order, and maintained troops. The governors of Multan, Kashmir, and Peshawar combined wide powers, and local government continued largely through established officials, village headmen, and landholders. Ranjit Singh preserved existing local arrangements wherever possible and relied on the continuity of revenue and law practices familiar from the Mughal and Durrani periods. Justice was administered through a mixture of local custom, the rulings of governors, and, for Muslim communities, the continued role of qazis. This reliance on local elites gave the empire its flexibility but limited the central government's control over its provinces.

The Lahore government patronized the major religious institutions of all three communities. For Sikhs, the most conspicuous act was the rebuilding of the Harmandir Sahib at Amritsar, whose upper storeys were covered in gilded copper and whose surrounding marble work was funded by the state, giving the shrine the appearance by which it is known as the Golden Temple. Ranjit Singh endowed gurdwaras, supported Sikh institutions, and sought the endorsement of Sikh religious leaders at Amritsar. He also made grants to Hindu temples and to Muslim shrines and institutions, and employed members of religious families in the administration. The record is not uniformly benign: some mosques in Lahore were put to military or other uses, and the treatment of particular institutions varied with place and time. The accurate picture is of a ruler who combined patronage of Sikh institutions with a practice of political inclusion, not of a state that either enforced one religion or treated all identically.

Diplomacy was the second pillar of Lahore's security. Ranjit Singh maintained a regular correspondence with the Company, exchanging envoys and gifts, and met Governor-General Lord William Bentinck at Rupar on the Sutlej in 1831 and Lord Auckland at Ferozepur in 1838. He balanced relations with the Company against relations with the Afghan Barakzai rulers of Kabul and Peshawar, with the hill rajas, and with the Talpur amirs of Sindh. Sindh was an area of particular interest: he claimed rights over Shikarpur and considered further expansion along the Indus, and in the 1830s the Company worked to restrain these plans, since British commercial and strategic planning envisaged the Indus as an avenue of its own influence. The Company's interest in navigating the river thus ran against Ranjit Singh's territorial interests, and both were bound up with the question of Afghanistan. Peshawar, Kashmir, Jammu, and the Indus frontier were central to Lahore's calculations because they controlled the revenue of the north, the shawl trade, the military manpower of the hills, and the routes from Kabul. In 1838 Ranjit Singh agreed to the Tripartite Treaty with the Company and the exiled Afghan ruler Shah Shuja, and a Sikh force advanced through the Khyber alongside the British army in the following year, though Lahore remained wary of Company control over a restored Afghan government at its border.

Everyday Life and Social History

Lahore and the Punjab Countryside under the Sikh Empire

For most people the empire was experienced locally. In the Punjab countryside, agriculture rested on wheat, other grains, and sugarcane, using wells and, in some districts, river inundation and canals. Peasant cultivators, many of them Jat, Rajput, or Arain in different parts of the province, paid revenue in a share of the crop or in cash to a revenue contractor or jagirdar, and village headmen mediated. Soldiers' families received land grants or pay, so that military service was a major source of livelihood in some districts, particularly in the central Punjab, and connected villages to the state.

In Lahore and Amritsar, merchants, bankers, shawl-makers, weavers, gunsmiths, and entertainers sustained the urban economy. Amritsar was a great commercial centre and pilgrimage destination, and Kashmiri shawls, Punjabi textiles, and goods moving along the Indus and overland to Afghanistan and Central Asia passed through its markets. The court's patronage supported painters, musicians, poets in Persian and Punjabi, and craftsmen. Evidence on women's lives is limited and largely concerns elite women, including the royal household, in which women such as the Maharani Jind Kaur later played political roles. Evidence for ordinary families is fragmentary, derived from travellers' accounts, revenue records, and Persian chronicles, each of which reflects the interests of its authors.

Section 65.4

The Death of Ranjit Singh and the Crisis of Succession, 1839–1845

Ranjit Singh died on 27 June 1839. The monarchy he left behind had been constructed around his personal authority: he had balanced the Dogra brothers against Sikh sardars, the army against the court, and the Company against the Afghans. No institutional mechanism existed for choosing or controlling a successor. His eldest son, Kharak Singh, was proclaimed maharaja but was widely regarded as weak, and real power passed to his son Nau Nihal Singh and to the chief minister Dhian Singh. Kharak Singh died in November 1840 and on the same day Nau Nihal Singh was killed when a gateway collapsed on him as he returned from his father's cremation, an accident that contemporaries suspected was contrived, although the circumstances remain uncertain. A struggle followed between Nau Nihal Singh's mother, Chand Kaur, and Ranjit Singh's son Sher Singh. Sher Singh gained control of Lahore in January 1841 with the support of the army and became maharaja.

Sher Singh's rule lasted until September 1843, when he was assassinated by members of the Sandhawalia family, who also killed Dhian Singh. Order was restored by Dhian Singh's son Hira Singh and the army, who proclaimed Ranjit Singh's youngest son, the five-year-old Duleep Singh, as maharaja, with his mother, Maharani Jind Kaur, as regent. Hira Singh became vizier but was killed in December 1844 following a conflict with the army and with court rivals. Jawahir Singh, Jind Kaur's brother, then rose to power but was executed by the army in September 1845 after being held responsible for the death of a prince. Within six years of Ranjit Singh's death Lahore had seen four maharajas, several viziers, and a series of murders, and these events weakened the court's credibility among its own officers and troops.

The army was the decisive new actor. During these years, regimental panchayats, committees of soldiers, appeared as a collective body that claimed a voice in political decisions. They demanded higher pay, which successive viziers could not always meet, and they intervened in appointments and removals. Their political role has been interpreted in different ways. British contemporaries described the army as an ungovernable mass; Punjabi sources and later historians often stress that the soldiers were defending the Khalsa state and their own interests against court factions who they believed were corrupt or willing to deal with the British. Both perspectives capture part of the picture. The army had grown to a very large size, its members were dependent on the state for pay and land, and the founder who had commanded it was gone. Soldiers who were also members of a sovereign community that regarded itself as the Khalsa could reasonably claim a political voice in the absence of a legitimate ruler.

It is misleading to describe the period simply as a collapse into disorder. Established institutions continued to function: revenue was collected, provincial governors kept their posts, and the court maintained diplomatic relations. But the fundamentals had changed. The ruler was a child, the regent a woman whose authority was contested by courtiers, the vizier a figure who survived only by managing the army, and the factions, Dogra, Sandhawalia, Attariwalla, and others, competed for patronage in the absence of an arbiter. External pressure compounded the instability. The Company's disaster in Afghanistan in 1842 and its conquest of Sindh in 1843, discussed in Division II, made the Lahore court wary of its southern neighbour, while the Company increased its garrisons near the Sutlej. By late 1845 a chain of tensions had accumulated: an army distrustful of its leaders, a court that feared its own soldiers, and a neighbouring power whose frontier preparations seemed menacing. Section 65.9 returns to the crisis that followed. First, the narrative turns south to Sindh, where the Company was meanwhile extending its power along the Indus.

Section 65.5

The Talpur Amirs of Sindh: Regional Government, the Indus, and Commercial Networks

Sindh, the lower Indus valley and delta, had been governed by the Kalhora dynasty through much of the eighteenth century, under varying degrees of Mughal and Durrani suzerainty. In 1783 the Talpurs, a Baloch tribal family that had served the Kalhoras, overthrew the last Kalhora ruler and established their own rule under Mir Fateh Ali Khan. Talpur rule was not a single centralized monarchy. Power was shared among several branches of the family, each headed by a mir or amir, who controlled a territory and its revenues. By the early nineteenth century the principal branches were the amirs of Hyderabad in the south, who held the senior position and the delta; the amirs of Khairpur in Upper Sindh, led for many years by Mir Sohrab Khan and later Mir Rustam Khan; and the amirs of Mirpur in the southeast, led by a branch of the family under Mir Sher Muhammad. These houses cooperated against outside threats but also competed over revenues, succession, and relations with outside powers.

The Indus defined Sindh's political economy. Annual flooding and a system of inundation canals made agriculture possible in a region of low rainfall. The amirs and their Baloch followers held much of the land through jagirs, and large tracts were reserved as hunting preserves, which British observers sometimes criticized. Cultivators, many of them Sindhi Muslims and also Hindus, paid a share of the crop to the ruler or jagirdar. Tribal chiefs, both Baloch and Sindhi, and religious figures such as the hereditary pirs and sayyids who headed shrines, held considerable local authority, and the amirs governed through them. Persian was the language of the court, while Sindhi was the language of most of the population; the Talpurs' own Baloch identity and language shaped the composition of the military and the court.

Commerce linked Sindh to a wide world. Karachi, Thatta, and smaller ports on the delta handled coastal trade with Kutch, Gujarat, Bombay, and the Arabian Sea, while river towns such as Hyderabad, Sukkur, and Shikarpur lay on the routes to Afghanistan and Central Asia. Shikarpur was the base of a remarkable banking and trading community of mostly Hindu merchants, whose credit instruments, or hundis, were honoured in markets as far away as Kabul, Kandahar, Bukhara, and beyond, and who financed caravan trade through the Bolan Pass. This commercial network meant that Sindh's economic significance exceeded its military power, and also that the commercial community had its own interests in political stability and, on occasion, in the arrival of new rulers.

Sindh's external relations were complicated by its location between Afghanistan, Punjab, Rajasthan, and the sea. The amirs had paid tribute to the Durrani rulers of Kabul and still owed it in theory; they were wary of Ranjit Singh's claims over Shikarpur; and they had links with Kutch and with the rulers of Jaisalmer and Bahawalpur. It would be a mistake to portray Sindh as a single government with one diplomatic policy. The Hyderabad, Khairpur, and Mirpur amirs frequently adopted different positions toward the Company. The elderly Rustam Khan of Khairpur, for example, was often more willing than some of the Hyderabad amirs to accommodate British demands, and within each house, family rivalry added further divisions. These divisions would be central to the story of Sindh's loss of independence, since the Company repeatedly exploited, and at times worsened, them.

Section 65.6

British Commercial and Strategic Intervention in Sindh, 1832–1842

The Company's first approach to Sindh was commercial and exploratory. British officials believed that the Indus could become a highway of trade between the sea and Central Asia, and they sent surveyors to chart the river. In 1831 Alexander Burnes was sent up the Indus with presents for Ranjit Singh, a mission whose commercial aims were officially declared to be secondary to its diplomatic ones, and Henry Pottinger conducted negotiations with the amirs. The resulting treaties of 1832, concluded between the Company and the amirs of Hyderabad and Khairpur separately, opened the river and roads of Sindh to the passage of merchants, prohibited the movement of military stores on the river, and established principles of friendship between the governments. They did not provide for British troops in Sindh or for any political supervision of the amirs' internal government. These treaties are best read as a limited commercial and diplomatic agreement, and not as a first step in a planned conquest. Yet they were also precedents, and the Company's later claims about the amirs' obligations drew on them.

British interest in Sindh increased in the mid-1830s as concern about Russian and Persian influence in Afghanistan grew. Pottinger was appointed Resident in Sindh in 1836, and his negotiations led to the abolition of some tolls and the formal opening of the river to trade. Ranjit Singh's intention to extend Sikh control over Shikarpur and the British determination to prevent it led to intervention by the Governor-General, Lord Auckland, who steered the dispute toward a settlement in 1838 that frustrated Lahore's designs and established the Company as the principal arbiter in the lower Indus. The amirs, as a result, found themselves in a new situation: the Company had protected them from Lahore, but it had done so on terms that gave it an increasing voice in their affairs.

The First Anglo-Afghan War transformed Sindh's position. In 1838 the Company resolved to replace the Afghan ruler Dost Muhammad Khan with the exiled Shah Shuja, and the Army of the Indus, assembled in Punjab and Sindh, needed to move through the Bolan Pass to Kandahar and Kabul. Passage through Sindh required supplies, transport, and permission, and British commanders pressed the amirs to provide them. Late in 1838 the Company imposed a treaty on Khairpur, and in early 1839 it demanded new terms from Hyderabad. The Hyderabad agreement approved by Governor-General Auckland on 11 March 1839 was made under pressure from the military campaign and after the capture of Karachi by naval forces in February 1839, which demonstrated the Company's readiness to use force. It differed in kind from the treaties of 1832: it provided for a British force within Sindh, financial contributions toward its maintenance, restriction on the amirs' dealings with other powers, and more complete freedom of navigation on the Indus.

Students should distinguish the treaties of 1832 and 1839 as separate agreements made under changing political circumstances. The first was a commercial arrangement between governments that regarded each other as sovereign. The second was drawn up in conditions of military pressure, under which the amirs had little practical freedom to refuse. The 1832 commercial agreements did not provide for a British garrison; the 1839 treaty expressly allowed a stationed British force but did not itself annex Sindh, while also placing the amirs’ foreign relations under Company supervision. The Company's political officers, among them Pottinger and, from 1839, James Outram as political agent in Lower Sindh, were charged with enforcing its terms, and their reports to Calcutta increasingly alleged that individual amirs had violated them by corresponding with Afghan or Persian courts or by obstructing British supply.

The disastrous retreat of the Kabul garrison in January 1842 altered the situation again. After the destruction of a British army in Afghanistan, the Company's military prestige was at stake, and the Army of Retribution was assembled to reoccupy Kabul. Sindh, through which supplies and reinforcements moved, became strategically vital. British officials now saw the amirs not as autonomous rulers but as potential threats to the line of communication, and began to regard their sovereignty as a hindrance to security. This reasoning, that fear of the Afghan frontier justified increased control over a neighbouring state that had no part in the Afghan crisis, is the core of the later controversy discussed in the Historical Debate below.

Historical Vocabulary
Subsidiary Alliance, Paramountcy, and Annexation

A subsidiary alliance, discussed in Chapter 64, was a treaty under which an Indian ruler accepted a Company force within his territory, paid for it, gave up an independent foreign policy, and received protection. Paramountcy was the later doctrine, developed through the nineteenth century and examined in Chapter 71, that the British Crown or the Company held supreme authority over Indian rulers who nevertheless retained their thrones and internal administration. Annexation was the direct incorporation of a state's territory into the Company's own government, ending the ruler's sovereignty.

The three describe different degrees of control, and their differences matter in this chapter. Sindh was largely annexed, with Khairpur as an exception; Punjab was annexed after a period of supervised regency; Bahawalpur and Jammu and Kashmir remained under paramountcy. The vocabulary was shaped by officials and often applied retrospectively, so historians treat it as an interpretive tool rather than a precise legal classification.

Section 65.7

Charles Napier and the Conquest of Sindh, 1843: Miani and Dubbo

The conquest of Sindh followed from the policies of Lord Ellenborough, who became Governor-General in 1842, and the temperament of the general he chose to command in the region, Charles James Napier. Napier arrived in Sindh in 1842 with orders that gave him both military command and political authority in Upper and Lower Sindh. He was an experienced and combative officer with firm views about the conduct of the amirs and the necessity of British control. Ellenborough, for his part, wished to settle accounts after the Afghan disaster and to secure the Indus line. Late in 1842 he authorized Napier to negotiate a revised treaty with the amirs, under which several amirs would be penalized for alleged misconduct, with territorial cessions and an end to certain tolls and obligations, and the language of his instructions left room for the use of force if the amirs refused.

The negotiations took place against a background of disagreement among British officers. Outram, who had known the amirs for years, believed that most had acted in good faith and that the accusations were exaggerated. Napier regarded the amirs as untrustworthy. The Khairpur succession dispute added further complexity: the aged Mir Rustam Khan transferred his turban, the symbol of headship, to his brother Mir Ali Murad, and Ali Murad became the Company's most useful local ally, receiving British support against his family. In February 1843 the Hyderabad amirs, under severe pressure and on the advice of some of their courtiers, signed the proposed treaty, but the Baloch chiefs and soldiers around them were angry at its terms. On 15 February a Baloch force attacked the British Residency at Hyderabad, where Outram was staying, and Outram withdrew with his escort. Whether the amirs authorized the attack, which they denied, or whether it reflected the independent action of tribal leaders, is disputed. For Napier it was a confirmation of hostile intent and the occasion for decisive action.

The Battle of Miani was fought on 17 February 1843 near Hyderabad. Napier's force, fewer than three thousand men by most accounts, comprised European infantry, Bombay and Bengal sepoys, and cavalry. The Baloch army, commanded by Mir Nasir Khan and other Hyderabad amirs, was several times larger by the estimates of British participants, though its numbers are uncertain and sourced largely from British reports. The Baloch held a position behind a dry riverbed with their flanks protected by woods and embankments, and fought with swords, matchlocks, and some artillery. The battle was bitterly contested, with fierce hand-to-hand fighting, but British artillery, infantry discipline, and a cavalry charge that turned the Baloch flank decided it. The Baloch suffered heavy losses, and the Hyderabad amirs surrendered to Napier within days. Hyderabad was occupied, and the amirs were sent into exile.

Resistance did not end. Mir Sher Muhammad Khan of Mirpur, who had not been at Miani, raised a force, and gathered Baloch and local followers; he is remembered in Sindhi tradition as the "Lion of Sindh" (Sher-i-Sindh). On 24 March 1843 Napier attacked his army at Dubbo (Dabo), near Hyderabad, and won another hard-fought battle. Napier's own report written on that day survives in published documentary collections, but it is a victor's account, concerned with the conduct of his troops and the assessment of the enemy. Sher Muhammad withdrew and continued to harass British authority for some months before withdrawing into Baluchistan. Although local resistance continued sporadically, the battles of Miani and Dubbo destroyed the amirs' effective political autonomy. They also showed the decisive advantages of disciplined infantry, artillery, and logistics over a tribal host with numerical superiority, advantages that the Company's army would have to rely on more heavily, and with less certainty, in the Punjab.

A popular story holds that Napier announced the conquest in a one-word Latin pun, "Peccavi," meaning "I have sinned." It is not found in his dispatches and is generally regarded as a joke published in the humorous magazine Punch in 1844. Students should treat it as a later invention and an example of how anecdote can attach itself to historical events.

Section 65.8

Annexation, Administration, and Resistance in Sindh after 1843

After the battles, the Company annexed most of Sindh. Ellenborough proclaimed the amirs deposed and the Company's authority established, and Napier was appointed governor, a post he held until 1847, when Sindh was attached to the Bombay Presidency and placed under a commissioner. The annexation incorporated the Hyderabad and Mirpur territories and Upper Sindh into the Company's direct administration. Mir Ali Murad of Khairpur, whose support was rewarded, continued to hold a diminished Khairpur as a princely ruler under British supremacy. Sindh therefore ended the conquest with two different statuses: directly governed territories and a surviving princely state, a distinction that anticipated the later structure of British India discussed in Chapter 71.

Napier's government was military, personal, and authoritarian. He reorganized the revenue system in the interests of simplicity and regular collection, reduced or abolished some of the privileges of the former ruling families and Baloch chiefs, disarmed parts of the population, and created a police force modelled partly on the Irish constabulary. The Baloch jagirdars and soldiers, whose military service had been the basis of the Talpur order, lost status and income, and many of the amirs' dependants and retainers lost their positions. In contrast, many Hindu merchants and officials, whose commercial interests did not depend on the Talpur court, found that the new order offered opportunities, and the Company relied extensively on Hindu clerks and accountants in its administration. Karachi, with its harbour and its position at the sea end of the Indus, grew rapidly under British rule from a modest port to a strategic and commercial centre, a transformation that began under Napier and continued for decades. Language policy changed later: in the 1850s, under the Commissioner Bartle Frere, Sindhi replaced Persian as the language of administration.

The conquest affected different groups differently. Landholders who had held under the amirs had to establish their claims under the new revenue regime. Cultivators found that the demand for revenue was assessed by officials unfamiliar with local practice and sometimes harsh in its operation, although some benefited from the end of arbitrary exactions. Irrigation, the lifeblood of the province, was managed through inundation canals whose maintenance had depended on local arrangements and labour; the British administration's attempts to organize it sometimes disrupted the established order, and larger irrigation works came later. Pastoral communities and tribal groups lost the autonomy that tribal chiefs had enjoyed. Resistance continued in the form of banditry, local rebellion, and occasional armed risings, which British officials described as lawlessness and which were also expressions of the loss of the old order. Sher Muhammad's resistance, remembered in Sindhi tradition, belongs in this wider pattern.

The legal and moral justification of the annexation was contested even among the British. Outram, who had been the Company's political agent in the region, wrote critical accounts of the conduct of the conquest, including a published commentary, and argued that the amirs had been treated unjustly. Others in the Company and in Parliament criticized the Governor-General's conduct, and Ellenborough was recalled by the Court of Directors in 1844 for reasons that included, though were not limited to, controversy over his policies. Napier's own brother William published a defence of Napier's administration. Napier's private journal, as published by his brother, is often cited for a remark acknowledging that the Company had no right to seize Sindh while predicting that doing so would be advantageous, but students should consult the published text itself before quoting it. Sindh's annexation is therefore a case in which the Company's expansion was disputed from within, and its history cannot be reduced to a settled consensus of the officials involved.

Historical Debate

Was the British Conquest of Sindh a Strategic Necessity or an Act of Imperial Aggression?

The question: Did the security of the Indus line and the amirs' alleged breaches of treaty justify annexation, or was the conquest an unjustified act of expansion?

Interpretation A — Strategic necessity: Supporters of the conquest, including Napier and Ellenborough, argued that after the Afghan disaster the Company needed a secure line of communication along the Indus, that the amirs had violated their treaty obligations by obstructing British movements and intriguing with hostile powers, and that the attack on the Residency demonstrated their hostility. In this view Sindh's fragmented rule could not be trusted to maintain order or to guarantee free navigation, and annexation was a regrettable but necessary result.
Interpretation B — Imperial aggression: Critics, including Outram and many later historians, argued that the amirs had been pressured into treaties they could not refuse, that the alleged violations were exaggerated or based on forged or doubtful evidence, that the amirs had not been responsible for the Afghan disaster, and that the Company pursued conquest for strategic and commercial advantage under cover of treaty language. In this view the decision to annex reflected the aims of the Governor-General and his general more than any real security threat.

Evidence: The debate depends on treaty texts, the political correspondence of Pottinger, Outram, and Napier, the dispatches of Ellenborough, parliamentary papers, and the surviving evidence of the amirs' own position. Modern Sindhi historians, working with local sources, have contributed alternative narratives of the conquest and of Talpur rule.

Why it matters: The debate distinguishes the strategic reasoning given by Company authorities from the separate question of whether that reasoning justified the act. It also bears on how the Company's expansion should be understood: as a response to security needs, or as a pattern in which security arguments supported expansion that other motives also encouraged.

Division III · Sections 65.9–65.12

The First Anglo-Sikh War and the Subordination of Lahore

The crisis of 1845, the four battles of the war, the treaties of 1846, and the transfer of effective power at Lahore to a British Resident.

Section 65.9

The Crisis of 1845: The Khalsa Army, the Lahore Court, and the Sutlej Frontier

Relations between Lahore and the Company deteriorated steadily after 1839. The Company's reverses in Afghanistan briefly raised Sikh confidence, since the Sikh state had contributed troops to the campaign and had aided the passage of the Army of Retribution. But the annexation of Sindh in 1843 showed that the Company would act decisively against an Indian state whose position it judged strategically inconvenient. Lahore's officers could see that the lower Indus was now British, and that the Punjab's southern flank was exposed. At the same time a series of smaller disputes arose over the estates of deceased Sikh chiefs in the Cis-Sutlej territories, over rights of passage, and over the treatment of fugitives, in which the Company's agents on the Sutlej, notably the political agent George Broadfoot, took a strict and sometimes provocative line.

The Company's military preparations also alarmed Lahore. From 1844 it strengthened its garrisons at Ferozepur, Ludhiana, and Ambala, constructed roads, and assembled river-crossing equipment and supplies, measures that Governor-General Lord Hardinge, who arrived in 1844, justified as prudent precautions against an army the Company regarded as undisciplined and unpredictable. From the perspective of the Lahore court and the regimental panchayats, however, these movements looked like preparations for an invasion, and the Sindh precedent suggested what such an invasion might aim to achieve. Both sides reasoned from fear, and each interpreted the other's precautions as hostile intent.

Within Lahore, the political situation was dominated by competing interests. Maharani Jind Kaur, acting as regent for the child Maharaja, sought to protect her son's position and her own; her brother Jawahir Singh had been executed by the army in September 1845. Lal Singh, who became vizier in the autumn, and Tej Singh, the commander of the army, were courtiers whose positions depended on the army's tolerance and whose personal loyalties were, according to some British and Punjabi accounts, divided. The army itself was powerful, politically assertive, and suspicious of the court. Historians have proposed several explanations of why Lahore moved toward war: that the court, unable to control the army, deliberately sent it against the British in the hope that it would be destroyed; that the army itself resolved to act against a perceived British threat; that the Company's own policy provoked a conflict it expected to win; or some combination. None of these explanations is proven, and they rest on evidence from participants with strong motives to place responsibility elsewhere.

In December 1845, Sikh forces began crossing the Sutlej. The National Army Museum, summarizing the evidence, gives 11 December as the date of the crossing while noting that the motives of leading participants remain disputed. The crossing violated the Treaty of Amritsar of 1809, as the Company declared in a proclamation that annexed the Sikh possessions on the left bank of the river. But the section must avoid portraying the outbreak of war as the straightforward result of an irrational invasion. Contemporary and later sources differ regarding the intentions of Sikh commanders, the conduct of the Lahore court, and the significance of British military preparations, and the evidence is insufficient to establish that the crossing was either a premeditated attack or a defensive response to British encirclement. What can be said with confidence is that a state with a powerful, politically engaged army and a divided court faced a neighbour that had recently annexed Sindh, and that the decision to cross brought about the first of the two wars.

Section 65.10

The First Anglo-Sikh War, 1845–1846: Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, and Sobraon

The war was fought in four principal engagements over about two months. The Company's army was commanded by the Commander-in-Chief, Sir Hugh Gough, with the Governor-General, Lord Hardinge, serving as his second-in-command, an unusual arrangement. It was composed of British regiments, European officers, and Indian sepoys of the Bengal Army, with the support of artillery. Its opponents, the Khalsa army, fielded disciplined infantry, a strong artillery arm, and cavalry, and fought on ground of its own choosing. Contemporary British officers acknowledged the quality of Sikh gunnery and the determination of the soldiers.

The first battle, at Mudki on 18 December 1845, was a confused encounter at dusk in which Company forces, arriving exhausted after a long march, engaged part of the Sikh army advancing under Lal Singh. The British forces held the field but lost many officers, including the veteran Sir Robert Sale. At Ferozeshah on 21–22 December, the Company attacked a fortified Sikh camp. The first day's assault failed to carry the entrenchments and darkness fell with the Company's army in a precarious position; contemporary accounts describe a night of confusion, heavy casualties, and genuine anxiety for the outcome. The next morning the attack was renewed and the position taken, but the Sikh forces then withdrew under circumstances that British accounts attribute to the unexpected inaction or retreat of Tej Singh's force, leaving the Company in possession of the field with its army severely damaged. Political agent George Broadfoot was among those killed.

The two battles had not defeated the Sikh army. The Company's position in January 1846 was difficult, and reinforcements and supplies, including heavy guns, had to be brought up. Meanwhile a Sikh force crossed the Sutlej at Ludhiana, and Sir Harry Smith, with a detachment, attacked it at Aliwal on 28 January 1846 and won a clear victory in an open field engagement in which the superior cavalry and artillery of the Company prevailed. This was the first unambiguous Company success.

The final battle was at Sobraon on 10 February 1846. The Khalsa army occupied a strongly entrenched position on the left bank of the Sutlej, in a bend of the river, supported by a bridge of boats across the river behind it and by heavy artillery. The British attacked after a prolonged bombardment, and the fighting was bitter. The Sikh forces repelled early assaults, but eventually the Company's infantry broke into the entrenchments. Many Sikh soldiers, with their retreat cut off when the bridge was damaged, were killed or drowned, and the veteran commander Sham Singh Attariwalla died in the fighting. Tej Singh, the Sikh commander, had withdrawn early in the day, and Lal Singh's role was also questioned. The Company's casualties were heavy, and the contest was neither easy nor effortless. The question of how far the conduct of Lal Singh and Tej Singh contributed to the outcome has been debated since 1846. British officers claimed that secret communication existed between these Sikh leaders and British political agents, and some Sikh and Punjabi traditions regard them as traitors. The evidence of private correspondence is partial, and historians distinguish between what is documented, namely that these commanders left their army at critical moments, and what is alleged, namely that they acted according to a prior agreement with the Company.

The First Anglo-Sikh War was therefore a difficult campaign and not an effortless British victory. The Khalsa army fought with discipline and determination, inflicted heavy casualties, and was defeated in part by the political division of its own leadership. These facts are important for the larger argument. The Company's military superiority was not the whole explanation for its success, and its victory in the first war left the Sikh army damaged but not destroyed, and the Sikh state diminished but not yet extinguished.

Two features of the Company's army deserve emphasis. First, most of its soldiers were Indian. The sepoys of the Bengal Army, recruited largely from Awadh, Bihar, and the Gangetic plain, fought Sikh opponents drawn from the Punjab, and the war was in that sense one in which Indian soldiers from different regions fought on both sides under different commands and for different employers. Their experience of the campaign, including the hardships of long marches, the loss of officers, and the strain of a winter war far from home, is less fully recorded than that of their British officers, and it would later bear on the question of service conditions discussed in §65.19. Second, the Sikh artillery was formidable. Entrenched guns, served by trained gunners and supported by infantry in prepared positions, forced the Company to rely on its own heavy guns and on costly infantry assaults, and British accounts of the war are marked by respect for the opponent they had expected to despise. The campaign also revealed the limits of the Company's logistics: ammunition, transport animals, and reinforcements had to be brought forward under pressure, and the pause in January 1846 owed as much to supply as to tactics.

Section 65.11

The Treaty of Lahore, March 1846, and the Transformation of Kashmir

After Sobraon, the Company's army crossed the Sutlej and advanced to Lahore. The settlement that followed consisted of two distinct treaties negotiated within a week, and students must keep them separate. The first, the Treaty of Lahore, was signed on 9 March 1846 between the Company and the Lahore Darbar, representing the young Maharaja Duleep Singh. Under its terms, the Lahore state ceded to the Company the Jalandhar Doab, the fertile territory between the Sutlej and the Beas, and gave up all claims to the territories south of the Sutlej. It agreed to pay an indemnity of one and a half crore of rupees, and because the Lahore treasury could not pay in full, it ceded in lieu of the remainder the hill territories between the Beas and the Indus, including Kashmir and Hazara. It also agreed to limit its army to a fixed establishment of twenty thousand infantry and twelve thousand cavalry, to disband mutinous regiments, and to surrender the guns that had been used against the British. The treaty recognized Duleep Singh as maharaja, with Lal Singh as vizier, and was supplemented by articles agreed on 11 March permitting a British force to remain at Lahore for the balance of 1846, originally for the protection of the Maharaja and reorganization of the army.

The second agreement, the Treaty of Amritsar of 16 March 1846, was a separate treaty between the Company and Gulab Singh of Jammu. Gulab Singh, the Dogra brother who had risen at Ranjit Singh's court, had remained largely aloof from the fighting and was used by the Company as an intermediary in the peace negotiations, and he had acted as a mediator in arranging the terms. Under the treaty the Company transferred to him, as an independent ruler, the hill territories it had acquired from Lahore between the Indus and the Ravi, including Kashmir, and Gulab Singh agreed to pay seventy-five lakh rupees to the Company, to acknowledge British supremacy, and to present each year symbolic tribute, a horse, shawl goats, and pairs of shawls. This created the state later known as Jammu and Kashmir, ruled by Gulab Singh and his successors under British paramountcy.

The Treaty of Amritsar of 1846 must not be presented as a simple commercial sale devoid of context. It was embedded in the military and diplomatic settlement of the war: the Company had demanded territory in lieu of an indemnity that Lahore could not pay; Gulab Singh had both the means to pay and an interest in obtaining a state of his own; and the Company wished to avoid the costs of administering the distant mountain territory and to create a buffer ruled by a figure who owed his position to it. For Gulab Singh the treaty fulfilled long-standing ambitions of the Dogra house. For the population of Kashmir, a Muslim-majority province with a distinctive shawl industry and its own political traditions, the transfer meant rule by a Hindu Dogra dynasty with no consultation. The Sikh governor of Kashmir, Sheikh Imam-ud-din, initially refused to surrender the province, and Gulab Singh took possession only with the help of Company-backed Lahore troops in the autumn of 1846. This history is central to later controversies over Kashmir's status, examined in Chapters 87 and 105, and later political memory has treated the 1846 transfer in sharply different ways.

The terms of March 1846 were designed to leave the Lahore state in existence but weakened, with its army reduced, its finances burdened, its most valuable northern territories removed, and a British force on its territory. They did not, however, resolve the political problem of who would control the Lahore Darbar. That question was answered by the Treaty of Bhairowal in December.

The financial clauses need to be read in their own sequence. The Lahore government's indemnity was reckoned at one and a half crore rupees, with the cession of hill territories assigned a substantial monetary value in the treaty. The separate cash-payment clause and the subsequent treaty with Gulab Singh cannot responsibly be merged into a single shorthand claim that Kashmir was merely sold for the entirety of Lahore's indemnity. Different instruments addressed the conquered state's liability, the Company's territorial acquisition, and the establishment of Gulab Singh's authority. The resulting documents also made economic geography a diplomatic instrument: the Jalandhar Doab offered land and revenue readily integrated with neighboring Company districts, whereas Kashmir and the mountains presented difficult communications, diverse local rights, and the necessity of continued negotiation with hill rulers. Treaties determined formal rights, but possession still required military and administrative action.

Section 65.12

The Treaty of Bhairowal, December 1846, and British Control of the Lahore Government

The Company had intended its British force to leave Lahore at the end of 1846. But the political situation made withdrawal seem dangerous. The Lahore Darbar was divided, the army had been reduced but was not entirely subdued, and Lal Singh, the vizier, was implicated in a conspiracy to prevent the transfer of Kashmir to Gulab Singh, an affair in which Sheikh Imam-ud-din had resisted the new ruler. Following an inquiry, Lal Singh was deposed and exiled in December 1846. The Company's agents, led by the Resident, Henry Lawrence, who took charge at Lahore as the settlement was worked out, argued that a withdrawal would cause the collapse of the government, and the sardars themselves, who feared the army and each other, asked the British to stay.

The Treaty of Bhairowal, signed on 16 December 1846 and ratified on 26 December, set out the new arrangement; these two dates should be distinguished. The Company's force would remain at Lahore until Duleep Singh came of age, then expected in 1854. During the minority, the government would be carried on by a Council of Regency composed of leading sardars, working under the control of the British Resident, whose authority extended to all departments of government. The Lahore state would pay twenty-two lakh Nanakshahi rupees each year for the British force and associated expenses. The Maharani Jind Kaur was removed from power and given a pension, and her political role ended; she was moved from Lahore in 1847 after allegations that she was intriguing against the arrangement, and later deported from the Punjab, a measure she and her supporters bitterly resented.

The effect was to transfer decisive power to the Company's representatives. The Resident could dismiss officials, direct policy, and control the army and finances, and his district officers, working as advisers to the Council's local administrators, extended British influence through the provinces. The Resident's authority was exercised in the name of a child Maharaja who remained on the throne, and the Sikh kingdom was nominally independent. But it was independent in form only. In the language of Chapter 71, the arrangement was not yet annexation but a form of supervised dependency, going further than the paramountcy exercised over rulers elsewhere because it reached into daily administration.

The arrangement had fundamental weaknesses. The Company's officers governed through intermediaries, relying on the Council, local revenue officials, and sardars whose loyalty to the British was uncertain. Soldiers discharged in the reduction of the army, landholders who lost status, and followers of the Maharani were left disaffected. The Resident's task was to hold together an administrative structure whose cooperation depended on a legitimacy the Company itself was undermining. Within two years that tension would be exposed in Multan.

The practical working of the regency in 1847 and 1848 reveals how supervision operated. Henry Lawrence and his assistants, notably Herbert Edwardes, John Nicholson, Harry Lumsden, and Reynell Taylor, were posted to districts and to the Lahore court, where they directed revenue collection, reviewed appointments, and began to reorganize the army, reducing the number of regiments and discharging soldiers whose loyalty was doubted. These officers combined the language of advising the Council with the reality of command, and some, like Lawrence, argued that the Sikh nobility should be treated generously. Others in the Company, including the Governor-General, were increasingly inclined to see the regency as a temporary measure. The tension between a policy of supporting a reformed Sikh government and a policy of replacing it was never resolved before events in 1848 forced the question.

The Residency’s personnel also changed in a way that illustrates the difference between continuous Company policy and the judgment of individual officers. Henry Lawrence, who preferred to work through influential sardars, departed on sick leave late in 1847. His brother John temporarily carried on the duties, and Frederick Currie formally assumed the Residency in March 1848. Henry Lawrence did not return to the post until February 1849, during the second war. A change of Resident could alter the tone of negotiations and the handling of a provincial dispute even while the decisive power remained with the Governor-General and the British-supervised council. The administrative responsibility attributed vaguely to “the Resident” must therefore be connected to particular men and dates, especially when evaluating the events at Multan.

Historical Vocabulary
Resident and Political Officer

A Resident was a Company official stationed at the court of an Indian ruler to represent the Company, report on the state's affairs, and, depending on the treaty, supervise its policy. A political officer or political agent was a Company official attached to a region or ruler, who combined diplomatic and often military or administrative functions. In 1846 Henry Lawrence at Lahore held the office of Resident, and he and other British officers worked as political agents for the Company in the Punjab.

These offices were the Company's principal instruments of indirect rule. Students should recognize that the same title could denote a role of courteous diplomacy in one place and of effective control in another: the Resident at Lahore after Bhairowal exercised far greater authority than the Resident at a court with which the Company had only a loose connection.

The Second Anglo-Sikh War and the Annexation of Punjab

The Multan uprising, the war of 1848–1849, the annexation of March 1849, and the first Company administration of Punjab.

Section 65.13

The Multan Uprising of 1848 and the Breakdown of the Lahore Settlement

The settlement of 1846 left unresolved grievances. Sikh soldiers discharged from the Khalsa army returned to villages where land and employment were uncertain. Sardars who had lost jagirs or influence under the Council of Regency, and officers who regarded the Resident's authority as an insult to the Khalsa, formed a reservoir of discontent. The Maharani Jind Kaur, removed from Lahore in 1847 and exiled from the Punjab in 1848, retained supporters who regarded her treatment as an injury to the royal house. Provincial governors, whose positions depended on the Lahore government, found themselves subject to demands from British officers whose authority they did not fully accept. The Resident, Sir Frederick Currie, who took charge in March 1848 after John Lawrence had served temporarily during Henry Lawrence’s sick leave, had few means of managing these tensions except through the Council and the Company's troops.

The crisis began in Multan, the southern province, where the governor was Diwan Mulraj, son of the former governor Sawan Mal, who had died in 1844. Lahore demanded from Mulraj a succession payment, or nazrana, and an increased revenue assessment, and the Company's officers pressed for reorganization of the province's administration. Mulraj, claiming that the demands were heavy, offered to resign. The Resident accepted, and sent Patrick Vans Agnew of the Bengal Civil Service and Lieutenant William Anderson, with a Sikh escort under Sardar Kahan Singh, to take over the administration. In April 1848 at Multan the two British officers were attacked; they were wounded on 19 April and killed the following day. The National Army Museum notes that Mulraj was unlikely to have ordered the killings, which broke out among his troops and in the city, although the violence helped precipitate his rebellion. His direct responsibility must not be treated as established. Having been drawn into rebellion, Mulraj mobilized Multan's resources, and the dispute he had begun as an administrative quarrel became a revolt against the Company's authority.

The Company's response, directed by the Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, who had arrived in January 1848, was initially cautious. The Commander-in-Chief, Gough, argued against a campaign in the hot season, and Currie's own forces were limited. It was young political officers who acted: Lieutenant Herbert Edwardes, then operating on the Bannu and Derajat frontier, raised levies of Pashtun and other irregulars, allied with the Nawab of Bahawalpur, and defeated Mulraj's forces in two engagements in the summer, confining him to Multan. These actions showed the Company's reliance on Indian allies and irregular forces. A Lahore force under Sardar Sher Singh Attariwalla was sent to assist the siege of Multan, but in September 1848 Sher Singh joined Mulraj, and in the north his father, Chattar Singh Attariwalla, governor of Hazara, rose in rebellion. These defections turned a provincial revolt into a wider movement, supported by soldiers of the Khalsa and by sardars who saw the revolt as a defence of Sikh sovereignty. The Afghan ruler Dost Muhammad Khan eventually allied with the rebels, motivated by his desire to recover Peshawar, and Afghan troops entered the Punjab in 1849.

A further distinction is necessary between a revolt beginning in a contested provincial succession and a fully coordinated effort to restore the Sikh monarchy. Mulraj had negotiated over the terms of surrendering his governorship and the fiscal claims upon Multan before the killings transformed the situation. The subsequent participation of Sher Singh and Chattar Singh, and the use of Sikh royal symbols by some insurgents, widened the issue into a defense of political sovereignty. Other Punjabi officers and rulers nevertheless continued to cooperate with Company officials, and the Nawab of Bahawalpur provided crucial military assistance against Multan. Those divergent choices complicate any portrayal of the second war as an undifferentiated struggle between two national camps. They also demonstrate that the Company's administration could exploit alliances across older religious and political boundaries while its own provincial policy created new grievances.

Cause and Effect

Why the Lahore Settlement Broke Down in 1848

Long-term causes

The collapse of central authority after 1839; the political power of the Khalsa army; the Company's supervision of Lahore after 1846; and the resentment of soldiers, sardars, and royal supporters at the loss of sovereignty.

Short-term causes

The dispute over Mulraj's revenue and succession payment; the replacement of the governor by British officers; the exile of Jind Kaur; and the killing of Agnew and Anderson in April 1848.

Immediate effects

Mulraj's revolt; Edwardes's levy campaign; the defection of Sher Singh Attariwalla; a rising in Hazara; and the Company's decision to prepare for a full campaign.

Long-term consequences

The Second Anglo-Sikh War, the annexation of Punjab, and the end of the experiment in supervised regency.

Historical complexity: A single incident at Multan did not by itself cause a war. It triggered a crisis because the political arrangement of 1846 had left many groups with reasons to oppose it, and because the Company's response was shaped by Dalhousie's own views about the future of the Punjab.

Section 65.14

The Second Anglo-Sikh War, 1848–1849: Ramnagar, Chillianwala, Multan, and Gujrat

The Company assembled an army under Gough in the autumn of 1848 and crossed the Ravi in November, while a separate force under General William Whish besieged Multan. The first major engagement was at Ramnagar on the Chenab on 22 November 1848, where a British cavalry attack on the Sikh position beside the river was repulsed with loss, including the death of Brigadier Charles Cureton. After a further skirmish at Sadullapur, Gough moved toward the Jhelum, where Sher Singh had taken up a strong position near the village of Chillianwala, supported by artillery and concealed in dense scrub and jungle.

At Chillianwala on 13 January 1849, Gough attacked late in the afternoon, against the advice of some of his officers, after an advance through difficult scrub. The battle was a confused and costly encounter. British brigades advancing through the jungle were met by heavy fire; cavalry units broke in disorder; guns and regimental colours were lost; and Company casualties were severe, well over two thousand killed and wounded by British official returns. Both sides claimed the field, and the news in Britain was received with shock and contributed to Gough's replacement by Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror of Sindh. However, Chillianwala did not produce a decisive strategic outcome for the Sikhs. The Sikh army withdrew soon afterward and the Company army, though shaken, remained intact, and the sardars lacked the means to follow up with an offensive across the Punjab.

Meanwhile Whish's besieging force at Multan continued its operations. Mulraj's garrison held out through December, but after a magazine explosion and the assault of the town in late December and early January, the city fell and the citadel surrendered on 22 January 1849. Mulraj was taken prisoner. The capture released Whish's troops, including substantial siege artillery, to reinforce Gough, and this reinforcement was decisive. Sher Singh, hoping to link up with the Afghan force advancing from Peshawar and Attock, manoeuvred north of the Chenab, with the Company's army and Whish's arriving columns converging against him.

The decisive battle was fought at Gujrat on 21 February 1849. Gough, now with greater numbers and a large concentration of artillery, opened with a long artillery bombardment, silencing the Sikh guns before the infantry advanced, a method that minimized the casualties that had been so heavy at Chillianwala. The Sikh and Afghan forces were routed; the Company's cavalry pursued the retreating army, and a column under Sir Walter Gilbert pursued the Afghan contingent to the Khyber, where it withdrew from the Punjab. Sher Singh and Chattar Singh surrendered at Rawalpindi on 14 March, and the Sikh army laid down its arms. The surrender of soldiers, according to British accounts, was accompanied by a ceremony in which Khalsa soldiers placed their weapons on the ground.

The distinction between military outcomes and the claims of commanders is important here. Gough defended his conduct at Chillianwala as a victory, and Napier and others criticized him. Dalhousie's own correspondence, and the accounts of the campaign by officers such as Edwardes and Whish, reflected their interest in securing credit and justifying the policy of annexation. The Sikh side of the campaign is less fully documented in English, and reconstruction depends on Punjabi traditions, Persian reports, and the testimony of participants. The causes of the Sikh defeat included the loss of Multan, the shortage of supplies and reinforcements, the weakness of coordination with the Afghan allies, and the Company's ability to concentrate its army and its guns.

Allies and logistics also shaped the campaign. The Company's forces drew on Indian allies and contractors: the Nawab of Bahawalpur and the levies raised by Edwardes at Multan, the princes of the Cis-Sutlej states who supplied men and supplies, and the commissariat contractors and grain merchants who fed and transported the army. The Sikh forces depended on the resources of the central Punjab and on the support of villages that sheltered and supplied them, but their hope of reinforcement by an Afghan army proved only partly realized. The movement of both armies across the Chenab, Jhelum, and their tributaries, which flooded and shifted with the seasons, shaped when and where they could fight.

Section 65.15

The Annexation of Punjab, March 29, 1849: Dalhousie, Duleep Singh, and the End of the Sikh Kingdom

The decision to annex followed victory but had been prepared by Dalhousie's own reading of the situation. The Governor-General, in his mid-thirties when he arrived in India, was convinced that the Lahore government had shown itself incapable of maintaining order and that the arrangement of 1846 had failed. In his view, the revolt of 1848, which involved sardars of the Council and the regular army, demonstrated that the Sikh state could not be trusted, and he preferred to settle the question permanently by direct rule. He acted without waiting for the approval of the Company's directors in London, who were informed after the event. Dalhousie's later reputation as a ruler who favoured annexation, expressed in the doctrine of lapse discussed in Chapter 69, owes much to the Punjab settlement.

The terms were presented to the Lahore Darbar and accepted by the young Maharaja and the sardars on 29 March 1849. They provided that Duleep Singh would resign for himself and his heirs all right to the sovereignty of the Punjab; that all property of the state would be confiscated to the Company to meet the debts and costs of the war; that the Koh-i-Noor diamond, acquired by Ranjit Singh from Shah Shuja in 1813, would be surrendered to the Queen of England; and that provision would be made for a pension of four to five lakh rupees annually for Duleep Singh and other named dependants, with his personal entitlement conditioned on his obedience and residence where directed, and that he would live under British guardianship at a place chosen by the Company. The Sikh Museum Initiative reproduces the terms as part of its Anglo-Sikh treaty project, and students should consult published texts for exact wording. Duleep Singh was placed under the care of a British surgeon, John Login, and taken to Fatehgarh in the North-Western Provinces; in 1853 he became a Christian, and in 1854 he was taken to England. Maharani Jind Kaur, separated from her son, was held at Chunar and escaped in 1849 to Nepal; they would not be reunited until January 1861. The Koh-i-Noor reached London in 1850 and was displayed at the Great Exhibition in 1851.

Annexation must be distinguished from the preceding Residency. After 1846 the Company's Resident had governed through a Sikh monarch and a Council, and the Company had held the Punjab's affairs in trust for a minor. In 1849, the Company's territorial government replaced the independent Lahore kingdom, not as a continuation of the same legal arrangement but as a new order in which the Company claimed sovereignty and the Punjab became part of its dominions. The change also had political implications within the Company's system: the Punjab had once been described as a buffer, and was now the Company's frontier territory.

Reactions varied. Some sardars and soldiers accepted the outcome with resignation, others with resentment. Many Muslim and Hindu subjects, whose experience of the later Lahore court had been of insecurity, may have been more concerned with whether the new rulers would provide order and reasonable revenue demands than with the fate of the dynasty, though the evidence for such attitudes is thin and has been interpreted differently by historians writing from Punjabi, British, and nationalist perspectives. In the Sikh community, the annexation was remembered as a loss of sovereignty, and the exile of Duleep Singh and Jind Kaur became part of Sikh historical memory.

The annexation's legal form merits close attention. The terms of March 29 were presented as a grant accepted by a ruler who was barely ten and whose court had already been defeated; it would be misleading to read his signature as evidence of an unconstrained negotiation. The document's provisions for sovereignty, state property, the Koh-i-Noor, and an allowance are separate matters: political title was extinguished, the material resources of the government were transferred, and the former ruler was placed within an enforceable framework of dependency. The Governor-General subsequently ratified the terms, while the administration created for Punjab operated under the Company's authority rather than the Lahore Darbar's. The new government's claims of legality should be considered alongside the coercive circumstances in which its instruments were executed.

Historical Debate

Was Punjab's Annexation Inevitable after Ranjit Singh's Death?

The question: Did the structure of Sikh politics and the Company's power make annexation inevitable by 1839, or did contingent events and decisions determine the outcome?

Interpretation A — Structural weakness: On this view, the Sikh state depended on a monarch whose personal authority could not be replaced, and its army's political independence made it unmanageable. Succession crises and factional murder destroyed the court's credibility, and the Company's military and fiscal resources made its eventual dominance probable. The war of 1845 and the revolt of 1848 followed from this weakness.
Interpretation B — Contingency and intervention: On this view, the Company's decisions mattered as much as Lahore's weakness. The conquest of Sindh and the military preparations at the Sutlej helped provoke the crisis of 1845; the Khalsa army fought two difficult wars and came close to defeating the Company at Ferozeshah and Chillianwala; the settlement of 1846 created resentments; and Dalhousie's decision to annex in 1849 was a choice that other governors-general might not have made.

Evidence: The debate rests on Company correspondence, Persian and Punjabi chronicles, treaties, the military record of the two wars, and the accounts of participants such as Lawrence, Edwardes, and Dalhousie. The conduct of commanders such as Lal Singh and Tej Singh remains disputed.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether Company expansion should be understood as an inevitable result of Indian weakness or as the product of choices made by both Indian and British actors. Many historians now combine the two interpretations, arguing that Lahore's instability created the opening but that the Company's decisions determined how it was used.

Section 65.16

The First British Administration of Punjab, 1849–1853: The Lawrence Brothers and the Board of Administration

The Company's first government of the Punjab was entrusted to a Board of Administration, created by instruments dated 31 March 1849 and consisting of three members: Henry Lawrence as president, responsible for political relations and the management of the sardars and the army; his younger brother John Lawrence, responsible for finance and revenue; and Charles Mansel, responsible for justice. Mansel was replaced in 1851 by Robert Montgomery. The Board governed through a staff of young officers, soldiers and civil servants who were granted wide discretion and expected to combine military, judicial, revenue, and police functions in their districts. The Punjab was a non-regulation province, that is, a province exempt from the formal regulations that governed the older Presidencies and administered through a simpler, more personal system.

The Board's responsibilities were extensive. It disarmed the population and demolished or reduced forts, organized police, restructured the courts, started surveys and a summary assessment of land revenue, planned roads and irrigation, and managed relations with the surviving sardars and local chiefs. It depended heavily on Indian personnel: Punjabi revenue officials who had served the Sikh state, village headmen who collected revenue and kept order, soldiers recruited from the former Khalsa, and local chiefs whose cooperation was rewarded. The Company's young officers could work only through such intermediaries because they were few, and often ignorant of local languages and custom.

The two Lawrences disagreed over policy. Henry Lawrence, who had worked closely with the Sikh elite since 1846, favoured leaving established landed and military leaders in possession of their jagirs and treating them with consideration as a means of securing their loyalty. John Lawrence, with his focus on fiscal efficiency, regarded the jagirdars as a financial burden and preferred to reduce their grants and to strengthen the direct relationship between government and the village cultivators. The disagreement was partly personal and partly a matter of principle, and in practice John's policy of reduced jagirs and a moderate, simple revenue demand prevailed. The disagreement became so serious that in 1853 Dalhousie abolished the Board, transferring Henry Lawrence to Rajputana and appointing John Lawrence as chief commissioner of the Punjab, with a more centralized authority and, beneath him, commissioners and deputy commissioners.

The Punjab administration in these years became known informally as the "Punjab system," a style of paternal, personal government in which district officers combined many functions, rules were kept simple, and the government sought to protect cultivators and win the acquiescence of village society. The sources for this administration are voluminous, written by the officers themselves, and they tend to present it favourably. Modern historians have read these records critically, noting both the effectiveness of the system and the military and fiscal priorities it served. Detailed analysis of land tenure and agrarian change belongs to Chapter 66; for present purposes the Board's revenue policy is significant as an explanation of how the Company financed and secured its new province.

The Board also had to resolve the status of the Sikh nobility and former soldiers. Many sardars retained part of their estates, usually reduced, and some received pensions; others lost everything because they had taken part in the rebellion. The Khalsa army was disbanded, its soldiers paid off and disarmed, and large numbers took up cultivation, entered the police, or enlisted in the new regiments the Board and the Punjab Irregular Force were raising. Revenue settlements were made quickly and at moderate rates in the hope of conciliating the cultivators, and the Board's officers toured districts to hear complaints and to settle disputes in person. Courts were simplified, and the Board promoted the building of roads, among them the continuation of the Grand Trunk Road, and surveys for canals. The Board's government was characteristically energetic, but its personal character also meant that outcomes varied from district to district with the abilities and prejudices of the officer in charge.

The administrative record makes possible a more exact assessment than a generalized contrast between paternalism and extraction. A letter dated March 31, 1849, preserved in British Library IOR/H/760, constituted the Board and allocated duties among the president and two colleagues. The same file includes correspondence on finance, currency, civil and criminal justice, education, district establishments, and customs. These categories show the immediate challenges of government: paying officials, deciding which coins and duties would be accepted, staffing local courts, and communicating decisions over a large province. Their existence demonstrates extensive administrative activity, but the colonial papers also require critical reading. A rule communicated from Lahore did not necessarily reach a village uniformly, and the stated intention to moderate assessments does not by itself establish what cultivators paid in every district.

Section 65.17

Frontier Administration: Peshawar, the Indus, and the Limits of Company Authority

The annexation of Punjab extended the Company's government to the foothills of the mountains between the Indus plain and Afghanistan. The Sikh state had held Peshawar and the trans-Indus districts, but with a combination of garrisons, revenue demands, and punitive expeditions that left much of the hill country effectively autonomous. The Company inherited this frontier and a set of problems that Sikh rulers had never fully solved. Its administered districts included Peshawar, Hazara, Kohat, Bannu, and Dera Ismail Khan, together with the crossing of the Indus at Attock and the approaches to the Khyber and other passes. Beyond them lay mountain regions inhabited by Pashtun tribal communities, among them Afridis, Yusufzais, Waziris, and others, whom the Company did not govern and could not easily control.

British frontier officers, often young military men who were also political officers, developed their own style of administration. Herbert Edwardes at Bannu, John Nicholson in the Bannu and later Peshawar region, James Abbott in Hazara, and Harry Lumsden, who raised the Corps of Guides in 1846, were typical of the group. They worked through local chiefs, village councils, and tribal custom, collected revenue in the settled districts, and used subsidies, hostages, and punitive expeditions in dealing with the hills. Forts were built or rebuilt at strategic points, including a fort at Bannu, roads were constructed, and a network of informants provided intelligence. The Company also raised local military formations: from 1849 and 1851, a Punjab Irregular Force, later known as the Frontier Force, recruited from Punjabis, Pashtuns, and others, was placed under the Punjab government's control and stationed along the frontier. Armed police and levies from local communities supplemented the regular garrisons.

The relationship with Afghanistan was also a matter of frontier policy. Dost Muhammad Khan had allied with the Sikh rebels in 1849 in the hope of regaining Peshawar, and the Company's victory at Gujrat ended that prospect. The two governments later moved toward a settlement: a treaty concluded at Peshawar in 1855 brought a formal arrangement of friendship and mutual respect for each other's territories, and the Company hoped that a friendly Afghanistan would serve as a buffer. The treaty reflected a lesson of the earlier Afghan war, that intervention in Kabul was costly, and a more cautious approach replaced it.

Two cautions apply. First, the Company's formally administered districts must be distinguished from the autonomous and partly controlled communities beyond them. The government's authority ended at a line that officials called the administrative border, and the communities beyond it dealt with the Company through subsidies, agreements, and periodic armed expeditions, as in the operations against hill communities in the early 1850s. Second, the administrative geography of 1849–1857 should not be confused with that of the later North-West Frontier Province, created in 1901. In these years the frontier districts were part of the Punjab government, and the political geography that later appeared as a distinct province, with its own boundaries, was the result of later policy.

Section 65.18

Direct Rule, Princely States, and the Political Settlement of Northwestern India

The conquests left the northwest with a mixed political order, which makes clear that British power did not take a uniform form. Sindh, apart from Khairpur, and the Punjab were directly administered, under non-regulation systems of government, by Company officers. But in the same region lay states that retained their rulers under treaty: Khairpur in Sindh, whose ruler Mir Ali Murad had received the Company's support; Bahawalpur on the Sutlej and Indus, whose Nawab had a treaty with the Company dating to 1833 and who had assisted Edwardes in the siege of Multan; and Jammu and Kashmir, ruled by Gulab Singh, who died in 1857 and was succeeded by his son Ranbir Singh. In the Cis-Sutlej region, rulers such as those of Patiala, Nabha, and Jind retained their thrones under British protection, a status that dated back to 1809.

These categories differed in important ways. In a directly annexed territory, the Company claimed sovereignty, appointed all officials, collected revenue, and administered justice. Under paramountcy, rulers retained their titles and internal government but accepted British control of foreign relations, were subject to the advice of Residents or political agents, and might have their succession approved or disputed by the Company. Treaty obligations varied from one state to another, as did the degree of autonomy actually exercised. A Resident who exercised close supervision at a court might intervene frequently; one dealing with a distant and compliant ruler might seldom do so.

The settlement of 1849 therefore did not create uniform government across northwestern South Asia. For communities in frontier districts, the conversion of earlier political relationships into colonial boundaries had significant consequences: arrangements with tribal communities that had been fluid under the Sikh and Afghan states were redefined in terms of administrative districts, borders, and subsidies. In Kashmir, the establishment of Dogra rule within a paramount power's framework created a state whose Muslim-majority population was governed by a Hindu dynasty, an arrangement with long consequences, discussed in Chapters 71 and 87.

The central distinction students must retain is between direct territorial administration and British paramountcy over rulers who retained their thrones. The choice between them was not predetermined. Dalhousie and others viewed annexation as a means of improving government and increasing revenue, but the Company also found princely states useful as allies, as buffers, and as sources of support in later crises. The same Company that annexed Punjab left Bahawalpur and Jammu and Kashmir in place, and the decision in each case reflected strategy, the ruler's usefulness, and the cost of administration.

Section 65.19

The Company State on the Eve of 1857: Military Recruitment, Revenue, and Political Tensions

By the mid-1850s the Company ruled, directly or through dependent rulers, nearly the whole of the subcontinent. Its army was its most important institution. The Bengal Army, with its large infantry component recruited largely among high-caste Hindu and Muslim peasants of Awadh, Bihar, and the Ganges plain, remained the largest, but the Punjab had become a vital addition. Soldiers of the former Khalsa army, whose disbandment after 1846 and 1849 left tens of thousands of trained men without employment, were enlisted into new regiments and irregular units. Sikhs, Punjabi Muslims, Pashtuns, and others were recruited for the Punjab Irregular Force and for new regiments, and the Company found them effective soldiers who were not drawn from the same communities as the Bengal sepoys. In the following years this recruitment would become one of the foundations of the Company's military position.

Recruitment reflected a developing set of ideas about the characteristics of different communities. British officers began to describe Sikhs, Gurkhas, and some other groups as particularly suited to military service, and to contrast them with the sepoys of the Bengal Army. These ideas were the early form of what became known as the "martial races" ideology, examined in Chapter 73. Students should not assume that the categories were fully formed or universally accepted before 1857: the full ideology emerged after the rebellion, and many officers in the 1850s held varied and sometimes contradictory views. But the practice of recruiting from particular communities and the habit of describing them in these terms began in the Punjab.

The northwestern annexations also affected the Bengal Army directly. Bengal sepoys had been sent to fight in Sindh and the Punjab, where allowances for service outside the Company's regular territories, known as batta, were reduced or withdrawn after annexation, since the territories were now part of the Company's dominions. This caused resentment among sepoys and some officers, and protest took the form of mutinous incidents, notably a refusal of duty by a regiment at Govindgarh in 1849. The same issue contributed to a quarrel between Napier, the Commander-in-Chief, and Dalhousie in 1850 over discipline and allowances, which ended with Napier's resignation. The grievance over foreign service and the larger anxiety about the Company's demands on its sepoys were to figure prominently in the events of 1857, which Chapter 69 examines.

Revenue and taxation also shaped the new order. The Punjab government set the land revenue demand in summary settlements shortly after annexation, with the stated aim of keeping it moderate, and public works such as roads, and the planning of canals, aimed at economic development and military mobility. Customs and transit duties were reorganized, and the Company's government in the Punjab was expected to be financially self-sufficient. The detail of agrarian change belongs to Chapter 66, but students should note that the Punjab's revenue sustained the military establishment that held the province. Social effects included the reduction of the privileges of sardars, the loss of jagirs, and the redistribution of status among landed groups.

On the eve of 1857, Punjab and the Bengal Army were therefore in very different circumstances. Punjab had recently been conquered and was governed by a small group of officers under a strong administrative system, with a population that included many former soldiers now employed by the Company. The Bengal Army was a long-established force whose soldiers felt their status and conditions of service threatened by new rules and annexations, including the annexation of Awadh in 1856, discussed in Chapter 69. Which of these circumstances would prove decisive in 1857 is a question for the chapters that follow, and this chapter reserves the events and interpretation of the rebellion itself for them.

Section 65.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the final stage of the Company's expansion across the subcontinent. Ranjit Singh built a powerful state that preserved its independence during the Company's earlier phase of expansion because it accepted the limit set by the Treaty of Amritsar and because it commanded an army and a revenue system that made it too costly to challenge. The empire's strength lay in its composite character, combining Sikh institutions and leadership with the participation of Muslims, Hindus, and Dogra Rajputs, and in the personal authority of the ruler who balanced them. Its weakness lay in the same dependence on one man. After 1839, succession crises, factional murder, and the growing political role of the Khalsa army undermined the court, while the Company's neighbouring power increased.

The conquest of Sindh offers a parallel and a precedent. The Talpur amirs governed a divided state whose strategic location mattered to the Company during the Afghan war. The sequence of treaties from 1832 to 1839, the pressure of the war, and the policies of Ellenborough and Napier led to annexation in 1843 after the battles of Miani and Dubbo, a decision disputed by British contemporaries. Sindh shows how treaty-making could convert diplomatic influence into control, and how the Company's use of divisions within a ruling house, such as the Khairpur succession, could shape the outcome.

The two Anglo-Sikh Wars show that military power alone does not explain the result. The Khalsa army fought hard and imposed heavy costs. Its defeat reflected divided leadership at Lahore, contested allegations about the conduct of commanders, the loss of Multan, and the Company's ability to concentrate guns and reinforcements, as well as the treaties of 1846 that reduced Lahore's resources and transferred Kashmir to Gulab Singh. The distinction among the Treaty of Lahore, the Treaty of Amritsar of 1846, and the Treaty of Bhairowal remains central, because each had different parties, dates, and consequences. The Multan revolt of 1848 and Dalhousie's decisions of 1849 completed the process.

The chapter also demonstrates the roles of Indian actors. Gulab Singh, Mir Ali Murad, the Nawab of Bahawalpur, Mulraj, Sher Singh Attariwalla, Lal Singh, Tej Singh, Maharani Jind Kaur, the sepoys of the Bengal Army, the soldiers of the Khalsa, the Shikarpur bankers, and local revenue officials all pursued their own interests and made choices that shaped events. The Company's expansion depended on Indian allies, intermediaries, and soldiers, and it met Indian resistance that was sometimes organized and sometimes spontaneous.

Annexation and indirect rule produced different political and administrative outcomes. Punjab and most of Sindh were governed directly under non-regulation systems; Khairpur, Bahawalpur, and Jammu and Kashmir remained under paramountcy. The choice reflected strategy, cost, and the usefulness of particular rulers. On the eve of 1857, the Company's territorial reach extended to the frontier hills and its authority rested on a large army, a revenue system, and a network of officers and intermediaries. Its limits were visible in the autonomy of the hill communities, the dependence on Indian soldiers, and the tensions between the newly conquered Punjab and the long-established Bengal Army. Chapter 66 turns to the land, agriculture, and the colonial economy, and Chapter 69 returns to the rebellion that would test the order described here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the East India Company's expansion reached the northwest of South Asia and ended the independence of the last major Indian-ruled states in the region. It argued that the outcome was shaped by diplomacy, strategic anxiety about Afghanistan, succession crises, treaties, military campaigns, and the choices of Indian actors, and that it should not be treated as the inevitable advance of British armies.

Ranjit Singh occupied Lahore in 1799 and was proclaimed maharaja in 1801. He consolidated the Sikh misls under a more centralized monarchy, and in the Treaty of Amritsar of 25 April 1809 accepted the Sutlej as the limit of his southward expansion in exchange for Company recognition of his authority to the north. Both parties observed the treaty for three decades.

The Sikh Empire expanded to include Multan, Kashmir, and Peshawar, and was supported by a regular army trained along European lines, an arsenal, and a revenue system based on contractors and jagirs. It was a multi-religious state: Sikh institutions were patronized, but most subjects were Muslims and Hindus, and its ministers, governors, and soldiers came from all communities. Lahore was a centre of Persian and Punjabi culture, and its diplomacy balanced the Company, the Afghans, and the hill rajas.

After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839, a series of successions and murders weakened the court, while the Khalsa army, organized in regimental panchayats, acquired an unusual political role. The period was not simply one of collapse, since institutions continued to function, but the authority that had balanced the army, the Dogra faction, and the sardars had gone.

In Sindh, the Talpur amirs governed through three ruling houses over a commercial and agricultural region linked to Afghanistan, Punjab, and the sea. Company interest began with the Indus trade and the treaties of 1832, and increased with the treaty of 1839 during the First Anglo-Afghan War. Under Ellenborough and Napier, a crisis in 1842–1843 led to the battles of Miani and Dubbo and the annexation of most of Sindh. The justification was disputed by British officials such as Outram, and the Historical Debate shows that the question remains open.

The First Anglo-Sikh War followed the crossing of the Sutlej in December 1845 and was fought at Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, and Sobraon. It was a difficult campaign for the Company, and the conduct of some Sikh commanders remains contested. The Treaty of Lahore of March 1846 ceded territory and imposed an indemnity; the separate Treaty of Amritsar of 16 March transferred Kashmir and neighbouring hills to Gulab Singh; and the Treaty of Bhairowal of December placed Lahore under a British Resident and a Council of Regency.

The Multan uprising of 1848, sparked by a dispute with Diwan Mulraj and the killing of two British officers, widened into a second war after the defection of the Attariwalla sardars. The battles of Ramnagar, Chillianwala, and Gujrat, and the capture of Multan, ended in Company victory. On 29 March 1849 Dalhousie annexed the Punjab, Duleep Singh lost his throne, the Koh-i-Noor passed to Queen Victoria, and a Board of Administration under the Lawrence brothers began the Company's first government of the province, replaced in 1853 by a chief commissionership.

Frontier administration extended the Company's government into regions that it could only partly control, and annexation produced different forms of rule: direct administration in Punjab and Sindh, and paramountcy over Khairpur, Bahawalpur, and Jammu and Kashmir. The Punjab became a recruiting ground for the Company's army, and grievances in the Bengal Army over allowances and conditions of service contrasted with the situation in Punjab on the eve of 1857.

The chapter's larger lesson is that Company expansion was a process shaped by both Indian and British decisions. It was enabled by military and fiscal resources but also by treaty, intermediaries, and the divisions of rival Indian states. The subjects of the next chapters, the colonial economy and the rebellion of 1857, grew from the territorial order established here.

The territories on the Company's side of the Sutlej, placed under British protection in 1809.
Non-regulation province
A province governed through simpler, more personal rules rather than the full body of regulations of the older Presidencies.
Chief commissioner
The single official who governed the Punjab from 1853 after the Board of Administration was abolished.
Irregular force
A military formation outside the regular army's rigid structure, such as the Punjab Irregular Force raised for frontier service.
Batta
An allowance paid to soldiers on service outside the Company's regular territories, whose withdrawal caused discontent.
Summary settlement
A rapid, provisional assessment of land revenue made soon after annexation, before detailed surveys.
Koh-i-Noor
A famous diamond acquired by Ranjit Singh in 1813 and surrendered to Queen Victoria under the terms of 1849.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the last major phase of Company expansion as a negotiated and contested process, not a simple triumph. The Sikh Empire and Talpur Sindh were real states with institutions, armies, and diplomatic strategies, and the Company's success depended on their internal divisions, on treaties that transformed influence into control, and on the services of Indian soldiers, allies, and administrators.

The developments traced here shaped later history in lasting ways. The creation of Jammu and Kashmir under Gulab Singh produced a princely state whose later status became a central question of South Asian politics. The incorporation of Punjab made it a recruiting ground and a strategic base, with consequences for the Company's army and, later, for colonial ideas about community and military service. The frontier arrangements of 1849–1857 anticipated administrative divisions that would endure in changed forms.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter completes Part XII's account of conquest. Chapters 62 to 64 explained the Company's rise in Bengal, its fiscal-military state, and its victories over Mysore and the Marathas; this chapter shows the final stage of that expansion. The chapters that follow turn from conquest to the structures of colonial rule, beginning with the economy in Chapter 66, and then to the resistance that culminated in 1857.
