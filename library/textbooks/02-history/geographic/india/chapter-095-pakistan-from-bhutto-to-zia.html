Pakistan from Bhutto to Zia

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 20 December 1971, four days after the surrender of Pakistani forces in Dhaka, Zulfikar Ali Bhutto flew back from New York, where he had been arguing Pakistan's case at the United Nations, to take charge of a state that had just lost its eastern wing. He became president and, unusually, the first civilian chief martial law administrator. His inheritance was an army discredited by defeat, tens of thousands of soldiers and officials held in Indian custody, a constitution that had never worked, an economy dislocated by war and the loss of its largest market, and a population in the western provinces asking what Pakistan was for.

Pakistan's eastern wing had held a majority of the country's people. Its separation as Bangladesh, narrated in Chapter 92, was therefore not only a territorial loss but a collapse of the founding idea that a shared religion could bind two regions a thousand miles apart. This chapter begins at that moment of collapse and asks a different question from Chapter 92. Chapter 92 asked how Bangladesh came into being. This chapter asks what remained of the Pakistani state, and what its leaders tried to build in its place.

The chapter follows the surviving Pakistan across seventeen years, from 1971 to 1988. In its first phase a populist civilian leader and a new political party attempted a reconstruction that combined parliamentary government, state ownership of industry, and a more explicit Islamic identity. In April 1973 the National Assembly adopted a parliamentary constitution with broad agreement across party lines, a genuine achievement in a country that had gone a quarter-century without a durable one. Within four years, however, that constitutional order had collapsed. A disputed election, mass protest, and a military coup on 5 July 1977 ended Bhutto's government, and on 4 April 1979 Bhutto himself was hanged.

The second phase belongs to General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, who ruled for eleven years. Zia suspended the constitution's normal operation, restricted parties and the press, and presided over a program of Islamization that reshaped criminal law, the courts, taxation, and the relationship between the state and religious institutions. Nothing in that program began from nothing. The Objectives Resolution of 1949, the Ahmadi amendment of 1974, and Bhutto's own concessions to religious opponents in 1977 all form part of the background. But Zia gave these precedents institutional force on a scale not seen before.

The third thread is external. In December 1979 Soviet forces entered Afghanistan, and Pakistan, with its long frontier and its own unresolved claims on the Pashtun borderlands, became the principal base for the Afghan resistance and host to millions of refugees. The war brought American and Saudi money, intelligence cooperation, weapons, narcotics, and new religious networks into Pakistani society. It also gave a military ruler who had lost legitimacy at home a strong standing abroad.

The chapter's governing question is therefore this: why did Pakistan's attempted democratic reconstruction after 1971 give way to prolonged military rule, and how did Islamization and the Afghan War reshape the country's institutions, society, and international position by 1988? The argument developed here is that civilian reconstruction, military intervention, religious policy, and Cold War geopolitics interacted rather than succeeded one another. The 1973 settlement restored civilian government without resolving the imbalance between civilian institutions and the army. Bhutto's government weakened the settlement through its own authoritarian practices; Zia's government rebuilt the state around the presidency and the army; and the Afghan War supplied the resources and external recognition that sustained that rebuilding.

The evidence base is unusually rich and unusually contested. Constitutional texts, ordinances, court judgments, election returns, refugee registration figures, and declassified diplomatic records survive in quantity. So do memoirs, party manifestos, and press accounts, many written by participants with strong interests. A Supreme Court opinion of March 2024 has now reassessed one of the period's central events, Bhutto's trial. This chapter tries to separate what the documents establish from what remains argued, and it treats Pakistan's provinces, parties, workers, women, minorities, and refugees as historical actors alongside Bhutto and Zia.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the defeat of 1971 and the loss of East Pakistan changed the legitimacy of the army and the meaning of Pakistani national identity.
  2. Describe the political program, social base, and governing style of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto and the Pakistan Peoples Party.
  3. Analyze the structure of the 1973 Constitution and distinguish its parliamentary, federal, and Islamic provisions.
  4. Assess Bhutto's nationalization and land-reform programs by distinguishing announced objectives from documented outcomes.
  5. Compare provincial constitutional opposition, armed insurgency, and state security responses in Balochistan and the North-West Frontier Province.
  6. Evaluate competing explanations for the breakdown of civilian government in 1977.
  7. Trace the stages by which Zia-ul-Haq consolidated authority, from martial law to the Eighth Amendment.
  8. Distinguish the specific legal changes of Islamization from broader claims about religion and the Pakistani state, and identify earlier precedents.
  9. Analyze how women, religious minorities, sectarian communities, and political movements experienced and resisted military rule.
  10. Explain why Pakistan became central to the Afghan War and how refugees, intelligence agencies, and resistance parties interacted.
  11. Interpret quantitative evidence on economic performance and refugee numbers while recognizing the limits of each series.
  12. Assess why the 1988 return to elected government was a genuine transition but not an end to military influence.

Historical Context

Pakistan came into being in August 1947 as a state in two parts, West Pakistan and East Bengal, separated by Indian territory. Its first constitution took nine years to produce (1956) and lasted barely two. In October 1958 General Muhammad Ayub Khan imposed martial law, and his 1962 constitution created a strong presidency elected by a restricted system of "Basic Democrats." Ayub's decade brought rapid industrial growth concentrated in a small number of business families, a widening gap between the two wings, and the war with India of 1965. Chapter 90 traces these developments.

Ayub's fall in March 1969 ended in a second martial law under General Yahya Khan, who promised a transfer of power through elections. The elections of December 1970, the first on a national adult franchise, produced a result that the army and the West Pakistani political class could not absorb: the Awami League under Sheikh Mujibur Rahman won nearly every seat in the East, and with it an absolute majority in the National Assembly, while Bhutto's Pakistan Peoples Party won the largest bloc in the West. The collapse of negotiations, the military operation in East Pakistan beginning in March 1971, the Bengali resistance, and the war with India of December 1971 are the subject of Chapter 92.

Three background conditions shaped what followed. First, civil–military imbalance: the army had ruled directly for roughly thirteen of the state's first twenty-four years and was the best-organized institution in the country, while political parties were fragmented, regionally based, and unaccustomed to governing. Second, the unresolved relationship between Islam and the state: the Objectives Resolution of 1949 had declared sovereignty to belong to God while assigning authority to the people's representatives, and every constitution since had defined this relationship differently. Third, federal tension: with East Bengal gone, Punjab became the single dominant province, containing a majority of the population, while Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province, and Balochistan each contained distinct linguistic and political traditions and, in several cases, grievances about control of resources.

Readers should note a terminological point used throughout the chapter. The province now named Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (since 2010) was called the North-West Frontier Province (NWFP) during the period studied here, and this chapter uses the historical name. "Pakistan" after December 1971 means the former West Pakistan; the chapter does not use "West Pakistan" for the post-1971 state except when describing the earlier period.

Section 95.1

Pakistan after the Loss of Bangladesh: Defeat, Territorial Reconstruction, and National Crisis

The surrender at Dhaka on 16 December 1971 was followed within days by a political reversal in Rawalpindi. Army officers, including several of Yahya Khan's own subordinates, made clear that the president could not remain, and on 20 December Yahya transferred authority to Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, whose party held the largest bloc of seats from the western provinces. Yahya was placed under house arrest. Bhutto assumed the offices of president and chief martial law administrator, an anomaly that placed a civilian at the head of a legal order created by two generals.

The crisis he faced was simultaneously military, territorial, diplomatic, and moral. Roughly ninety thousand soldiers and civilian officials were held by India as prisoners of war or internees, and their return depended on negotiations that Pakistan could not conduct from strength. India still held territory in the west captured in the December fighting. The army's senior ranks were demoralized. In the weeks after taking office Bhutto retired a number of senior generals and installed new commanders, a step that put him, for a time, in a position of authority over the military that few civilian leaders in Pakistan have held. In March 1972 General Tikka Khan became chief of army staff.

The loss of the east altered the country's internal arithmetic. With East Bengal gone, the Punjab held more than half the remaining population, and the other three provinces, Sindh, the NWFP, and Balochistan, became smaller and more distinctive in relation to it. The "two-nation" argument that had justified Pakistan's creation, that Muslims of the subcontinent formed a single political community, had not prevented a majority-Muslim region from seceding. Pakistani writers and politicians responded in different ways. Some emphasized a more explicit Islamic identity as the remaining basis of unity. Others argued that the failure demonstrated the costs of military rule and centralization, and that a genuinely federal and democratic Pakistan was the only viable alternative. Both arguments entered the political debates of the next decade.

Two instruments addressed the aftermath. In December 1971 the government appointed a judicial commission under Chief Justice Hamoodur Rahman to inquire into the circumstances of the defeat and surrender. A first report was submitted in 1972, and a supplementary report, based on testimony from officers who returned from captivity, was delivered in October 1974. The government did not publish either at the time. The supplementary report entered public circulation only in 2000, when excerpts appeared in an Indian magazine and were reprinted in Pakistan, after which the government declassified the material. Its findings, paraphrased in the Primary Source Window below, criticized senior commanders and the political role the army had acquired under martial law.

The diplomatic response was the Simla Agreement, signed by Bhutto and Indian Prime Minister Indira Gandhi on 2 July 1972. The agreement committed both states to resolve differences bilaterally and by peaceful means, and to respect the line of control in Jammu and Kashmir resulting from the ceasefire of 17 December 1971 without prejudice to either side's position. Indian and Pakistani forces withdrew to the international border in the south. The return of prisoners was settled in stages through an agreement in Delhi in August 1973 and a tripartite arrangement with Bangladesh in April 1974, which followed Pakistan's recognition of Bangladesh in February 1974 after the Lahore summit. Pakistan had already left the Commonwealth in January 1972 in protest at its members' recognition of Bangladesh.

For Pakistani citizens the war's aftermath was material as well as symbolic. Trade with the former eastern wing, which had supplied raw jute and absorbed West Pakistani manufactured goods, ended. Families had members in Indian camps for two years. Bengali civil servants and officers who had remained in the west were interned or repatriated, and non-Bengali families (often called Biharis) in Bangladesh sought to move west, a process that continued for years. Within Pakistan, the question of who was responsible for the defeat became a lasting argument, one that the unpublished state of the Hamoodur Rahman report ensured would not be settled by documents.

Section 95.2

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto: Civilian Leadership, the Pakistan Peoples Party, and Political Reconstruction

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto was born in 1928 into a landed Sindhi family of Larkana and educated at Berkeley and Oxford. He entered government under Ayub Khan and served as foreign minister from 1963 to 1966, a period of closer relations with China and of the 1965 war. After resigning from Ayub's cabinet, he founded the Pakistan Peoples Party in Lahore in November 1967. The party's slogan, "bread, clothing, and shelter," was backed by a four-part formula: Islam as faith, democracy as polity, socialism as economy, and power to the people.

The PPP's strength lay in the combination of its parts. It appealed to industrial workers and urban lower-middle-class voters, to tenant farmers in Punjab and Sindh, to students and intellectuals, and to a section of landlords who saw the party as a vehicle for power. Its socialist language, its critique of the "twenty-two families" said to control industry and finance, and Bhutto's personal rhetoric gave it momentum in the 1970 election, where it won 81 of the 138 seats allotted to the western provinces. That success was concentrated in Punjab and Sindh, and the party was weak in the NWFP and Balochistan, where other parties dominated. This regional pattern shaped its later conflict with provincial governments.

Bhutto's authority in 1972 rested on three foundations that did not entirely agree. One was the electoral mandate of 1970 in the west. A second was the transfer of power from the army, which made him dependent on the armed forces' acquiescence. The third was the expectation that he could restore national confidence. He moved quickly. Martial law was lifted on 21 April 1972 with the adoption of an interim constitution, the National Assembly began work on a permanent one, and he pursued diplomacy with India, the Soviet Union, China, and the Muslim world. Within the year he had addressed workers, announced education and labor policies, and reshaped the military leadership.

The restoration of civilian leadership should not, however, be confused with institutionalized accountability. Bhutto was a leader of unusual intellectual and rhetorical gifts, and his government relied heavily on his personal authority. The PPP itself had weak internal democracy. Early dissenting party figures were dismissed or sidelined, and in 1972 Bhutto created a paramilitary Federal Security Force answerable to the prime minister, which was later accused by opponents of acting against political critics. He also leaned heavily on the bureaucracy and on provincial leaders whose loyalty was personal rather than institutional.

His foreign-policy priorities reflected both ideology and necessity. Pakistan left the Commonwealth in 1972 and withdrew from the South East Asia Treaty Organization in 1973 while remaining in the Central Treaty Organization (CENTO) until 1979. Bhutto cultivated relationships with China, with Iran under the Shah, and with Arab oil-producing states, and organized the Islamic summit at Lahore in February 1974. He sought to balance the loss of the American security guarantee that had always been thin in practice, and he laid foundations for the nuclear program treated in Chapter 97.

The comparison with the army is instructive. Within months of taking power, Bhutto had retired the generals who had presided over the defeat and secured wide support in West Pakistan. Yet he left the army's corporate structure, its budget, and its intelligence agencies largely intact, and in March 1976 he chose Zia-ul-Haq as army chief over several more senior generals. Bhutto expected loyalty from a general he thought non-political. The choice has been read, with hindsight, as a consequential misjudgment, though contemporaries could not know this.

The machinery of government also changed. In 1972–73 Bhutto reorganized the civil service, abolishing the privileged status of the elite Civil Service of Pakistan and opening senior posts to lateral entry from outside the cadre, and he dismissed hundreds of officials. These reforms were presented as a democratization of the bureaucracy and a curb on a class that had governed since the colonial period. They also made officials more dependent on political patronage and weakened the professional independence that had given the bureaucracy some capacity to resist political pressure. The result was a civil service that served the party in office more directly, and that proved adaptable when the government changed in 1977.

The PPP's relationship with its own mass base was ambivalent. Its rhetoric invited workers and peasants into politics, and its rallies of 1970–72 drew very large crowds. Yet candidates for office were drawn heavily from established landed families, and the party's organization below the national level depended on local notables. Historians therefore describe the PPP as a populist movement that incorporated established elites, a combination that helped it win elections but limited its capacity for structural change. This tension runs through the debates over land reform and nationalization in the following sections.

Section 95.3

The Constitution of 1973: Parliamentary Government, Federalism, and Islamic Identity

The constitution-making of 1972–73 produced a document that has survived, with extensive amendment, to the present. In April 1972 the National Assembly appointed a committee to draft a permanent constitution, and bargaining among the PPP, the National Awami Party, the Jamiat Ulama-e-Islam, and other parties produced a bill introduced on 2 February 1973. Disputes were intense, especially over provincial powers and the position of the opposition-led governments in the NWFP and Balochistan, but the National Assembly adopted the constitution on 10 April 1973 with the support of opposition members, and it came into effect on 14 August 1973.

The constitution set up a parliamentary system. The president was head of state, elected by an electoral college of the two houses of parliament and the provincial assemblies, and was bound in most matters to act on the advice of the prime minister. Real executive power lay with the prime minister and cabinet, who were responsible to the National Assembly. Parliament consisted of a directly elected National Assembly (initially 200 general seats with additional seats reserved for women for a transitional period) and a Senate chosen by the provincial assemblies. The drafters added provisions designed to protect governments from easy removal and Article 6 defining the subversion of the constitution as high treason.

The federal arrangements divided powers among a Federal Legislative List and a Concurrent List and gave the provinces residual authority. The Senate and the Council of Common Interests were intended to give the smaller provinces equal voices, since Punjab's population could outvote the others in the National Assembly. Fundamental rights, including freedom of speech, association, and equality before the law, were set out in Part II, though several could be restricted by law, and the emergency provisions allowed suspension of rights.

The constitution's treatment of Islam combined existing and new elements. The Objectives Resolution formed the preamble. The state was named the Islamic Republic of Pakistan, Islam was declared the state religion, the president and prime minister were required to be Muslim, and the Council of Islamic Ideology was established to advise on the compatibility of laws with Islamic injunctions. The state was directed to enable Muslims to order their lives in accordance with Islam and to bring existing laws into conformity with the Quran and Sunnah. These provisions were largely advisory. They were not self-executing, and courts had limited power to strike down laws on religious grounds, a feature that later changed under Zia.

The settlement's importance should not be understated. It was the first constitution adopted by an elected assembly with cross-party consent, it reestablished a parliamentary framework, and it gave the smaller provinces institutional claims. But it also contained structural weaknesses. The army was not integrated into the civilian system by clear provisions on civil–military relations, beyond provisions making martial-law-style subversion treason, a clause that proved meaningless when tested. The amendments of 1974–77 narrowed judicial review of detention and altered the judiciary's independence. And the executive's strength in a parliamentary system dominated by a single party meant that a prime minister who commanded his party faced few internal checks.

The settlement also made institutional compromises that could be tested only in practice. The Senate gave the provinces equal representation despite their sharply unequal populations, whereas the National Assembly reflected population and therefore gave Punjab substantial electoral weight. The Council of Common Interests was designed to manage shared resources and intergovernmental disputes, yet its usefulness depended upon convening it and accepting negotiated outcomes. The constitutional text could distribute authority but could not itself guarantee that provincial leaders would remain in office when the centre regarded them as hostile. For this reason, the dismissal of the Balochistan government during the drafting process was more than a temporary provincial dispute: it exposed the difference between federal guarantees on paper and political tolerance in government. Constitutional bargaining thus proceeded in the shadow of coercive administrative powers.

Historical Vocabulary
The Objectives Resolution

Adopted by the Constituent Assembly in March 1949, the Objectives Resolution declared that sovereignty over the universe belongs to God and that authority is delegated to the people as a sacred trust to be exercised within limits set by Him, while also promising democracy, equality, tolerance, minority rights, and an independent judiciary. It appeared as a preamble in the constitutions of 1956 and 1962 and again in 1973. In 1985 Zia's government made it a substantive part of the constitution as Article 2A. Historians read it differently: as a foundational compromise between religious and secular conceptions of the state, or as an opening for later Islamic claims. Its significance in this chapter is that it shows that Islam's place in the state's constitutional language predates Zia.

Section 95.4

Nationalization and the Expansion of State Economic Authority

On 2 January 1972 the government announced the nationalization of thirty-one large industrial units in ten basic industries, among them iron and steel, heavy engineering, basic metals, petrochemicals, cement, and fertilizers. Life insurance followed in March 1972, and in 1974 the government nationalized thirteen domestic banks, shipping companies, and vegetable-ghee producers. In July 1976 it took over cotton ginning, rice husking, and flour milling units, a measure that reached into smaller businesses and agricultural marketing.

The rationale combined several arguments. Concentration of industrial and financial ownership in a few families, identified in the late Ayub period with the "twenty-two families," was widely resented, and the PPP's mandate depended on addressing it. Nationalization was presented as a means to protect workers, to direct investment toward heavy industry, and to place "commanding heights" under public control. It also had political purposes: it removed the economic base of potential opponents and expanded the patronage available to the government.

Implementation produced mixed results. Public-sector enterprises were placed under state management with limited technical preparation, and many managers were civil servants rather than experienced industrialists. Large-scale private investment declined sharply, partly because of nationalization and partly because of political uncertainty. The war's aftermath, the loss of the eastern market, the devaluation of the rupee in May 1972 from about 4.76 to 11 per US dollar, the floods of 1973, the oil price shock of 1973–74, and a poor cotton harvest in 1974 and 1976 complicated the picture. Inflation was high in the mid-1970s. Some state-led projects, such as the Pakistan Steel Mills at Karachi, built with Soviet assistance, and the Heavy Mechanical Complex, pursued long-term industrial capacity, though they were slow to produce returns.

Assessing the program requires care. World Bank analysis of the period and later retrospective assessments, including those by the United Nations Development Programme, generally treat the nationalizations as having damaged private-sector confidence and administrative efficiency without producing the broader distribution of economic power that the government had promised. The extension of state control to small agro-processing units in 1976 was widely criticized as harmful to small traders and growers. Defenders of the policy, in contrast, point out that Pakistan's growth in the 1970s was disrupted by factors outside the government's control, that remittances and public investment sustained consumption, and that nationalization changed the political position of the industrial elite. The Quantitative Study below presents indicative figures, with the caution that causation cannot be inferred from them.

The policy's lasting consequences were administrative and political. The state's share in banking, insurance, and heavy industry remained large through the 1980s, even under a government that was ideologically hostile to Bhutto. Zia's government returned some small units to their owners but left the main nationalized sectors in place until later reform. In this respect the nationalization program is an example of continuity across the Bhutto–Zia divide, a theme developed in Section 95.20.

Ownership, however, was not the same thing as control over economic results. Nationalized factories remained dependent on managers, imported machinery, access to credit, and the conditions of domestic and foreign markets. Workers could support public ownership as a corrective to concentrated private wealth while also protesting wage arrears, arbitrary dismissals, or politically appointed administrators. Business proprietors, meanwhile, faced uncertainty about which sectors would next be brought under state control, a problem separate from the performance of particular public enterprises. Pakistan's growth figures for these years should be read alongside the global oil-price rise, weak harvests, and the loss of economic ties with East Pakistan. Such multiple influences prevent a reliable historian from using later growth comparisons as a referendum on a single policy. The important institutional legacy was the enduring size of the public sector and the political importance of decisions about prices, credit, and appointments.

Section 95.5

Land Reform, Labor Policy, Education, and the Limits of Bhutto's Economic Program

Bhutto's social program rested on three instruments: land reform, labor legislation, and educational policy. The land reform of March 1972 (Martial Law Regulation 64) lowered the ceiling on individual holdings to 150 irrigated or 300 unirrigated acres, or an equivalent in a produce-index measure, well below the 500 and 1,000 acres allowed under Ayub Khan's 1959 reform. It also strengthened tenants' security against eviction. A second reform, enacted in 1977, lowered the ceilings further to 100 irrigated and 200 unirrigated acres.

The results fell far short of the rhetoric. Landowners avoided ceilings by transferring land to relatives and dependants, by classifying land as unirrigated, and by retaining the best land. The area resumed by the state was a small fraction of cultivated land, and most landlords remained in place. The PPP itself had landed supporters, and the bureaucracy that implemented the reform depended on local landlord networks. The reform's political value was greater than its distributive effect. It told peasants that the state recognized their grievance, while leaving the structure of rural power largely intact, and in later years Sindhi and Punjabi landowners continued to dominate provincial politics.

Labor policy was announced in February 1972. It increased workers' bonus entitlements, provided for workers' participation in management committees, expanded social security and old-age benefits, and made dismissal harder. The early period saw intense industrial action. In June 1972 police fired on striking workers in Karachi's industrial areas, and the confrontations showed that the party's constituency and its government could be at odds. Industrial relations later stabilized under tighter control, and unions linked to the PPP gained influence, but critics argued that nationalization placed worker discipline under state officials. Zia later restricted union activity.

Education policy, announced in March 1972, aimed at wider access and a national curriculum, and the government nationalized private schools and colleges in 1972. The measure was intended to reduce inequality of access, but it strained public administration, depressed morale in the teaching profession, and drew criticism from missionary and private institutions. Zia's government returned many institutions to private ownership. Other welfare commitments, including health and rural works, were limited by resource constraints.

Different groups experienced these policies differently. Large industrialists lost firms and influence. Industrial workers gained formal entitlements and some wage gains but also faced administrative control, and in small units outcomes were mixed. Large landlords lost some land but kept political power; many tenants gained legal protection that was only partly enforced. Public administrators gained new responsibilities with little preparation. Business groups reacted by reducing investment and, in some cases, by moving capital abroad. The program's gap between redistribution as a political objective and the actual restructuring of economic power is a central finding of recent scholarship.

Agriculture was the setting for much of this politics. Since the 1960s, high-yielding wheat varieties, tubewell irrigation, and mechanization had increased output in Punjab and parts of Sindh, and the benefits flowed disproportionately to medium and large farmers with access to credit and water. Bhutto's government raised support prices and established state agencies for procurement and fertilizer distribution, but floods in 1973 and 1976 and pest damage to cotton cut harvests, and in several years the country imported wheat. Because landowning families supplied many PPP legislators and local officials, the party had limited incentive to enforce ceilings strictly. The gap between the political claims of land reform and its effects on rural power helps explain why rural Punjab and Sindh remained politically conservative even under a populist government, and why Zia could later build alliances with landed families as well.

Implementation was particularly difficult where powerful families could distribute land among relatives or use legal exemptions to retain effective control. The size of a recorded holding was not always the size of the economic operation it represented; conversely, a tenant cultivating land without secure title might receive no practical benefit from a formal ceiling. Administrative capacity also varied from district to district. A credible assessment must therefore distinguish acreage declared surplus, acreage actually taken into state possession, acreage redistributed, and the conditions under which beneficiaries held it. Women's access to land was further constrained by family inheritance practices and limited bargaining power. Labor policy similarly worked through intermediaries: factory-level unions, employers, government inspectors, and the courts. A promised right could be powerful in a well-organized industrial workplace yet distant from the everyday experience of agricultural laborers or informal workers.

Section 95.6

Provincial Autonomy, Balochistan, and the Crisis of Federal Relations

The 1970 elections had produced provincial majorities for the National Awami Party (NAP), working with the Jamiat Ulama-e-Islam (JUI), in Balochistan and the NWFP. When the interim constitution came into effect in April 1972, Bhutto negotiated an accommodation with these parties, and in spring 1972 NAP–JUI coalition governments took office in both provinces, with Ataullah Mengal as chief minister in Balochistan and Mufti Mahmud in the NWFP. These governments were the test of whether the new federal system could accommodate parties that did not share the PPP's national outlook.

The test failed within a year. The NAP's leaders favored wide provincial autonomy, control of provincial resources, the use of regional languages, and local appointments. Bhutto and the federal bureaucracy saw these demands as a threat to national unity, coming so soon after East Pakistan's secession. Tensions grew over appointments, police powers, and the Baloch demand for control of local resources. In February 1973 police in Islamabad searched the Iraqi embassy and announced the discovery of a weapons cache, which the government alleged was intended for insurgents in Balochistan. The Balochistan government was dismissed on 15 February 1973, the Governor, Ghaus Bakhsh Bizenjo, and the chief minister were arrested, and the NWFP ministry resigned in protest. The NAP leadership faced charges, later tried before a special tribunal at Hyderabad.

Armed conflict followed. From 1973 to 1977 insurgent groups, many drawn from the Marri, Mengal, and Bugti tribal regions, fought the Pakistani army and paramilitary forces in the mountains of Balochistan. The federal government deployed substantial military forces, which were supported by equipment and, according to reports, helicopters supplied by Iran, whose Shah worried about Baloch nationalism spreading to his own territory. Casualty estimates vary widely, from several thousand deaths on both sides to higher figures, and no authoritative count exists. Civilian suffering, displacement, and the destruction of livestock and settlements are documented in memoirs and regional accounts. Some Baloch fighters crossed into Afghanistan, whose Daoud government sheltered them, a factor in the later Afghan-Pakistani tension discussed in Section 95.13.

It is useful to distinguish three things that often merge in political memory. The first was constitutional provincial opposition, the claim by an elected provincial government to exercise powers granted under the constitution. The second was armed insurgency, conducted by groups that were not all connected to the NAP leadership or accepted its authority. The third was the state's security response, which included dismissal of an elected government, mass arrests, and large-scale military operations. Federal officials treated all three as one problem. Provincial leaders argued that the dismissal had provoked the insurgency. The federal government argued that the NAP's leaders had encouraged separatism. Historians generally agree that the dismissal damaged the credibility of the 1973 federal settlement, since it showed that provincial governments could be removed at the center's discretion within weeks of the constitution's adoption.

The NAP's conflict continued in the NWFP. In February 1975 a bomb killed Hayat Muhammad Khan Sherpao, a close PPP associate, at Peshawar University. The government banned the NAP, a decision upheld by the Supreme Court later that year, and arrested its leaders. This removed the main left-nationalist opposition from legal politics, and many former NAP members later regrouped in other parties. Sindh had its own disputes in this period, including language riots in 1972 following the Sindh provincial assembly's language bill. They revealed that Bhutto's Sindhi base and the Urdu-speaking migrants who had come at Partition remained in tension. The federal settlement's weaknesses were thus visible in all four provinces.

The Balochistan conflict illustrates why provincial autonomy cannot be measured by constitutional clauses alone. A provincial cabinet needed administrative control, dependable revenues, and confidence that a disagreement with Islamabad would not automatically be treated as a security threat. Baloch political leaders differed over what autonomy should mean, and armed groups differed over their goals and organization; none can be treated as the voice of an undifferentiated province. The deployment of troops generated new grievances among communities experiencing village searches, displacement, and restrictions on ordinary movement, while the government insisted that the campaign was required to preserve territorial integrity. For students, the central evidentiary problem is how to compare state statements, opposition testimony, and later scholarship without assuming that every civilian grievance endorses an insurgent attack or every insurgent attack justifies collective punishment. This difficulty would recur in Pakistan's later provincial disputes.

Section 95.7

Bhutto's Political Opposition: Religious Identity, Authoritarian Practices, and the Struggle for Legitimacy

Between 1973 and 1976 the political ground shifted against the government. Opposition parties, divided in ideology, converged in objecting to Bhutto's methods. Constitutional amendments in 1974–76 extended the executive's powers of preventive detention and narrowed the courts' ability to grant bail and review detention. The Federal Security Force and provincial police were accused by opponents of harassing, arresting, and in some cases physically attacking critics. Press restrictions tightened. In November 1974 the murder in Lahore of Nawab Muhammad Ahmad Khan, father of an opposition politician who had criticized Bhutto, would later become the basis of the prosecution that ended Bhutto's life. The extent of the government's responsibility for specific acts of political violence was disputed then and remains argued now, but the pattern of coercion, which also included disqualification of opponents and use of state patronage, is well documented.

Religious parties were prominent among the critics. The Jamaat-e-Islami, led by Mian Tufail Muhammad after Abul A'la Maududi's retirement, the Jamiat Ulama-e-Pakistan, and the Jamiat Ulama-e-Islam argued that the government's socialist rhetoric and personal conduct were inconsistent with an Islamic state. Bhutto's response was to accommodate religious demands where he could. The most important instance was the Second Constitutional Amendment of 7 September 1974.

The amendment followed a violent episode at Rabwah, the Ahmadi community's center, in May 1974, which prompted nationwide agitation against the Ahmadiyya. The National Assembly sat as a committee of the whole, heard representatives of the community, and then voted to amend Article 260 so that a person who does not accept the finality of Muhammad's prophethood, or who recognizes any later claimant to prophethood, is a non-Muslim for the purposes of the constitution and law. The amendment designated Ahmadis, in both the Qadiani and Lahori branches, as non-Muslims. Ahmadis argued that they identified as Muslims. The amendment's significance was thus twofold: it gave a legislature the authority to define membership in a religious community for legal purposes, and it created a legal category on which later restrictions could be built.

Care is required in interpreting this episode. The amendment had earlier precedents, including the anti-Ahmadi disturbances in Punjab in 1953 and the Munir Report of 1954 that investigated them. It was adopted under Bhutto, by a PPP-controlled assembly, and with the participation of nearly all parties. It would be incorrect to attribute Pakistan's later treatment of religious minorities entirely to Zia's government. Zia's Ordinance XX of 1984, discussed in Sections 95.11 and 95.12, added criminal penalties on religious practice, but it built on a legal category created ten years earlier.

The Lahore Islamic summit of February 1974, attended by leaders of dozens of Muslim states, illustrated how domestic and foreign policy interacted. It gave Bhutto prestige abroad, assisted Pakistan's recognition of Bangladesh, and strengthened links with oil-rich states. At home, however, the claim of Islamic leadership did not quiet the religious parties. By 1976 the opposition, though divided, shared a desire to challenge the PPP's electoral dominance, and this convergence set the stage for the alliance of 1977. In April 1977, in the midst of the crisis, Bhutto announced a ban on alcohol sales, nightclubs, and horse racing and the adoption of Friday as the weekly holiday, concessions that came too late to satisfy his religious opponents. They show that Islamic politics was already a language of mass opposition before Zia's coup.

The constitutional classification of Ahmadis in 1974 was a critical illustration of the state's capacity to define religious membership by law. It emerged from political mobilization and debates that long predated Bhutto, but its adoption by a civilian parliament gave the distinction a new constitutional status. This did not itself enact the restrictions later imposed through Zia's Ordinance XX. The sequence matters because attributing all measures to the same government would obscure how legal exclusion accumulated across different political regimes. Nor did religious organizations enter opposition to Bhutto with identical goals: some prioritized elections and accountability, others advocated a more explicit Islamic legal order, and some made tactical alliances despite longstanding ideological differences. The political rhetoric of 1977 condensed these purposes into a common challenge to Bhutto's continued authority.

Section 95.8

The General Election of 1977: The Pakistan National Alliance and the Breakdown of Political Order

On 7 January 1977 Bhutto announced that National Assembly elections would be held on 7 March, earlier than required. Two days later nine opposition parties, ranging from the religious right to centrist and regional groups, formed the Pakistan National Alliance (PNA), led by Mufti Mahmud with Rafiq Bajwa as secretary-general, and agreed on a common program. The alliance included the Jamaat-e-Islami, the Jamiat Ulama-e-Islam, the Jamiat Ulama-e-Pakistan, Asghar Khan's Tehrik-i-Istiqlal, the Pakistan Muslim League under Pir Pagara, and the National Democratic Party. It adopted a symbol, a plow, and a program calling for an Islamic order, which became known as Nizam-e-Mustafa, the system of the Prophet.

The campaign saw unusual opposition enthusiasm, but also irregularities. Bhutto and the chief ministers of the four provinces were declared elected unopposed, in several cases after their opponents were alleged to have been prevented from filing nominations. In Bhutto's own constituency of Larkana, his opponent was reportedly abducted. The reported results on 7 March gave the PPP about 155 of the 200 contested seats and the PNA about 36. The PNA claimed that the outcome was achieved by fraud, rejected the results, boycotted the provincial elections three days later, and called for a movement to force new elections.

Protests began in March and expanded in April. They drew in urban merchants, students, professionals, religious organizations, and a section of the PPP's own urban base that had turned against the government. The movement was strongest in Punjab's cities, in Karachi, and in Hyderabad. Police and paramilitary forces used force, and curfews and martial-law-like measures were imposed in several cities. In April the army was called to support civil authority under Article 245 in Lahore, Karachi, and Hyderabad. Credible reports, partly contested, indicate that senior officers in Lahore expressed unwillingness to fire on crowds, and that Asghar Khan wrote to the armed forces urging them not to obey unlawful orders. Estimates of those killed during the months of protest range into the hundreds.

Negotiations between Bhutto and the PNA leadership, begun in early June, produced by 3 July an agreement in principle on new elections and a framework for supervising them, though details were still being settled. On the night of 4–5 July 1977 the army, under General Zia-ul-Haq, arrested Bhutto and the PNA leaders and took power in an operation named Fair Play. Zia stated that his intention was to hold elections within ninety days.

The central analytical question is what combination of factors led to this outcome. Electoral fraud was a major trigger: even if the PPP had won a majority, many observers and opponents thought the scale of its result implied manipulation, and the government's own offer to hold new elections in some constituencies suggested a recognition of problems. But the crisis cannot be reduced to fraud alone. The PNA had an interest in the government's overthrow and encouraged the army to intervene, Bhutto had narrowed the space for ordinary politics, and the army had its own institutional interests, including its position within the state and its wish to avoid being used against civilians. The Debate box below sets out these explanations.

The social composition of the movement mattered. Urban merchants and bazaar traders, many of whom had lost income to nationalization of small processing units in 1976 and to the government's price controls, closed shops during the strikes. Students, lawyers, and religious preachers supplied organization, and mosques became meeting points and sources of slogans. Women took part in demonstrations in some cities, an indication that the movement was not confined to a single social group. Rural areas, particularly in Sindh, remained largely loyal to the PPP. The geography of protest, concentrated in the cities of Punjab and Sindh, indicates that the crisis was in part an urban revolt against a government that had alienated a section of its earlier supporters, as well as a movement driven by the opposition's leaders.

The role of foreign governments was also alleged at the time. Bhutto charged that outside powers, particularly the United States, were financing the opposition. No documentary evidence has established such a plan, and historians treat these claims as part of the political argument and not as demonstrated fact. They do show that the government interpreted domestic opposition in terms of external threat, a habit visible in other crises of the period.

Historical Debate 1

Why Did Civilian Government Collapse in 1977?

The question: Was the breakdown caused principally by electoral malpractice, Bhutto's governing practices, opposition strategy, the armed forces' institutional interests, or the interaction of these?

Interpretation A: A crisis of legitimacy created by the government. The pattern of coercion in 1973–77, the unopposed returns of 1977, the reported scale of the PPP's majority, and the use of force against protesters show a government that had lost the consent of significant sections of society and used the state to retain power. On this view the army exploited a crisis the government had produced.
Interpretation B: A crisis exploited by opposition and army. The PNA's refusal to accept any result, its use of religious slogans to mobilize crowds, and its leaders' appeals to the military show that opposition strategy aimed at removing Bhutto by extra-electoral means. The army's own interests, including its desire to restore its position after 1971, and Zia's personal ambitions, made intervention likely once negotiations approached success.

Evidence: Both rest on the same facts: election returns, constituency reports, protest casualties, negotiation records, and memoirs by participants. Both can be partly true. Documents on the army's internal decision-making remain limited, and the timing of the coup, just after a negotiated agreement, is itself debated.

Why it matters: If the crisis arose mainly from government practices, the lesson concerns accountability within civilian politics. If opposition and army roles were decisive, the lesson concerns the willingness of political actors to invite military intervention. Most historians stress the interaction of both. The documented facts of the election and the repression are not themselves matters of opinion.

Section 95.9

The Coup of July 5, 1977: Martial Law, Political Repression, and Bhutto's Execution

General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, born in Jalandhar in 1924, commissioned in the British Indian Army and promoted to army chief in March 1976 over several more senior officers, took power in the early hours of 5 July 1977. In a broadcast he announced that the armed forces had acted to prevent a collapse of order, that he would serve as chief martial law administrator, and that elections would be held in October. The constitution was not abrogated. It was declared "held in abeyance," a formulation that allowed the military to claim continuity while suspending its operation: the National Assembly and provincial assemblies were dissolved, fundamental rights were suspended, and martial-law regulations took precedence. President Fazal Ilahi Chaudhry remained in office until September 1978, when Zia assumed the presidency himself.

The promised elections were postponed repeatedly. By the autumn of 1977 Zia had decided that "accountability" of politicians should come first, and in October 1979, after Bhutto's execution and amid fears that the PPP would win, he canceled the scheduled elections and banned party political activity. Political leaders were arrested, newspapers were censored, and military courts tried a wide range of offenses, including political ones. Journalists were among those sentenced to imprisonment and flogging in 1978. In March 1981 the government issued a Provisional Constitution Order requiring judges to take a new oath, and several senior judges who declined were removed. The effect was to subordinate the superior judiciary to the military government.

Bhutto was released on 28 July 1977, campaigned among supporters, and was arrested again in early September on a charge of conspiracy to murder Nawab Muhammad Ahmad Khan in 1974. The case was heard by the Lahore High Court, which, under an amendment to the interim constitutional order, sat as the trial court. It convicted Bhutto and sentenced him to death on 18 March 1978. The key prosecution evidence came from Masood Mahmood, a former head of the Federal Security Force who became an approver. Bhutto appealed to the Supreme Court, which, on 6 February 1979, dismissed his appeal by four votes to three. A review was rejected, appeals for clemency from foreign leaders were refused, and Bhutto was hanged at Rawalpindi on 4 April 1979.

The proceedings have been disputed from the start. Supporters of the PPP described the trial as a judicial killing carried out under pressure from a military government, and pointed to the chief justice of the Lahore High Court's visible hostility, to Bhutto's boycott of parts of the proceedings, and to the divided Supreme Court. The government and its defenders held that the courts had followed the law, that the evidence supported conviction, and that Bhutto was treated as any accused would be. These disagreements are distinct from questions the documents can answer, such as the procedural record, the composition of the benches, and the vote count.

Retrospective legal assessment In 2011 President Asif Ali Zardari, Bhutto's son-in-law, filed a presidential reference under Article 186 of the constitution asking the Supreme Court whether the trial had been fair. After years of delay a nine-member bench headed by Chief Justice Qazi Faez Isa heard the matter, and on 6 March 2024 it unanimously opined that the proceedings in the Lahore High Court and the Supreme Court did not meet the constitutional requirements of a fair trial and due process. This was an advisory opinion. The court indicated that it could not itself re-examine the evidence or undo the 1979 judgment, and a detailed opinion followed later in 2024. The opinion is evidence of how Pakistan's highest court assessed the case forty-five years later, not evidence of what happened in 1978–79. Historians should treat it as a retrospective judicial finding, not as a contemporaneous source.

Bhutto's execution became a defining event. It removed the PPP's founder, gave the party a martyr and a lasting claim to legitimacy, and left Zia with an enduring problem: a large part of the population, particularly in Sindh and among PPP workers, regarded his government as the product of an unjust act. The party's leadership passed to his widow, Nusrat Bhutto, and his daughter Benazir, both of whom were repeatedly detained. Their political careers form the thread leading to Section 95.19.

Section 95.10

Military Authoritarianism: Constitutional Orders, Political Control, and the Restructuring of Government

Zia's rule combined direct military authority with a deliberate effort to build civilian-looking institutions around it. He did not rely on force alone. The bureaucracy, which had served earlier regimes, continued to run the state, and the military expanded its presence in civil administration, public enterprises, and the judiciary. Military courts handled political offenses, and the regime made extensive use of detention, censorship, and public punishment. The press was subject to prior censorship for long periods. Student unions were banned in 1984. Political parties were restricted, then banned from 1979, and party activity operated at the margin of legality.

Opposition regrouped despite the restrictions. In February 1981 ten parties, led by the PPP, formed the Movement for the Restoration of Democracy (MRD), which demanded the lifting of martial law and fresh elections under the 1973 constitution. It was weakened by arrests, by a hijacking of a Pakistan International Airlines aircraft in March 1981 by a group claiming to act for Bhutto's sons (an episode that led the government to intensify repression), and by divisions among its members. In August 1983, after Zia announced a timetable for elections without party participation, the MRD launched a civil-disobedience movement. It was strongest in rural Sindh, where Bhutto's memory and Sindhi nationalist sentiment overlapped, and the army was deployed to suppress it. Estimates of deaths range from dozens to several hundred, and the movement ended in the autumn without achieving its aims. It left deep bitterness in Sindh, and it showed the limits of an opposition without access to the Punjab's main urban centers.

Zia then turned to a program of controlled political liberalization. On 19 December 1984 a referendum asked voters whether they endorsed the "process" of Islamization and, implicitly, Zia's continuation as president for five years. Official returns reported a turnout of about 62 percent and a "yes" vote above 97 percent; independent observers and opposition parties disputed both figures, noting that opposition parties had called for a boycott and that voting practices were irregular. The referendum should be understood less as a measure of opinion than as an instrument of legitimation.

Elections to the National Assembly and provincial assemblies followed in late February 1985 on a non-party basis. Candidates stood as individuals, and the MRD boycotted. The result was not the complete restoration of parliamentary democracy: no parties contested as such, separate electorates were restored for non-Muslims, and the powers of the elected assembly were limited by the president's legal authority. Zia chose Muhammad Khan Junejo, a Sindhi politician from a landholding family, as prime minister in March 1985. Junejo's government, with a loyal Muslim League faction, gradually asserted its own priorities, including a renewed interest in political activity and in the Afghan negotiations.

The constitutional settlement was crystallized in the Eighth Amendment, passed by the newly elected assembly in November 1985 after bargaining with Zia. In exchange for lifting martial law on 30 December 1985, the president gained the power under Article 58(2)(b) to dismiss the National Assembly at his discretion, subject to later review by the Supreme Court, and wider powers to appoint military chiefs, provincial governors, and the prime minister. The amendment also gave constitutional cover to acts of the martial-law period through an indemnity. Zia remained both president and army chief. The result was a hybrid, in which the constitution's parliamentary form coexisted with a presidency able to remove the elected government. The amendment's reach was displayed on 29 May 1988 when Zia dismissed Junejo, as described in Section 95.19. The table below compares the constitutional stages.

Zia also sought to create institutions that gave a measure of representation without party competition. In 1979 and again in 1983 his government held local-government elections on a non-party basis, which brought forward local notables and weakened national parties. In December 1981 he established a nominated Federal Advisory Council, soon renamed the Majlis-e-Shoora, to advise the government. Such bodies had no power over the military executive but provided a means of consultation and patronage. They are typical of a style of rule that preferred appointed or non-party arrangements to competitive politics, and they contributed to the personalized and clientelist character of political life in the 1980s.

The constitutional engineering of the 1980s should be considered in stages. Military rule initially depended on proclamations and orders that subordinated ordinary representative institutions. Zia then sought legitimacy through appointed consultative bodies, a referendum framed around Islamization, and elections in which candidates competed without formal party labels. The resulting assembly could debate ordinary government business, and Junejo could exercise some administrative independence, but the presidency retained extraordinary reserve powers. The Eighth Amendment constitutionalized significant aspects of this altered hierarchy, notably presidential authority to dissolve the National Assembly under Article 58(2)(b). This helps explain why ending martial law did not mean restoring the balance contemplated in 1973. The chief question was not simply whether civilians held offices, but whether their mandates could prevail against a president backed by the military establishment.

Section 95.11

Islamization: The Hudood Ordinances, Shariat Institutions, and Religious Legislation

Zia presented his government as an instrument of an Islamic order and used the term "Nizam-e-Mustafa" from the 1977 protests. Islamization had several overlapping components: legal reform, new institutions, state-administered religious taxation, changes in education and the media, and political alliances with religious parties, notably the Jamaat-e-Islami, which supported him in the early years. It should be understood as a program of statutory and institutional change, whose contents can be read in the legislation, and not as a general transformation of the beliefs of Pakistan's diverse communities.

On 10 February 1979 the government promulgated four Hudood Ordinances, on offences against property, on zina (adultery and fornication, including rape), on qazf (false accusation of zina), and on prohibition of alcohol, together with an ordinance on whipping. They prescribed punishments, including stoning, amputation, and flogging, for specified offenses when strict standards of proof were met, and discretionary punishments for lesser cases. In practice the harshest hadd punishments were seldom carried out, since appeal courts generally set them aside, but the ordinances had significant effects. The Zina Ordinance placed rape and consensual extramarital sex within the same legal framework, and a woman who reported rape but could not meet the evidentiary standard could herself face prosecution. The case of Safia Bibi, a blind girl convicted in 1982 after reporting rape, was widely publicized, and the Federal Shariat Court later set aside her conviction.

In June 1980 the Zakat and Ushr Ordinance introduced compulsory deduction of zakat, at 2.5 percent, from certain bank accounts and the collection of a tithe on agricultural produce, with proceeds distributed through committees. Shia Muslims, who follow a different jurisprudence on zakat, protested, and in July 1980 a large demonstration in Islamabad led to a decision to exempt Shia from the deductions. The episode was a landmark in Shia political organization, and it showed that a "uniform" Islamic law implied choices among Islamic traditions.

The Federal Shariat Court, created in May 1980, was empowered to examine whether laws were repugnant to Islamic injunctions as set out in the Quran and Sunnah, subject to appeal to a Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court. Important exclusions, including the constitution, Muslim personal law, court procedure, and, initially, fiscal laws, limited its reach. The court added a religious-legal body to a judicial hierarchy that already included the civil courts, and it changed the relationship among the executive, the judiciary, and religious scholars, since the court included ulema as well as judges. In 1984 the Law of Evidence (Qanun-e-Shahadat) replaced the older law, and in some matters a woman's testimony was given half the weight of a man's. In 1988 an ordinance declared Shariah the supreme law, but it lapsed with Zia's death.

Blasphemy-related provisions expanded in these years. A provision of 1980 criminalized derogatory remarks about holy personages of Islam, a provision of 1982 targeted the defiling of the Quran, and in 1986 a new section on insulting the Prophet Muhammad was added to the Penal Code. Ordinance XX of 1984 prohibited Ahmadis from calling themselves Muslims, calling their places of worship mosques, or using Islamic greetings and practices, and imposed imprisonment. The effect of these laws on the interaction between courts, executive authority, and religious bodies, as well as on minorities, intensified in later decades.

Islamization did not create the relationship between religion and the state. The Objectives Resolution, the Islamic provisions of the constitutions, the Council of Islamic Ideology, the Ahmadi amendment of 1974, and Bhutto's measures of 1977 all preceded Zia. What Zia contributed was scale, enforcement machinery, the constitutional elevation of the Resolution, and the use of criminal law. The next box evaluates explanations of why he did so.

The program reached beyond criminal law. A system of prayer committees was introduced to encourage regular prayer, an ordinance of 1981 restricted public eating and drinking during the fasting hours of Ramadan, and state media were instructed to follow Islamic norms in programming and dress. The curriculum in schools and colleges was revised to emphasize Islamic studies and a particular reading of Pakistan's ideological foundation, and an international Islamic university was established in Islamabad in 1980. Religious scholars and organizations received positions on official bodies, and funds from the zakat system supported religious institutions. These measures expanded the visibility of religion in public life and gave religious organizations a role in state-supported networks. They did not, however, transform the private beliefs and practices of Pakistan's diverse Muslim communities, which included Sunni schools of several traditions, Shia communities, and Sufi shrine cultures that did not conform to the state's model.

The legal record requires attention to timing and jurisdiction. The Hudood Ordinances were promulgated in February 1979, but the Federal Shariat Court was established by a distinct constitutional order in May 1980. The Zakat and Ushr Ordinance followed in June 1980. These instruments did different things: criminal law addressed specified offenses and evidentiary standards; a religious-tax system collected and distributed particular payments; and a specialized court reviewed some laws for conformity with Islamic injunctions, subject to jurisdictional exclusions. They were not a single law and did not affect every religious community in the same way. Contemporary controversy also concerned practical administration, including who could establish a religious obligation, the position of Shia legal practice, and whether court processes protected women facing accusations. Later amendments and judicial interpretations altered the original legal arrangements, so students consulting present-day consolidated codes must not assume their wording reproduces the provisions as enacted under Zia.

Historical Vocabulary
Hudood (hadd)

Hudood (singular hadd, "limit") refers in classical Islamic jurisprudence to a category of crimes with fixed punishments said to be prescribed by scripture and subject to strict standards of proof. In Pakistan the term came to name the 1979 ordinances, which applied a modern statutory form of these categories. Classical jurists differed on evidence and procedure, and Pakistani legislation reflected specific choices. The term should not be confused with the whole of Islamic law or with the practices of other Muslim-majority states.

Historical Vocabulary
Article 58(2)(b) and the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment of 1985 inserted into Article 58 a clause that allowed the president to dissolve the National Assembly "in his discretion" where he judged that the government could not be carried on in accordance with the constitution and an appeal to the electorate was necessary. It became the principal instrument by which presidents dismissed governments in 1988, 1990, 1993, and 1996. It was repealed in 1997 and restored in a different form in 2002 before final repeal in 2010. This chapter is concerned with its origin and first use.

Historical Debate 2

Was Zia's Islamization Primarily an Ideological Transformation or a Strategy of Political Legitimation?

The question: Did Zia act mainly from religious conviction, or did he use Islam to compensate for the absence of electoral legitimacy?

Interpretation A: Ideological commitment. Zia's personal piety, his links with religious parties, and the breadth of the legal and educational changes suggest a sincere project to remake the state according to a particular conception of Islam. The institutional detail, including the courts, the taxes, and the curricula, points to a lasting program.
Interpretation B: Legitimation. A ruler who took power by coup, executed an elected prime minister, and postponed elections needed a source of authority. Islamic policy built a constituency among religious parties and conservative groups, divided the opposition, and justified military rule as a mission. The timing of key measures, such as the 1984 referendum, supports this reading.

Evidence: Legislation, speeches, party alliances, referendum wording, policy implementation, and later testimony. The two interpretations are not exclusive. Zia's piety could coexist with political calculation, and institutions created for one purpose could acquire their own constituencies.

Why it matters: If the program was largely instrumental, it might be reversible once the regime ended; if it was ideological and institutional, it would persist. In fact many measures persisted, though others, including Zia's vision of a unified Islamic order, met resistance from Shia, women, and minority groups. The contents of the laws and the fact of repression are documented and are not matters of opinion; the question concerns motive and effect.

Section 95.12

Society under Zia: Women, Religious Minorities, Sectarian Relations, and Political Resistance

The legal changes of the Zia years were experienced differently by different communities. Women were affected most directly by the Hudood Ordinances, the evidence law, restrictions on dress and public appearance in state media, and the broader climate of cultural conservatism. At the same time women's participation in education and employment continued to grow, remittance income altered family economies, and women organized in response. The Women's Action Forum (WAF), founded in Karachi in September 1981, brought together lawyers, teachers, journalists, and social workers to oppose discriminatory legislation. In February 1983, when a draft evidence law threatened to halve the weight of women's testimony, WAF organized a procession in Lahore, which the police attacked with tear gas and batons. The episode became a symbol of the movement. WAF's campaigns, along with the work of lawyers and the Human Rights Commission, which was founded in 1986, kept pressure on the legal changes and contributed to later partial reforms.

Religious minorities faced distinct pressures. Ahmadis, already classified as non-Muslims by the 1974 amendment, became subject to criminal penalties under Ordinance XX of 1984. Their leader, Mirza Tahir Ahmad, left Pakistan for London soon after its promulgation. Christians, Hindus, and other non-Muslims were affected by the reintroduction of separate electorates in 1985, which replaced the joint electorates used in earlier elections and placed minority voters in separate constituencies, and by the blasphemy provisions whose application to minorities became a source of fear in later decades. Minority communities also faced the broader social effect of a state that defined citizenship in religious terms.

Sectarian relations within Islam changed as well. The Zakat ordinance, the 1979 revolution in Iran, Saudi and Iranian funding of religious institutions, and the state's patronage of particular religious bodies contributed to a new competitiveness among Sunni and Shia organizations. In 1979 the Tehrik-e-Nifaz-e-Fiqh-e-Jafariya was formed to defend Shia interests. In 1985 the Anjuman Sipah-e-Sahaba was founded in Jhang in Punjab, by Sunni activists opposed to what they saw as Shia influence, and it became the nucleus of later militant sectarian organizations. Researchers differ about how much the state encouraged these developments and how much they grew from local conflicts, such as in Jhang where landowning families and merchant communities were divided along sectarian lines. It is useful to separate three things: religious activism, meaning organization and advocacy within a religious community; political opposition, meaning parties and movements contesting power; and organized violence, meaning armed groups that targeted persons. The three overlapped but were not the same, and most religious activists did not engage in violence.

Regional and ethnic tensions also shaped society. The Muhajir community, descended from migrants from India, faced competition with Sindhis and Pashtuns in Karachi, and in April 1985 the death of a student, Bushra Zaidi, in a traffic accident sparked ethnic violence that helped accelerate the rise of the Muhajir Qaumi Movement. The sense of exclusion felt by Sindhis after Bhutto's execution and the suppression of the MRD movement led to growing Sindhi nationalism. Punjab, whose large cities had supported the PNA and where the army recruited most heavily, was generally less affected by the repression.

The relationship between Zia's state-building project, institutional authority, and social resistance can be summarized as follows. The regime was successful in building institutions that outlived it: the Federal Shariat Court, the Hudood laws, the presidential powers of the Eighth Amendment, and a close relationship between the army and religious networks. It was less successful in creating consent. Resistance came from the MRD, from Sindhi nationalists, from women's groups, from minorities, from Shia organizations, from lawyers and journalists, and from the many Pakistanis who simply continued their lives outside the state's moral program. Resistance did not overthrow Zia, but it limited the regime's ability to present itself as the embodiment of a national consensus.

Women's rights activists built organizations and legal arguments despite the constraints of military government. The Women's Action Forum brought together lawyers, writers, academics, and other professionals who challenged legislation and public statements that reduced women's legal or political standing. Protest did not always resemble a national party campaign: it could involve litigation, public meetings, professional associations, and support for individuals caught in discriminatory proceedings. Shia protests against state-administered zakat similarly revealed that a government claiming Islamic consensus confronted substantial disagreement among Muslims over religious authority. For Ahmadis, the distinction between the constitutional declaration of 1974 and the penal restrictions of 1984 was especially consequential in everyday worship and public identification. These examples demonstrate that opposition to authoritarian policy did not come from a single ideological position; participants could disagree profoundly about religion while defending different forms of legal security.

Section 95.13

Afghanistan's Crisis and the Soviet Intervention of 1979

Pakistan's relationship with Afghanistan was shaped by a frontier that neither state had chosen. The Durand Line, agreed in 1893 between the Afghan amir and the government of British India, divided the Pashtun-speaking population, and Afghan governments, while accepting it as a practical boundary, never formally recognized it as an international border after 1947. Kabul supported the idea of "Pashtunistan," a Pashtun political entity or autonomy, and in 1955 and 1961–63 border tensions led to closures of the frontier. For the Pakistani military establishment, Afghanistan was therefore both a neighbor with unresolved claims and a space whose political orientation mattered for security, particularly given tension with India on the other frontier.

In July 1973 Mohammad Daoud, a former prime minister, overthrew the Afghan monarchy and proclaimed a republic, reviving the Pashtunistan question. The Bhutto government responded by supporting Afghan Islamist opponents of Daoud, many of whom had been educated at Kabul University and fled to Peshawar after a failed uprising in 1975. These included Gulbuddin Hekmatyar and Burhanuddin Rabbani. This early support, undertaken under Bhutto and not Zia, is important evidence that Pakistani involvement in Afghan politics predates the Soviet intervention.

On 27–28 April 1978 officers allied with the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA) overthrew Daoud in the Saur Revolution. The new government under Nur Muhammad Taraki, from the Khalq faction, undertook rapid reforms in land, marriage, and education, imposed by force on a rural society, and carried out repression of opponents, including religious leaders. Rural revolts spread, and an uprising in Herat in March 1979 included the killing of Soviet advisers. The PDPA itself divided. In September 1979 Hafizullah Amin removed and later killed Taraki, and thereafter the Soviet leadership, concerned about Amin's reliability and the possibility of a collapse of a friendly regime, decided to intervene. Soviet forces entered in late December 1979, killed Amin, and installed Babrak Karmal.

The Soviet intervention changed Pakistan's position immediately. A Soviet army, rather than an unstable neighbor government, now stood on the Durand Line. Pakistani leaders feared encirclement, possible Soviet pressure on Balochistan, and the movement of refugees. They also saw an opportunity: the war would bring the United States back as a security partner, give the army a central role, and provide an opportunity to install a friendly government in Kabul. This analysis, often described as seeking "strategic depth" against India, was articulated more explicitly in later years. In January 1980 Pakistan hosted a meeting of Islamic foreign ministers in Islamabad that condemned the intervention. In September 1979 Pakistan had also joined the Non-Aligned Movement, and it used both forums in its diplomacy.

The chapter's focus is Pakistan, not Afghan national history, and it is important to note what is not covered. The causes of the Saur Revolution, the nature of the PDPA's factions, the Soviet decision-making process, and the course of the fighting inside Afghanistan lie outside this chapter's scope. What matters for Pakistan is that, by early 1980, a regime in Kabul that Pakistan regarded as hostile was backed by a superpower, millions of Afghans were beginning to cross the frontier, and a military government in Islamabad was seeking to convert the crisis into diplomatic and military advantage.

Pakistan's frontier concerns also had a history independent of the Cold War. Afghan governments had questioned the legitimacy of the Durand Line, and Pashtun political affiliations crossed the state boundary. Islamabad therefore assessed developments in Kabul through the possibilities of hostile interference, refugee flows, and the vulnerability of provincial politics. Yet Afghanistan was not a chessboard occupied only by Pakistan and the Soviet Union. Rural insurgencies emerged in response to the Kabul government's own coercive reforms, factional struggles, and resistance by religious and local leaders. The sequence of intervention matters: Afghan conflict intensified before Soviet troops crossed the border in December 1979. Pakistan's subsequent support for armed resistance answered existing regional tensions as well as a new global strategic opportunity. The distinction prevents the foreign invasion from being mistaken for the sole origin of the war.

Section 95.14

Pakistan's Support for the Afghan Mujahideen: Intelligence, Training, and Cross-Border Warfare

The Inter-Services Intelligence Directorate (ISI), directed by Lieutenant General Akhtar Abdur Rahman from 1979 to 1987, became the principal channel for support to Afghan resistance organizations. It had expanded in importance under Bhutto and especially under Zia, and the Afghan war enlarged its funding, personnel, and influence in domestic and foreign policy. Its Afghan bureau, led for several years by Brigadier Mohammad Yousaf, who later wrote an account of the program, coordinated the distribution of weapons, training, and intelligence.

The program was a collaboration among several states. The United States, through the Central Intelligence Agency, provided funds and arms, initially at modest levels and then, as the war continued, at several hundred million dollars annually by the late 1980s. Saudi Arabia matched much of the American contribution. China supplied some weapons. The arrangement left Pakistan in control of the distribution: American and Saudi supplies were delivered to Pakistani authorities, who passed them to the Afghan groups. This control gave the ISI considerable leverage over the resistance and gave Zia a central place in American strategy. The supply of Stinger surface-to-air missiles from 1986 altered the war in the air. The extent of American knowledge of, and influence over, the allocation of aid among Afghan groups remains a matter of scholarly discussion.

Pakistan allowed only a limited number of Afghan political organizations to operate from its territory, and recognized seven in the alliance known as the Peshawar Seven: the Hizb-i Islami of Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, the Jamiat-i Islami of Burhanuddin Rabbani, a second Hizb-i Islami led by Yunus Khalis, the Ittehad-i Islami of Abdul Rasul Sayyaf, and three more moderate or traditionalist groups led by Sibghatullah Mojaddedi, Sayyid Ahmad Gailani, and Mohammad Nabi Mohammadi. Pakistan favored the Islamist parties, particularly Hekmatyar's, over royalist and traditionalist ones, in part because they were better organized and more ideologically aligned with the Jamaat-e-Islami, and because they were less likely to revive Pashtunistan claims. Afghan Shia groups based in Iran, and commanders operating inside Afghanistan who were not attached to a Peshawar party, such as Ahmad Shah Massoud for much of the period, received less or different support. The distribution of aid thus shaped the balance among Afghan factions and, later, the civil war of the 1990s.

It is necessary to avoid treating the resistance as a single unified movement. The seven parties competed, sometimes violently. Commanders inside Afghanistan often acted autonomously. Ethnic, tribal, and sectarian differences divided Pashtuns, Tajiks, Hazaras, and Uzbeks. A small number of Arab and other foreign volunteers came, particularly in the mid-1980s, drawn through networks in Peshawar, but they were a small fraction of the fighters and were not the main channel of Pakistani or American support. Distinguishing Afghan resistance parties, Pakistani political and religious organizations, foreign volunteers, and the later militant groups that emerged in the 1990s prevents several common errors.

Pakistan paid a price for its involvement. The Afghan government's intelligence service, KhAD, with Soviet help, carried out a campaign of bombings in Pakistani cities between 1986 and 1987, in Karachi, Peshawar, Rawalpindi, and elsewhere, killing hundreds of civilians. Aircraft violations and cross-border shelling occurred. The Pakistani government sought, in the negotiations that began in Geneva in 1982 under United Nations auspices, to secure Soviet withdrawal and the return of refugees. The war thus brought both resources and risks to the state. The following source window concerns the documentary record.

Logistics were a significant part of the program. Weapons arrived in Karachi, were moved by road and rail to storage depots in the Rawalpindi area, and were then passed to Afghan parties for transport across the frontier, by truck to the border and by mule and on foot inside Afghanistan. The army's National Logistics Cell, a transport organization, is reported to have carried much of this cargo. Camps in the frontier areas trained Afghan fighters in the use of weapons, and Pakistani officers advised on tactics, though Pakistani policy avoided direct participation by regular troops inside Afghanistan. The scale of the pipeline meant that opportunities for diversion, corruption, and resale were considerable, and the question of how much of the materiel reached its intended recipients was discussed during the war and afterward.

The assistance pipeline was a system of political selection as well as military supply. Afghan parties maintained offices and recruitment networks in Pakistan, but access to external support depended on their relationships with Pakistani officials and other sponsors. Funneling resources through recognized intermediaries could improve coordination while narrowing the influence of groups outside those channels. Rival parties competed for recruits, recognition, weapons, and the right to represent Afghan resistance abroad; their supporters did not all share a common plan for the country after a Soviet withdrawal. American and Saudi contributions had different institutional routes, and publicly documented assistance should be separated from covert programs whose details became available only later. Declassified United States memoranda show that policymakers considered refugee burdens, Pakistan's security needs, nuclear restrictions, and regional diplomacy together. A claim that the war was simply fought for Washington would neglect Islamabad's independent calculations and the Afghan parties' own agency.

Section 95.15

Afghan Refugees in Pakistan: Displacement, Humanitarian Assistance, and Social Transformation

Afghans began to cross into Pakistan after the Saur Revolution of April 1978, and the numbers rose sharply after the Soviet intervention. By the end of 1979, government-registered refugees were about 400,000. The Government of Pakistan informed UNHCR of the arrivals in April 1979, UNHCR opened an office in Islamabad in 1980, and in October 1980 Pakistan created an office for Afghan refugees that later became the Commissionerate for Afghan Refugees. Registration figures reached roughly one million by mid-1980 and about 2.3 million by the end of 1981. They peaked at approximately 3.2 to 3.3 million in 1990, with additional unregistered refugees estimated at several hundred thousand.

Most refugees were Pashtuns from eastern and southern Afghanistan, and they settled mainly in the NWFP and Balochistan, with significant numbers also in Punjab and Karachi. Pakistan was not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention but allowed Afghans to enter, and chose not to confine them to closed camps. Instead, it established "refugee villages," which were administered with assistance from UNHCR and the World Food Programme. Refugees received monthly food rations, basic health services, and some education. By the mid-1980s, according to a UNHCR report, more than 650 primary schools taught Afghan children in their own languages. In the early years the refugees lived in tents, and conditions in many settlements improved only after the middle of the decade as tents were replaced by mud-brick houses and hopes of an early return faded. Refugee registration and assistance frequently operated through Afghan political parties, which gave the parties influence over the refugee population.

The refugees' relations with host communities were complex. Shared language and kinship among Pashtuns on both sides of the border eased reception. Many refugees found work in agriculture, transport, construction, and trade, and in some areas they competed with local laborers. Local residents complained of pressure on firewood, grazing land, water, and housing, and of rising prices and congestion in Peshawar and Quetta. Others benefited from international assistance, from increased trade, and from the new employment. Over time, refugee households developed commercial networks that extended across the border, and some maintained a foothold in both countries. The international assistance programs were significant: UNHCR's budget reached about $109 million in 1981, though Pakistan's own burden, which its government estimated at $160 million in that year, was also substantial.

The presence of refugees had political and security dimensions. The refugee villages were also recruiting and rest areas for the resistance, and the line between civilian and military roles was blurred. The state's willingness to host so many people was supported by external funds and by a political consensus that Afghans were fellow Muslims fleeing a Soviet-backed regime. Later chapters trace how the refugee population, which remained after the Soviet withdrawal, became a source of tension in the 1990s and beyond.

Refugee settlement changed the fabric of frontier towns and rural districts. Provincial administrations, camp officers, international organizations, and local charities had to coordinate food distributions, registration, health provision, and school places while operating with limited information about how long families would remain. Registration could matter directly to a household's access to rations; consequently, it was an administrative category, not a perfect measure of the number of people who had crossed the border. Families frequently moved between camps and towns or supplemented assistance through work, trade, and kinship connections. The state and host communities were not uniform in their responses. Shared language and religion could support hospitality, but pressure on housing, grazing land, wages, and public services also produced disputes. A gender-sensitive history asks how women navigated childcare, access to medical care, and restrictions on mobility, rather than treating refugees only as recipients of grain or as potential combatants.

International reports provide snapshots rather than a single definitive population trajectory. UNHCR reported approximately 400,000 government-registered Afghan refugees at the beginning of 1980, around 1.4 million at the start of 1981, and over 2.375 million identified by the Pakistani government at the end of that year. These figures refer to distinct dates and administrative processes; they cannot be used to calculate precise arrivals or deaths by subtraction. Humanitarian agencies also changed their programs as the emergency lengthened, moving from short-term relief toward education, health care, and livelihoods. The central historical point is that the refugee presence became a lasting feature of Pakistan's economy and governance, not a brief interruption expected to end with one military campaign.

Section 95.16

The Domestic Consequences of the Afghan War: Militarization, Arms, Drugs, and Religious Networks

The war had far-reaching domestic effects. The flow of weapons, much of it intended for the resistance, led to diversion into Pakistani markets. The "Kalashnikov culture," a term used in the 1980s, referred to the spread of automatic weapons in urban and rural Pakistan, especially in Karachi and the NWFP, and it altered the conduct of political, ethnic, and sectarian disputes. A large explosion at the Ojhri ammunition depot near Rawalpindi on 10 April 1988, which scattered rockets across the twin cities, killed scores of people, and led to political conflict between Junejo and the army over inquiry into the arms pipeline, illustrated the risks.

Narcotics trafficking grew in parallel. The tribal areas and Afghanistan became major sources of opium, and heroin refining expanded in the frontier region. Pakistan, which had had relatively little heroin use before 1979, saw estimates of addicts in the mid-1980s rise to several hundred thousand and, in some estimates, over a million, with reports of involvement by traffickers with local political and official links. The numbers are uncertain and depend on survey methods, but there is broad agreement that heroin addiction became a serious public-health problem in these years.

Religious networks changed as well. The number of religious schools, or madrasas, increased during the 1980s, though estimates vary considerably, and the Zakat system, foreign donations, and state patronage supported many. Some prepared students for religious leadership, others had links with Afghan resistance parties and with Pakistani religious parties, and some recruited volunteers. The expansion of such networks, together with the growth of sectarian organizations, formed part of the context of later militancy. However, scholars caution against treating these schools, the mujahideen, the refugees, and later militant groups as interchangeable. Most madrasas were not militant training centers, most refugees were not fighters, and the groups that emerged in the 1990s drew on many causes, including the Kashmir conflict, the Afghan civil war, regional politics, and developments after the Cold War.

Contemporary debate concerns whether Pakistan's support for the Afghan resistance, and its policy toward Afghanistan afterward, laid the basis for long-term instability. Critics argue that the program built an intelligence-military apparatus with strong interests in the use of armed proxies and distorted domestic politics. Defenders argue that Pakistan had few alternatives given the Soviet presence on its border, that the policy contributed to Soviet withdrawal, and that later outcomes owed much to decisions made after 1989, when external attention faded. A responsible account avoids attributing every later militant movement to a single cause, and treats the 1980s as one important condition among several.

The war's diplomatic conclusion came with the Geneva Accords, signed on 14 April 1988 by Afghanistan and Pakistan, with the United States and the Soviet Union as guarantors. They provided for non-interference, the return of refugees, and Soviet troop withdrawal, which began on 15 May 1988 and was completed on 15 February 1989. The accords did not settle the question of who would govern Kabul, and Zia and Junejo had disagreed on whether to accept them without an interim government. The consequences after 1989, including the Afghan civil war and Pakistan's relations with Afghanistan in the post-Cold War period, belong to later chapters, and this chapter now turns to Pakistan's wider international position.

The effects of conflict crossed institutional boundaries. Commercial transport that supplied civilian markets also carried military goods; traders and intermediaries profited from changing border demand. Weapons that moved through conflict networks could circulate within Pakistan, but tracing a particular later act of violence to a specific shipment is much more difficult than describing the growth of illicit markets overall. The narcotics economy likewise involved multiple producers, traffickers, and enforcement agencies and should not be reduced to a government conspiracy without documentary proof. Armed organizations benefited from training networks, religious rhetoric, and external funding, while the great majority of Afghan refugees had no combat role. Pakistani authorities themselves disagreed over the future: some military strategists emphasized influence in Kabul, while civilian officials confronted diplomatic costs, refugee administration, and demands for a negotiated end. Such disagreements show why the phrase 'Pakistan's Afghan policy' conceals several competing institutions.

Section 95.17

United States–Pakistan Relations: Nuclear Concerns, Sanctions, and Cold War Cooperation

American–Pakistani relations had been close in the 1950s, when Pakistan joined the Baghdad Pact and SEATO, but cooled after the arms embargoes of 1965 and the 1971 war. Bhutto's years brought cordial but limited ties. The principal obstacle was Pakistan's nuclear activity. After India's test of 1974 (treated in Chapter 97), Pakistan pursued uranium enrichment, and in 1977–79 American law restricted aid to states importing enrichment technology. In April 1979 the Carter administration suspended most economic and military assistance under the Symington Amendment, citing Pakistan's enrichment program. Zia's execution of Bhutto against appeals from many governments, including the United States, added to the coolness. On 21 November 1979, after a false rumor that Americans had been involved in the seizure of the Grand Mosque in Mecca, a mob attacked and burned the US embassy in Islamabad, killing two Americans.

The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan changed the calculation. In January 1980 the Carter administration offered Pakistan about $400 million in economic and military aid over two years, which Zia dismissed as insufficient. The Reagan administration negotiated a larger arrangement, agreed in 1981, of approximately $3.2 billion in economic and military assistance, divided roughly equally, over five to six fiscal years (sources describe it as a five-year or fiscal 1982–87 program), together with the sale of F-16 aircraft. Congress passed a waiver of the Symington restrictions in December 1981. A second package of about $4 billion, covering the years from 1987 to 1993, followed in 1986.

The relationship was transactional. Washington wanted Pakistan as the base for supporting the Afghan resistance, and Pakistan wanted American arms, money, and political recognition. Both set aside their disagreements. Washington regarded Pakistan's nuclear program with concern but, as long as the Afghan war lasted, avoided actions that would end cooperation. In 1985 Congress adopted the Pressler Amendment, requiring the president to certify annually that Pakistan did not possess a nuclear explosive device as a condition of aid. Presidents Reagan and Bush made the certification through 1989. In October 1990, after the Soviet withdrawal and with Pakistan judged to have crossed the threshold, President Bush declined to certify and aid was suspended. This 1990 decision lies beyond the main period of this chapter and is noted here only as a consequence of the 1980s arrangements.

The relationship strengthened Zia's position in several ways. American aid provided resources for the military and the budget. International recognition helped counter the stigma of a regime that had executed a prime minister. And the Western press, which had criticized the regime in 1979, gave less attention to its human-rights record in the 1980s. Critics inside and outside Pakistan argued that the United States had tolerated authoritarianism and proliferation for the sake of the Afghan war. Defenders of American policy argued that it advanced a legitimate Cold War objective and that Pakistan was a sovereign actor with its own aims. Both points of view recognize that security assistance, diplomatic priorities, and proliferation-related restrictions were intertwined, and that the sequence of suspension, resumption, and renewed suspension reflected shifts in American priorities rather than a settled principle.

American policy also changed within the administrations that pursued it. Before December 1979, nuclear nonproliferation concerns constrained security cooperation with Pakistan; after Soviet intervention, strategic planners argued that a vulnerable Pakistan required assistance. Declassified memoranda record debate about aid instruments, military equipment, and the consequences of appearing to endorse martial law. Congressional restrictions were not simply forgotten: waivers, certifications, and competing statutory requirements shaped the assistance relationship. For Pakistani officials the issue was whether outside support could be secured without surrendering what they regarded as essential security interests, including the nuclear program. For critics of Zia, foreign assistance risked strengthening a government that had never won a competitive national election. The same agreement therefore looked like regional stabilization to one audience and authoritarian entrenchment to another.

Section 95.18

Pakistan's International Position: China, Saudi Arabia, the Muslim World, and Regional Security

China was Pakistan's most consistent partner. Since the 1960s, when the two states settled a border and developed political ties, China had supplied weapons and technology, and Pakistani leaders valued it as a counterweight to India and a source of arms not tied to political conditions. The Karakoram Highway, built jointly across the Himalayan passes and opened in the late 1970s, linked Pakistan's northern areas with Xinjiang. China also provided assistance, according to numerous reports, in the nuclear and missile fields, a subject treated in Chapter 97. Chinese support did not depend on the American alliance and continued whether Washington and Islamabad were close or distant.

Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states formed the third pillar. Under Bhutto, Pakistan had cultivated the Muslim world through the Lahore summit. Under Zia, ties with Saudi Arabia deepened through financial support, oil supplies, joint funding of the Afghan resistance, and religious cooperation. Under a 1982 arrangement Pakistan stationed a military contingent in the kingdom, reported at up to about 20,000 personnel, and the army and air force trained Saudi personnel. At the same time Pakistani workers, in large numbers, found jobs in the Gulf, and their remittances became a major source of foreign exchange and household income, a topic that Chapter 97 treats in detail. Saudi and other Gulf funding of religious institutions in Pakistan accompanied these ties and affected the religious landscape described in Section 95.12.

Pakistan's relations with Iran and with the wider Muslim world were more complicated. The Iranian revolution of 1979 removed the Shah, an important ally, and created a state with a revolutionary Shia ideology. Pakistan maintained correct relations with Tehran and sought neutrality in the Iran–Iraq War while balancing its dependence on Saudi Arabia and the sensitivities of its own Shia minority. It used the Organization of the Islamic Conference to build support for its position on Afghanistan, and it joined the Non-Aligned Movement in 1979, which helped it present the Afghan question as one of sovereignty and not of Cold War alignment.

With India, relations were marked by the Simla framework and by recurrent tensions. The 1980s saw the beginning of the conflict on the Siachen glacier in 1984, a military crisis in 1986–87 during Indian exercises near the border, and Zia's visit to India in early 1987 under the cover of a cricket match, which helped reduce tension. Pakistan's nuclear program formed a strategic background, and Pakistani officials saw nuclear capability as a deterrent to India's conventional superiority. Chapter 97 treats this program, and Chapter 94 treats the Indian side of the Punjab crisis, which also affected relations.

These partnerships strengthened Zia's international position even as his government faced criticism at home. Foreign aid reduced the pressure on his government to reform. Diplomatic recognition by the United States, China, and Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan's role in the Afghan war, gave the regime a significance in international politics that it lacked domestically. They also created dependence. By the late 1980s Pakistan's military, budget, and diplomacy were tied to outside sponsors and to the continuation of conditions that would change once the Soviet Union left Afghanistan.

Labor migration and remittances connected foreign relations to household economies. Pakistani workers in Gulf countries sent earnings that supported consumption, construction, education, and small enterprises at home, and helped ease pressure on foreign-exchange reserves. These transfers were not equivalent to government aid: they originated from workers and their families, and their distribution reflected unequal access to overseas employment. Relations with Saudi Arabia therefore had an economic dimension distinct from its contributions to Afghan resistance networks or its religious and diplomatic ties with Islamabad. China, for its part, remained an important strategic partner with interests not identical to either Washington's or Riyadh's. Zia's government could draw upon several relationships at once, yet this flexibility also made domestic planning vulnerable to conditions beyond Pakistan's control.

Section 95.19

Zia's Death and the Return to Electoral Politics, 1988

By 1988 the arrangements of 1985 were under strain. Prime Minister Junejo had begun to assert independence, particularly on the Geneva negotiations, where he supported an agreement that Zia felt left insufficient guarantee of a friendly Kabul, and over the Ojhri inquiry. On 29 May 1988, Zia used Article 58(2)(b) to dismiss Junejo and dissolve the National Assembly and provincial assemblies, promising elections within ninety days. He remained president and army chief, and he appointed a caretaker cabinet.

On 17 August 1988 Zia's C-130 aircraft crashed shortly after leaving the Bahawalpur area, where he had attended a demonstration of an American tank. Zia was killed, together with General Akhtar Abdur Rahman, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee and former head of the ISI, several other senior Pakistani officers, the American ambassador, Arnold Raphel, and an American brigadier general, among about thirty people. Investigations did not establish a definitive cause. A Pakistani inquiry reportedly concluded that sabotage was likely but identified no perpetrator, while American investigators indicated that mechanical failure could not be excluded and that sabotage could not be proven. Theories have pointed to Soviet or Afghan agents, to Indian involvement, to domestic opponents, to rivals within the military, and to other actors. None has been demonstrated. Historians accordingly treat the cause as unresolved, and the competing accounts as allegations.

Succession proceeded constitutionally. Under Article 49 the chairman of the Senate, Ghulam Ishaq Khan, became acting president, and General Mirza Aslam Beg, the vice chief of army staff, became army chief. Beg and Ishaq Khan chose to keep the election schedule. A Supreme Court decision in early October 1988 held that restrictions on party participation in elections were invalid, allowing party-based competition for the first time since 1977. The PPP, led by Benazir Bhutto, who had returned from exile in 1986 to a large welcome, contested as a party, while an alliance of right-of-center parties, the Islami Jamhoori Ittehad (IJI), led by Nawaz Sharif and including parties linked to Zia, opposed it.

The National Assembly election of 16 November 1988, followed by provincial elections three days later, produced a divided outcome. The PPP emerged as the largest party without an outright majority, while the IJI won about 55 elected seats and the Muhajir Qaumi Movement won a significant bloc in urban Sindh. The Inter-Parliamentary Union archival page gives inconsistent PPP seat figures (92 in its narrative, 94 in its results table), so a single uncontested count cannot be inferred from that page. The PPP formed a government at the center with the support of smaller parties, while the IJI controlled the Punjab under Nawaz Sharif. On 2 December 1988 Benazir Bhutto, aged 35, was sworn in as prime minister, the first woman to head the government of a modern Muslim-majority state. She took office after understandings with the president and the army about continuing arrangements, including the retention of Ghulam Ishaq Khan as president, who was formally elected on 12 December, and the continuation of the foreign minister, Sahabzada Yaqub Khan.

The return of an elected civilian government was a major transition. It ended eleven years of military rule, brought back party-based politics, and returned the daughter of the executed prime minister to office. But it should not be equated with the disappearance of military influence. The president retained the power to dismiss the government, the army chief remained independent, the army's role in foreign and security policy, particularly on Afghanistan and India, continued, and the new prime minister's freedom of action was limited. The outcome of the 1988 election was not the decisive victory that many PPP supporters had hoped for, and within two years the president would use Article 58(2)(b) to dismiss her government.

The political significance of Benazir Bhutto's return deserves emphasis. After her father's execution she had been repeatedly detained or placed under house arrest, left Pakistan for medical treatment and exile in 1984, and returned in April 1986 to enormous crowds in Lahore. Her party's victory in 1988, even without a majority, was widely understood as a verdict on the Zia years, particularly in Sindh, where it won decisively. At the same time the IJI's success in Punjab, the country's most populous province, showed that Zia's legacy retained a constituency, so the contest was not simply one between dictatorship and democracy. The balance between these two forces would define the politics of the following decade, and Chapter 101 takes up that story.

The November election's party totals require careful handling. The Inter-Parliamentary Union's archived account states in its narrative summary that the PPP won 92 seats, but its results table records 94; the archive does not explain the discrepancy within that page. Later compilations sometimes use different denominators or include subsequently assigned seats. Accordingly the safe conclusion for this chapter is that the PPP was the largest party but lacked an outright majority, requiring alliances to form a government. It would be misleading to insert an unqualified single figure while presenting the same archival page as definitive evidence. The disputed count does not change the essential constitutional transition: Benazir Bhutto assumed office in December 1988, while the presidency and armed forces retained independent sources of influence.

Section 95.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Civilian Reconstruction, Military Authority, Islamization, and the Afghan War

Four transformations run through the preceding nineteen sections. The first is the reconstruction of Pakistan after 1971. The state that remained after the loss of Bangladesh was smaller, more Punjabi in its demographic center of gravity, and more preoccupied with questions of unity. Bhutto's government responded with a new constitution, a program of state-led economic change, a reordered foreign policy, and settlement with India at Simla. These were substantial achievements and they stabilized a state that many observers thought might fragment further. The second transformation is the weakness of civilian constitutional government. The 1973 settlement, however broadly agreed, did not integrate the army into a clear civilian framework, did not provide mechanisms by which provincial governments could be secure against dismissal, and was weakened by the government that had helped create it. The Balochistan crisis, the banning of the NAP, the expansion of preventive detention, and the election of 1977 left the constitutional order without the legitimacy needed to withstand a crisis.

The third transformation is the institutional and social consequences of Zia's military rule and Islamization. Zia rebuilt the state around the presidency and the army, subordinated the courts, restricted parties and the press, and then devised a hybrid constitutional form in the Eighth Amendment. He used Islamic law and institutions as sources of authority, with lasting effects on criminal law, courts, minorities, women, and sectarian relations. The fourth transformation is the strategic repositioning through the Afghan war. The war brought American, Saudi, and Chinese support, millions of refugees, an enlarged intelligence service, new religious networks, arms, and narcotics. It turned a state that had been isolated in 1979 into a frontline partner of the United States by 1981, and it placed the army at the center of foreign and security policy.

The Bhutto and Zia eras should not be treated as simple opposites. In several respects there is continuity. Both relied on strong central executive authority, with Bhutto's centralization through the party and the Federal Security Force and Zia's through martial law and the presidency. Both found federal accommodation difficult: the Balochistan insurgency continued into Zia's time under different conditions, and Sindh's alienation grew after 1977. Both used religious language and policy to meet opposition, as Bhutto's 1974 amendment and 1977 concessions show. Both preserved the large public sector. And both depended on external patrons, Bhutto on China, Iran, and the Gulf, Zia on the United States, Saudi Arabia, and China. The differences were equally real. Bhutto drew authority from electoral mandate and populist mobilization, Zia from the army and an appeal to religious legitimacy; Bhutto nationalized, Zia relied on remittances and aid; Bhutto's constitution was parliamentary, Zia's amended form was presidential; and Zia's repression of opposition went beyond Bhutto's, though Bhutto's treatment of opponents was itself authoritarian.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Pakistan, 1971–1988

What changed: The state moved from a parliamentary constitution to martial law and then a hybrid presidential order; religious law expanded; the Afghan war changed external alignments; refugee populations and arms networks reshaped the frontier.

What remained continuous: The army's institutional weight; the strength of the central executive; strain between center and provinces; a large public sector; and recourse to Islamic language in politics.

Who experienced change most directly: Minorities, women, Sindhis and Baloch, political prisoners, and the Afghan and border populations. Who experienced greater continuity: Army officers, senior bureaucrats, and many landowning families.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that dramatic political changes can coexist with stable underlying structures.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Pakistan between 1971 and 1988 through the interaction of civilian reconstruction, military intervention, religious policy, and Cold War geopolitics. Its governing question was why an attempted democratic reconstruction after the loss of East Pakistan gave way to prolonged military rule, and how Islamization and the Afghan War reshaped the country's institutions, society, and international position.

The defeat of December 1971 destroyed the army's prestige and the assumption that religion alone could unite two distant regions. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, taking power as president and civilian martial law administrator, reorganized the military leadership, negotiated the Simla Agreement of July 1972, and secured the return of prisoners and Pakistan's recognition of Bangladesh in 1974. The Hamoodur Rahman Commission examined the defeat, but its findings stayed unpublished until 2000.

The 1973 Constitution, adopted on 10 April and in force from 14 August, was the first broadly agreed constitutional settlement. It established a parliamentary system, a federal structure with a Senate, listed fundamental rights, and gave Islam a defined but largely advisory role. It did not, however, bind the army to civilian authority, and amendments between 1974 and 1977 strengthened executive powers of detention.

Bhutto's economic and social program included nationalization of major industries, insurance, banks, and, in 1976, small agro-processing units, together with land reform, labor policy, and educational nationalization. World Bank and later assessments generally find that the program weakened private investment and administrative efficiency without producing the broader redistribution promised, and that land ceilings were widely evaded. Growth in the 1970s was also shaped by war damage, floods, the oil shock, and poor harvests.

The federal settlement faltered in the provinces. The dismissal of the Balochistan government in February 1973 and the resignation of the NWFP ministry led to an insurgency and large-scale military operations, and the banning of the National Awami Party in 1975 closed a space for left-nationalist opposition. At the same time religious political parties challenged the PPP, and Bhutto responded with the Second Amendment of 1974 and, in 1977, further religious concessions. These show that Islamic politics predated Zia.

The elections of March 1977, followed by opposition allegations of fraud, the protests of the Pakistan National Alliance, and army involvement, led to the coup of 5 July 1977. Historians debate the balance among electoral malpractice, Bhutto's governing practices, opposition strategy, and military interests. Bhutto was convicted of conspiracy to murder in March 1978 and executed on 4 April 1979. In March 2024 the Supreme Court gave a retrospective opinion that the trial and appeal did not meet constitutional requirements of a fair trial, though it did not itself overturn the judgment.

Zia ruled through martial law, military courts, censorship, and bans on parties, faced the MRD movement in 1981 and 1983, and sought legitimacy through the 1984 referendum and the 1985 non-party elections. The Eighth Amendment gave the president power to dismiss the assembly, and martial law ended at the close of 1985. His Islamization included the Hudood Ordinances, the Zakat and Ushr Ordinance, the Federal Shariat Court, changes to evidence law, and expanded blasphemy provisions. These changes built on earlier precedents but on a much larger scale, and affected women, Ahmadis, Shia, Christians, and others differently, producing organized resistance from groups such as the Women's Action Forum.

The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979 transformed Pakistan's strategic position. Pakistan supported the Afghan resistance through the ISI, with American and Saudi funding, hosted millions of registered refugees, and received a major United States assistance package from 1981. The war brought arms, narcotics, religious networks, and the threat of retaliation, and scholars caution against treating refugees, mujahideen, religious schools, and later militants as a single category. The Geneva Accords of 1988 led to Soviet withdrawal by February 1989.

Zia's death in an aircraft crash on 17 August 1988, whose cause has not been established, led to constitutional succession, party-based elections on 16 November, and Benazir Bhutto's assumption of office on 2 December. The transition was real, but the president's powers, the army's autonomy, and the inherited institutional tensions remained. Continuities between the Bhutto and Zia eras, in central executive authority, federal difficulty, and use of religious language, were as significant as their differences.

Chapter Reflection

The years from 1971 to 1988 show how a state can reconstruct its institutions after a national catastrophe and still lose the stability it sought. The 1973 Constitution was a real achievement, produced by negotiation among parties that disagreed deeply. Its fate shows that a constitutional text depends on the willingness of the powerful to be bound by it, and that a government that undermines its own settlement gives others reason to discard it.

The Zia period shows a different lesson, about how a regime without electoral legitimacy can use religion, law, and foreign alliances to hold power. Many of its institutions, from the Federal Shariat Court to the presidential powers of the Eighth Amendment, outlived it, and later governments inherited both the laws and the social divisions that accompanied them. At the same time the resistance of women, minorities, lawyers, and political activists shows that the state's project was never accepted without challenge.

The Afghan war connected Pakistan to the Cold War and to the wider Muslim world, and brought resources and risks. Its consequences, from refugee settlement to the arms trade and the growth of religious networks, did not end with the Soviet withdrawal. Chapter 96 turns to Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the smaller states in the same period, Chapter 97 to nuclear politics and regional interdependence, and Chapter 101 returns to Pakistan after 1988. In the six-volume sequence this chapter belongs to the age of upheaval that followed independence's first generation, when states across South Asia discovered how difficult it was to make institutions endure.
