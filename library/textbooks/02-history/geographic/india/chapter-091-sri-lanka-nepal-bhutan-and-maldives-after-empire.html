Part XVI — Building the Postcolonial States, 1947–1971
91

Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives after Empire

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Between 1948 and 1968, four South Asian states, none of them India or Pakistan, redrew the terms of their own sovereignty. On 4 February 1948 Ceylon became a self-governing Dominion with a parliament elected on a nearly universal franchise. In February 1951 Nepal's king proclaimed the end of a century of hereditary prime-ministerial rule. In 1953 Bhutan's king convened a National Assembly, and in July 1965 the Maldives ended seventy-eight years as a British-protected sultanate. Each event was a landmark. None of them looked like the others.

Chapter 90 followed Pakistan from independence to military rule, and Chapters 86 and 87 examined the Partition and the successor-state settlement that created India and Pakistan. Those events dominate popular memory of South Asia's decolonization. But the region did not end at the Radcliffe Line. Alongside the two large successor states stood an island dominion that had been a Crown colony, a Himalayan kingdom that had never been colonized, a mountain monarchy whose foreign relations had been guided by British India, and an atoll sultanate under a loose British protectorate. Their histories after 1947 are the subject of this chapter.

The central question is how Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives established, preserved, or redefined their sovereignty after the end of British imperial predominance, and why their political institutions took such different forms. The question has to be asked separately for each state, because the word "decolonization" does not describe all four. Ceylon negotiated a transfer of power from Britain. Nepal was never a British colony; its 1951 transformation overthrew a domestic oligarchy. Bhutan did not become independent in this period; it renegotiated a pre-existing relationship with a new partner, India. The Maldives moved from protection to full independence and then, after an earlier failed attempt, to a republic.

The chapter develops three connected arguments. First, sovereignty took different forms across the region: dominion status, a monarchy-led constitutional transition, treaty-guided autonomy, and protectorate-to-republic independence. Second, political modernization did not necessarily produce democratic government. Ceylon had universal suffrage and competitive elections, yet restricted the citizenship of a large minority. Nepal held a landmark parliamentary election and then saw elected government dissolved by the crown. Bhutan expanded schooling and roads under a monarchy that remained the central political institution. The Maldives became a republic without becoming a competitive multiparty polity. Third, smaller states exercised real agency. They bargained with India, Britain, and, increasingly, the Cold War powers, and their own political actors shaped outcomes that larger neighbors did not control.

Evidence for these four histories is uneven, and students should read the chapter with that in mind. Ceylon left a rich record: Hansard debates, statutes, census returns, commission reports, newspapers, and a large scholarly literature. Nepal's record includes royal proclamations, constitutions, party documents, and the journals and memoirs of its political actors, but much archival material remains difficult to access. For Bhutan, the documentary base consists of treaties, official histories, and law codes, with few independent statistics for the 1950s. For the Maldives, historians depend on constitutional texts, British diplomatic records, and a modest body of local scholarship. The chapter's source windows distinguish carefully between authenticated texts and editorial paraphrase, and the Linked Source Register at the end identifies where students can consult the primary documents themselves.

This chapter is also a lesson in scale. A history of South Asia organized only around its largest states would miss the ways that citizenship was defined, monarchy reinvented, and sovereignty practiced in smaller polities. These four states also demonstrate that the large issues of the age, including majority rule, minority protection, development, the role of monarchy, and relations with a dominant neighbor, were not confined to the Indian and Pakistani cases. Later chapters return to each: Chapter 92 on the regional wars of 1962–1971, Chapter 96 on Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives between 1971 and 1991, and Chapters 103 and 104 on the twenty-first century.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Ceylon's constitutional path from the Donoughmore reforms through the Soulbury settlement to Dominion independence on 4 February 1948.
  2. Explain how the citizenship and franchise legislation of 1948–1949 affected Indian-origin plantation Tamils, and distinguish their position from that of Sri Lankan Tamils.
  3. Analyze the political, social, and religious sources of Sinhala nationalism and the significance of the Official Language Act of 1956.
  4. Evaluate why federalist Tamil politics, nonviolent protest, and negotiated agreements failed to produce a durable settlement between 1957 and 1968.
  5. Distinguish Nepal's 1951 revolution, which ended Rana oligarchic rule, from decolonization or the grant of independence.
  6. Assess the significance of the 1959 election and the B. P. Koirala government, and explain why King Mahendra's actions of 1960 and 1962 ended the parliamentary experiment.
  7. Interpret the India–Bhutan Treaty of 1949 in its original postcolonial context, and distinguish Bhutan's evolving sovereignty from independence in that period.
  8. Describe Bhutan's institutional reforms from 1953 to 1971 and explain why they should not be equated with later parliamentary democracy.
  9. Reconstruct the sequence from the protectorate of 1887 through the republic of 1953, the sultanate's restoration of 1954, independence in 1965, and the second republic of 1968 in the Maldives.
  10. Explain the United Suvadive Republic as a dispute over state consolidation, peripheral economic interests, and external influence.
  11. Compare how the four states defined sovereignty, citizenship, representation, and executive authority, distinguishing constitutional design from political practice.
  12. Assess the relationship of each state with India and the limits of treating smaller states as passive recipients of Indian policy.

  2. How could Ceylon sustain competitive parliamentary politics while restricting the citizenship of a large minority?
  3. What were the social and political forces behind the rise of Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism, and why did it find expression in language policy?
  4. Why did the efforts at compromise between Sinhala and Tamil political leaders fail between 1957 and 1968?
  5. What did the overthrow of the Rana regime change in Nepal, and what did it leave unresolved?

  8. Why did the Suvadive Republic arise, and what does its collapse reveal about the consolidation of the Maldivian state?

Historical Context

When the British Raj ended in 1947 (Chapter 87), Britain's presence in the region took many forms, and the legal relationships between London and the smaller states were not alike. Ceylon had been a Crown colony since the early nineteenth century, administered separately from British India, after earlier periods of Portuguese and Dutch coastal control (Chapters 49 and 61). Nepal had fought the Anglo-Nepal War of 1814–1816, signed the Treaty of Sugauli, and thereafter maintained a distinctive relationship in which Britain kept a Resident in Kathmandu, recruited Gurkha soldiers, and eventually, by the treaty of 1923, recognized Nepal's independence. Bhutan had signed treaties with British India in 1865 and 1910 that limited its external relations but left internal administration to its rulers. The Maldives, a sultanate of coral atolls, had accepted British protection in 1887 on terms that left internal governance to the sultan. Chapter 71 described how the Indian princely states fitted into the architecture of paramountcy. None of these four was a princely state, and each requires its own analysis.

A second context is the changing regional balance. British withdrawal left India as the dominant power in the subcontinent. India inherited British strategic concerns about the Himalayan frontier, the sea-lanes of the Indian Ocean, and the open borders and migration patterns that crossed colonial administrative lines. At the same time, the victory of the Chinese Communist Party in 1949 and the extension of Chinese authority over Tibet in 1950–1951 transformed the northern frontier and gave Nepal and Bhutan new geopolitical significance. The Cold War placed a further layer on regional politics: Britain retained air and naval facilities in Ceylon and the Maldives into the 1950s and 1960s, while the United States, the Soviet Union, and China sought influence through aid and diplomacy. Chapter 92 examines the wars and frontier disputes of this era in detail.

A third context concerns the legacies of colonial economy and society. Ceylon's plantation sector, built on tea, rubber, and coconut and staffed to a large extent by Tamil labor recruited from southern India in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (Chapter 75), shaped both the island's export economy and its politics of belonging. Nepal's land system and its Rana-era isolation left a narrow educated class and limited infrastructure. Bhutan's economy centered on subsistence agriculture, with serf-like obligations in some regions and very few roads. The Maldives lived from fishing and maritime trade, with dispersed communities and strong regional variation among atolls. These inherited conditions did not determine what followed, but they set the terms within which political actors made choices.

Students should also note the terminological cautions. The state called Ceylon in 1948 became the Republic of Sri Lanka only in 1972; this chapter uses Ceylon for the period before then. Nepal's 1951 transformation was a political revolution within a state that had long been formally independent. Bhutan's monarchy dates from 1907, long before any of the treaty changes discussed here. And "independence" in the Maldives means the end of protectorate status in 1965, which is a different event from the monarchy's abolition in 1968.

Division I

Ceylonese Independence, Sinhala Nationalism, and Tamil Political Grievances

§§91.1–91.4 · 1931–1971, principally 1947–1970
How Ceylon's relatively peaceful constitutional independence and durable parliamentary institutions coexisted with increasingly divisive policies on citizenship, language, religion, and national identity.
Section 91.1

Ceylonese Independence: Constitutional Reform, the Soulbury Settlement, and Dominion Government

Ceylon's independence was the end of a long and notably gradual constitutional process, not a rupture. The decisive early step was the Donoughmore Constitution of 1931, named for the commission that recommended it. It introduced universal adult franchise on the island: every adult aged twenty-one and over, women as well as men, could vote regardless of literacy or property. This placed Ceylon among the first countries in Asia to adopt a franchise of this breadth, years before most of British India. The constitution also created a State Council with executive committees, which gave elected Ceylonese members experience in administration, although the Governor retained reserved powers over defense, foreign affairs, and the civil service. Territorial constituencies replaced the communal electorates that earlier reforms had used, a change that minority leaders, especially among the Tamils, criticized as exposing them to permanent Sinhalese majorities.

Through the 1930s and 1940s, Ceylonese politicians pressed for fuller self-government. The island's wartime role as a British base in the Indian Ocean, including the headquarters of South East Asia Command at Kandy, increased its strategic importance, and Britain agreed to examine further reform. The Soulbury Commission, which visited in 1944–1945, recommended a parliamentary system on the Westminster model. The Ceylon Constitution Order in Council of 1946 gave it legal form, and the Ceylon Independence Act passed by the British Parliament in 1947 prepared the transfer of status. Ceylon's leaders also concluded defense and external-affairs agreements with Britain that allowed Britain to retain naval and air facilities at Trincomalee and Katunayake, a reminder that independence did not immediately end all British military presence.

The constitutional structure was parliamentary. The Crown was represented by a Governor-General, who acted on ministerial advice in most matters. Executive power rested with the Prime Minister and a cabinet responsible to the House of Representatives, the lower house of a bicameral Parliament. The House had 101 members, 95 elected from constituencies and six nominated by the Governor-General to represent otherwise unrepresented interests. A Senate of thirty members, half elected by the House and half appointed, acted as a revising chamber. A distinctive feature was section 29 of the constitution, which prohibited Parliament from passing laws that made members of any community or religion liable to disabilities not imposed on others or conferred privileges on one community alone. The provision was meant to reassure minorities, and, as §91.2 shows, its meaning would be contested.

The first general election under the new framework took place in August–September 1947. The United National Party (UNP), formed in 1946 by D. S. Senanayake from a coalition of Sinhalese and Muslim political groups and the Ceylon National Congress, won a plurality of the elected seats. It governed with the support of independents and nominated members. The main opposition consisted of the Marxist Lanka Sama Samaja Party and the Communist Party, together with the Ceylon Tamil Congress led by G. G. Ponnambalam, which had campaigned for balanced representation among the island's communities, and the Ceylon Indian Congress, which represented plantation workers. Senanayake became Prime Minister, and Ceylon became a self-governing Dominion within the Commonwealth on 4 February 1948. The occasion was marked by ceremony and by a considerable sense of national achievement. Independence had been negotiated, not won by mass confrontation, and Ceylon's leaders could point to it with reason as a peaceful model in a year that also saw Partition violence on the mainland (Chapter 86).

Students should be careful about the term "independence" here. In 1948, Ceylon's head of state remained the British Crown, represented by the Governor-General, and the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London was still the final court of appeal. The Soulbury constitution remained in force until the Republican Constitution of 1972 renamed the state the Republic of Sri Lanka, abolished the Governor-General, and, together with earlier legislation, ended the Senate and appeals to the Privy Council. The period examined in this chapter, therefore, is Ceylon's Dominion period, 1948–1972, not the history of the later republic.

The achievements of the early state were real. Parliamentary government functioned; elections were competitive; governments changed through the ballot in 1956, 1960, 1965, and 1970; and the military did not intervene in politics, unlike the Pakistani case examined in Chapter 90. The inheritance of universal suffrage and free education, the latter established in the 1940s through the reforms associated with C. W. W. Kannangara, produced a literate, politically engaged electorate. Yet independence also carried forward unresolved questions: the status of Indian-origin plantation workers, the relations between Sinhala-speaking and Tamil-speaking communities, and the role of the English-educated elite who had negotiated the transfer. These questions occupy the rest of this division.

Ceylon's Soulbury Constitutional Arrangements and Independence Legislation

Source type: Constitutional instrument and parliamentary legislation · Created: 1946–1947, by the British Crown in Council and the United Kingdom Parliament · Where to consult: Parliament of Sri Lanka's historical handbook, linked in the Source Register

Context: The 1946 Order in Council and the 1947 Ceylon Independence Act were the legal foundation of the Dominion. They were drafted in London, building on the Soulbury Commission's report and on negotiations with Ceylonese leaders, notably D. S. Senanayake.

Status of text: No verbatim quotation is reproduced in this window. The summary below is an editorial paraphrase of the instruments' general effect. Students should read section 29 and the provisions on the Governor-General directly from the linked texts.

Editorial paraphrase: The constitution established a bicameral Parliament and a cabinet responsible to the lower house. It restricted Parliament's power to enact laws discriminating against particular religious or communal groups, and it made the section protecting minorities difficult, though not impossible, to amend, since it required a two-thirds parliamentary majority.

Analysis: These instruments show how Ceylonese independence rested on legal continuity with the colonial order. Their drafters hoped constitutional text would protect minorities. Historians note, however, that a clause binding Parliament depends on the courts' willingness to enforce it and on political actors' willingness to respect it. Both turned out to be uncertain. The documents are also silent on the voices of those without political representation, such as most plantation workers.

Documentary reference: Original 1946/1947 Orders in Council, the 1947 Independence Act, and parliamentary historical overview

Section 91.2

Citizenship and Exclusion: The Plantation Tamil Question and the Post-Independence State

Within a year of independence, Ceylon's Parliament passed laws that redefined who belonged to the political community. To understand why, one must first distinguish two Tamil-speaking populations whose histories and positions were different. The Sri Lankan Tamils, sometimes called Ceylon Tamils, had lived on the island for many centuries, with their principal concentration in the Jaffna peninsula and the Northern and Eastern provinces, and they formed a significant presence in the colonial civil service and professions. The Indian-origin Tamils, often called Up-country or plantation Tamils, descended largely from laborers brought from southern India from the mid-nineteenth century to work on coffee, then tea and rubber estates in the central highlands (Chapter 75). In the 1946 census, Sinhalese made up about two-thirds of the population, Sri Lankan Tamils roughly one-ninth, and Indian Tamils somewhat more than one-ninth. Their histories were not interchangeable: the plantation population was largely poor, resident on estates under the supervision of planters' managers, and organized by trade unions and the Ceylon Indian Congress under leaders such as S. Thondaman.

The Ceylon Citizenship Act of 1948 defined citizenship in two ways: by descent and by registration. Citizenship by descent required, for persons born before the Act, that the father be born in Ceylon or, if the father was born abroad, that the paternal grandfather and great-grandfather had also been born in Ceylon. These tests were difficult for plantation families to satisfy, because their ancestors had arrived from India within recent generations, because birth records were scarce, and because many families lacked the documentary evidence the administrators demanded. The alternative, citizenship by registration, required a long period of residence and a formal application. A second statute, the Indian and Pakistani Residents (Citizenship) Act of 1949, created a registration process for persons of Indian or Pakistani origin, with residence requirements of seven years for unmarried and ten years for married applicants, together with proof of "permanent" settlement. A further 1949 amendment to the parliamentary elections law removed from the electoral registers anyone who was not a citizen.

The effect was to render a large population stateless in practice. Applications were few relative to the population, and the proportion approved was small, in part because the administrative process was slow and cumbersome and in part because applicants had to meet standards of proof that were hard to meet. By the early 1960s, hundreds of thousands of people of Indian origin remained without citizenship of either Ceylon or India. The same people lost the vote, so that the Ceylon Indian Congress, which had won several seats in the 1947 election, lost its parliamentary representation. The political consequences of this exclusion were significant for the balance of seats in the highlands, as it reduced the electoral weight of constituencies in which plantation workers lived.

The arguments for these measures were stated openly. Supporters, including leaders of the governing UNP, argued that a young state was entitled to decide who its citizens would be, that migrant labor under colonial rule had not created a claim to political membership, and that the plantation population's ties to India meant that India should accept responsibility for it. Some Sinhalese leaders also expressed fears that large Indian-origin communities, especially if enfranchised, could change the political balance in the highlands and strengthen Tamil political power. Critics, including the left parties and the Ceylon Indian Congress, replied that workers who had lived and labored on the island for generations had earned citizenship, and that the laws used technical definitions to produce a result that section 29 of the constitution should have prohibited. Legal challenges were brought on exactly that ground, but the courts, including the Privy Council in the early 1950s, upheld the legislation, reasoning that the laws defined citizenship and did not impose communal disabilities on citizens. This outcome showed the limits of a constitutional safeguard that depended on judicial interpretation.

The political reaction among the Sri Lankan Tamils was divided. G. G. Ponnambalam's Tamil Congress, which had earlier criticized the government, supported the citizenship legislation and later joined Senanayake's cabinet. S. J. V. Chelvanayakam, a Tamil Congress member who had opposed the legislation, broke with Ponnambalam, and in December 1949 helped to found the Ilankai Tamil Arasu Kachchi, usually called the Federal Party. This split is historically important. It reveals that Tamil political opinion was not monolithic, and that the plantation Tamils' disenfranchisement became one of the founding grievances of Tamil federalism, even though the plantation community and the northern Tamil community had distinct interests and social positions.

The consequences extended well beyond the franchise. Plantation workers continued to supply the labor for Ceylon's principal export industry, yet they lacked a voice in the legislature that taxed that industry's profits and spent them on welfare programs from which statelessness could exclude them. The question also entered diplomacy. India declined to accept the Ceylonese position that all Indian-origin residents were its responsibility, and negotiations dragged on through the 1950s, until the 1964 agreement discussed in §91.17. Meanwhile, the early state demonstrated a pattern that recurs in postcolonial South Asia: universal electoral institutions were compatible with restrictions on who counted as a member of the political community.

Historical Vocabulary
Citizenship and Statelessness

Citizenship is legal membership in a state, carrying rights such as voting and protection, and obligations such as taxation. Under colonial rule, people moved across imperial territories as "subjects" of the Crown, and the sharp legal distinction between nationalities arose only when new states defined their own memberships. A stateless person is one who is not recognized as a citizen by any state under its laws. In Ceylon, many plantation families were in effect stateless after 1948–1949 because Ceylon denied them citizenship and India had not conferred it.

This vocabulary matters because it shows that independence did not simply transfer power to "the people"; the new state had to decide who "the people" were, and that decision could exclude entire communities.

Trade unions such as those affiliated with the Ceylon Indian Congress and later the Ceylon Workers' Congress negotiated wages and conditions, and they provided a form of collective political voice even when parliamentary representation was lost. Historians must distinguish this lived experience from the statutory text. Law defined categories, but families met the state through estate managers, clerks, and union officials, and their experiences varied by estate, region, and generation.

Section 91.3

Sinhala Nationalism: Buddhism, Language, Electoral Mobilization, and the 1956 Political Transformation

The political transformation of 1956 had intellectual roots in the late colonial period. A Buddhist revival associated with figures such as Anagarika Dharmapala had, from the late nineteenth century, linked the defense of Buddhism and the Sinhala language to resentment at missionary education and colonial hierarchies. English-medium schools and the colonial civil service had produced a bilingual elite, often Christian or Westernized in culture, who dominated the professions and the government. By contrast, the Sinhala-educated, including village teachers, Buddhist monks, indigenous physicians, and small traders, found their language marginal in government and their routes to advancement narrow. These groups felt that independence had changed the faces at the top without changing the structure of privilege. A political coalition of such groups, sometimes described in Sinhala political rhetoric as the "five great forces" of the sangha, indigenous medicine, teachers, farmers, and workers, would carry S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike to power.

Bandaranaike was himself an Oxford-educated Anglican convert to Buddhism and a former UNP minister who had left the government in 1951 to found the Sri Lanka Freedom Party. His appeal rested on a combination of Sinhala language policy, a Buddhist cultural program, and a moderate economic nationalism. The year 1956 supplied a potent symbol: it was the 2,500th anniversary of the Buddha's parinirvana according to the Theravada reckoning, and Buddhist organizations used it to press for state support for Buddhism. A Buddhist commission of inquiry published a report that year, criticizing what it described as the neglect of Buddhism under colonial rule. The UNP government of Sir John Kotelawala, which had succeeded Dudley Senanayake in 1953, was seen by many as out of touch with this mood, and at the end of 1955 it adopted a policy of both Sinhala and Tamil as official languages before retreating to a Sinhala-only position, damaging its credibility with all sides.

In the general election of April 1956, Bandaranaike's Mahajana Eksath Peramuna coalition, centered on the SLFP, won a landslide of 51 of the 95 elected seats, while the UNP was reduced to a handful. The election was widely understood as a popular verdict for Sinhala as the official language, and it showed that electoral competition could be organized around language and religion. The victory was not the work of a single bloc, however. Left parties, particularly the Lanka Sama Samaja Party led by N. M. Perera and Colvin R. de Silva, campaigned for parity of status for Sinhala and Tamil, arguing that denying parity to Tamil would divide the country, and they warned that one language would produce two nations while two languages could sustain one. Many Sinhala-speaking voters were motivated by employment concerns as much as by religion or ethnic loyalty.

The Official Language Act, No. 33 of 1956, was passed by Parliament in June 1956 and received assent in July. It declared Sinhala the one official language of Ceylon for the purposes of the Act. Supporters presented it as correcting the dominance of English, which for most citizens was an obstacle to public employment, and as securing for the majority language the status held by official languages elsewhere. For Tamil-speaking citizens, however, the Act appeared to impose heavy disadvantages. Public servants who lacked proficiency in Sinhala faced pressure to learn it, and future recruits were required to demonstrate it, which narrowed opportunities in government employment, in which Tamils had been well represented. Courts, local administration, and the educational system were also affected. Because the Act did not by itself provide for the reasonable use of Tamil, the Tamil Language (Special Provisions) Act of 1958 later authorized limited Tamil usage in the Northern and Eastern provinces and for certain purposes, but the regulations to implement it were slow in coming and incompletely applied.

Bandaranaike's government did not remain stable. Pressure from Sinhala nationalists on one side and from Tamil leaders on the other left him balancing incompatible demands, a difficulty that §91.4 examines. On 25 September 1959, he was shot at his residence by a Buddhist monk, Talduwe Somarama, and he died the next day. The assassination showed that the politics of Buddhism and language contained rivalries within the Sinhala community itself, since the assassin's grievance involved disputes concerning the government's policies and the interests of some of its associates. W. Dahanayake served briefly as Prime Minister. After a hung result in March 1960 and a new election in July, the SLFP returned to power under Bandaranaike's widow, Sirimavo Bandaranaike, who was sworn in on 21 July 1960 as the world's first woman prime minister. Her election owed much to sympathy and to the SLFP's organizational base, and she was not a symbol alone: her government took over denominational schools in 1960–1961, a measure aimed at the influence of Christian missions on education but opposed by the Catholic Church, and it implemented Sinhala as the language of the courts from 1961.

Students should avoid treating these developments as the expression of a single will. Sinhala-speaking Buddhists held diverse opinions: leftist politicians argued for language parity, some monks and laypeople supported accommodation, and many voters were concerned mainly about jobs and prices. Majoritarian politics is the result of political organization, electoral competition, and the strategies of parties, which is why the label "Sinhala nationalism" must be applied to specific movements and policies, not to all members of a community. At the same time, the effects of the 1956 legislation on Tamil-speaking citizens were severe enough to transform Tamil politics, as the next section shows.

Section 91.4

Tamil Political Grievances: Federalism, Civil Resistance, Communal Violence, and Unfulfilled Compromises

The Federal Party's program, formulated from its founding in 1949, called for a federal arrangement in which the Tamil-speaking Northern and Eastern provinces would exercise self-government within a united Ceylon, for parity between Sinhala and Tamil as official languages, and for protection of the citizenship rights of Indian-origin Tamils. Its argument was that a unitary state with a Sinhalese majority would leave Tamils permanently outvoted on matters concerning language, land, and employment, and that federalism offered a way of preserving unity by distributing power. In the 1956 election, the Federal Party won most of the Tamil-majority seats in the north and east, displacing the Tamil Congress, whose cooperation with the UNP now seemed discredited. This shift showed how strongly the language Act had altered Tamil opinion.

The party's methods during this period were constitutional and nonviolent, drawing in part on the example of Gandhian satyagraha that had been prominent in the Indian nationalist movement (Chapter 79). On 5 June 1956, as Parliament debated the language bill, Federal Party members and supporters staged a sit-in protest at Galle Face Green in Colombo. They were attacked by a crowd, and police did not prevent the violence. In the weeks that followed, disturbances spread to eastern areas, especially around the Gal Oya colonization scheme, where Sinhalese and Tamil settlers lived in competition over irrigated land, and a number of people were killed. These events showed that language and state-directed settlement of land in the dry zone, a policy that moved Sinhalese farmers into areas historically populated by Tamil and Muslim communities, had become connected grievances.

In July 1957, Bandaranaike and Chelvanayakam reached the agreement known as the Bandaranaike–Chelvanayakam Pact. It was a compromise: the Federal Party gave up its demand for outright parity and for full federalism in exchange for recognition of Tamil as a minority language, regional councils, and a say in colonization schemes. But it met organized opposition. The UNP, led by J. R. Jayewardene, organized a march to Kandy in October 1957 in protest, and Buddhist monks demonstrated against the Pact at Bandaranaike's residence. Under this pressure, Bandaranaike abrogated the agreement in April 1958. The collapse suggests an important structural point: leaders in a competitive electoral system could be punished by their own side for concessions, and the opposition could gain by condemning them. A pact that depended on the goodwill of both major Sinhala parties was unlikely to survive when one of them saw advantage in opposing it.

Communal violence followed within weeks. A campaign by Sinhala activists to replace English letters on vehicle license plates with the Sinhala letter "Sri" provoked a counter-campaign in Tamil areas to deface it, and in late May 1958 violence broke out, partly triggered by incidents in the east, and spread to Colombo and other parts of the island. The anti-Tamil riots of 1958 killed a substantial number of people, with estimates varying considerably in the sources, and displaced thousands of Tamils, many of whom were evacuated by ship to the north. Property was destroyed, and the government declared a state of emergency, detained leaders of the Federal Party and some Sinhala nationalists, and temporarily banned some organizations. The violence marked the first major intercommunal rupture in independent Ceylon, and historians generally regard it as a turning point in the erosion of trust between communities. Students should note that violence was organized and incited by identifiable actors and that many ordinary Sinhalese sheltered Tamil neighbors.

When the Sirimavo Bandaranaike government extended the use of Sinhala in the courts and administration in 1961, the Federal Party launched its largest campaign of civil disobedience. Beginning in February 1961, its supporters obstructed government offices in the Northern and Eastern provinces, including the Jaffna kachcheri (the district administrative office), and organized alternative postal arrangements. The government responded by declaring an emergency in April, deploying security forces, detaining party leaders, and banning the party's activities for a period. The satyagraha showed both the strength of Tamil organization in the north and the limits of nonviolent pressure against a government that had a parliamentary majority and popular support among Sinhalese voters.

A further attempt at accommodation followed the election of March 1965, when Dudley Senanayake formed a UNP-led coalition with the support of the Federal Party. The Senanayake–Chelvanayakam agreement of 1965 promised that Tamil would be used for administrative and judicial purposes in the Northern and Eastern provinces, that district councils would be established, and that colonization policy would take account of the interests of Tamils in their regions. A Tamil Language (Special Provisions) regulation followed in 1966, but the district councils bill was withdrawn in 1968 after protests, including from sections of the Sinhala opposition and Buddhist clergy, and the Federal Party left the government. The pattern repeated: negotiated agreements were weakly implemented and then abandoned under political pressure.

Why did the compromises fail? Explanations offered by historians emphasize several factors: electoral competition between the two main Sinhala parties, each of which risked being outflanked on ethnic issues; the weakness of institutional guarantees, since an agreement between leaders had no legal force unless enacted; the influence of Buddhist clergy and nationalist organizations as political actors; and the growing skepticism among Tamil voters that the central parties would deliver. Tamil politics also had its own internal rivalries. The politics of this period must be distinguished from later developments. The Tamil movement of the 1950s and 1960s was parliamentary and nonviolent in its main strategy, and its demand was for federal autonomy within Ceylon. The armed separatist organizations that emerged in the 1970s and later belong to the period studied in Chapter 96, and students should not read their goals and methods back into this earlier phase.

Historical Debate 1

Can Electoral Democracy Remain Inclusive When Political Power Is Organized around Majority Identity?

The question: Ceylon sustained free elections and peaceful transfers of power while adopting citizenship laws and a language policy that disadvantaged minorities. Did the electoral system itself encourage this outcome, or were the failures of accommodation the result of particular choices by political actors?

Interpretation A — Structural incentives of majority politics: In a system where the two main parties competed for a Sinhala-speaking majority, concessions to Tamil demands were electorally costly and were therefore withdrawn. On this view, majoritarian competition made inclusive reform unlikely, and the 1956 victory showed that appeals to language and religion could win elections. Minority safeguards written into the constitution were too weak to prevent this dynamic.
Interpretation B — Contingent choices and missed alternatives: Other historians stress that outcomes were not fixed. The left parties offered a parity program; Bandaranaike and later Dudley Senanayake signed agreements; and the language question might have been managed differently by different leaders, for example through a more gradual approach or with legal guarantees for Tamil usage. On this view, particular decisions, such as the 1956 legislation and the repeated abandonment of pacts, mattered as much as structure.

Evidence: Both views draw on parliamentary debates, election results, party manifestos, the texts of the 1957 and 1965 agreements, and accounts of the 1958 violence. The two readings are not mutually exclusive: structural incentives shaped the options, while leaders' choices determined which options were taken.

Why it matters: The debate bears on a general question: whether majority rule requires institutional constraints to protect equal citizenship, and what kinds of constraints can survive political pressure.

Section 91.5

Nepal before 1951: The Shah Monarchy, Rana Oligarchy, and the Limits of Political Sovereignty

Nepal's political order before 1951 combined two layers of authority that were separated in law and in practice. The Shah dynasty, descended from the rulers who had unified the Gorkha state and the surrounding principalities in the late eighteenth century, held the throne. After 1846, however, effective power belonged to a hereditary line of prime ministers. In that year, following a court massacre at the Kot, Jung Bahadur Kunwar seized control, took the title Rana, and established a regime in which the office of prime minister, and with it control of the army, the administration, and the treasury, passed within the Rana family by a fixed order of succession. The Shah kings were retained as sacred, ceremonial figures, effectively secluded in the palace. The arrangement lasted just over a century and is often described as an oligarchy because a single family monopolized the senior offices.

It is crucial to understand Nepal's international position. Nepal was never incorporated into British India. After the Anglo-Nepal War of 1814–1816 and the Treaty of Sugauli, it lost territory, accepted a British Resident at Kathmandu, and thereafter kept a careful relationship with the British Empire. Jung Bahadur and his successors offered military support, most notably during the Indian Rebellion of 1857 (Chapter 70), and recruited Gurkha soldiers for the British Indian Army, an arrangement that gave the Ranas both revenue and political backing. Britain in turn recognized Nepal's independence in a treaty of 1923 and refrained from interfering in its domestic politics. The Rana regime also restricted foreign contact, limited the entry of outsiders, and kept education and public political life under tight control. Nepal was thus a sovereign state with a restricted society, not a colony, and the restrictions on political life came from within.

Opposition to Rana rule emerged slowly. Reformist currents began among Nepalis educated or resident in India, especially in Banaras and Calcutta, who were inspired by the Indian national movement. In the 1930s, organizations such as the Praja Parishad pressed for reforms. The regime reacted with severity: four members of the Praja Parishad were executed in 1941, and they became martyrs in later nationalist memory. In 1947, the Nepali National Congress was formed in India under leaders including B. P. Koirala, and other groups, including the Nepal Democratic Congress, formed in 1948. In 1950 these parties merged into the Nepali Congress. Left organizations also developed, including the Communist Party of Nepal, founded in 1949. Rana leaders attempted limited reforms to forestall change; Padma Shamsher promulgated a constitutional document in 1948 that provided for a limited advisory legislature but kept Rana control, and its failure to satisfy critics showed how narrow the regime's concessions were.

Changes in the regional environment created the moment for revolution. The departure of the British from India in 1947 removed the imperial patron on which the Ranas had relied. The new Indian government, under Nehru, was sympathetic to Nepali democrats but also concerned with security on its northern frontier, and the establishment of Chinese authority in Tibet in 1950 sharpened that concern. The India–Nepal Treaty of Peace and Friendship, signed on 31 July 1950 (§91.17), formalized close ties just before the final crisis. For the Rana regime, then, external and internal supports were weakening simultaneously.

The central point of this section is analytical. Nepal's transformation in 1951 was primarily the overthrow of a domestic oligarchy in a state that was already formally sovereign, in a changed regional context. It was not the end of colonial rule. This distinction matters for comparison: Ceylon's questions concerned the constitutional inheritance of colonial rule, while Nepal's concerned how to replace a hereditary autocracy and define the relationship between a restored monarchy and popular representation.

Section 91.6

The Revolution of 1950–1951: King Tribhuvan, Nepali Congress, and the End of Rana Rule

The crisis began in the palace. King Tribhuvan, who had long been restricted by the Ranas, had come to sympathize with the reformers. In early November 1950, he and members of his family took refuge in the Indian embassy in Kathmandu, and a few days later they were flown to Delhi. The Rana prime minister, Mohan Shamsher, responded by declaring the king deposed and installing Tribhuvan's infant grandson, Gyanendra, on the throne, but India refused to recognize the substitution, and most governments followed its lead. The king's flight gave the movement a legitimizing figurehead and left the Rana regime without recognized royal authority.

At the same time, Nepali Congress organizers launched an armed uprising. Volunteers, known as the Mukti Sena (liberation army), operated from bases in India and attacked government posts in the Terai and in several hill and eastern districts, capturing some towns for a time. The revolt was not large by the standards of full-scale warfare, and the Rana army was not defeated militarily, but it showed that the regime lacked broad support and could not be restored simply by force. Some leaders, including Matrika Prasad Koirala, B. P. Koirala's elder half-brother, sought a political settlement, while others pressed for continued struggle. The movement's success depended on coordination among the king, the Congress, and sympathetic Indian officials.

India's role has been described in different ways, and students should treat it carefully. The Indian government hosted the king, mediated among the parties, and provided a venue for negotiations in Delhi. It used influence to bring about a compromise that it believed would preserve stability on its northern border: neither a complete Rana restoration nor a revolution it judged too radical. Yet Nepali actors, including the king, the Congress leaders, and even reform-minded Ranas, negotiated for their own interests and did not simply obey Indian directions. The final settlement, known as the Delhi compromise, reflects bargaining among several parties, with India as an influential mediator.

Under the compromise, the king would return, the Rana prime-ministerial regime would end, and a government would be formed with both Rana and Nepali Congress ministers until a constituent assembly could be elected to write a constitution. Tribhuvan returned to Kathmandu in mid-February 1951, and on 18 February he issued a proclamation announcing the end of the Rana system and promising a democratic constitution. Mohan Shamsher continued as prime minister of an interim coalition cabinet, with an equal share of Rana and Congress ministers, among them B. P. Koirala, who held the home portfolio. An Interim Government of Nepal Act, issued later in 1951, supplied a provisional constitutional framework, giving the king substantial authority as head of state while attributing real executive responsibility to the cabinet.

The settlement contained contradictions. It ended the hereditary prime ministership, a major achievement, but it left two questions open. The first was the actual power of the monarchy: a king restored by a revolution fought partly in his name could claim a legitimacy that the parties lacked, yet the Congress and other parties envisaged a constitutional monarch. The second was the pace and character of reform, since the interim coalition combined people with opposed goals. The coalition did not last: Mohan Shamsher resigned in November 1951, and a Congress government under Matrika Prasad Koirala took office. The 1950s were then marked by short-lived governments, factional splits within the Congress, the emergence of the Communist Party and other groups, and an unfulfilled promise: the constituent assembly election, repeatedly postponed, was never held in the form originally pledged.

For historians, the revolution of 1950–1951 is best seen as both a genuine rupture and an incomplete transition. The Rana regime fell, a political public emerged, and Nepal opened to the world; it joined the United Nations in 1955 and established diplomatic relations with several countries. But the monarchy remained the central institution, and its role in the new order was unsettled.

Section 91.7

Constitutional Experiment, the 1959 Election, and B. P. Koirala's Parliamentary Government

The eight years after 1951 saw repeated experiments in government. Cabinets rose and fell, sometimes led by Congress and sometimes by independents or by the king's own appointees. In 1952, a Rana-linked attempt at a coup, the Raksha Dal rebellion, was suppressed, and the Communist Party was banned. From 1951 to 1955, King Tribhuvan, whose health declined, spent much time abroad for treatment, and the political parties struggled to organize elections in a country with few roads, little literacy, and no tradition of electoral administration. On 13 March 1955, Tribhuvan died in Switzerland, and his son Mahendra succeeded him. Mahendra, unlike his father, took a direct interest in governing and soon showed that he regarded the crown as the source of political direction. For some time, he ruled with appointed prime ministers, among them Tanka Prasad Acharya, K. I. Singh, and Subarna Shamsher, and he announced that elections would be held under a constitution that he would himself grant.

That constitution was promulgated in February 1959. It provided for a bicameral parliament, with an elected House of Representatives and a partly elected, partly nominated upper house, and for a cabinet responsible to the lower house. But it also reserved significant authority to the king, including the power to dismiss governments in some circumstances, to declare an emergency, and to command the army. The constitution was thus a granted document, bestowed from above, rather than one drafted by a constituent assembly as promised in 1951, and many Nepalis recognized the difference. Even so, it established the first framework for nationwide competitive elections.

Those elections, held in stages from February to April 1959, were the first parliamentary elections in the country's history. The Nepali Congress, led by B. P. Koirala, won a decisive majority in the lower house, around 74 of 109 seats, against competition from the Gorkha Parishad, the Communist Party, the Nepali National Congress, and other groups. The result demonstrated that a mass electorate, many of them voting for the first time, could participate in a competitive election and could deliver a clear mandate. B. P. Koirala was sworn in as prime minister in May 1959. His government proposed reforms in several areas: land reform, including measures against birta (tax-free land grants to elites), changes in the administration of religious endowments, expansion of education, and efforts to improve public administration and tax collection. It also aimed to reduce corruption and to build foreign relationships, including with both India and China.

The result also reveals a distinction between electoral victory and institutional security. Nepali Congress could assemble a national parliamentary majority even though political communication remained uneven and many voters lived far from roads or administrative centers. Campaigning therefore relied on local intermediaries, personal networks, and the reputation of the anti-Rana movement, as well as on proposals for reform. The large majority did not settle how ministers should control the civil service, how landholding interests would respond to taxation, or whether officials would answer to elected representatives rather than the palace. Those unresolved relationships were not technical details: they determined whether parliamentary decisions could be carried into effect. The elected government represented a genuine new source of public legitimacy, but it entered institutions whose chains of command still reflected monarchical authority.

The government's room for maneuver was limited. The king retained constitutional authority over the army and a reserve of emergency powers, and he held, alongside the cabinet, an influential role in foreign policy and appointments. The reforms threatened landlords and the nobility, who had political influence at the palace. Opposition parties and conservative interest groups criticized the Congress, and the government faced unrest and economic difficulties. Whether the government's policies were radical or moderate, they fell on a country in which administrative capacity was thin, so that implementation lagged behind legislation.

The achievement of 1959 should be assessed on its own terms. Though short-lived, the election showed that Nepal's population was capable of participating in modern electoral politics and that a party with a reform program could win broad support. The memory of 1959 later became a point of reference for democratic movements, and later constitutional debates (Chapters 96 and 104) invoked it as a precedent. At the same time, its brevity is a reminder that elections alone do not determine who holds final authority. The constitution had given the king the means to end the experiment, and in December 1960 he used them.

Section 91.8

King Mahendra and the Panchayat System: The Royal Coup and Partyless Government

On 15 December 1960, King Mahendra dismissed the Koirala government, dissolved Parliament, and had B. P. Koirala and many other political leaders arrested. Koirala was held in detention until 1968, and many Congress members went into exile in India. The king suspended the constitution, banned political parties, and assumed direct rule. The action was called a royal "coup" by critics and was defended by the monarchy as an exercise of constitutional prerogative, and the contested terminology itself reflects the dispute about legitimacy.

The official justifications emphasized several points. Mahendra's statements, as summarized by historians, argued that the parliamentary system had produced instability, corruption, and the threat of disorder; that party politics divided a nation which needed unity in order to develop; and that Nepal required institutions better suited to its conditions than a transplanted Western model. These were political arguments, and students should evaluate them as such. The government had held office for only about a year and a half, and the instability of the 1950s had largely resulted from the delay in holding elections and from the king's own role. Critics also noted that the king had a direct interest in curbing a popularly elected government that threatened aristocratic privileges and his own authority. The king's action also reflected concerns about reform measures and about the Congress's relations with India, as well as tensions on the northern border following the Chinese consolidation in Tibet.

After two years of direct rule, Mahendra proclaimed a new constitution on 16 December 1962. It established the Panchayat system, a partyless political order in which the "pyramid" of councils, from village or town assemblies up through district and zonal levels to a national legislature, the Rastriya Panchayat, was elected indirectly: residents elected representatives at both village and town levels, while district, zonal, and national arrangements were chiefly indirect and included forms of royal appointment. The country was divided into 14 zones and 75 districts. Political parties were prohibited, so that candidates could not contest elections on party platforms. The constitution declared Nepal a Hindu monarchical state, placed sovereign executive authority in the king, and gave him power over the government, the army, the choice of the prime minister, and the right to amend or suspend the constitution in an emergency. The Panchayat institutions had real functions in local administration and development, but they operated under royal oversight.

The difference between local elections and parliamentary accountability was crucial. Villagers and townspeople could participate directly in choosing local Panchayat representatives, while district and higher bodies were selected through more indirect arrangements, alongside appointed elements and royal influence. The availability of a local ballot did not permit voters to replace the national executive through an organized opposition party. A rural council might convey requests about a road or school without acquiring authority to challenge the king's policy or the political prohibition on parties. It is therefore misleading to count the existence of elected Panchayats as proof that the system preserved the parliamentary democracy of 1959. Historians must ask who selected representatives at each tier, which decisions those councils could make, and where accountability ultimately ended.

The system was accompanied by a program of state-building. A new national legal code, the Muluki Ain of 1963, replaced the Rana-era code of 1854 and formally ended legal distinctions among castes, although social discrimination persisted. The Land Reform Act of 1964 set ceilings on landholding and provided for tenant protections, though its implementation was uneven and its effects were limited by evasion and by weak administration. Roads and schools expanded with foreign aid; the construction of highways linking Kathmandu with the Indian border and later with Tibet (Chapter 92 examines the frontier context) reduced the isolation of the valley. In 1967, the government launched a "Back to the Village" campaign meant to mobilize rural participation in development under Panchayat supervision.

Cultural policy deserves special attention. The Panchayat state promoted a national identity centered on the monarchy, the Hindu religion, the Nepali language, and a shared national dress, and it sought to integrate the country's diverse linguistic and ethnic communities, including the Newars, Tharus, Tamangs, Limbus, Madhesis of the Terai, and many others, under this common model. For many, this brought opportunities for advancement through education and administration; for others, it meant the marginalization of their languages and customs, a grievance that would reappear in Nepali politics in later decades (Chapter 104). Historians continue to debate how far the Panchayat era expanded national integration and how far it entrenched the dominance of hill, high-caste, Hindu, Nepali-speaking elites.

The central tension of the period, then, was between an expanding state and a narrow political space. The regime built administrative institutions, extended infrastructure, and began land and legal reform, but it did so without competitive elections or an independent opposition, and it relied on royal authority for legitimacy. Political prisoners, press restrictions, and exile were the costs for those who dissented. The Panchayat order would last until 1990 (Chapter 96 continues the account), but already in 1971 its limits and its capacity to endure were clear.

Historical Vocabulary
Panchayat

The word panchayat, historically a council of five in village India, was adopted by the Nepali monarchy in 1962 for a pyramid of councils from village to nation. In Nepal's usage, "Panchayat system" meant a partyless polity in which political parties were prohibited and the king held final authority. It is not equivalent to the panchayati raj institutions of village self-government introduced in India (Chapter 89), although the shared word can mislead.

It matters because it illustrates how a term associated with grassroots participation could be used to organize a centralized, royal-led political order.

Section 91.9

Bhutan's Monarchy and International Position: Sovereignty, British Relations, and the Treaty with India

Bhutan's modern monarchy was established in 1907, when an assembly of monastic and lay leaders chose Ugyen Wangchuck, the governor (penlop) of Trongsa, as the first hereditary king, Druk Gyalpo. The choice ended a period in which authority had been divided among regional governors, a dual system of civil and religious heads, and competing factions. It also followed a long history of Buddhist state-building in the Himalayan valleys associated with the Drukpa Kagyu school and, in the seventeenth century, with the lama Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal. The new monarchy combined regional administration with the support of the monastic establishment, which remained a powerful institution in landholding, education, and ritual life. For this reason, the Bhutanese state cannot be understood as secular administration alone; the relation between throne and monastic body was part of its constitution.

Bhutan's relationship with the British Indian government developed in stages. After a conflict in 1864–1865 (the Duar War), the Treaty of Sinchula ceded the Duars, the southern foothill territories, to the British in return for an annual allowance. In 1910, the Treaty of Punakha, signed under the first king, increased the subsidy and provided that the British would not interfere in Bhutan's internal administration, while Bhutan agreed to be guided by British advice in its external relations. The treaty therefore limited Bhutan's foreign policy while preserving internal autonomy, and the distinction mattered. Bhutan was never incorporated into British India or governed by it: no British administration operated in its valleys, no colonial land system applied, and its king remained the ruler. It was, in the language used in Chapter 71, outside the Indian princely-state structure, even though the British treated it for some purposes as comparable to a protected polity.

The end of British rule posed the question of how Bhutan's treaty relationship would be continued. Negotiations with the independent government of India led to the India–Bhutan Treaty, signed on 8 August 1949 at Darjeeling. Under its terms, India agreed not to interfere in Bhutan's internal administration, and Bhutan agreed to be guided by the advice of the Government of India in regard to its external relations. India also returned a small tract of territory at Dewangiri that had been annexed in the nineteenth century, increased the annual payment to Bhutan, and the treaty provided for trade and transit arrangements, including provisions on the import of arms and ammunition through India. Each side thus gained something. India secured a predictable security arrangement along a frontier that mattered more after 1950 as Tibet came under Chinese control, while Bhutan received recognition of internal autonomy and a stable relationship.

Students must read this treaty in its own context. In 1949, the phrase about being "guided" by India's advice was understood within the framework of the 1910 treaty, and what that guidance meant in practice was shaped by the political relationships of the following decades. It was later replaced by the India–Bhutan Friendship Treaty of 2007, which revised the language on external relations, and the later wording should not be read back into 1949. Equally, the treaty should not be treated as a declaration of independence or as a surrender of it. Bhutan did not become independent in 1947, 1949, or 2008; its monarchy and sovereignty antedated these dates, and what changed was the form of its external relationship, from a British-Indian one to an Indian one. This continuity, with adjustments, is central to the Bhutanese case.

The treaty also reflected Bhutanese agency. The monarchy chose to maintain a close relationship with India and to avoid dependence on Tibet or on China, in part because of the geography of trade and communication, and in part because of the difficulties Tibetan authorities had created in earlier decades. The Bhutanese leadership understood that treaty guidance could be a protection as well as a limit, and that its management would depend on diplomacy. This view shaped the choices examined in the sections that follow.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorate, Treaty Guidance, and Sovereignty

A protectorate is a state that keeps its own government but has ceded control over some functions, usually defense and foreign relations, to a stronger power. The Maldives under the 1887 arrangement is an example. Treaty guidance describes a relationship in which a state agrees to consult or follow the advice of another in particular areas, as Bhutan did under the 1910 and 1949 treaties. Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory and, externally, recognition by other states. These categories overlap and have varied in practice: a state could be sovereign in domestic matters while limited in its foreign affairs.

The terms matter in this chapter because the four states were not related to Britain or India in the same way, and the word "independence" has different meanings across them.

Section 91.10

Jigme Dorji Wangchuck: Administrative Reform, the National Assembly, and Changes in Social Relations

Jigme Dorji Wangchuck became the third king in 1952, after the death of his father, Jigme Wangchuck, and he was crowned later that year. Educated in part in India and England, he came to the throne at a time when the end of British rule in India, the change in Tibet, and the beginning of development aid presented Bhutan with an altered environment. His approach, as documented in official histories and in later scholarship, was to prepare institutions for change gradually, preserving the role of the monarchy and the monastic establishment while creating new bodies and rules.

The most notable institutional innovation was the National Assembly, or Tshogdu, established in 1953. Its first sitting had thirty-six members according to Bhutan’s parliamentary record: fifteen representatives of the people, sixteen nominated officials, and five members of the monastic body. Popular representation was mediated through local selection arrangements rather than competitive national party elections. The Assembly held early sessions at Paro and Thimphu; members discussed matters submitted to it, among them administrative questions, laws, and proposals for reform. The Assembly was a consultative and deliberative body, and its relationship with royal authority is best described as one in which the king consulted it and, over time, devolved some responsibility to it, rather than one in which it controlled the executive. The Assembly was nevertheless significant because it gave villages an institutional channel to the throne, and because it became the nucleus for later reform, including changes in the 1960s examined in §91.12. It should not be described as an elected parliament in the modern sense or confused with the Parliament established under the 2008 constitution, which belongs to a later period (Chapter 104).

For the founding session, Bhutan's own parliamentary institutional history gives a specific membership of thirty-six: fifteen people's representatives, sixteen nominated officials, and five representatives of the monastic body. Some later retrospectives describe a much larger Assembly, sometimes around 130 members, without clearly identifying whether they mean the initial session or an expanded institution. These are not interchangeable figures. The smaller founding composition underscores the experimental nature of the 1953 body; its importance lay less in the number of seats than in establishing a recognizable forum through which the monarchy could hear regional concerns, introduce legislation, and create an official record of deliberation. The subsequent enlargement and increased authority of the Assembly belong to the history of institutional change, not to a single founding event.

Social reform focused on landholding and labor. Rural society in many areas had involved obligations, including labor services and tied relationships between landholders and dependents, that historians describe as forms of serfdom or bonded labor, although practices varied by region, estate, and the type of obligation. In the mid-1950s, the king and Assembly adopted measures to end these relations, to allocate land to cultivators, to rationalize taxes, and to reduce unpaid labor obligations. A Land Act and related reforms, followed by law codes in the late 1950s, provided the legal framework. Historians stress, however, that announced reform and its implementation are different things. Without surveys, a large bureaucracy, or reliable records, the application of such measures was gradual, and the sources, which consist mostly of official statements and later accounts, offer limited evidence about how individual households experienced the change. Some landholders and monastic estates lost privileges; many cultivators gained clearer rights; and the transition took years.

A further reform concerned nationality. The Nationality Law of 1958 defined who was a Bhutanese citizen. As reported in the English text of the law, citizenship could be acquired by descent from a Bhutanese father, and in other cases through residence over a prescribed period and ownership of land, with provision for registration. The law's importance lies in its institutionalization of citizenship: it made legal nationality a matter of statute at a time when the administrative state was expanding. Its application, particularly to settlers in the southern foothills of Nepali origin, would become a controversial subject in later decades. Chapter 96 and later chapters treat those developments, and students should not read the later controversies into the original text without examining the evidence.

Taken together, the reforms of the 1950s were substantial in scope, and they strengthened both the state and the rights of cultivators. But they were reforms from above, designed and approved by the monarchy and its advisers, and they did not create competitive parties or mass electoral politics. To call them the beginnings of "democracy" would be to impose a later category; they are better understood as the early stages of institutional modernization within a monarchical framework.

Section 91.11

Bhutan's First Five-Year Plan: Roads, Education, Health, and the Expansion of State Capacity

Until the late 1950s, Bhutan had almost no motorable roads. Travel between valleys took days on foot or by pony, along trails crossing high passes, and the ability of central government to reach distant districts was correspondingly limited. The year 1959 changed Bhutan's strategic situation. Tibet's uprising and its suppression led to the flight of the Dalai Lama to India and the closing of the Bhutan–Tibet border, ending a historic pattern of trade and travel across the northern frontier. Bhutan's economic and political connections thus turned decisively to the south. In September 1958 Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru had visited Bhutan, and later Indian statements affirmed India's concern for Bhutan's territorial integrity; Chapter 92 discusses the Himalayan frontier more fully.

The First Five-Year Plan, launched in 1961 and running to 1966, was the framework for development. It was prepared with Indian technical and financial assistance, and its funding came almost entirely from India. Its priority was infrastructure, especially road building: a road connecting the border town of Phuntsholing to Thimphu was a central project, with Indian engineers and the Border Roads Organisation helping to build it, and it was followed by other links. Health and education also received attention, with the establishment of the first modern schools and rudimentary medical facilities, and with efforts to improve agriculture and animal husbandry.

The significance of roads was administrative as well as economic. A road reduced travel times for officials, traders, and teachers, enabled the movement of goods, and made it easier for the state to collect revenue, enforce regulations, and provide services. It also changed relations between the center and the districts, because a government that could be reached in days instead of weeks became a more continuous presence. At the same time, road building required labor, and mobilizing it, as in many mountain states, raised questions of obligation and compensation, which historians have only begun to examine in detail. Construction in steep, landslide-prone terrain was costly and dangerous, and it depended on Indian engineers and on workers from across the Himalayan region.

Education underwent a similar transition. Monastic schools had long taught reading, religious texts, and ritual, and they remained central. The first modern schools, which taught in English and in Hindi or Dzongkha, with curricula influenced by Indian models, were limited in number, and many early students continued their studies in India. Schooling was thus pluralist: monastic and secular institutions coexisted, and the long-term question was how a modern education system would relate to Bhutan's religious and linguistic traditions. In health, early facilities were small, and communicable diseases and limited access to care remained major problems.

The limits of the evidence deserve emphasis. In the 1960s Bhutan had no census or statistical bureau capable of measuring literacy, mortality, or income with the precision available for larger states. Assertions about the plans' results rely on official reports, Indian aid records, and later retrospective accounts, which may overstate or understate impact. Students should therefore treat quantitative claims cautiously and rely on qualitative evidence: the existence of roads, the founding of schools, and the expansion of administrative institutions are better documented than their effects on household welfare.

Section 91.12

Monarchical Reform and Political Limits: The Royal Advisory Council, State Institutions, and Bhutan's Regional Position

The institutional framework continued to expand during the 1960s. In 1965, the king established the Royal Advisory Council (Lodoi Tshogde), a small body representing different parts of the country and the monastic community, which advised the king and monitored the implementation of policy. In 1968, a cabinet or Council of Ministers, the Lhengye Zhungtshog, was set up to provide more specialized administration of government departments, replacing earlier arrangements in which a small number of senior officials had exercised broad authority. These changes reflected the growth of government: with more roads, schools, and health posts, an administration organized by function became necessary.

The reforms in 1967–1968 also affected the National Assembly. According to the Parliament of Bhutan's institutional history, the Assembly was given a stronger legislative role in this period, and its members were encouraged to discuss and decide matters with greater independence. Reports on further steps in 1969, such as the king's offer to waive certain powers, indicate his willingness to share authority, but the details are best read from the primary institutional record, because later accounts differ on the exact arrangements. What can be said confidently is that the Assembly, the Advisory Council, and the Council of Ministers formed an increasingly differentiated structure around the monarchy, and that the king remained the central political authority.

The change was more specific than a general promise to consult. In the autumn of 1968, the king relinquished the requirement that Assembly legislation receive his assent, a major revision of the institution's formal place in government. An additional measure adopted in 1969 at the king's initiative provided a mechanism for a vote of no confidence, although the procedure did not produce a conventional cabinet responsible to a competitive legislature and was later altered. Later historical summaries sometimes telescope these measures into an undifferentiated reform program. Keeping their sequence clear permits a more accurate assessment: a ruler deliberately ceded a defined part of his formal prerogative while the political system as a whole continued to operate without party competition.

Political tension was not absent. In April 1964, Jigme Palden Dorji, the king's chief minister and a member of a powerful family closely linked to the throne, was assassinated at Phuntsholing during a period of factional struggle, which also involved conflicts over the pace of modernization, the role of the army, and personal rivalries within the elite. The crisis was handled by the king, with India's attention to the stability of its neighbor, and it was followed by changes in office-holding. This episode is a reminder that gradual reform was not conflict-free, and that the monarchy's management of elite factions was a continuing requirement.

Bhutan's geopolitical position also shaped its choices. With Tibet under Chinese control, Bhutan was a buffer state between India and China, and its security depended on both its own caution and India's concern. After 1960, Bhutan closed its frontier with Tibet and ended trade, and it maintained no diplomatic relations with China. It steadily widened its international contacts: it joined the Colombo Plan in 1962, the Universal Postal Union in 1969, and the United Nations in 1971, a step that confirmed its status as a sovereign state in the international system. Whether India supported or guided these steps, and how, is a subject on which historians differ. The record suggests that the Bhutanese monarchy used UN membership to demonstrate its statehood, while India saw the step as consistent with the 1949 treaty.

The section's conclusion concerns the advantages and limitations of reform from above. The gradual strategy allowed Bhutan to build institutions without immediate social upheaval or the factional violence seen elsewhere, and it combined modernization with continuity of monarchy, religion, and culture. But it also postponed questions about representation: the Assembly was not an independent legislature, parties were absent, and decisions about the scope of change rested with the king. Bhutan in 1971, then, is best described as a monarchy undertaking institutional modernization, a different thing from either Nepal's partyless Panchayat state or Ceylon's parliamentary democracy. The transition to a constitutional monarchy with elected parliament came much later.

Section 91.13

The Maldive Sultanate and British Protection: Island Geography, Maritime Economy, and Political Authority

The Maldives are a chain of coral atolls running roughly north to south in the Indian Ocean, southwest of India and Sri Lanka, and including many hundreds of small islands, only a fraction of them inhabited. The land is low, with almost no hills, and the sea is the principal resource and the principal barrier. This geography shaped political life. Communities were small and scattered across wide stretches of water; travel between atolls depended on sailing vessels and seasonal winds; and the central authority at Malé, on a small island in the Kaafu (Malé) atoll, had to govern through appointed atoll chiefs and local officials. The distance between Malé and the southern atolls, Addu, Huvadhu, and Fuvahmulah among them, was particularly great.

The Maldive Sultanate had a long history as an Islamic state. Its rulers governed through a court, a council of ministers and notables, and a system of atoll administration, and its law drew on Islamic jurisprudence and local custom. The economy rested on fishing, especially tuna, which was dried and exported to Ceylon and beyond, on the collection of cowrie shells in earlier centuries, on coir rope and mats, and on maritime trade. The wealth gained from this trade was unevenly distributed. Malé's merchants and officials controlled the key export channels, particularly the trade via Colombo, while the southern atolls had their own commercial links, including to Ceylon, and often resented the central government's taxes and trade rules.

In 1887, the Sultan accepted a British protectorate. Under this arrangement, Britain took responsibility for the Maldives' external relations and defense and agreed not to interfere in internal administration, while the Sultan paid an annual tribute and maintained certain obligations. The British exercised their role through the Governor of Ceylon, so that the Maldives was administered in its foreign affairs from Colombo. The protectorate thus left the Maldivian state intact but placed limits on its external sovereignty, as the vocabulary box in §91.9 explains. It was different from the colonial rule of Ceylon, where the British administered the island directly, and from the Indian princely states.

The protectorate's everyday operation depended on the channels through which correspondence, trade permissions, and outside representations traveled. Malé could administer island affairs through its own officials, but it could not deal with a foreign power on precisely the same diplomatic footing as an independent state. Nor did the archipelago's limited infrastructure allow the central government to supervise every island in the same way. Officials governing distant atolls worked through relationships with merchants, boatowners, religious authorities, and local notables. The resulting uneven reach of central government was not simply evidence of backwardness; it followed from the combination of sea distances, monsoon-dependent transport, commercial networks, and the division between internal administration and British-protected external affairs.

Political debate did not wait for independence. In the early twentieth century, a group of reformers sought a written constitution to limit the arbitrary use of power and to define the roles of ruler, ministers, and a representative body. The 1932 Constitution, promulgated under Sultan Shamsuddeen III, provided for a Majlis and set out principles of government, but it was contested by conservative elements, and the sultan's government soon curbed its operation. The episode demonstrates that debates about constitutional government, accountability, and the distribution of authority were part of Maldivian politics well before 1965, and that they involved conflicts among the sultan, officials, merchants, and reformers. Historians of the Maldives rely on the surviving constitutional texts, British reports from Colombo, and a small number of local chronicles, and the documentary base for these early debates is thinner than for Ceylon.

Section 91.14

The First Maldivian Republic of 1953: Mohamed Amin Didi, Reform, and the Restoration of the Sultanate

Mohamed Amin Didi was the most prominent reformer of the 1940s and early 1950s. A well-educated official who had served as prime minister under the sultanate, he promoted modernization of education, administration, and the economy, and he came to believe that a republican system was needed to carry out his program. The political crisis of the early 1950s gave him the opportunity. The earlier sultan Hassan Nooraddeen II had left the throne in 1943; a subsequent sultan-elect, Abdul Majeed Didi, lived abroad and died in 1952, leaving the future form of government unsettled. On 1 January 1953, the Maldives was proclaimed a republic, and Amin Didi became its first president. The Parliament of the Maldives identifies this event as the country's first republican experiment.

The succession that preceded the republic requires precision. Hassan Nooraddeen II had already ceased to reign in 1943. Abdul Majeed Didi was subsequently chosen as sultan but did not assume the throne in Malé, and his death in 1952 reopened the question of the monarchy's future. Amin Didi was offered the throne and instead supported a republican settlement, which voters endorsed in a 1952 referendum. The first republic consequently began on 1 January 1953, not as the direct replacement of an absent reigning Hassan Nooraddeen II. These distinctions matter because they show that constitutional transition followed a succession problem and political negotiations, rather than simply one reformer's abrupt decision to abolish an incumbent monarch.

The government's reform agenda, as reported in historical accounts, included the promotion of education, including for girls; efforts to improve public administration and tax collection; measures concerning health; and attempts to organize the economy through state involvement in the fish trade, which was the country's central export. Changes in the fish trade, in particular, affected many households and the merchants who handled the exports. The republic also attempted to alter constitutional arrangements by creating the office of the president and a ministerial structure, and by establishing a representative council.

The official presidential record dates Amin Didi's tenure from 1 January to 21 August 1953 and his death to 19 January 1954. It is important not to collapse the end of his presidency in August into the formal restoration of the sultanate in 1954. Between the two there was a period of uncertain leadership and political adjustment. The speed of the first republic's collapse also makes it difficult to infer how its social reforms would have performed over a longer period. Educational initiatives, economic controls, and administrative change met opposition for different reasons; a dispute over the fish trade was not identical to religious objections or elite resistance to a redistribution of offices. A source-based interpretation should identify the affected groups and distinguish their contemporary explanations from accounts written after the collapse.

The republic lasted less than a year. A combination of economic distress, including food shortages, opposition from merchants, officials, and religious leaders who disliked the speed and style of reform, and the republic's own limited administrative capacity led to a revolt in Malé in August 1953. Amin Didi was overthrown, injured by a mob, and detained, and he died in 1954. In 1954, the sultanate was restored under a new sultan, Muhammad Farid Didi, and the experiment ended. The Majlis's account identifies 1954 as the year of restoration and 1968 as that of the second republic.

The episode carries several lessons. First, it shows the importance of distinguishing constitutional form from the distribution of authority: the republic changed titles and offices, but its authority rested on a narrow group and was vulnerable. Second, it illustrates the tension between reform and consent: reforms intended to benefit the population provoked resistance from groups whose interests they affected, and a government without a broad base could not sustain them. Third, it shows that republican ideas were not imported solely at independence but had a local history, and Amin Didi's memory later served as a symbol for Maldivians interested in constitutional change. Historians must be careful when using sources, however, because accounts of the 1953 events differ depending on the political sympathies of their authors.

Section 91.15

Malé, the Southern Atolls, and the United Suvadive Republic, 1959–1963

The southern atolls had a distinct economic and social life. Addu Atoll, at the far south, Huvadhu (called Suvadiva in older usage), and Fuvahmulah lay far from Malé, had relatively large populations, and traded with Ceylon and other ports directly. Their people considered themselves culturally and economically distinct from the capital's elite, and relations with central authorities were sometimes strained, especially over taxation, trade controls, and the appointment of officials.

The British military presence added a new element. During the Second World War, Britain had established facilities in Addu Atoll, and in 1956 the British and Maldivian governments reached an agreement allowing Britain to reestablish and use the Gan airfield for a long term, in exchange for payments and conditions. The base brought employment to local residents, a market for goods and services, and links to the wider world, and it changed the economy of Addu in ways that Malé did not control. It also raised the question of who in the Maldives could negotiate with Britain and how the benefits of British facilities would be shared. Ibrahim Nasir, who became prime minister in 1957, was an advocate of strong central authority and was suspicious of southern autonomy.

The proposed airfield arrangements unfolded in stages. Terms discussed and initialed in 1956 generated dispute over the length of Britain's access and the privileges associated with construction. Once Nasir became prime minister, renegotiation created tension between the central administration's claim to approve foreign agreements and the employment and commercial benefits already reaching Addu. The agreement signed on 14 February 1960 was a separate legal stage, not simply the unchanged implementation of the earlier proposal. Its successor, the 26 July 1965 agreement, again redefined the parties' relationship. Reading these documents as successive bargains makes the dispute intelligible without assuming that islanders, Malé officials, and British commanders all shared a single interest.

The chronological labels also require separation. The Addu uprising and local declaration occurred at the turn of 1958–1959, while the process by which Fuvahmulah and Huvadhu joined produced a three-atoll separatist formation during February and March 1959. Maldivian accounts report that the news of the combined secession was publicly broadcast on 13 March. Calling the entire three-atoll state a January proclamation therefore gives a misleading impression of an instantaneous, uniform rebellion. Southern communities differed in their responses and experienced repression at different moments. Local testimony and retrospective narratives are valuable for reconstructing these events, but their dates and terminology need comparison with official correspondence and contemporary reports.

Tensions erupted in 1958–1959. Disputes arose over central government efforts to assert control, including the appointment of officials and the collection of taxes, and over the position of Addu's inhabitants in relation to the British base. The Addu uprising at the beginning of 1959 preceded the adhesion of Fuvahmulah and Huvadhu. The broader three-atoll United Suvadive Republic emerged during February and March, with Abdulla Afeef Didi as its leader, declaring separation from the Maldivian central government. The republic aimed to administer the southern atolls independently and sought recognition from Britain. Britain did not extend full recognition; British officials had interests in maintaining the Gan facility, in preserving their relations with Malé, and in avoiding a regional conflict, and their positions shifted over time. The signed Anglo-Maldivian agreement of 14 February 1960 reaffirmed Britain's ties with the central Maldivian government and its rights concerning Gan, while British officials continued to deal with southern leaders in ways that those leaders saw as at least partial acknowledgment.

The separatist government did not survive. The central government, under Nasir, used economic pressure and, in 1963, sent armed forces to the south, and the Suvadive Republic collapsed in that year. Its leader fled abroad, and the southern atolls were brought back under Malé's authority, followed by what southern accounts describe as reprisals and harsh treatment. The details of these events, including the scale of coercion, are contested in the sources, and students should regard casualty and damage claims cautiously. The British role was ambiguous: Britain neither actively backed the southern republic nor prevented its suppression, and later commentators have debated how far British policy tolerated or encouraged the central government's actions.

The episode should not be reduced to a regional rebellion. It was a dispute over state consolidation in a dispersed territory: who could tax, appoint officials, regulate trade, and speak for the Maldives to foreign powers. It involved peripheral economic interests, since Addu's prosperity was linked to Gan and to trade with Ceylon, as well as the political ambitions of southern leaders and the centralizing project of Malé's government. It also involved external actors, since British facilities and diplomacy affected the balance of power. By 1963, the central government had asserted control, which was a necessary step to independence in 1965 because it made possible a single Maldivian voice in negotiations with Britain. The costs, borne by southern communities, remained part of local memory.

Uncertainty about political authority was itself a daily burden, in a place where officials, taxes, and trade licenses determined the terms of livelihood.

Section 91.16

Independence in 1965 and the Second Republic of 1968: Sovereignty and Centralized Government

With the southern challenge ended, Nasir's government pressed Britain to end the protectorate. On 26 July 1965, an agreement signed at Colombo terminated Britain's responsibility for Maldivian external affairs and recognized the Maldives as fully independent. The agreement preserved British access to Gan, whose use remained significant for Britain until its withdrawal in the 1970s, a continuing link between formal independence and strategic reality. The Maldives was admitted to the United Nations in September 1965, which confirmed international recognition. For a small state of dispersed islands and limited resources, UN membership was a means of securing legitimacy and of dealing with larger states on an equal legal footing.

The independence agreement itself allows unusually precise analysis. Signed at Colombo on 26 July 1965, it explicitly superseded the agreement of 14 February 1960 and recognized the Maldive Islands as a fully independent sovereign state able to conduct direct international relations. At the same time it preserved Britain's unrestricted use of Gan and a specified 110-acre area on Hithadhoo, nominally until 15 December 1986, and restricted the granting of comparable military facilities to other states without British consent. These terms did not abolish Maldivian sovereignty over the areas; the agreement expressly retained it. They show instead how independence could be negotiated alongside continuing base rights and security limitations. Britain's actual withdrawal in the 1970s was earlier than the treaty's specified terminal date.

The next question concerned the form of government. The sultanate, reinstated in 1954 after the first republic's failure, remained in place after 1965, but Nasir and his supporters considered the monarchy an obstacle to the centralized, modernizing state they wanted. A referendum in March 1968 showed a large majority in favor of a republic, and on 11 November 1968 the second Republic of the Maldives was proclaimed, with Nasir as president. The sultanate, which had lasted for centuries, was abolished.

International recognition should also be dated separately from constitutional reform. The Maldive Islands joined the United Nations on 21 September 1965 while the sultanate still existed, demonstrating that republican government was not a prerequisite for state membership. The republic adopted in November 1968 therefore changed the domestic source and organization of executive authority, not the date on which the country first became independent. Students should read the referendum, constitutional arrangements, and personnel changes together: the transformation from sultan to president altered political offices, but not necessarily the local networks through which resources, public appointments, and administrative decisions were controlled.

The constitution of the second republic concentrated executive authority in the president, with a Majlis (People's Majlis) whose members were chosen without competitive party politics. Political parties did not exist in the sense of organized national competition, and the political community was small, with personal and family networks significant. Nasir's government invested in education, communications, and administrative organization, and developed relations with other states. Historians note that the republic's institutions in this period depended heavily on presidential authority, and that tests of accountability, such as an independent press or organized opposition, were limited. Students should therefore distinguish sovereignty from democratization: the end of the protectorate and the monarchy gave the Maldives full statehood and a republican form, but did not create competitive multiparty politics.

By 1971, the Maldives was a recognized sovereign state participating in international organizations, with a republican constitution and a centralized government. Its economy still depended on fishing and traditional trade; the tourism industry that later transformed the economy, and the multiparty constitutional changes of the twenty-first century, belong to later chapters (Chapters 96 and 104) and should not be read back into this period.

Section 91.17

India and the Smaller South Asian States: Treaties, Security, Trade, and Diplomatic Asymmetry

India's size, population, economy, and central position gave it a weight in the region that no other state could match, and each of the four smaller states had to define its relationship to that fact. India's concerns were partly inherited from the British Raj: the security of the northern frontier, the protection of sea routes, the management of migration across open borders, and the question of how far Indian influence would be accepted by neighbors who had their own histories and aspirations. The relationships that resulted were asymmetrical, but not identical, and each was shaped by treaties, geography, and political choice.

With Nepal, the foundation was the Treaty of Peace and Friendship signed on 31 July 1950. As commonly summarized, it provided for mutual recognition of sovereignty and independence, for consultation on security matters, and for reciprocal privileges for the nationals of each state in residence, property, employment, and trade, and it was associated with an open border between the two countries. Letters exchanged at the time concerned security and arms supplies. The treaty was signed under the Rana regime but shaped the relationship in the following era. It was valued by Indian planners as a basis for security cooperation, and many Nepalis welcomed its open-border provisions, which allowed free movement and work in India. Yet others, especially after the Panchayat government came to power, viewed it as a mark of Indian predominance, and Nepal sought to diversify its foreign relations through a 1956 agreement with China, a 1961 boundary treaty with China, and its own policy of balancing. In the late 1960s, the Nepali government asked India to withdraw its military liaison group and checkposts from the northern border, and India did so, a sign that Nepal could press its claims.

With Bhutan, the 1949 treaty established a different relationship, in which India accepted a role as guide in external affairs and provided subsidies and later development assistance. Section 91.11 showed how Indian financial and technical help underwrote the First Five-Year Plan. India's involvement in Bhutan was thus deep, but Bhutan retained its monarchy, internal administration, and, increasingly, its international contacts, culminating in UN membership in 1971. The relationship illustrates an asymmetry that was partly negotiated and partly accepted as a strategic choice by the Bhutanese leadership.

Ceylon's relationship was shaped by the maritime border, by migration, and by the plantation Tamil question. Ceylon was a Commonwealth state like India, but it pursued its own foreign policy, including a nonaligned course under Sirimavo Bandaranaike's government. The dispute over the status of Indian-origin residents was the main bilateral problem. After years of inconclusive negotiation, Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike and Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri concluded an agreement in October 1964. As generally reported, it covered about 975,000 people of Indian origin: roughly 525,000 were to be repatriated to India and granted Indian citizenship, about 300,000 were to be granted Ceylonese citizenship, and the fate of the remainder was left for later settlement. The repatriation was to take place over a period of fifteen years. Students must distinguish what was agreed in 1964 from what was subsequently done: implementation was slow and contested, many people did not wish to leave, and the unresolved portion required later agreements, discussed in Chapter 96. Moreover, the agreement was concluded between the two governments, and the plantation population itself had little formal say in a decision about its citizenship.

The Maldives had a more distant relationship with India during these years, since its external affairs had been managed through Ceylon and Britain until 1965, and its trade and migration links ran largely to Ceylon. After independence, India recognized the Maldives as a sovereign state, and the two countries developed relations over time, with the Maldives' position in the Indian Ocean becoming a point of strategic interest. The relationship in this period was modest compared with those with Nepal and Bhutan, and later developments belong to subsequent chapters.

Two cautions are necessary. India should not be treated as an all-controlling regional actor: its leaders often held back from direct intervention, disagreed internally about policy, and were constrained by limited resources and by the Cold War context, and the 1962 war with China, discussed in Chapter 92, exposed the limits of Indian power. Nor should the smaller states be treated as lacking diplomatic agency. Nepal's balancing, Bhutan's selective alignments, Ceylon's nonalignment, and the Maldives' pursuit of UN membership all show purposeful policy. The politics of asymmetry meant that smaller states had less margin for error, but not that they had no choices.

Section 91.18

Development, Welfare, Social Hierarchy, and Political Mobilization across the Four States

The four states faced different material conditions. Ceylon was the most developed in social indicators. From the 1940s, free education from primary to university level, free health services, and a system of subsidized rice rations had produced high literacy and relatively low mortality compared with other low-income countries. Successive governments maintained these programs, and the state's welfare orientation was part of the political culture. But the economy depended on exports of tea, rubber, and coconut, vulnerable to price changes, and the growth of the educated population outpaced the supply of suitable jobs. Rural unemployment and underemployment, especially among educated young people, created discontent. The language policies discussed above also affected access to public employment, which many saw as the main route to advancement.

That discontent culminated in the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) uprising of April 1971. Led by Rohana Wijeweera, the JVP drew its support mainly from young, Sinhala-speaking men from rural and lower-middle-class backgrounds, many of them educated but unemployed or underemployed. Its ideology combined Marxist ideas with nationalist and anti-establishment themes. On 5 April 1971, JVP insurgents attacked police stations across the island, and the government of Sirimavo Bandaranaike, elected in 1970 at the head of a left-leaning coalition, declared an emergency and, with help from several foreign states, suppressed the revolt within weeks. Casualty estimates vary widely, with reported deaths in the thousands, and tens of thousands of suspects were detained. The uprising matters for the argument of this chapter: it shows that competitive elections and substantial welfare institutions did not prevent severe generational and social conflict. Students must also distinguish it from the Tamil federalist movement. The JVP was a predominantly Sinhala insurgency motivated largely by economic and political grievances, not an ethnic movement, and its goals and base differed from those of the Federal Party.

Nepal began the period with little modern infrastructure. Few roads existed outside the Kathmandu Valley and the Terai, literacy was very low, and land tenure was dominated by large holdings, including birta grants and landlord-tenant arrangements that left many cultivators with insecure rights. The state's capacity was minimal at the start of the 1950s, and development was largely donor-financed, with India, the United States, China, the Soviet Union, and other countries building roads, airfields, and public works. Under Mahendra, development was tied to royal authority: the monarchy sought legitimacy through delivering schools, roads, and land reform, and it presented the Panchayat system as a vehicle for national development. How far this raised living standards is difficult to establish because statistics are limited, and uneven implementation of land reform left structural inequalities intact.

Bhutan's development, as §91.11 showed, started from an even lower base of infrastructure but proceeded with Indian support and royal direction. Its main challenges were mountain terrain and thin administrative capacity, and its achievements, such as road links, schools, and basic health facilities, were significant relative to the starting point. The Maldives, a sparse and dispersed population dependent on fishing and maritime transport, had to provide public services across many islands. Education, health care, and communications were difficult to deliver across atolls, and they tended to be concentrated in Malé. The British facility at Gan produced local wage employment in the south, creating a distinctive pattern.

The comparative question is what development programs did politically. In Ceylon, welfare expanded participation and expectations but also created pressures on a state unable to meet them. In Nepal, development under the monarchy strengthened central authority and the state's presence in the countryside without widening political participation. In Bhutan, development extended administrative reach and linked districts to the center while preserving monarchical leadership. In the Maldives, the central government's capacity to provide services was part of its argument for consolidation. These outcomes show that development could expand participation, strengthen central authority, improve welfare, or do some combination of these, depending on the political setting.

Section 91.19

Divergent Constitutional Paths: Citizenship, National Identity, Monarchy, and Democracy

This section compares the four political trajectories directly. The comparison begins by recognizing that the question each state faced was different, and that no single model of colonial rule, independence, parliamentary democracy, and development fits them all.

Ceylon maintained competitive parliamentary institutions and changes of government through elections, but it restricted minority participation, first through the citizenship laws and later through the language policy. Its sovereignty was the product of negotiated transfer, and its national identity was increasingly defined in terms of the Sinhala language and Buddhism, as the 1956 election showed. The constitution included a safeguard for minorities that proved ineffective in the courts. The case illustrates that representative institutions and the protection of equal citizenship are separate matters: elections made governments accountable to majorities, but they did not guarantee that majorities would respect minorities.

Nepal's trajectory was different. The Rana oligarchy ended through a revolution in which the monarchy and the Nepali Congress cooperated, and a parliamentary constitution and election followed in 1959. But the same constitution preserved royal powers that the king used in 1960 and replaced in 1962 with a partyless system. Sovereignty was never in question, but the location of political authority was. Nepal's national identity was defined by the monarchy in Hindu and Nepali-language terms, which privileged some groups. The comparison with Ceylon is instructive: in Ceylon, a majority used electoral democracy to define the nation narrowly; in Nepal, a monarch used royal authority to end electoral democracy and define the nation.

Bhutan preserved its monarchy and gradually built institutions through reforms from above. Its sovereignty rested on a treaty relationship that limited its external autonomy but protected its internal order. Citizenship was defined by statute in 1958, representation was channeled through the Assembly, and political authority remained with the king. It would be wrong to characterize Bhutan as either a democracy or merely an autocracy: it was a monarchy in which the king actively consulted institutions and shared some authority over time, but without parties or elections of the competitive kind.

The Maldives achieved independence in 1965 and became a republic in 1968, with executive power centralized in the presidency. Sovereignty here meant the end of a protectorate, and national identity rested on a shared Islamic and maritime culture and on a consolidated state after the Suvadive episode. Its constitutional history shows frequent changes of form, but the underlying pattern was of elite-based politics in a small society, with limited institutions of competitive representation.

A useful comparison begins by identifying which questions each political institution actually answered. A constitution could specify who appointed ministers, but not guarantee that ministers were responsible to citizens; a citizenship statute could define membership, but not ensure that long-resident communities qualified; a treaty could recognize sovereignty, but reserve significant foreign security privileges. In Ceylon, the key test was whether parliamentary safeguards restrained legislation affecting minorities. In Nepal, it was whether an electoral mandate could survive a monarch's intervention. In Bhutan, the question was how formal authority transferred within an evolving monarchy. In the Maldives, it was whether a sovereign republican state offered more public accountability than the sultanate it replaced. Applying the same test to all four without these differences would conceal rather than illuminate their historical experiences.

The timing of each transformation complicates the comparison further. Ceylon had broad suffrage well before formal independence; Nepal's first national election came after the Rana regime fell; Bhutan institutionalized consultation before it developed an extensive modern bureaucracy; and the Maldives secured international recognition while retaining a sultan. Political sequences thus moved in different orders. This makes it impossible to infer from a single milestone, such as an election or a constitution, that a state had reached the same stage of political development as its neighbors.

Three distinctions organize the comparison. The first is constitutional design versus political practice: Ceylon's section 29 and Nepal's 1959 constitution looked protective on paper, but practice depended on courts, parties, and the king. The second is political liberalization versus economic modernization: all four states expanded schools, roads, or services, but these gains did not correspond to expanded political competition. The third is sovereignty versus democracy: sovereignty meant recognition and control within borders, whereas democracy meant accountable representation, and states could have the first without the second.

Historical Debate 2

Can Monarchy Produce Political Modernization without Competitive Democracy?

The question: Under King Mahendra, Nepal built administrative institutions, extended roads and schools, and began legal and land reform while banning parties. Under King Jigme Dorji Wangchuck, Bhutan developed institutions gradually from above. Did these monarchies deliver modernization effectively, and at what cost to representation?

Interpretation A — Centralized authority enabled stability and development: Supporters of this view argue that in societies with little infrastructure and few administrative institutions, a strong central authority could mobilize resources and impose reforms that fragmented party politics might have blocked. They point to roads, schools, and legal codes introduced under the monarchies, and to the absence of the factional violence seen elsewhere.
Interpretation B — The costs of excluding opposition: Critics argue that development without accountability weakens reform, since policies lack broad ownership and abuses go unchecked. In Nepal, they note, the arrest of elected leaders, the ban on parties, and the privileging of a narrow national identity produced grievances that resurfaced later. In Bhutan, they note the postponement of questions of representation and citizenship.

Evidence: Constitutions, royal proclamations, development plans, aid records, and later scholarship. The two cases are not equivalent: Nepal's monarch dismissed an elected government and prohibited parties, while Bhutan's king created consultative institutions in a state that had never had parliamentary parties, and the two operated in different geopolitical contexts.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether modernization requires political liberalization, and on how historians should judge reforms from above.

Section 91.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Smaller States and the Making of Postcolonial South Asia

The four histories examined in this chapter converge on a set of connected themes: sovereignty, citizenship, political legitimacy, national identity, monarchy, constitutional government, regional inequality, and external relationships. Each state's inheritance shaped its choices without determining them. Ceylon inherited universal suffrage, a parliamentary framework, a plantation economy, and communal divisions sharpened under colonial rule. Nepal inherited an oligarchy, a restricted society, and a monarchy with symbolic authority. Bhutan inherited a young monarchy, a monastic establishment, and a treaty relationship. The Maldives inherited a sultanate, a protectorate, and a dispersed geography. In each case, political actors, including parties, kings, officials, clergy, regional leaders, and foreign governments, made decisions whose consequences were not predetermined.

The roles of those actors varied. In Ceylon, parties and electorates dominated politics, while the civil service, the Buddhist clergy, and trade unions shaped outcomes. In Nepal, the monarchy and the Nepali Congress were the main actors, with India as a mediator and the army as a reserve of royal power. In Bhutan, the king and the monastic establishment worked with Indian advisers. In the Maldives, Malé's elite, southern leaders, and British officials were the principal players. Foreign governments mattered everywhere but acted through local partners, and the outcomes were negotiated rather than imposed.

The consequences of the different arrangements can be compared. Majoritarian electoral politics in Ceylon produced democratic continuity but exclusion and recurring conflict. Monarch-led modernization in Bhutan produced institutional growth and continuity, with representation deferred. Centralized republican government in the Maldives produced a consolidated state and international recognition, with limited political competition. Royal political control in Nepal produced administrative expansion and an end to parliamentary experiment, with grievances that persisted. No path can be called simply successful or unsuccessful; each solved some problems and created others, and judgments depend on criteria.

Comparison must also account for different kinds of exclusion. In Ceylon, formal elections continued while citizenship and language rules placed particular communities at a disadvantage. In Nepal, exclusion operated through a prohibition on organized parties and concentration of national authority in the monarchy. In Bhutan, the central limitation was the absence of competitive party government even as consultative institutions grew. In the Maldives, centralization, island geography, and personal networks shaped the narrow field of national political competition. These contrasts matter because a single scale of “more” or “less” democracy cannot explain the concrete mechanisms by which public voice was included or constrained.

The source base also affects the certainty of these conclusions. Parliamentary debates and election returns make Ceylon's disputes unusually visible, while the internal deliberations of Bhutanese and Maldivian institutions are more often known through later official histories or diplomatic records. Nepal's political opposition generated valuable testimony but also retrospective accounts written with the memory of royal rule. Where archival coverage is thinner, historians should make narrower claims, not infer that opposition or social conflict was absent. The regional perspective is strongest when it compares what can be documented and openly identifies what remains uncertain.

The central conclusion is that the smaller South Asian states were integral participants in the formation of the postcolonial regional order, not peripheral additions to the histories of India and Pakistan. They shaped regional diplomacy through their treaties and balancing, they faced the same questions of citizenship and minority rights as their larger neighbors, and they demonstrated that decolonization and state formation took many forms. Their histories show that sovereignty, democracy, citizenship, and modernization could reinforce one another but could also diverge. These distinctions will recur in the following chapters. Chapter 92 turns to the wars, frontier disputes, and regional nationalisms that reshaped South Asia between the 1950s and 1971, including the Sino-Indian War of 1962, the wars between India and Pakistan, and the creation of Bangladesh.

Cause and Effect

The Failure of Parliamentary Government in Nepal, 1959–1962

Long-term causes

A century of Rana isolation had left few political institutions; the monarchy retained symbolic legitimacy; Nepal's society was divided by region, caste, and language, and its administrative capacity was thin.

Short-term causes

The 1959 constitution was granted by the king and preserved royal powers; the Koirala government's reform program threatened landed and aristocratic interests; the king sought to keep control of the army and the direction of policy.

Event or transformation

The dismissal of the Koirala government and the suspension of parliamentary government in December 1960.

Immediate effects

Arrest of political leaders, exile of Congress members, and a ban on parties, followed by the Panchayat constitution of 1962.

Long-term consequences

A partyless polity until 1990, expansion of state institutions under royal authority, and unresolved grievances over representation and identity that later movements invoked.

Historical complexity

The outcome was not inevitable: the king's choice, India's cautious role, domestic opposition, and regional politics all contributed, and no single cause explains it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Imperial Predominance to Postcolonial States

What Changed

  • Ceylon moved from Crown colony to self-governing Dominion; the Maldives from protectorate to independent republic
  • Nepal's Rana oligarchy gave way to a monarchy-centered state
  • Bhutan's external relationship passed from British India to India, and state institutions expanded
  • Citizenship, language, and national identity became matters of state law

What Stayed the Same

  • Monarchies persisted in Nepal and Bhutan as central institutions
  • Geography continued to shape integration and external relations
  • Dependence on larger neighbors and external aid persisted
  • Plantation labor and subsistence or fishing economies remained foundational

Who experienced change most directly: plantation Tamils in Ceylon, political leaders and cultivators in Nepal, serf-like laborers and landholders in Bhutan, and southern islanders in the Maldives. Who experienced greater continuity: many rural households in all four states, whose livelihoods changed slowly. Why the pattern matters: it shows that political change at the top did not translate uniformly into social change below.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that postcolonial South Asia cannot be understood through India and Pakistan alone. Ceylon, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives each redefined sovereignty between 1948 and 1971, but they did so in different ways, because their relations with Britain and India had been different. Ceylon negotiated a transfer of power and became a Dominion in 1948. Nepal, never a colony, overthrew its Rana oligarchy in 1950–1951. Bhutan, a monarchy since 1907, renegotiated its treaty relationship with India in 1949. The Maldives ended its British protectorate in 1965.

In Ceylon, the Donoughmore and Soulbury constitutions created a parliamentary democracy with universal suffrage and competitive elections. Yet the citizenship legislation of 1948–1949 excluded most Indian-origin plantation Tamils from citizenship and the vote, and the minority safeguard in the constitution did not prevent this. The distinction between Sri Lankan Tamils and plantation Tamils is essential to understanding the period.

The election of 1956 and the Official Language Act showed how Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism, rooted in resentments toward an English-educated elite, could be mobilized electorally. The Act disadvantaged Tamil-speaking citizens in public employment and administration. The Federal Party responded with demands for federal autonomy and with nonviolent protest. The Bandaranaike–Chelvanayakam Pact of 1957 and the Senanayake–Chelvanayakam agreement of 1965 were attempts at compromise, but both were abandoned under political pressure, and the violence of 1958 marked a rupture in intercommunal trust. The Tamil politics of this period was federalist and parliamentary, not yet separatist.

Nepal's revolution ended Rana rule, with King Tribhuvan, the Nepali Congress, and India all playing roles. The settlement of 1951 left unresolved the powers of the monarchy and the promised constituent assembly. The parliamentary election of 1959 gave the Nepali Congress a decisive victory and B. P. Koirala the premiership, but King Mahendra dismissed the government in December 1960 and in 1962 proclaimed the partyless Panchayat constitution, which concentrated authority in the crown while expanding state institutions.

Bhutan's monarchy, established in 1907, preserved its sovereignty through treaties with British India and then India, and in the 1950s and 1960s King Jigme Dorji Wangchuck created a National Assembly, ended bonded labor, passed a nationality law, launched the First Five-Year Plan, and established an advisory council and ministerial cabinet. These were reforms from above, not the establishment of parliamentary democracy, and the evidence for their social effects is limited.

In the Maldives, constitutional debate predated independence. The first republic of 1953 collapsed within a year, and the sultanate was restored. The southern atolls, with their own trade and the British base at Gan, proclaimed the United Suvadive Republic in 1959 in a dispute over state consolidation. After its suppression in 1963, the Maldives became independent on 26 July 1965 and a republic on 11 November 1968, with executive power concentrated in the presidency.

Comparison across the four reveals that sovereignty, democracy, citizenship, and modernization were distinct processes. India's asymmetrical relations, expressed through the 1949 and 1950 treaties and the 1964 agreement, constrained but did not eliminate the agency of smaller states. Development programs expanded schools, roads, and services, but their political effects varied. The JVP uprising of 1971 showed that welfare institutions and competitive elections did not prevent social conflict.

The chapter's central lesson is that postcolonial South Asia contained multiple models of state formation. The histories of the smaller states are essential to the region's history, and they set up the questions taken up in later chapters on regional wars, the Panchayat era, Sri Lankan conflict, and twenty-first-century transitions.

Key Terms

Dominion
A self-governing state within the British Commonwealth that retained the Crown as head of state; Ceylon held this status from 1948 to 1972.
Soulbury Constitution
The parliamentary constitution of Ceylon, based on the Soulbury Commission's recommendations, in force from 1947–1948 until 1972.
Governor-General
The Crown's representative in a Dominion, who normally acted on ministerial advice.
Universal adult franchise
The right of all adults, regardless of sex, property, or literacy, to vote; introduced in Ceylon in 1931.
Section 29
The Soulbury constitutional provision restricting laws that imposed disabilities on, or conferred privileges for, particular communities or religions.
Stateless person
A person not recognized as a citizen by any state, as many plantation Tamils were after 1948–1949.
Citizenship by descent
Citizenship acquired through the birthplace of one's parents or grandparents, which plantation families often could not prove.
Plantation (Up-country) Tamils
Tamil-speaking people of Indian origin descended from estate laborers, distinct from Sri Lankan Tamils.
Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism
A political and cultural movement emphasizing the Sinhala language and Buddhism, which shaped the 1956 election and later policy.
Official Language Act (1956)
Ceylon's Act No. 33 of 1956, which made Sinhala the official language of the state.
Federal Party
The Ilankai Tamil Arasu Kachchi, founded in 1949, which advocated Tamil regional autonomy within a united Ceylon.
Satyagraha
Nonviolent civil resistance, used by the Federal Party in the 1956 and 1961 protests.
Bandaranaike–Chelvanayakam Pact
The 1957 agreement proposing regional councils and recognition of Tamil, abandoned in 1958.
Rana oligarchy
The hereditary rule of Rana prime ministers in Nepal from 1846 to 1951.
Shah monarchy
The royal dynasty of Nepal, ceremonially secluded under the Ranas and restored to central authority after 1951.
Nepali Congress
The democratic party that led the anti-Rana movement and won the 1959 election.
Delhi compromise
The 1951 settlement among the king, Rana leaders, and the Congress, negotiated with Indian mediation.
Birta
Land grants, often tax-free, given to elites in Nepal; the target of reform efforts.
Panchayat system
Nepal's partyless pyramid of councils under royal authority, established by the 1962 constitution.

Powers reserved to a monarch, such as dismissing a government or declaring an emergency.
Tshogdu
Bhutan's National Assembly, established in 1953 with village, monastic, and official members.

The treaty between Bhutan and British India limiting Bhutan's foreign relations while preserving internal autonomy.
India–Bhutan Treaty (1949)
The agreement under which India pledged noninterference in Bhutan's internal administration and Bhutan accepted Indian guidance in external relations.
Nationality Law (1958)
Bhutan's statute defining citizenship by descent and by residence and registration.
Five-Year Plan
A state plan for development; Bhutan's first ran from 1961 and prioritized roads, schools, and health.
Protectorate
A state that retains internal government while a stronger power controls foreign relations and defense; the Maldives from 1887 to 1965.
Atoll
A ring-shaped coral island group; the Maldives consists of chains of atolls.
United Suvadive Republic
The separatist state proclaimed in 1959 by leaders of the southern atolls, collapsed in 1963.
Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna
A radical Sinhala-majority youth movement that launched the April 1971 insurrection in Ceylon.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it widens the frame of postcolonial South Asian history. A narrative built around India and Pakistan can suggest that the end of empire produced two big states and a handful of footnotes. The histories of Ceylon, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives show instead that sovereignty, citizenship, and legitimacy were negotiated in many forms, and that smaller states faced the same large questions in different settings.

The chapter also reveals processes that shaped later events. The citizenship and language disputes in Ceylon laid the ground for later conflict, examined in Chapters 96 and 103. Nepal's Panchayat order, and the grievances it left, shaped the political movements discussed in Chapters 96 and 104. Bhutan's gradual reform prepared a very different transition in the twenty-first century. The Maldives' consolidation and its tradition of centralized presidential authority would influence its later constitutional history.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter connects the large-state narratives of Chapters 88 through 90 to the regional wars and the creation of Bangladesh in Chapter 92. It shows that Volume VI is a history of a region, and that the distinctions among sovereignty, democracy, citizenship, and modernization, which this chapter has emphasized, are tools students will need as they read about the 1970s and after.

Finally, the chapter models a historical habit: to compare without flattening. The four states were not versions of one story, and the evidence for each differs in quality and kind. Careful historians keep both the comparison and the differences in view, and they remain candid about what the sources can and cannot show.
