Part XVI — Building the Postcolonial States, 1947–1971

Pakistan from Independence to Military Rule

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the afternoon of 21 February 1952, students at the University of Dhaka gathered to defy an order banning public assemblies. They were protesting a decision that most of the population of their province could not read the language in which the state intended to conduct its business. Police opened fire near the Dhaka Medical College. Several young men died. Within days a makeshift memorial stood on the site, and within a few decades the date was marked as a day of remembrance by millions of people. Four years earlier, in a different city and a different chamber, a Bengali member of Pakistan's Constituent Assembly had made a modest procedural request: that Bengali be allowed alongside Urdu and English in the Assembly's proceedings. The request was refused. Between those two moments lies much of the early history of Pakistan.

Chapter 88 examined the establishment of India's constitutional democracy, and Chapter 89 examined its developmental and social-reform programs. This chapter turns to the other large successor state created in August 1947. Pakistan was brought into being through a demand for Muslim political self-determination, a demand explored in Chapters 77, 82, 83, and 85. It began life with two territorial wings separated by roughly a thousand miles of Indian territory, a population of diverse languages and regional histories, a provisional constitution inherited from the British, and an administrative apparatus that had to be assembled in a matter of weeks. This chapter traces what Pakistan's leaders and citizens did with that inheritance between independence in 1947 and the fall of General Muhammad Ayub Khan in March 1969.

The chapter's central question is why Pakistan's early efforts at constitutional government gave way to military-bureaucratic rule, and how disputes over language, political representation, economic development, and regional autonomy deepened divisions between East and West Pakistan. Four arguments are developed. First, Pakistan inherited extraordinary administrative, financial, and territorial challenges. Second, the distribution of political power among provinces, wings, parties, and institutions became increasingly contested. Third, military and bureaucratic institutions accumulated authority as representative institutions weakened. Fourth, the eastern wing's grievances grew even in periods of substantial national economic growth.

Two cautions shape the whole account. The first is against teleology. It is tempting to read these two decades as a prelude to the war of 1971 and the creation of Bangladesh, but the actors of the 1950s and 1960s did not know that outcome. Most Bengali political leaders of the period sought autonomy and fair representation within Pakistan, not separation. Constitutional compromises were reached, however imperfectly, and alternatives were debated. Military rule in 1958 was a choice made by identifiable individuals, not a constitutional necessity. The second caution is against reducing Pakistan to its coups. These years also saw the drafting of two constitutions, rapid industrial growth, the building of canals and dams, the creation of a new capital, and vigorous public debate, student mobilization, and electoral politics.

The evidence is unusually varied. Constituent Assembly debates, constitutional texts, court judgments, party manifestos, election returns, planning documents, and economists' studies survive in quantity. Newspapers, memoirs, and the testimony of participants add perspectives from both wings. These sources are not neutral. Officials wrote to justify decisions, politicians wrote to mobilize supporters, and later memoirists wrote with knowledge of 1971. This chapter therefore distinguishes documentary evidence from retrospective interpretation throughout, and signals where figures or readings remain disputed.

The chapter has five divisions. Division I examines independence, the founding leadership, refugee settlement, and the first civilian government. Division II studies the language question and the contest over representation. Division III analyzes constitution-making, the Objectives Resolution, the Assembly's dissolution, the Constitution of 1956, and the failure of parliamentary government. Division IV follows Ayub Khan's regime, its controlled political system, and its development program. Division V examines East–West inequality and the autonomy movement, and the final section synthesizes the chapter. The 1970 election, the breakdown of negotiations, the war of 1971, and the creation of Bangladesh belong to Chapter 92.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the territorial, administrative, financial, and demographic challenges Pakistan faced at independence in 1947.
  2. Assess Muhammad Ali Jinnah's role as founding leader, distinguishing documented statements from later interpretations of his intentions.
  3. Explain how refugee migration shaped Karachi, Sindh, Punjab, and the institutions and coalitions of the new state.
  4. Trace the origins and stages of the Bengali Language Movement from 1948 to the constitutional recognition of Bengali in 1956.
  5. Analyze the 1954 East Bengal election and the United Front's Twenty-One Point Programme as a demand for autonomy within Pakistan.
  6. Evaluate the dispute between representation by population and parity, and the effects of the One Unit scheme.
  7. Interpret the Objectives Resolution of 1949 and the competing readings it has received.
  8. Explain the constitutional crisis of 1954–1955 and the Federal Court's ruling in the Tamizuddin Khan case.
  9. Compare the Constitutions of 1956 and 1962 in executive structure, legislature, representation, and rights.
  10. Distinguish competing explanations for the collapse of parliamentary government and the 1958 military takeover.
  11. Evaluate Ayub Khan's development decade, its achievements, and its unequal distribution of benefits.
  12. Analyze how political, administrative, military, and economic disparities between the wings, together with the Six-Point Programme, shaped the crisis of 1969.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a state founded on a shared religious identity find it so difficult to build a shared political settlement?
  2. How did the administrative inheritance of 1947 shape which institutions grew strong and which remained weak?
  3. Why did language become the first great test of national integration, and what did Bengali and Urdu each symbolize?
  4. Was the dispute between population and parity a problem with a workable compromise, or a deeper conflict about who would govern?
  5. How should historians weigh party weakness, bureaucratic ambition, military interest, and executive intervention in explaining the events of 1958?
  6. What distinguished the constitutional design of 1956 from that of 1962, and why did these differences matter?
  7. In what sense was the Ayub era both a period of growth and a period of rising grievance?
  8. How can historians compare East and West Pakistan fairly, without treating either wing as a uniform bloc?
  9. How did the demand for provincial autonomy differ from the later demand for independence, and why does the distinction matter?
  10. How can historians explain Pakistan's trajectory without treating military rule or the separation of East Pakistan as inevitable?

Historical Context

The state that came into existence in August 1947 emerged from the final negotiations described in Chapters 85 through 87. The Indian Independence Act of 1947 provided for two dominions, India and Pakistan. Pakistan was composed of the Muslim-majority provinces and districts of the northwest, namely Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province, Balochistan, and West Punjab, and of East Bengal, together with the Sylhet district of Assam. The partitions of Punjab and Bengal, examined in Chapter 86, had cut across densely integrated economies, irrigation systems, railways, and jute-producing and manufacturing regions. Princely states in the west, such as Bahawalpur, Khairpur, and Kalat, had separate legal status and acceded or were integrated on different timetables, as Chapter 87 described.

Pakistan inherited the Government of India Act of 1935 as its interim constitution, adapted by the Provisional Constitution Order, and the Governor-General held substantial powers under it. The new state also inherited the British administrative traditions of the Indian Civil Service, a regular army with its British-trained officers, and a tradition of provincial governance. What it lacked was a capital with offices, an established central secretariat, fully developed financial institutions, and a trained cadre at every level. Many Hindu and Sikh officials, traders, bankers, and professionals had left for India. Karachi, a port city of several hundred thousand people, had to become a federal capital almost overnight.

The Pakistan movement had been built on a coalition that was never ideologically uniform. It included landed notables of Punjab and Sindh, Urdu-speaking professionals and merchants from Muslim minority provinces, and Bengali peasant and middle-class politicians. It also included religious scholars who held varied views on the state's relationship to Islamic law. The Muslim League had been strongest in the provinces where Muslims were a minority, whose leaders then migrated to a state that was largely new to them. Its organization in the provinces that became Pakistan was weaker. In Bengal, politicians such as H. S. Suhrawardy and A. K. Fazlul Huq had their own political traditions, and in the northwest, provincial elites such as the Unionist landlords of Punjab, the Khan brothers in the Frontier, and Sindhi leaders held strong local positions.

Foreign relations also framed domestic politics. The conflict over Kashmir, which began in 1947 and led to a ceasefire on 1 January 1949, kept Pakistan's leaders focused on security and drew a disproportionate share of the budget to defense. In the 1950s Pakistan joined US-sponsored alliances, including the 1954 Mutual Defense Assistance Agreement, SEATO in 1954, and the Baghdad Pact (later CENTO) in 1955. These brought military and economic aid and strengthened the army and the civil bureaucracy as institutions of national power. Historians continue to debate how much this external alignment contributed to the later weakness of civilian politics.

For this chapter, the main primary sources are constitutional documents and Assembly debates, judicial decisions, election results, party manifestos, planning documents, and economic studies. Banglapedia, the National Assembly's historical record, and the Punjab Judicial Academy's constitutional history provide convenient institutional summaries, but they are secondary. Students should treat the document windows in this chapter as editorial studies of sources, with exact wording to be checked against verified editions.

Division I · §§90.1–90.4 · 1947–1951

Independence, Leadership, and the Difficult Construction of Pakistan

Pakistan's initial institutional challenges, and the leadership, decisions, and crises that shaped its early political development.

Section 90.1

Pakistan at Independence: Territory, Population, Institutions, and Inherited Challenges

Pakistan came into existence at the end of the British Raj as a state of two parts. East Bengal, soon to be renamed East Pakistan in 1955 under the One Unit arrangement, lay at the head of the Bay of Bengal and included the delta of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna rivers. West Pakistan comprised Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province, Balochistan, West Punjab, and a number of princely states and tribal areas, stretching from the Arabian Sea to the Hindu Kush. Roughly a thousand miles of Indian territory lay between them. Distance shaped everything that followed. Communication, trade, defense, and political coordination had to cross the territory of a neighboring state that was, from the first, in a tense relationship with Pakistan. Sea routes ran around the whole of the peninsula, and the first air connections were long and costly.

Dates require care. The ceremonies marking the transfer of power took place in Karachi on 14 August 1947, when Lord Mountbatten addressed the Constituent Assembly. The legal appointed day under the Indian Independence Act of 1947, however, was 15 August, and it was on that day that Muhammad Ali Jinnah was sworn in as Governor-General. Pakistan later came to observe 14 August as its Independence Day. Students should not treat the two dates as contradictory. They record a ceremonial occasion and a legal one.

The new state's population was approximately seventy million, and the census of 1951 would show that a majority lived in the east. The two wings were not simply eastern and western. Each contained several regions with distinct languages, social structures, and histories. East Bengal was densely populated, overwhelmingly agrarian, dominated by small peasant holdings, and centered on the production of jute and rice. Its Hindu minority was significant, especially among landholders, professionals, and urban traders, and a great many members of this minority left in the following years. West Pakistan included the irrigated plains of Punjab, large landed estates in Sindh and Punjab, the tribal and Pashtun societies of the Frontier, and the Baloch tribal areas and princely states. Language alone reveals the diversity. Bengali was spoken by the majority of Pakistanis, Punjabi by the largest western community, and Urdu as a first language by only a small minority, perhaps seven percent in the 1951 census. Sindhi, Pashto, Balochi, Brahui, Gujarati, and many other languages were also spoken. A shared religious identity, however powerful in the politics of 1946 and 1947, did not eliminate other forms of belonging.

The administrative inheritance was uneven. Pakistan received a share of the assets, personnel, and military stores of British India under the partition arrangements. These arrangements were administered by the Partition Council and proved contentious. Disputes over the division of cash balances, military equipment, and the allocation of state resources left lasting suspicion. Many officials of the Indian Civil Service and other services opted for Pakistan, but the numbers were small compared with the tasks. A relatively small group of senior Muslim civil servants, many from Punjab and the United Provinces, formed the nucleus of the central bureaucracy. The military faced a similar problem. The army was inherited in fragments, with officers who had mostly served in junior positions in the British Indian Army and with fewer arsenals and training establishments than the new government needed.

Karachi became the federal capital. It was a port city that had been a provincial town of Sindh, and its transformation was immediate. Ministries took over hotels, commandeered buildings, and set up offices in barracks and temporary huts. In 1948 the federal capital was separated from Sindh as a centrally administered area, a decision that Sindhi politicians resented. The city's population grew explosively with the arrival of refugees (§90.3). The government had also to create a financial system. The State Bank of Pakistan began operations in 1948. A currency had to be issued, a tax system built, and a budget balanced when the army was claiming a very large share of expenditure because of the conflict in Kashmir.

Constitutionally, Pakistan began as a dominion under the Government of India Act of 1935, adapted by the Provisional Constitution Order. The Act gave the Governor-General broad powers, including the ability to dismiss provincial ministries and to legislate by ordinance. This mattered. Jinnah dismissed the Congress ministry of Dr. Khan Sahib in the North-West Frontier Province in August 1947 and later removed other provincial ministers. These precedents did not make authoritarian rule inevitable, but they established that the center could override provincial political majorities when it judged this necessary. The Constituent Assembly, elected indirectly in 1946 for undivided India, doubled as the legislature and was to draft the constitution. Its members from East Bengal were a majority, forty-four of its sixty-nine seats, though most of its leading members were from the Muslim minority provinces.

Historians have distinguished administrative weakness from the later emergence of authoritarian government. The civil service and army were not strong in the sense of possessing abundant resources. They were strong in comparison with other institutions: political parties that were thinly organized, a Muslim League that had to transform itself from a movement into a governing party, and provincial legislatures whose composition reflected the politics of 1946. In a state that lacked a settled constitution, the institutions that existed and functioned acquired disproportionate influence. This is the first of the chapter's themes, and the sections that follow trace how it developed.

Section 90.2

Muhammad Ali Jinnah: Founding Leadership, State Authority, and Competing Visions of Pakistan

Muhammad Ali Jinnah held, in 1947, an extraordinary concentration of formal and informal authority. He was Governor-General, president of the Constituent Assembly, and president of the Muslim League. By constitutional convention the Governor-General in a dominion was a largely ceremonial representative of the Crown, acting on the advice of ministers. Jinnah, however, chose to preside over cabinet meetings on occasion and to exercise personal supervision of major matters. The title Quaid-i-Azam, "great leader," reflected an authority that no one else in Pakistan could claim. His health was already poor. He suffered from tuberculosis and other illnesses that were known to few, and his period of leadership was brief.

In this role he faced immediate administrative tasks: selecting a capital and organizing a government, handling the refugee crisis, managing relations with princely states, and addressing the war in Kashmir. He worked closely with Liaquat Ali Khan, who served as Prime Minister and who handled day-to-day administration, with senior civil servants such as Chaudhri Muhammad Ali, and with provincial leaders whose independence he sometimes curbed. In 1948 he dismissed the Sindh ministry of Muhammad Ayub Khuhro, partly over the question of the separation of Karachi from Sindh. His decisions, like those in the Frontier, reinforced the pattern in which the Governor-General could act decisively against provincial governments.

The address that Jinnah delivered to the Constituent Assembly on 11 August 1947 has become the most debated text of Pakistan's founding. He spoke of the first duty of a government as maintaining law and order, and he condemned corruption, black marketing, and nepotism. He spoke at length about the communal violence of the preceding months, urging that the past be put behind and that all citizens regard themselves as equal members of one state. On religion, he said that people were free to go to their places of worship and that membership in any religion or caste "has nothing to do with the business of the State." He added that over time the religious differences would cease to matter in the political sense, as all became citizens of one state.

Primary Source Window 1

Jinnah's Address to the Constituent Assembly, 11 August 1947

Author: Muhammad Ali Jinnah, president of the Constituent Assembly · Date and place: 11 August 1947, Karachi · Issuing authority: Constituent Assembly of Pakistan · Provenance: Assembly proceedings and contemporary press; official transcripts and reports differ slightly in wording, and a well-known controversy concerns a period in which the speech was reportedly given reduced publicity.

Context: Delivered on the day after the Assembly's first meeting, as violence in Punjab and Bengal was intensifying, and before the formal transfer of power.

Short authenticated quotation: membership of any religion or caste "has nothing to do with the business of the State." Editorial paraphrase of the surrounding argument: Jinnah urged that Pakistan's citizens, whatever their community, be equal in rights and obligations, and that the old communal divisions would diminish in political life.

Analysis: The address is a primary source for Jinnah's public stance on citizenship at a specific moment. It is not a constitutional text, and it was delivered in the setting of an Assembly that had not yet begun drafting a constitution. Interpreters differ on its meaning. Some read it as a clear commitment to a state neutral on religion. Others read it as a pragmatic reassurance to minorities, consistent with other statements in which Jinnah invoked Islamic principles of justice and said that Pakistan's constitution would be democratic. Both sides cite his later speeches. The text does not by itself resolve what Jinnah would have supported in a drafted constitution.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the relationship between religion and the state?
  2. Who was the audience, and how might that have shaped Jinnah's emphasis?
  3. What can the speech reveal about Jinnah's position in August 1947?
  4. What can historians not establish from this speech alone about Pakistan's later constitutional identity?

The competing readings of this speech illustrate a wider problem. After Jinnah's death, different groups sought to claim his authority. Secular and liberal writers cited the 11 August address as the foundation of a plural state. Religious and conservative writers cited his references to Islamic principles and his earlier insistence that Muslims constituted a nation. Historians generally caution that Jinnah, a constitutional lawyer by training and a political tactician, made statements adapted to particular audiences and political situations. His own preferences for the details of an eventual constitution cannot be reconstructed with certainty. What can be said is that the founding leader left a record that supported more than one trajectory, and his successors had to decide which to follow.

Jinnah's relationship with Liaquat Ali Khan was close but not equal. Liaquat, a landholder from the United Provinces and the Muslim League's general secretary, ran the government and Muslim League organization, while Jinnah retained final authority. The arrangement worked while Jinnah lived. It produced a structure in which the Governor-General's office and the prime minister's office each retained the potential for conflict, and the later struggle between those offices (§90.10) was in this sense foreshadowed.

Jinnah visited Dhaka in March 1948, a visit that proved consequential for the language question (§90.5). He spent the summer of 1948 in Balochistan seeking to recover his health. Jinnah died in Karachi on 11 September 1948. The consequences were immediate. The state lost the one figure who could mediate among provinces, party factions, bureaucracy, and the army. Khawaja Nazimuddin, a Bengali, became Governor-General, while Liaquat Ali Khan continued as Prime Minister. The balance of authority shifted toward the prime minister for the next three years.

Historians should avoid two simplifications. One idealizes Jinnah as a founder whose vision, had he lived, would have prevented all later difficulty. The other holds Jinnah responsible for the centralizing and language policies that caused later conflict. Neither is satisfactory. Jinnah created a strong central executive in a context of emergency and held it in his own hands, which left no tested institutional alternative at his death. But the decisions that shaped the 1950s were taken by many people, and Pakistan's later difficulties cannot be reduced to one man's intentions.

Section 90.3

Refugees and State Formation: The Muhajirs, Karachi, and Post-Partition Reconstruction

Chapter 86 described the violence and displacement of Partition. This section examines how the arrival of millions of refugees shaped Pakistan's institutions and social coalitions. By the early 1950s several million Muslims had moved from India to Pakistan, the great majority to West Pakistan. The largest movements involved the exchange of populations across Punjab, where Muslim refugees from East Punjab settled in West Punjab districts that had been vacated by Hindus and Sikhs. The social effects in Punjab were substantial but the language, religion, and agricultural background of the migrants overlapped with those of the host population. The province absorbed the migrants in ways that largely preserved its social structure.

Sindh and its capital city experienced something different. Hundreds of thousands of Muslim migrants, many of them Urdu-speaking professionals, clerks, traders, and artisans from the cities of Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Bihar, and other regions, arrived in Karachi, Hyderabad, and other Sindhi towns. Karachi's population multiplied within a few years. At the same time many Hindu Sindhis, who had played a large role in commerce and the professions, left for India. The newcomers were commonly called muhajirs, from the Arabic term for those who migrate, and the label often implied Urdu-speaking migrants, though refugees also included Punjabi, Gujarati, Bihari, and Bengali-speaking groups, among others. Muhajirs were not a single community. They differed by region of origin, class, and occupation. But in Sindh, they created urban majorities in important cities where Sindhis felt outnumbered.

The government created a refugee administration, ministries and rehabilitation departments, and a system for allocating evacuee property, that is, land, houses, and businesses left by those who had gone to India. Claims were complicated and evidence was often poor. The allocation of property produced opportunities for enrichment and corruption, and it benefited well-placed claimants, including bureaucrats, politicians, and entrepreneurs. These patterns contributed to the formation of an urban commercial and professional class and enlarged landholdings in some rural areas. The new federal bureaucracy recruited many muhajirs, who brought education and administrative experience at a time when Pakistan lacked trained personnel. The civil services, the professions, and commerce therefore had a heavy Urdu-speaking and Punjabi presence in the early years.

The social consequences interacted with language politics. Urdu had been the language of Muslim political culture in northern India, and its elevation to the status of national language fitted the preferences of many migrants and of the Muslim League leadership. For Sindhis, the loss of Karachi as part of Sindh and the arrival of numerous migrants raised fears about employment, land, and cultural identity. For Bengalis, the concentration of the federal bureaucracy in Karachi, staffed largely by Urdu-speaking officials, meant that the state apparatus appeared distant and unrepresentative. The refugees themselves were not responsible for these political outcomes, many were destitute and traumatized, and their numbers made humanitarian demands that no administration could easily meet. But the way the state used and settled them shaped coalitions that would affect its politics for decades.

Section 90.4

Liaquat Ali Khan and the Early Civilian Government: Administration, Security, and Political Instability

Liaquat Ali Khan served as Pakistan's first Prime Minister from August 1947 until his death. He had been Jinnah's chief lieutenant and a skilled administrator, and after Jinnah's death he became the dominant political figure. The Muslim League was the governing party, but it faced the transition from a nationalist movement to a party of government. Its organization in the provinces that formed Pakistan was weak, its members disagreed over policy, and its leadership was drawn partly from migrants who lacked provincial political bases. Rather than develop competitive party organization, the leadership tended to treat opposition as disloyalty, and several provincial political figures were removed under special powers, including Khuhro and others, through measures such as the Public and Representative Offices (Disqualification) Act of 1949.

Security shaped the government's agenda. The war over Kashmir (Chapter 87) ended in a ceasefire on 1 January 1949, with Pakistan holding part of the territory and India holding the larger part. The conflict left lasting hostility and kept defense expenditure high. In 1950 Pakistan and India signed the Nehru–Liaquat Pact on 8 April, committing both governments to protect minorities and to establish minority commissions after the communal disturbances in East and West Bengal. The pact eased tensions in the short term but did not end migration, and the status of Hindus in East Bengal remained a concern. Financial pressure was intense. The trade dispute with India in 1949–1950, after Pakistan, unlike India, declined to devalue its currency following the sterling devaluation of 1949, disrupted exports, though the Korean War boom later raised the prices of jute and cotton and improved Pakistan's finances.

One of the most revealing episodes was the Rawalpindi Conspiracy of 1951. In March, the government announced that it had discovered a plot by a group of army officers, led by Major-General Akbar Khan, and several civilians, including the poet Faiz Ahmad Faiz and communist activists, to overthrow the government. The details remain disputed. Some accounts say the officers were frustrated by the outcome of the Kashmir war and by the government's policies, while others argue the plot was less advanced than the government claimed. A special tribunal tried the accused, and they received prison terms. The episode demonstrated both the army's potential for political involvement and the government's willingness to use security laws against political opponents, and it contributed to a ban on the Communist Party in 1954.

Liaquat was assassinated on 16 October 1951 at a public meeting in Rawalpindi. The assassin, Said Akbar, was shot at once, and the motive and any conspiracy behind the killing have never been fully established. Official inquiries were limited and the question remains debated. Liaquat's death removed the last of the senior leaders of the founding generation. Khawaja Nazimuddin, then Governor-General, moved to become Prime Minister, and Ghulam Muhammad, a former finance minister and a senior civil servant, became Governor-General.

This reorganization carried significance. The early political system depended heavily on individuals and senior administrative institutions rather than a stable electoral framework. No general election had been held, the Assembly had been elected before independence and no longer reflected popular opinion, and the Muslim League had not tested its support in East Bengal. Provincial elections held in Punjab in 1951 and in the Frontier and Sindh soon afterward were marked by allegations of manipulation. The new Governor-General, Ghulam Muhammad, was a bureaucrat with strong views and no electoral base, and he was determined to use the office's powers. Nazimuddin, a mild and honorable politician, held the premiership but lacked a political machine. In 1953, as Section 90.10 will show, the Governor-General dismissed him despite his retaining the Assembly's confidence. Liaquat's death therefore marked a turning point. After it, civilian government increasingly rested on administrators and on the army's tacit support, not on parties with deep organizational roots.

It would be wrong to portray Liaquat's period as only failure. His government managed the early years of the refugee crisis, created a functioning central administration, secured Pakistan's early finances, and introduced the Objectives Resolution (§90.9). But it also left questions unanswered: how the Assembly would be elected, how power would be shared between the wings, and what the constitution would say about language and religion. These questions occupy the remainder of the chapter.

Section 90.5

Urdu, Bengali, and the Language Question: The Origins of the Bengali Language Movement

The question of the state language was raised almost as soon as Pakistan existed. Urdu was the first language of only a small minority of Pakistanis, but it had a prominent history in Muslim political and literary culture in northern India, it was used by many of the Muslim League's leaders, and it was widely understood as a lingua franca in parts of the west. For many leaders of the Pakistan movement it served as a symbol of Muslim identity, and it seemed a practical means of uniting a state whose regions spoke different languages. Bengali, by contrast, was the mother tongue of the majority of the population, with a rich literature and a strong tradition of use in education, journalism, and political life. Bengali Muslims had also supported the Pakistan movement in large numbers, and few of them imagined that their language would be excluded from public life in their own state.

Early signs of tension appeared in 1947. Bengali intellectuals and students, including the educator Abul Kashem, who founded the cultural organization Tamaddun Majlis in September 1947, argued that Bengali should be an official language. Currency notes, postal stamps, and recruitment examinations that used only Urdu and English increased concern. The question for Bengalis was not simply symbolic. The civil service and federal employment depended on examinations in languages in which Bengali candidates were less proficient. In a state where government employment was one of the few paths to social advancement, language policy had direct consequences for career prospects.

The matter reached the Constituent Assembly in February 1948. Dhirendranath Datta, a Bengali Hindu member from Comilla and a Congress representative, proposed an amendment to the Assembly's rules to allow Bengali to be used in its proceedings alongside Urdu and English. The motion was modest. It did not ask that Bengali replace the other languages. Liaquat Ali Khan opposed it, arguing that Pakistan was a Muslim state whose people required a common language, which in his view could only be Urdu. Other speakers also spoke against the amendment. Some Bengali Muslim members, including Khawaja Nazimuddin, spoke in ways that conveyed reluctance to oppose the leadership. The motion was rejected, and in East Bengal the Assembly's decision was widely reported and widely resented.

He spoke strongly in favor of Urdu as the state language, telling the crowd that it would be "Urdu and no other language," while also stating that the provincial government could choose the official language of the province. He characterized agitation on the issue as the work of agents of disruption. The audience at the convocation reportedly protested. Historians differ about how to weigh the statements. For the leadership in Karachi, a common language was a means of building a nation out of different peoples. For many Bengalis, the demand for recognition was an expression of political equality and an argument that a majority should not be treated as a regional minority. The mismatch was not about religion, since both communities were largely Muslim. It was about what kind of nation Pakistan would be: one defined by a single cultural form, or one that recognized its several languages. The question remained unresolved until 1952.

Section 90.6

The Language Movement of 1952: Student Protest, State Repression, and Political Identity

The language question returned in January 1952, when Khawaja Nazimuddin, now Prime Minister, visited Dhaka and stated on 27 January that Urdu would be the state language of Pakistan. The statement was understood as a rejection of the 1948 agreement. Protest followed quickly. On 31 January an all-party committee of action was formed in Dhaka, with Maulana Bhashani presiding. It included politicians, students, teachers, and cultural activists, and it called for a general strike and demonstrations on 21 February. The provincial government, led by Chief Minister Nurul Amin, responded by imposing Section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which banned assemblies of more than a few persons, in Dhaka from 20 February.

On 21 February, students met at the University of Dhaka and debated whether to defy the order. After deliberations, in which some leaders argued for compliance and many students argued for defiance, groups of students began to leave the campus in small processions. The police used tear gas, and then fired on crowds near the Dhaka Medical College. Several protesters died that day and the next. Accounts name Abul Barkat, Rafiquddin Ahmed, Abdul Jabbar, and others, and the exact total is disputed, with some estimates higher than official counts. The deaths provoked further demonstrations, and further shootings, including on 22 February. A temporary memorial, the Shaheed Minar, was erected overnight on 23–24 February, and it was demolished by the police a few days later. It was later rebuilt, and a permanent monument was constructed in subsequent years.

The movement developed beyond language policy into a challenge to unequal political power. It brought together students, teachers, cultural organizations, journalists, and opposition politicians, and no single party can claim exclusive leadership. The Awami Muslim League, the Youth League, the student organizations, the Tamaddun Majlis, and many individuals contributed. They also gave the opposition a cause that linked language, democracy, and regional rights.

Recognition came in stages, and students should distinguish them. After the 1952 events, the central leadership continued to resist, and Bengali was not recognized immediately. The United Front's victory in the 1954 provincial election (§90.7) brought political concessions: in May 1954 the Constituent Assembly adopted a provision recognizing Bengali as one of the state languages, and the new provincial government declared 21 February a holiday and committed to building a permanent memorial. The Constitution of 1956 then provided in its text that Urdu and Bengali would be the state languages of Pakistan. English was to continue in official use for a transitional period. In later decades the date became an international observance when UNESCO proclaimed 21 February as International Mother Language Day in 1999, observed from 2000. In the 1950s, however, the lasting effect was political. The movement showed that East Bengal's politics could be mobilized around cultural and democratic demands, and that a state that treated a majority's language as secondary risked creating a persistent grievance.

The campus confrontation was not a spontaneous reaction to one day's ban. The language campaign had developed organizational continuity through student meetings, publicity, committees, and contacts between universities and opposition groups. Participation carried different risks for different actors. University students might face expulsion or arrest; workers and shopkeepers could lose wages by joining a stoppage; parents feared that demonstrations would expose their children to police violence. The prohibition on assembly turned a dispute about the language of the state into a demonstration of who could claim the right to assemble and speak in public. The shootings sharpened that connection between cultural recognition and civil liberty.

The memorialization of the dead was itself a political activity. Lists of those killed varied among contemporary newspapers, witnesses and later commemorations, so a single undifferentiated death toll would conceal changes in the date range and evidence being counted. Funeral processions, poetry, commemorative gatherings and the rebuilding of memorials converted personal grief into a common civic language. Such activities did not erase internal disagreements over religion, economic policy or the proper degree of provincial autonomy. They did, however, furnish networks and symbols that could be used in the next provincial election. This helps explain the movement's consequences without imagining a unified Bengali political program already formed in 1952.

Section 90.7

The 1954 East Bengal Election: United Front Victory and the Demand for Provincial Autonomy

East Bengal's politics had been dominated since independence by the Muslim League, but the party's popular support weakened as dissatisfaction grew. In June 1949 a group of dissident Muslim Leaguers, including Maulana Bhashani, Shamsul Huq, and the young Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, formed the Awami Muslim League in Dhaka. It positioned itself as an opposition party concerned with provincial rights and democratic politics. H. S. Suhrawardy later became its principal national leader. The party dropped "Muslim" from its name in 1955, reflecting an effort to appeal to non-Muslim voters and to a broader secular platform. The Krishak Sramik Party, led by A. K. Fazlul Huq, drew on the peasant-based politics that Huq had led in undivided Bengal. Other groups, including the Ganatantri Dal and Nizam-i-Islam, joined the opposition alliance.

Provincial elections were repeatedly postponed. When they were held in March 1954, the opposition parties formed a coalition called the United Front, with a joint program known as the Twenty-One Points. Its program included recognition of Bengali as a state language, the release of political prisoners, the building of a memorial to the language martyrs and the observance of 21 February, the abolition of zamindari without compensation, measures to support jute growers and improve the position of cultivators, expanded education, and a demand for provincial autonomy on the basis of the 1940 Lahore Resolution, with the central government limited to defense, foreign affairs, and currency. It also called for a naval headquarters in East Bengal.

Section 90.8

Population versus Parity: East–West Representation and the One Unit Scheme

From the beginning of constitutional debate, the central question was how to represent two unequal wings. East Bengal contained a majority of Pakistan's people, a little more than 55 percent in the 1951 census and a little over 54 percent in 1961. A parliament elected by population would therefore have a Bengali majority. Leaders in West Pakistan, including the Punjabi landed and bureaucratic elites that dominated the army and civil service, feared permanent domination by a single province. They argued that the western wing, comprising several provinces with different interests, needed protection and that the two wings, which were separated geographically and differed in economy and culture, should be represented on equal terms. Bengali leaders replied that representation by population was the democratic principle and that formal parity would deprive a majority of its rights.

Successive constitutional proposals tried to find a middle path. The Basic Principles Committee's reports of 1950 and 1952 proposed different formulas. The 1952 report, which proposed a bicameral legislature with a combination of population-based and equal representation, was resisted by Bengalis. In 1953, Prime Minister Mohammad Ali Bogra, a Bengali politician who had been brought in from outside the party leadership after Nazimuddin's dismissal, offered a compromise, known as the Bogra Formula. It proposed a bicameral legislature, with a lower house of 300 members distributed by population (East Bengal 165 seats) and an upper house of 50 members with ten seats for each of five units, including East Bengal, and with the two houses having equal powers on important questions. The formula allocated 135 lower-house seats and 40 upper-house seats to the western units, compared with East Bengal's 165 and 10. In a combined sitting that would produce exactly 175 seats for each wing, so the eastern numerical majority in the lower house would not become a majority in the joint legislature. The proposals also contained cross-wing concurrence requirements on specified decisions. This was a designed compromise, not population-based representation without qualification.

Historical Vocabulary
Parity and One Unit

Parity refers to the principle that East and West Pakistan would have equal numbers of seats in the national legislature regardless of population. It was adopted in the Constitution of 1956. One Unit refers to the merger of the provinces and princely states of western Pakistan into a single province of West Pakistan, so that two provinces, East and West, could be set against each other in the parity formula.

These terms matter because they reveal that the representation debate was less about numbers than about who would govern. Parity secured the west against a Bengali majority, while One Unit reduced the number of units in the west so that the equal division was administratively simple. Modern readers should not assume parity means fairness for all groups. It was fair between wings but not within them.

The One Unit scheme was announced in November 1954 and carried into law as the Establishment of West Pakistan Act in September 1955, taking effect on 14 October 1955. It merged Punjab, Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province, Balochistan, Bahawalpur, Khairpur, and other specified territories into the province of West Pakistan, subject to important statutory exceptions and distinct arrangements in certain tribal and special areas, with Lahore as its capital. Its proponents argued that it would reduce administrative costs, prevent provincial rivalries, and create a corresponding western administrative unit for purposes of the parity scheme. Critics gave several objections. Punjab, which contained a majority of western Pakistan's population, would dominate the unit. Sindhis, Pashtuns, and Baloch feared the loss of their separate provincial identities and the dilution of their political voice. The Frontier leader Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan and Sindhi nationalists such as G. M. Syed opposed the scheme, and Baloch leaders and the Khan of Kalat resented the loss of the separate status of their territories. Its implementation in some places involved pressure and the removal of opponents. The scheme was therefore opposed in both wings, though for different reasons.

The larger question was whether administrative centralization and formal parity could produce national unity when population distribution and provincial identities remained unequal. One Unit created a symmetrical structure on paper but did so by suppressing differences within the west. It was a device for managing the Bengali majority and not a solution to the problem of representation. Its eventual cancellation in 1970 (Chapter 92) acknowledged this, and its legacy of resentment among smaller western provinces would affect politics well beyond the period of this chapter.

Parity was not merely an abstract preference for equality among regions. It determined whether legislators from the population majority could enact ordinary laws, authorize expenditure, choose a head of state, or amend a constitution without consent from representatives of the western wing. The Bogra proposal attempted to balance population in one chamber with regional units in another, and it added concurrence rules designed to prevent one wing from overriding the other. But proposals for parity were evaluated differently by different western politicians: many Sindhi and Pashtun leaders feared that a western bloc administered from Lahore would obscure their own minority positions within that wing. Thus there were multiple minority questions, not a simple choice between an undifferentiated east and an undifferentiated west.

Administrative design had practical consequences. Merging western provinces could centralize decisions on recruitment, budgeting, schooling, policing and the recognition of regional languages even when some special or tribal jurisdictions retained distinctive arrangements. A Sindhi civil servant or Pashtun political organizer might experience One Unit as a reduction in local access to power while a central planner described it as a simplification of government. Nor did equal wing representation mean identical treatment of their inhabitants: East Bengal's larger population made each national legislative seat represent more people than a western seat. Those differences help explain why a constitutional settlement apparently balanced on paper could generate opposition in both wings.

Section 90.9

The Objectives Resolution of 1949: Islam, Sovereignty, and Constitutional Identity

The Objectives Resolution was introduced in the Constituent Assembly by Liaquat Ali Khan on 7 March 1949 and adopted on 12 March. It was intended to set out the principles on which the future constitution would be based, and it has remained a reference point in Pakistani constitutional debate. The resolution declared that sovereignty over the universe belongs to God and that the authority He has delegated to the state of Pakistan, to be exercised through its people's chosen representatives within limits He prescribed, is "a sacred trust." It stated that the state would exercise its powers through elected representatives, that principles of democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance, and social justice as enunciated by Islam would be fully observed, and that Muslims would be enabled to order their lives in accordance with Islamic teachings. It also provided that adequate provision would be made for minorities to profess and practice their religions and develop their cultures, that fundamental rights would be guaranteed, and that the judiciary would be independent. The text provided for a federal structure with autonomous units.

The debate was extended and significant. Liaquat presented the resolution as the most important measure since independence and as an assertion of the nation's ideals. Religious scholars, among them Maulana Shabbir Ahmad Usmani, welcomed the principle of divine sovereignty and urged that Islamic law have a central place. Several Muslim members questioned the drafting or the way the resolution should be interpreted. Hindu members of the Assembly, including Sris Chandra Chattopadhyaya and Bhupendra Kumar Datta, objected strongly. They argued that the reference to divine sovereignty and Islamic principles would make non-Muslims second-class citizens, that the resolution contradicted the spirit of the 11 August speech, and that the constitution should not prescribe a religious basis for the state. Several argued that the vote was being rushed and that amendments should be considered. The resolution was adopted over their opposition, and the non-Muslim members voted against it.

The resolution is ambiguous, and that ambiguity has been important. It combined two sources of authority, divine sovereignty and popular representation, without specifying how they would relate. It promised democracy and minority rights while committing the state to Islamic principles, and it did not state how disagreements between these would be resolved. Different groups read it differently. Some emphasized the democratic and rights-based language, regarding the reference to God as a statement of moral foundation. Others read it as a basis for an Islamic state in which legislation would conform to religious law. Later governments invoked it in various ways. It became the preamble of the 1956 Constitution, was later made a substantive part of the constitutional text in 1985, and remains cited in judicial and political argument. These are later developments, and the 1949 text should not be read as if it already contained them.

The debate shows why the relationship between Islam, representative sovereignty, minority protections, and federal authority was contested from the beginning. It also shows that the Assembly was not representative of the people it governed. Its Muslim members were elected in 1946 under a limited franchise, East Bengal's non-Muslim members argued with a sense of vulnerability, and Bengali Muslim members were divided. The Objectives Resolution was therefore not only a statement of principles. It was also a moment in which the question of who belonged to the political community was raised and answered by a body whose composition and mandate were open to question.

Section 90.10

The First Constituent Assembly, the 1954 Dissolution, and the Constitutional Crisis

Constitution-making was slow. The Assembly created a Basic Principles Committee in 1949, which presented an interim report in September 1950. The report met strong criticism in East Bengal, particularly for its provisions on representation and its silence on Bengali, and it was withdrawn. A second report in December 1952 followed, introduced by Khawaja Nazimuddin. Its proposal for a bicameral legislature with equal powers and parity in the upper house aroused opposition from Bengali leaders. Other disputes concerned the role of Islam, the powers of the center, and the status of the head of state. The disagreement within the Assembly was only one source of delay. Another was the Governor-General's relationship to it. Ghulam Muhammad, who became Governor-General in 1951, took the view that the Assembly was incapable of agreement and that he and the bureaucracy were better placed to decide.

Two further crises showed the patterns. In early 1953, agitation against the Ahmadiyya community, directed at its religious status and demanding the removal of Ahmadi officials, including the foreign minister Zafrulla Khan, led to riots in Lahore and other Punjab cities. The army was called in and martial law was declared in Lahore in March 1953, a major early precedent for military authority over civilian administration in Pakistan. A judicial inquiry led by Justice Muhammad Munir and Justice M. R. Kayani reported in 1954, criticizing the politicians and religious leaders involved and raising questions about the nature of an Islamic state. The episode demonstrated the army's potential role in civil affairs and the fragility of civil order.

Second, on 17 April 1953, Ghulam Muhammad dismissed Nazimuddin's government even though it had recently passed its budget with a large majority in the Assembly. The Governor-General appointed Mohammad Ali Bogra, then Pakistan's ambassador in Washington, as Prime Minister. The action showed that the constitutional convention that a prime minister with a legislative majority could not be removed by the head of state had been disregarded. It also showed how the Governor-General, the civil service, and the army chief, Ayub Khan, who was brought into the cabinet as defense minister in October 1954, had become key players.

In September 1954 the Assembly, whose Muslim League majority was weakening after the East Bengal election, passed amendments to the Government of India Act, curbing the Governor-General's powers to dismiss ministers and moving toward the completion of a constitution. On 24 October 1954, Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the Constituent Assembly, declaring that it had lost the confidence of the people and could not function. He formed a reconstituted cabinet of "talents" that included Ayub Khan and Iskander Mirza. The dissolution was a decisive act: it ended the body that had been working on the constitution for seven years and replaced it, for the time being, with executive authority.

The Speaker, Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan, challenged the dissolution in the Chief Court of Sindh. In February 1955 the court, in a judgment by Chief Justice George Constantine, held that the Governor-General had no power to dissolve the Assembly and that the relevant provisions did not require his assent. The government appealed to the Federal Court. On 21 March 1955 the Federal Court, led by Chief Justice Muhammad Munir, reversed the decision by a majority, with Justice A. R. Cornelius dissenting. The majority's ruling was based on a technical ground. It held that the 1954 amendment which had given the Chief Court jurisdiction over such petitions (section 223A) had not received the Governor-General's assent, and therefore did not have the force of law. The court did not deliver a broad doctrine of executive supremacy, though the practical effect was to leave the dissolution in place. Cornelius argued in dissent that the Governor-General's assent was not required for constitutional measures of this sort and that the Assembly's status as a sovereign body should be upheld.

Section 90.11

The Constitution of 1956: The Islamic Republic, Parliamentary Government, and Federal Parity

A second Constituent Assembly was established in mid-1955, elected indirectly by the provincial assemblies. Its membership reflected the new political landscape: the Muslim League no longer held a clear majority, the United Front's heirs and other parties were represented, and equal numbers of members came from East and West Pakistan, which was consistent with the parity principle. Chaudhri Muhammad Ali, a senior civil servant who had become Prime Minister in August 1955, led a coalition of the Muslim League and United Front elements. After intense negotiation, the drafting committee produced a constitution, which the Assembly passed on 29 February 1956. Iskander Mirza, who had become Governor-General in October 1955, gave assent on 2 March, and the Constitution came into force on 23 March 1956, which was observed as Republic Day. Mirza became the first President.

The Constitution declared Pakistan an Islamic Republic. It established parliamentary cabinet government in which the Prime Minister and cabinet were responsible to a unicameral National Assembly, and a President, who had to be a Muslim, served as head of state. The National Assembly was to have 300 general seats divided equally between East and West Pakistan, 150 each, with ten additional reserved seats for women. It established a federal structure with federal, provincial, and concurrent lists of legislative powers, and a Supreme Court. Fundamental rights were included and were made enforceable, subject to certain restrictions, as were directive principles of state policy. On Islam, the Constitution provided that no law should be enacted repugnant to the injunctions of Islam as laid down in the Quran and Sunnah, and that existing laws should be brought into conformity with them, with a commission to recommend measures. The Objectives Resolution formed its preamble. Urdu and Bengali were recognized as state languages, with English to continue for official use for twenty years.

Students should understand both the achievement and the limits. Pakistan had finally produced a constitution after almost nine years, through negotiation that reached compromise on two of the most divisive questions, language and the composition of the legislature. The Constitution was therefore a genuine accomplishment. But it was accepted only partially. Bengali leaders, including Suhrawardy and Fazlul Huq, argued that parity denied the majority its due and that provincial autonomy was inadequate. Many Awami League members continued to press for autonomy and joint electorates. In the west, supporters of the pre-One Unit provinces resented the merger. Religious parties argued that the Islamic provisions were insufficient, while others thought they went too far. The Constitution also left the president with significant reserve powers, including the ability to dismiss ministers in certain circumstances and to declare emergencies, and it was brought into force without a general election. Its test was whether the new institutions would work, and the next two years (§90.12) answered that question.

The structure had consequences for future political competition. A general electorate in East Pakistan could not by itself confer a majority in a National Assembly where each wing held the same number of seats. Nor would the formal presence of fundamental rights automatically settle disputes about preventive detention, executive influence or provincial finance. The new Constitution created procedures through which these disputes might be argued, but their operation depended on the willingness of rulers and party leaders to accept electoral results and constitutional limits. That willingness was never tested through a nationwide general election under the document. Its premature termination in 1958 means that historians can evaluate its institutional design more fully than its possible performance under ordinary electoral conditions.

Section 90.12

The Failure of Parliamentary Government, 1956–1958: Political Parties, Presidents, and Executive Authority

After 1956 the National Assembly was filled with shifting coalitions. Chaudhri Muhammad Ali, whose government oversaw the Constitution's adoption, resigned in September 1956 after the Muslim League split. H. S. Suhrawardy of the Awami League became Prime Minister, leading a coalition with the Republican Party, a new party of West Pakistan politicians created in November 1956 under Iskander Mirza's sponsorship and led by Dr. Khan Sahib, the former Frontier leader. Suhrawardy's government lasted until October 1957. It sought to continue foreign policy ties with the United States and to maintain a working coalition, but it faced disagreements over One Unit, joint versus separate electorates, and the allocation of resources between the wings. Mirza reportedly pressured Suhrawardy to resign, and he did so in October 1957. I. I. Chundrigar of the Muslim League formed a short-lived coalition lasting about two months. In December 1957 Feroz Khan Noon of the Republican Party took office and held it until October 1958.

Provincial politics in both wings experienced similar instability. Governments rose and fell in West Pakistan, where Dr. Khan Sahib was chief minister of the unified province until 1957, when he was replaced. In East Pakistan, chief ministers changed repeatedly, and central rule was imposed again in 1958 in the context of a political crisis. In September 1958, violence broke out in the East Pakistan Assembly, and the Deputy Speaker, Shahid Ali, was struck and later died. The incident was widely reported as evidence of the breakdown of parliamentary conduct, and it was used to argue that the system could not work. Historians note, however, that in practice the instability reflected the competitive struggle for power under a constitution whose institutions had not yet matured.

Iskander Mirza, who had been the Governor-General and then President, played a central role. He involved himself in the formation and dismissal of cabinets, cultivated politicians whose loyalty he valued, and created a party of his own to support his position. His relationship with the army chief, Ayub Khan, and with senior bureaucrats gave him an alternative base of power. The weakening of the parliamentary system owed much to this intervention. Governments were not allowed to settle into stable routines, and politicians learned that ministries depended on the President's approval. The first nationwide general election under the 1956 Constitution had been promised and was scheduled for early 1959, but it had not taken place when the regime was overthrown. It is a central question whether elections held on time might have changed the pattern.

Historical Debate 1

Why Did Pakistan's Parliamentary System Collapse in 1958?

The question: Was the end of parliamentary government in 1958 chiefly the result of weak political parties and unstable coalitions, or of the accumulated power and ambitions of the bureaucracy, the president, and the army?

Interpretation A — Weakness of parties and politicians: The Muslim League's fragmentation, the personalization of politics, frequent defections, the lack of party organization in the provinces, and the absence of national elections prevented the formation of stable majorities. On this view, the system failed because the politicians failed, and military intervention reflected a breakdown that already existed. Ayub Khan and Mirza themselves made this argument in 1958.
Interpretation B — Institutional power of the bureaucracy, the executive, and the army: The colonial-era administrative apparatus retained power from the beginning, and senior civil servants and soldiers held the key offices. The Governor-General's dismissals of 1953 and 1954 and Mirza's intervention after 1956 undermined parliamentary government from above. The army's growing share of resources and its alliance with the United States strengthened its autonomy. On this view, the system was not allowed to work, and instability was in part produced by the executive.

Evidence: Both draw on the same record of cabinets, party splits, court decisions, and memoirs. Interpretation A emphasizes ministerial turnover and the Assembly's disorders, Interpretation B emphasizes the specific interventions of 1953, 1954, and 1956–1958, and the fact that the 1959 election was not held. Some scholars add the security environment and the contested question of parity as contributing factors.

Why it matters: If the politicians were chiefly to blame, military rule may appear a response to failure. If executive and bureaucratic interference were central, the takeover appears as a culmination of earlier choices. Students should distinguish the causes of instability from the arguments later used to justify intervention. The record does not show that instability required military rule.

Student task: Identify three pieces of evidence for each interpretation and explain whether the interpretations are mutually exclusive or complementary.

Events moved quickly in October 1958. Elections were approaching, and Mirza, who feared that the polls would produce a government he could not control, and Ayub Khan, whose own interests and views of national security were shaped by the army's role, found common ground. On 7 October 1958, Mirza abrogated the 1956 Constitution, dissolved the central and provincial legislatures, dismissed the cabinets, banned political parties, and proclaimed martial law, appointing General Muhammad Ayub Khan as Chief Martial Law Administrator. The next section reconstructs what followed. The narrative must not portray this as a constitutional necessity simply because parliamentary government had experienced instability. Instability is a feature of many young parliamentary systems, and in Pakistan the decision to end the system was made by a president and an army chief acting outside the constitutional order.

Military-Bureaucratic Rule under Ayub Khan

Pakistan's first sustained military regime: its political institutions, development programs, and eventual breakdown.

Section 90.13

The Military Takeover of 1958: Iskander Mirza, Ayub Khan, and the Consolidation of Power

The sequence of October 1958 must be kept precise. On 7 October, President Iskander Mirza issued the proclamation that abrogated the Constitution, dissolved the legislatures, abolished political parties, and imposed martial law, naming Ayub Khan as Chief Martial Law Administrator. Mirza, not Ayub, was the proclaimer. In the days that followed Ayub was named Prime Minister of a cabinet that included generals and civil servants, along with a few civilians such as Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, who entered the government as a young minister. The relationship between the two men was strained. Mirza apparently hoped to remain as a civilian president backed by the army, while Ayub wanted the army's supremacy. On the night of 27 October, a group of generals visited Mirza and obtained his resignation. Ayub then assumed the presidency, and Mirza was sent into exile in London, where he lived the remainder of his life.

Several groups played roles. Military commanders, including generals such as Azam Khan and Burki, supported the move and the arrest of Mirza. Senior civil servants, who had long worked with the army and who would staff the new administration, largely welcomed an arrangement that restored their authority. Political parties, banned from activity, were not consulted. The judiciary soon had to decide whether the new order was legal. Many politicians were arrested or disqualified. In 1959 the regime issued the Elective Bodies Disqualification Order (EBDO), which allowed tribunals to bar politicians found guilty of misconduct from public office for a number of years, and the Public Offices Disqualification Order (PODO). The press was brought under control by the Press and Publications Ordinance and the takeover of the Progressive Papers group, which had included the newspaper Pakistan Times. Land reforms, administrative reforms, and anti-corruption measures were announced to give the regime a reformist character.

Historical Vocabulary
Doctrine of Necessity and Revolutionary Legality

The doctrine of necessity holds that acts which would otherwise be unconstitutional may be validated when circumstances require them to preserve the state or the legal order. Revolutionary legality, drawing on the jurist Hans Kelsen's theory, treats a successful revolution as creating a new legal order whose validity the courts may recognize.

In Pakistan the doctrine of necessity was applied to validate executive acts in 1955, and the Dosso case of 1958 recognized the martial-law regime on the basis of revolutionary legality. The two ideas are related but distinct, and it would be inaccurate to say that the doctrine of necessity was invented in Dosso. Both later became central to critiques of Pakistan's judiciary.

The judicial question was decided in State v. Dosso, a case that had begun as a challenge to a regulation applied to frontier tribal areas. The Supreme Court of Pakistan, headed by Chief Justice Muhammad Munir, held that the abrogation of the Constitution had been a successful revolution and that the new regime was therefore the valid source of law, drawing on Kelsen's concept of a "grundnorm." The ruling gave legal recognition to the martial-law regime and suggested that a successful seizure of power was itself a source of legal authority. Critics have called it a surrender of judicial independence, and the Supreme Court overruled it in 1972 in Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab. The recognition of necessity in 1955 and of revolutionary legality in 1958 together formed a pattern. Courts accepted the executive's reading of necessity and thereby did not constrain it.

Martial law altered the relationship among law, political representation, and executive power. Under it, the Constitution was replaced by a Laws (Continuance in Force) Order that preserved most existing laws but subordinated them to martial-law regulations, and military courts could try offenses. Political activity was restricted. The legislature that might have checked the executive was gone, and the courts deferred. Executive power came to be concentrated in a president backed by the army and the civil service, a structure that Chapter 91 and later chapters will contrast with other South Asian experiences. Initially the regime enjoyed some public acceptance, particularly in cities, where many were weary of political instability and welcomed anti-corruption and anti-hoarding measures. This should not be mistaken for consent to a permanent change of system. Many politicians, notably in East Pakistan, regarded the move as the end of the hope for representative government.

The Supreme Court judgment in State v. Dosso must be read in the context of the Constitution's destruction. A judicial finding that an effective revolutionary regime supplied a new legal order differed from finding that the coup complied with the Constitution it had displaced. The judgment's use of a legal theory associated with Hans Kelsen gave the military regime a powerful form of recognition, but its jurisprudential rationale was contested and would later be reconsidered in Pakistan's constitutional history. Describing the court accurately matters: the Federal Court heard the 1955 Tamizuddin dispute, while the Supreme Court decided Dosso on 27 October 1958.

Section 90.14

Basic Democracies and the Constitution of 1962: Controlled Political Participation

Ayub Khan set about designing a political system that would, in his view, suit Pakistan's conditions. He regarded Western-style parliamentary democracy as unsuited to a society with low literacy and with factional politics, and he favored a system with a strong executive and participation at the local level. In October 1959 the regime introduced the Basic Democracies Order. It created a four-tier system of local councils: union councils in rural areas and town or union committees in towns, then tehsil or thana councils, district councils, and divisional councils. About 80,000 members, 40,000 from each wing, were elected on a non-party basis to the lowest tier, and these members, called Basic Democrats, formed both a local administrative structure and an electoral college for higher offices. The councils were given functions in local development, such as rural works, and were closely supervised by the civil service.

Historical Vocabulary
Basic Democracies

Basic Democracies was the name given to the system of tiered local councils introduced in 1959 and used as an electoral college under the 1962 Constitution. Voters elected local representatives, and those representatives, a few tens of thousands in number, elected the president, national assembly members, and provincial assembly members.

It is an example of indirect and controlled participation. It widened local involvement in administration and development work, but it replaced direct election with a small electorate that was susceptible to influence by officials and patrons, and it kept party politics out of the lowest tier. The system's reach should be distinguished from universal adult franchise, which was not used for national offices.

In February 1960 a referendum was held in which the Basic Democrats were asked whether they had confidence in Ayub Khan as president. The result, reported as an overwhelming majority in his favor, gave him plebiscitary legitimacy for the next phase. A Constitution Commission under Justice Muhammad Shahabuddin, a former Federal Court judge, was appointed to recommend a constitution. Its report favored a more representative system, but Ayub and his advisers drafted a different one. The Constitution of 1962 was promulgated on 1 March 1962 and came into force on 8 June 1962, when martial law was lifted.

The 1962 Constitution established a presidential system. The president was head of state and head of government, elected indirectly by the Basic Democrats, and could appoint ministers who were not members of the legislature. The National Assembly was unicameral, with 156 seats, including six reserved for women, evenly divided between the two provinces, and elected by the Basic Democrats. The president could return bills to the legislature and had substantial emergency powers. The original text contained a list of principles of policy but did not include enforceable fundamental rights; fundamental rights were restored by the measure styled the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1963, passed in December 1963 and given presidential assent on 10 January 1964. The name of the state was initially given as the Republic of Pakistan, with the word "Islamic" restored by that amendment. An Advisory Council of Islamic Ideology and an Islamic Research Institute were established. Provincial autonomy was limited, and the system left the center with dominant powers. A Political Parties Act in 1962 allowed parties to function under restrictions, and Ayub's supporters formed the Convention Muslim League, while opponents formed the Council Muslim League.

Section 90.15

Ayub's Development Decade: Economic Growth, Industrialization, and Unequal Benefits

The regime justified itself in large part by economic performance. Pakistan under Ayub Khan experienced rapid growth, with gross domestic product rising at average rates of roughly five to six percent per year over the decade, a high rate by the standards of the time, and with industrial output, especially in large-scale manufacturing, growing much faster. The state's development planning, conducted by the Planning Commission, set out successive Five-Year Plans, the Second Plan (1960–1965) and the Third (1965–1970). The government encouraged private investment through tax incentives, tariffs and import licensing that protected domestic manufacturers, the Export Bonus Voucher scheme, which gave exporters special entitlements to import, and the provision of credit through development finance institutions. Foreign aid, mainly from the United States and later through international consortia, financed a large part of the investment, and advisers, including economists associated with Harvard University, shaped policy.

Agriculture was modernized in specific areas. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, mediated by the World Bank, settled the division of the Indus system's rivers between India and Pakistan and led to the construction of replacement works, including the Mangla Dam on the Jhelum (completed in the late 1960s) and, later, the Tarbela Dam on the Indus. Tubewell irrigation, drainage and salinity-control projects, and the introduction of high-yielding wheat and rice varieties, together with fertilizer and credit, raised output in Punjab and parts of Sindh in the later 1960s, a development sometimes described as the early Green Revolution (compare Chapter 94 for India). A new capital was planned at Islamabad, near Rawalpindi, at a site chosen in part for its proximity to army headquarters at Rawalpindi, and construction began in the early 1960s. In East Pakistan, projects included the Kaptai hydroelectric project in the Chittagong Hill Tracts, which displaced many Chakma and other residents, and the expansion of the port of Chittagong and of jute mills.

These policies had several consequences. The state, the civil service, the military, and private capital became more closely linked. Industrial licensing and protection favored a small group of entrepreneurs, many of whom were members of migrant trading communities or Punjabi families, who obtained concentrated holdings. The economist Mahbub ul Haq publicized in 1968 the claim that some twenty-two families controlled a very large share of industrial assets, banking, and insurance, and the figure, though debated in its details, entered political discourse and became a symbol of inequality. The agricultural modernization strengthened landlords and larger farmers who could afford inputs, and tenants were sometimes displaced. Reforms of landholding and the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance of 1961, which regulated marriage, divorce, and polygamy and was a major reform in family law, reflected a modernizing outlook but also encountered opposition from religious groups.

Students should give balanced treatment to the concept of a "development decade." The regime celebrated the Decade of Development in 1968, and there was genuine growth. Many people experienced improvement: expanding employment in industry and services, rising agricultural output in irrigated areas, schools, roads, and electricity. At the same time, gains were distributed unequally between social classes and regions. Real wages for industrial workers did not rise as quickly as productivity, many rural cultivators saw little benefit, and urban prices increased in the late 1960s. Regional disparities widened, as Section 90.18 will show. Growth was real and it was uneven, and each fact must be recorded without being allowed to cancel the other.

Section 90.16

The 1965 Presidential Election, War, Political Opposition, and Ayub Khan's Fall

The first presidential election under the 1962 Constitution was held on 2 January 1965. Ayub Khan, standing as the candidate of the Convention Muslim League, was challenged by Fatima Jinnah, the sister of the founder, who accepted the nomination of the Combined Opposition Parties. The opposition coalition included the Awami League, the National Awami Party, the Council Muslim League, the Jamaat-e-Islami, and others, an alliance of parties that differed widely in ideology. The electorate consisted of the Basic Democrats, then numbering about 80,000 (later enlarged), who were subject to pressure from local officials. Fatima Jinnah drew large crowds, especially in Dhaka, Karachi, and other cities, and the campaign was marked by restrictions on opposition meetings and press freedom. Ayub won about 63 percent of the electoral college vote, a margin that the opposition and many observers considered a result of the system's design and of official influence. The campaign demonstrated both the regime's control and the extent of dissent, and it raised questions about the legitimacy of indirect election.

In August and September 1965 Pakistan and India fought a war, with fighting beginning in Kashmir and spreading across the international border. The conflict, treated in Chapter 92, ended with a ceasefire in September and the Tashkent Declaration of January 1966. Here the relevant point is its effect on the regime. The war initially produced a surge of national feeling, but the outcome, which was perceived by many as inconclusive, cost the government prestige and money and led to the suspension of some American military and economic aid. East Pakistanis noted that the province had been defended thinly during the fighting, a point that reinforced a sense of vulnerability. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, the foreign minister, broke with Ayub over Tashkent and formed the Pakistan People's Party in 1967.

Between 1966 and 1968 the opposition grew, and its sources were diverse. In the west, the Pakistan People's Party, with Bhutto's populist message, attracted students, industrial workers, and urban groups. Price increases and the contrast between official celebrations of development and the experience of many households created discontent. In 1968 the government celebrated the "Decade of Development," and in the months that followed, protests among students and workers spread. Ayub Khan's health declined in early 1968, which added to uncertainty. In East Pakistan, the Awami League's autonomy movement (§90.19) gained force and combined with student and labor protest. By November 1968 the unrest in the west and the east had joined, and by early 1969 strikes, demonstrations, and clashes with police were widespread.

Ayub tried to meet the crisis by negotiation and concessions. In February 1969 he announced that he would not stand for reelection, withdrew the Agartala case, and convened a Round Table Conference of political leaders at Rawalpindi in March. The talks, in which Mujibur Rahman, released from prison, took part, discussed parliamentary government and direct elections, but did not produce agreement. Violence continued. On 25 March 1969 Ayub announced that he would step down. The army commander, General Yahya Khan, proclaimed martial law the same day, abrogated the 1962 Constitution, and dissolved the legislatures. Yahya immediately exercised authority as Chief Martial Law Administrator on 25 March and assumed the office of President formally on 31 March 1969, according to the Pakistan Senate chronology; some institutional officeholder lists use 25 March as the start of his presidency. The sequence illustrates the way in which authority returned to the army and not to a civilian constitutional process.

Ayub's regime combined economic growth and state-building with restrictions on representative politics and rising social and regional opposition. It had built institutions, reduced the immediate disorder of the 1950s, and presided over rapid economic change. It had also suppressed parties and the press, constructed a controlled electoral system, and strengthened the interests that gained from its policies. These features, along with the East–West disparities examined next, explain why it fell when it did and why the settlement that followed was so difficult to reach.

The final transfer of power exposed a constitutional contradiction. The 1962 system assigned the presidency and succession roles within a written framework, yet the decisive transfer in March 1969 was made to the army commander rather than carried out through its civilian succession mechanism. Yahya's immediate control under martial law on 25 March should be distinguished from the formal presidency dated 31 March in the Senate's historical account. This difference is not a mere technicality: it illustrates the separation between coercive authority and constitutional office that had become a recurring feature of Pakistani government.

Section 90.17

Two Wings, Unequal Political Power: Representation, Civil Service, and Military Authority

The political relationship between the two wings cannot be understood from seat allocations alone. East Pakistan had a demographic majority, but parity in the legislature, adopted in 1956 and retained in 1962, meant that it did not have a majority in the National Assembly. Even where Bengali politicians held high office, such as Nazimuddin, Bogra, and Suhrawardy as prime ministers, the power of the office depended on those who controlled the executive. Governor-Generals and presidents were frequently drawn from the bureaucracy or the army, and with the exception of Nazimuddin, who held the post briefly, none was a Bengali until much later. Cabinet posts were distributed with some regard to regional balance, but important ministries, particularly defense, finance, and interior, were often held by westerners or by officials from the center.

Control over institutions mattered as much as representation. The federal capital was in Karachi, and then in Rawalpindi and Islamabad, and the major federal institutions, headquarters of the army, the central secretariat, and the State Bank's principal offices were in the west. Recruitment to the Central Superior Services and to the officer corps of the army drew heavily on Punjab and the Frontier and on the Urdu-speaking communities of the cities. Bengalis, who were the largest linguistic group, were underrepresented. Estimates of Bengali numbers vary by year and by service, but the available scholarship agrees that they were a small minority of the officer corps, well under ten percent in the 1950s, and a minority, though a growing one, in the senior civil service. The army, which received the largest share of the budget and had become the most powerful institution, drew its recruits from "martial" districts of the west, a legacy of colonial recruitment policy examined in Chapter 73, and the new state did not change these patterns quickly. East Pakistan's defense was provided by a small force, and the war of 1965 reinforced a belief that the east was secondary in military planning.

Differences within both wings complicate the picture. The west was not a unified bloc. Punjabis, who formed the largest community and were heavily represented in the army and bureaucracy, held a dominant position, but Sindhis, Pashtuns, and Baloch had grievances of their own, over One Unit, over the allocation of water and land, and over the settlement of migrants. The east was not uniform either. Hindu Bengalis, who constituted perhaps a fifth of the population in the early 1950s, were politically vulnerable, and many left. Muslim Bengalis differed by class, region, and political outlook, and the minorities of the Chittagong Hill Tracts and the non-Bengali Bihari migrants had distinct positions. A simplistic depiction of all westerners as beneficiaries and all easterners as politically identical should be avoided.

Nonetheless, Bengali politicians and intellectuals increasingly argued that the formal structures of Pakistani government did not reflect population-based democratic representation. They pointed to the removal of provincial governments in 1954, the dismissal of the Assembly, the One Unit scheme, and the 1958 and 1962 changes, and they concluded that the system, as it operated, did not allow the majority to influence national policy. These arguments, which were framed within Pakistan, gradually shifted from a demand for fairer representation to a demand for substantial autonomy. The next two sections examine the economic and political dimensions of that shift.

A national legislature is only one site of power. Even when a Bengali held the office of prime minister, decisions concerning defense procurement, senior appointments, foreign exchange, the movement of troops, and the location of new federal facilities could be shaped by institutions with very different recruitment patterns. The headquarters of the armed forces and the federal secretariat gave western-based officials extensive informal networks and easier access to ministers. Bengali critics accordingly wanted more than a proportionate share of seats. They demanded representation within the machinery that implemented decisions, including civilian services and military command. Any estimate of regional officer representation must specify service branch, rank, year and the definition of Bengali, rather than reproducing a floating percentage.

The eastern wing nevertheless had significant internal divisions. Bengali Muslim politicians did not agree on land reform, Islam's place in government, trade policy, or relations with the western provinces. Hindu citizens experienced distinct pressures and could not assume that either central or Bengali provincial politicians would consistently protect their interests. Non-Bengali refugees in the east and communities in the Chittagong Hill Tracts also occupied positions that are obscured by a simple linguistic majority-minority binary. These differences matter because political autonomy did not automatically specify which groups within East Pakistan would exercise the new powers or how minorities there would be treated.

Section 90.18

Economic Disparities between East and West Pakistan: Jute, Investment, Foreign Exchange, and Development

The economic grievances of the east centered on how the nation's resources were earned and spent. East Pakistan produced most of the world's raw jute, and jute and jute goods were Pakistan's principal export in the 1950s, providing a large share of its foreign-exchange earnings. These earnings financed imports of machinery, raw materials, and consumer goods that went disproportionately to the west, where industrial investment was concentrated. Foreign exchange was allocated by the central government through import licensing, and licenses and industrial credit went largely to entrepreneurs based in Karachi and Punjab. Bengali critics, including economists such as Rehman Sobhan and Nurul Islam, argued that this arrangement transferred resources from east to west. Defenders, including some central planners, argued that investment was directed to where returns were highest, that the west had the infrastructure and entrepreneurs, and that development of the whole depended on this strategy.

Public development expenditure showed a similar pattern. In the First Five-Year Plan period, the eastern wing received a share of development expenditure considerably below its share of the population. The share rose in the Second and Third Plans, but critics argued that the increase was insufficient to close the gap. Private investment was heavily concentrated in the west, in part because of the established business communities of Karachi and Punjab and the location of financial institutions. East Pakistan did have important industries, including the Adamjee jute mills and the paper and textile mills, many owned by entrepreneurs from the west or from migrant communities. Infrastructure, including roads, power, and ports, was less developed in the east, whose riverine geography made construction costly and exposed it to cyclones and floods. The cyclone and flood of 1970, treated in Chapter 92, would later expose the state's limited response capacity.

Careful comparison requires care. Both wings grew in absolute terms during the 1950s and 1960s, so the question is relative. Per capita income was already lower in the east at independence, and the available estimates indicate that the gap widened during the period. Estimates differ by source, price base, and definition, and figures should be compared only when they cover the same dates and use the same price basis. With these cautions, studies from the Planning Commission and from later economists, and retrospective analyses by international institutions such as the IMF and the World Bank, document an increase in the regional disparity in per capita income across the 1960s, and a contested pattern in the allocation of foreign exchange earned through exports.

Conversely, the growth of colleges, professional associations and economic research in East Pakistan supplied the skilled critics who documented disparity and formulated autonomy programs. Material grievances and the intellectual capacity to express them thus developed together rather than in opposition.

Section 90.19

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, the Six-Point Programme, and the Mass Uprising of 1969

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman had been a student activist in Calcutta and then Dhaka, a founding member of the Awami Muslim League, and an organizer who spent long periods in prison, including during the language movement of 1952. After Suhrawardy's death in 1963, he became the leading figure in the Awami League and its president in 1966. The party opposed the 1958 takeover and the 1962 Constitution and took part in the Combined Opposition in 1965.

On 5 February 1966, at a conference of opposition leaders in Lahore called to discuss the Tashkent Declaration, Mujib presented a Six-Point Programme, which the Awami League adopted. It proposed, in summary, that the Constitution provide for a federation on the basis of the 1940 Lahore Resolution with a parliamentary government and a legislature elected directly on the basis of adult franchise, with the legislature supreme. It proposed that the federal government deal only with defense and foreign affairs, with all other subjects held by the federating units. On currency, it proposed either two separate freely convertible currencies for the two wings or one currency with effective constitutional safeguards against the transfer of capital from east to west and a separate banking and fiscal policy for the east. It proposed that taxation power rest with the units, with the federal government receiving a share to meet its needs, that each wing hold separate foreign-exchange accounts and be able to conduct trade arrangements abroad, and that East Pakistan have its own militia or paramilitary force.

Historical Debate 2

Could Pakistan Have Reconciled National Unity with Bengali Majority Rule and Provincial Autonomy?

The question: Was there a constitutional arrangement in the 1950s or 1960s that could have combined a united Pakistan with majority participation by Bengalis and substantial provincial autonomy?

Interpretation A — A workable compromise was available: The 1954 election showed that Bengali demands were for autonomy within Pakistan. The 1956 Constitution demonstrated that the wings could agree on language and parity. A federal arrangement with real provincial powers and fair resource allocation, as proposed in the Twenty-One Points and Six Points, might have preserved unity. On this view, the failure was a failure of will on the part of central leaders, who repeatedly overrode election results and blocked autonomy.
Interpretation B — Structural obstacles made compromise unlikely: The geographic separation, the entrenched power of the army and bureaucracy, the security fears of western leaders, and the divergent economic interests of the two wings made a stable settlement hard. Parity and majority rule were incompatible, and autonomy sufficient to satisfy Bengali leaders would have drastically reduced central powers that the army and bureaucracy would not accept. On this view, the underlying conflict was too deep to be resolved by constitutional drafting alone.

Evidence: The 1954 election returns, the Twenty-One Points, the 1956 text, and the Six Points support A in showing that autonomy within Pakistan was the stated aim and that compromises were reached. The central dismissals of 1953, 1954, and 1958 and the army's role support B. Interpretations also depend on assumptions about the intentions of leaders on both sides.

Why it matters: The debate distinguishes historical possibilities available to political actors at the time from judgments shaped by knowledge of the 1971 outcome. Hindsight can make the end seem inevitable. Historians must ask what alternatives were actually proposed and what choices were open.

Student task: Select one moment (1954, 1956, or 1966) and explain what a different decision by central leaders might have looked like and what constraints would have affected it.

Conclusions should be drawn carefully. The autonomy program of the 1960s should be distinguished from the demand for an independent Bangladesh that emerged in the following crisis. In 1969 Mujib and most Awami League leaders continued to seek a settlement within Pakistan, and the Round Table Conference included Awami League proposals on autonomy. At the end of Ayub's presidency a fundamental question remained: whether Pakistan could establish a representative federal arrangement capable of satisfying its different regions. Ayub's fall did not answer it. It transferred it to a new military government.

The Six Points were an institutional proposal, not merely a declaration of grievance. Limiting federal powers to defense and external affairs would have required an extensive reassignment of taxation, banking and commercial regulation to the provincial level. Separate foreign-exchange accounts or monetary safeguards raised difficult questions about how to pay for shared defense and service national obligations. These were genuine design problems for supporters as well as opponents. Yet dismissing the program as disloyal without debating its mechanics reinforced the perception that the center rejected the principle of meaningful federal negotiation. The movement drew power from both the precise constitutional demands and the experience of official repression.

Student activists helped connect the economic arguments of professionals with street-level demands for elections, educational reform and political freedoms. The eleven-point student program that emerged during the uprising added concerns that could not be reduced to the economic interests of a provincial elite. Meanwhile, western opposition to Ayub involved different political agendas, including Bhutto's populism and demands from smaller ethnic communities. Their temporary convergence weakened the presidency without creating an agreed successor settlement. The authorities' decision to withdraw the Agartala case and release Mujib was therefore a retreat under pressure, not a comprehensive settlement of the conflicts that had produced the demonstrations.

Section 90.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Constitutional Failure, Military Rule, and the Crisis of Pakistani Federalism

This chapter has traced two decades of state-building. Pakistan began with administrative insecurity, a divided territory, a refugee crisis, and the loss of its founding leaders within three years. The inherited institutions, particularly the civil service and the army, were more developed than the political parties, and the constitution that would have specified how power was shared took nine years to produce. During these years, language conflict in East Bengal revealed that the state's founding idea did not settle the question of how a diverse population would be governed. The 1952 movement and the 1954 election showed that a majority of East Bengalis wanted recognition and a voice, and the central response, whether dismissal of a provincial government or the dissolution of the Assembly, deepened distrust.

The 1956 Constitution was a genuine achievement, reached by compromise on language and representation. Its limits were its narrow political base, its dependence on parity, which Bengalis saw as unequal, and the failure to hold national elections under it. The military takeover of 1958 altered the constitutional framework. It was a decision by Mirza and Ayub, and then by Ayub alone, to end parliamentary government. The Ayub regime built institutions, promoted growth, passed reforms, and undertook large infrastructure projects. It also restricted representative politics through martial law, indirect election, and control of the press, and its development strategy benefited some classes and regions more than others.

Bengali politicians and intellectuals: Parity denied a democratic majority its rightful influence. The settlement, in their view, gave formal equality but kept control with institutions dominated by the west, and they sought autonomy and population-based representation.
Western political and bureaucratic leaders, especially from Punjab: Parity and One Unit protected against domination by a single province and simplified administration. Many were also motivated by concern for security and for the army's and bureaucracy's role.
Sindhi, Pashtun, and Baloch leaders: One Unit erased provincial identities and gave Punjab a decisive share in the new western province. They saw the settlement as imposed and sought restoration of the provinces.
Muhajirs and Karachi's urban communities: Many favored a strong center and Urdu, with which their positions in the bureaucracy and commerce were linked, while their separation of Karachi from Sindh provoked Sindhi resentment.
Minorities, especially Hindus in East Pakistan: They experienced the era as a time of insecurity, emigration, and limited political voice, and some supported language and autonomy demands as paths to broader citizenship.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Institutional Power from 1947 to 1969

What Changed

  • Parliamentary government gave way to a presidential system under military-backed rule
  • The Muslim League dominance of 1947 gave way to a fragmented party system, then to restricted parties
  • The Bengali language moved from exclusion to constitutional recognition
  • The economy grew rapidly and industrialized, with new regional gaps

What Remained Continuous

  • The colonial-era civil service and army remained the strongest institutions
  • Central executives overrode provincial and legislative bodies, using inherited powers
  • The location of federal institutions and recruitment patterns remained largely western
  • Parity remained the formula for the wings' representation

Who experienced change most directly: politicians and parties, who lost influence, and East Bengalis, whose hopes for representation were repeatedly frustrated. Who experienced greater continuity: senior officials and officers, whose institutional position strengthened. Why the pattern matters: it shows that the formal constitution changed far more often than the underlying distribution of institutional power.

Cause and Effect

The Military Takeover of 1958

Long-term causes

Colonial administrative inheritance; weak party organization; delayed constitution; refugee settlement that concentrated power in the center; alliance with the United States that strengthened the army.

Short-term causes

Cabinet instability in 1956–1958; presidential intervention; fear that the approaching election would not produce a government Mirza and Ayub could accept; disorder in the East Pakistan Assembly.

Event

Abrogation of the Constitution on 7 October and Ayub's assumption of the presidency on 27 October 1958.

Immediate effects

Dissolution of legislatures; ban on parties; disqualification of politicians; press control; judicial recognition in Dosso.

Long-term consequences: a presidential system with controlled participation, a precedent for later takeovers, and a deepening of East Bengal's sense of exclusion. Historical complexity: no single cause is sufficient. Structural conditions made intervention possible, but individuals chose it, and alternatives existed.

Efforts to build national unity through religious identity, centralized government, and economic development did not resolve conflicts over representative authority, regional identity, and resource allocation. Religion proved a powerful founding bond but not a sufficient political settlement, since it did not answer questions about language, representation, or resources. Centralization secured the state's administrative capacity but at the cost of provincial and regional consent. Development created growth but also visible disparity. Pakistan had established substantial state institutions by 1969, but it had not secured a political settlement accepted across its regions. Its early postcolonial history was shaped by a persistent mismatch between centralized institutional power and the political, cultural, and economic diversity of its population.

The transition to Chapter 92 is explicit. Yahya Khan's military government promised elections, and in December 1970 Pakistan held its first nationwide direct elections. The results, the breakdown of negotiations over the transfer of power, the military crackdown of March 1971, the war, and the creation of Bangladesh require separate treatment and are not compressed here. Chapter 91 intervenes to examine Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives, whose different paths provide comparison. Students should carry forward the central lesson of this chapter: the military takeover of 1958 changed the constitutional framework but did not resolve the underlying problem, and the collapse of Ayub's government in 1969 demonstrated that economic growth and administrative capacity could not substitute indefinitely for representative legitimacy or accepted federal arrangements.

Several comparisons help specify the outcome. Pakistan's federal dilemma concerned both majority rule and the protection of minorities within each wing. Equal legislative seats could protect the western wing against a Bengali majority but also deny a citizen in the east the same electoral weight as a western citizen. Central institutions could coordinate defense and national projects yet also make local demands appear futile when provincial ministries were dismissed and public funds were allocated at a distance. Economic growth could expand the national productive base without establishing consent to the distribution of its benefits. None of these tensions was unique to Pakistan; their combination with an unresolved constitution and an unusually influential army made them especially consequential in this case.

There were paths not taken. Earlier direct general elections might have changed party incentives; a more persuasive arrangement over language and provincial taxation might have reduced the appeal of far-reaching autonomy; legal limits on executive dismissal might have helped legislators hold cabinet officials accountable. The historical record does not prove that any one reform would have prevented later violence, and it would be misleading to present such possibilities as predictions. They demonstrate instead that leaders faced choices over institutions and policy. By 1969 administrative capacity, industrial production and international alliances had grown, but no broadly accepted method for transferring political authority between center and provinces had been secured.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Pakistan's first two decades, from independence in August 1947 to the fall of Ayub Khan in March 1969. Its central argument is that Pakistan developed strong administrative and military institutions faster than representative ones, and that this imbalance, together with disputes over language, representation, and resources, produced a military-bureaucratic state without a settlement accepted across its regions.

Pakistan began as a dominion with two wings separated by Indian territory, an interim constitution inherited from the Government of India Act of 1935, a shortage of trained officials, and the task of making Karachi a capital. The Governor-General's broad powers and the early dismissals of provincial ministries set precedents for central intervention. Jinnah, as Governor-General and president of the Assembly, held extraordinary authority, and his address of 11 August 1947 has been read in competing ways. His death in September 1948 removed the one figure able to mediate among the state's institutions and regions.

Refugee migration reshaped Karachi, Sindh, and the federal bureaucracy, and created social coalitions that influenced politics for decades. Liaquat Ali Khan's government managed the early crises, but the Rawalpindi Conspiracy, the lack of general elections, and his assassination in 1951 left civilian government dependent on individuals and on senior officials.

Language became the first major test of national integration. Urdu, associated with Muslim nationalism, was favored for the state language, while Bengali was the language of the majority. Datta's 1948 proposal, Jinnah's Dhaka statements, and the shootings of 21 February 1952 turned a cultural question into a political one. Recognition came in stages: concessions in 1954 and constitutional provision in 1956. The 1954 East Bengal election, in which the United Front swept the Muslim seats on a program including autonomy within Pakistan, was followed by the dismissal of its ministry.

The representation dispute pitted population against parity. One Unit merged the western provinces in 1955 to create a unit matching East Bengal, at the cost of the smaller provinces' identities. The Objectives Resolution of 1949 defined the state's constitutional identity in terms that remained open to competing interpretations. The dissolution of the first Constituent Assembly in October 1954, and the Federal Court's technical ruling in 1955, showed that the executive could override elected bodies.

The Constitution of 1956 was a genuine achievement, creating an Islamic Republic with parliamentary government, parity, and recognition of both Urdu and Bengali. Parliamentary government nonetheless failed within two years, through party fragmentation, presidential interference, and the absence of elections. Iskander Mirza proclaimed martial law on 7 October 1958, and Ayub Khan took the presidency on 27 October. The judgment in Dosso gave the new order legal recognition.

Ayub's regime constructed Basic Democracies and the Constitution of 1962, a presidential system with indirect election. Its development decade produced rapid growth, industrialization, and agricultural change, but gains were unequally distributed among classes and regions. The 1965 election and war exposed the regime's limits.

East–West disparities in representation, civil-service and military recruitment, investment, and income, together with the central overriding of Bengali political choices, shaped the autonomy movement. The Six-Point Programme of 1966, the Agartala case, and the uprising of 1968–1969 forced Ayub from office. He was succeeded by Yahya Khan under renewed martial law.

Throughout, the chapter has argued against teleology. Military rule and the separation of East Pakistan were not inevitable, and actors made choices with alternatives available. It has also argued that Pakistan's state-building was real, but that it was marked by a persistent mismatch between centralized institutional power and the diversity of its people. Chapters 91 and 92 continue the regional story, and Chapter 92 examines the crisis of 1970–1971.

Chapter Reflection

Pakistan's first two decades show how a state can acquire the machinery of government more quickly than the political agreements on which that machinery depends. The civil service, the army, and the courts functioned, and the constitution-makers did eventually produce a text. What was missing was a settlement about who would hold power and on what terms, and that absence shaped every institutional choice.

The chapter also shows how the movement for language recognition, the election of 1954, and the autonomy program of 1966 were attempts to negotiate inclusion within the state. That they were repeatedly rebuffed matters, and so does the fact that they were framed as demands for participation in Pakistan. Recognizing this protects history from the temptation to read each episode as an early stage of separation.

The comparison with Chapters 88 and 89 invites reflection on how states emerging from the same empire, with similar inherited institutions, followed different paths. Such comparisons should avoid simple explanations. Contingent decisions, different party structures, different relations between civil and military authority, and different external environments all played parts.

Chapter 91 now turns to Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives, and Chapter 92 returns to the crisis that followed Ayub's fall. The questions this chapter raises about federalism, representation, and the relation between the state and its diverse peoples recur throughout the later history of South Asia.
