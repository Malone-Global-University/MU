Caste, Gender, Religion, and Social Change

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research as of November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

During 2026 India began the long-postponed Census 2027 through a staggered schedule rather than a single nationwide enumeration day. Online self-enumeration began in selected jurisdictions on April 1, followed by house-to-house houselisting from April 16 there; other states and territories used later windows. The census would collect information about homes, water, fuel, and assets, while its population-enumeration phase included a decision to ask residents about caste. Special population enumeration was held in certain snowbound areas in September 2026, and other jurisdictions were assigned dates extending into 2027. Caste had been counted broadly in colonial censuses through 1931 but not comprehensively for the general population after independence. The April 30, 2025 decision to restore caste enumeration demonstrated that an institution once expected to fade with modernization remained central to law, political representation, and public statistics.

This chapter studies the social institutions and conflicts that shaped everyday life in South Asia between 1991 and 2026: caste and other systems of descent-based hierarchy; the status of women and the organization of family life; religious majorities and minorities; the position of sexual and gender minorities and of persons with disabilities; the politics of language and indigenous identity; and the voluntary associations and protest movements through which citizens tried to change all of these. Its subject is not "tradition" in general. It is the specific, documented history of how people in seven countries (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and Maldives) have defended, challenged, reformed, and reinterpreted inherited arrangements during a period of liberalization, democratic competition, expanding education, migration, and new communications technology.

The chapter's purpose, as set by the series outline, is to examine these institutions without treating them as unchanged survivals from the ancient past. That instruction matters because popular and political discourse often presents caste, patriarchy, or religious division as timeless features of South Asian civilization. The earlier chapters of this book have shown otherwise. The varna scheme of the Vedic and later Brahmanical texts was not identical to the jati communities of medieval and early modern society (Chapters 7 and 37). Colonial censuses, ethnography, and law reshaped the categories through which people were counted and governed (Chapter 73). Reform movements of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries redefined religion, marriage, and women's roles (Chapters 74 and 80). The social institutions examined here are therefore historical products, continually remade, and the period after 1991 is one more chapter in that remaking.

Two cautions govern the narrative. The first is that law and lived experience must be kept distinct. A constitutional article abolishing untouchability, a statute guaranteeing a daughter's inheritance, or a judgment decriminalizing private conduct is evidence of what a state has committed itself to do. It is not evidence that discrimination has ended. The history of South Asian social change since 1991 is largely a history of the gap between formal and substantive equality, and of the efforts of citizens to narrow it. The second caution is regional. Caste operates differently in a Tamil village, a Kathmandu neighborhood, a Punjabi town, and a Jaffna temple. Religion stands in a different constitutional relationship to the state in Bhutan, Bangladesh, India, and Maldives. Gender relations vary by class, region, and community as much as by nation. The chapter therefore compares rather than generalizes, and it avoids using India's experience as a template for the region.

The chapter also resists two opposite temptations. One is to portray South Asia as a society trapped in inherited hierarchy, in which every reform is merely cosmetic. The other is to suppose that education, urbanization, and constitutional rights automatically dissolve discrimination. The evidence supports neither. Dalit, Adivasi, Janajati, women's, religious-minority, queer, and disability movements won real legal and institutional changes, and many individuals experienced genuine mobility. Yet violence against Dalits and religious minorities persisted, women's formal rights outran their enforcement, and new inequalities of school quality, language, and access to private institutions emerged alongside old ones. Marginalized communities appear in this chapter as intellectuals, litigants, organizers, voters, and institution-builders, not only as victims.

The evidence is unusually varied and, for the most recent years, unusually fluid. Constitutions, statutes, and court judgments are primary documents. Census and survey data (including the National Family Health Surveys of India, the Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in several neighboring countries, and national censuses) provide quantitative evidence, though caste and religion have been enumerated unevenly and sometimes disputedly. Commission reports, police and crime statistics, memoirs, literature, newspapers, organizational records, and oral testimony add further perspectives. Each has limits. Crime statistics measure reporting and registration as well as crime; survey categories shape the identities respondents can claim; judgments record what courts decided, not how decisions were implemented. The chapter flags these limits where they matter.

Within the sequence of this book, the chapter draws on the political histories of Chapters 99 through 105 and on the economic and demographic analysis of Chapter 106, while reserving media, literature, and cultural production for Chapter 108, environmental vulnerability for Chapter 109, and international and diaspora relations for Chapter 110. It closes the book's social history before Chapter 111 turns to the question of how South Asians remember and argue about their past. Its research-as-of date is November 20, 2026. Developments after that date, and outcomes still unfolding on it, are not treated as settled history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among varna, jati, Dalit, Scheduled Caste, Adivasi, Janajati, and religious-minority categories, and explain why these terms have different historical origins and legal meanings.
  2. Trace the development of India's reservation policies from the Constitution through the Mandal era to the 2024 sub-classification judgment, and distinguish judicial rulings from their implementation.
  3. Compare caste, descent-based hierarchy, and inclusion policies in India, Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka without applying one national model to the region.
  4. Analyze the political, intellectual, and cultural contributions of Dalit and Indigenous movements, giving these communities agency rather than treating them solely as victims.
  5. Explain how constitutional rights, legislation, judicial interpretation, and enforcement differ, using women's legal status as the principal case.
  6. Evaluate how expanded education, migration, and urbanization affected marriage, household authority, and social mobility, and whether they weakened or reproduced hierarchy.
  7. Compare the constitutional and social positions of religion and minorities in the seven South Asian countries, and explain the different meanings of secularism.
  8. Assess major episodes of communal violence since 1992 by distinguishing documented events from contested claims and without attributing collective responsibility to whole communities.
  9. Analyze the evidence for everyday coexistence and local repair after violence, and explain why communal polarization is historically produced rather than inevitable.
  10. Distinguish decriminalization, antidiscrimination protection, gender recognition, partnership recognition, and social acceptance in the history of LGBTQ+ and transgender rights.
  11. Explain how disability rights, language policy, and cultural recognition expanded the idea of citizenship.
  12. Interpret primary sources, including constitutional provisions and a Supreme Court judgment, and evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of the effects of legal reform.

Historical Context

The social order that confronted South Asia in 1991 was the product of several layers of history. Hierarchical ideas about purity, occupation, and birth had long circulated in Brahmanical texts, and regional systems of jati, kinship, landholding, and ritual status had developed over many centuries, differing greatly from Kerala to Punjab and from Bengal to Sri Lanka. The colonial state classified and enumerated these communities, sometimes hardening boundaries (Chapter 73). Reformers and anti-caste thinkers such as Jotirao Phule, Periyar, and B. R. Ambedkar attacked hierarchy from within and outside inherited religious frameworks (Chapters 74 and 80). The independence-era constitutions then converted some of these struggles into law. India's Constitution of 1950 prohibited discrimination on grounds of caste and religion, abolished untouchability, and authorized special provisions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and "backward classes" (Chapter 88).

By 1991 the postcolonial settlement had been under strain for some time. Land reform and the Green Revolution had altered rural power (Chapter 94). Mandal-era politics, treated in Chapter 99, transformed the party system by organizing intermediate and lower castes as electoral constituencies. The demolition of the Babri Masjid in December 1992 intensified the debate over secularism and religious identity in India. Elsewhere, the independence of Bangladesh in 1971 had shown the limits of religion as the sole basis of nationhood (Chapter 92); Pakistan's Islamization under Zia-ul-Haq had altered law and public life (Chapter 95); the Sri Lankan civil war embedded ethnic and religious conflict in the state (Chapters 96 and 103); Nepal's monarchy was succeeded by a federal republic that wrote inclusion into its constitution (Chapter 104); and Bhutan and Maldives defined their national identities through particular traditions of Buddhism and Islam.

The period from 1991 to 2026 added new conditions. Liberalization (Chapter 98) and the economic changes analyzed in Chapter 106 expanded private education, urban labor markets, and migration, including Gulf migration, with consequences for family structure. Competitive electoral politics gave caste, ethnic, and religious communities leverage as voting blocs, while constitutional courts became arenas for rights claims. Communication technology allowed new forms of organization and, as Chapter 108 shows, new forms of communal mobilization. International instruments such as the conventions on the elimination of discrimination against women and on the rights of persons with disabilities offered frameworks that domestic activists cited, though ratification did not guarantee implementation.

Students should understand the evidentiary asymmetry of this period. Because the chapter addresses recent decades, much of the evidence consists of statutes, judgments, survey data, and reporting that scholars have not yet fully assessed. Disputes over contemporary events often carry present-day political stakes. The chapter therefore distinguishes established legal provisions from allegations and from competing political interpretations, notes where figures are contested, and avoids predicting the outcome of developments that were still in progress on November 20, 2026.

Caste, Social Hierarchy, and the Struggle for Inclusion

Section 107.1

Caste in Contemporary South Asian Society

Any account of caste since 1991 must begin by separating several concepts that popular usage merges. Varna is a fourfold scheme of Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra, articulated in Sanskrit normative texts, with a further category of people outside it. Jati refers to the thousands of endogamous, locally rooted communities, differing by region, through which people actually organized marriage, occupation, and ritual status. The English word "caste," derived from Portuguese usage, has been applied to both, and colonial census-takers (Chapter 73) often forced jati into varna categories. Modern law adds a third layer: statutory categories such as Scheduled Caste, defined by the state for purposes of protection and entitlement. A person's jati, varna claim, and legal category may not coincide, and none of them describes a fixed set of occupations. Historians therefore treat caste in the present as an institution continually reconstituted by law, politics, and social practice, not as a direct survival of the Vedic order.

Several features nonetheless persisted through the period. The most durable was endogamy, marriage within the community. Survey evidence such as the India Human Development Survey of 2011–12 estimated that only a small minority of marriages crossed caste lines, on the order of five percent, a figure that changed slowly despite rising education and urban migration. Caste also shaped social networks that mattered for employment and credit, patterns of land ownership in rural areas, and residential segregation in many villages, where Dalit hamlets sat apart from dominant-caste neighborhoods. Dalits were also overrepresented among landless agricultural laborers and in the most stigmatized work, including manual scavenging, which Parliament prohibited in 1993 and again, with broader definitions, in the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act of 2013, yet which continued in some places.

Other features changed. Urbanization and migration weakened some forms of ritual avoidance, because commuters on trains, workers in factories, and students in universities could not observe the exclusions practiced in a village. Education created Dalit and lower-caste professionals, writers, and officials. Democratic participation gave numerically large lower-status groups electoral leverage, so that parties sought their votes and sometimes represented their interests (Chapters 94 and 99). Dominant intermediate castes, such as Jats in the north and Patidars, Marathas, and others in the west, which had gained from land reform and the Green Revolution, themselves mobilized in the 2010s to demand inclusion in reservation categories, showing that caste had become a claim on state resources as well as a ritual hierarchy. Caste also operated among Muslims, Christians, and Sikhs, despite the egalitarian ideals of those traditions. The Pasmanda movement in India, organized from the late 1990s, argued that Muslims of lower status, who formed the large majority of the community, had been overshadowed by an ashraf elite in both community leadership and political representation.

Crime statistics illustrate both the persistence of violence and the difficulty of interpreting data. India's National Crime Records Bureau recorded tens of thousands of cases annually under the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act of 1989, exceeding fifty thousand a year in recent reports. Such figures reflect registration as well as incidence, since fear of retaliation, police reluctance, and dominant-caste pressure suppress complaints, while awareness and activism raise them. Particular cases became national events: the killings at Khairlanji in Maharashtra in 2006, the public flogging of Dalit men at Una in Gujarat in 2016, and the death of the doctoral student Rohith Vemula at the University of Hyderabad in January 2016, which prompted nationwide protests about discrimination in higher education.

Comparison shows that caste is not uniquely Indian, but neither is it uniform. Nepal's Muluki Ain of 1854 had embedded caste ranking in state law, and after its repeal in 1963 caste discrimination persisted; Nepal criminalized it by statute in 2011, and the Constitution of 2015 recognized Dalit rights (Section 107.2). Dalits made up roughly thirteen percent of Nepal's population in recent censuses. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, descent-based status groups existed among Muslims (occupational kammi communities in Punjab and Sindh, hierarchical biradari networks) and among Hindu Scheduled Caste communities, often poor and concentrated in southern Sindh. Christian sanitation workers in Pakistan occupied a stigmatized niche whose origins lay in caste-based occupations converted to Christianity. In Sri Lanka, caste distinctions persisted among Sinhalas and, with notable rigidity, among Tamils in the Jaffna peninsula, where temple-entry struggles had been waged in the 1960s and where the civil war's disruption of old hierarchies was partial and contested. Each of these societies thus developed its own relationship between inherited status, the state, and equality.

The 2025 decision to enumerate caste in India's Census 2027 reflects how caste had moved from social description to political resource. The Union Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs decided on April 30, 2025, that caste would be included in the population census. Houselisting was organized in staggered 30-day state and territory windows between April and September 2026; some areas began online self-enumeration on April 1 and house-to-house visits on April 16. Population enumeration was conducted in September 2026 in designated snowbound areas, while Goa, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, and non-snowbound Uttarakhand began online self-enumeration on November 16 ahead of their December 2026–January 2027 fieldwork. Most remaining areas were scheduled for population enumeration in early 2027. The Bihar government's own caste survey, published in October 2023, had already reported that extremely backward and other backward classes together made up roughly sixty-three percent of that state's population, and it intensified demands for a national count. Advocates argued that data were needed to target welfare and test claims of exclusion; critics warned that enumeration might harden identities, and some questioned how fine-grained categories would be classified. Because the count had not been completed by the chapter's research date, its results and political consequences belong to the future, not to history.

Historical documentation: India's Census 2027 schedule is documented in the Government of India phase-one schedule and the November 2026 population-enumeration notice. These official schedules distinguish self-enumeration, houselisting, and population enumeration.

Section 107.2

Reservations, Affirmative Action, and the Politics of Representation

India's system of reservations, or quotas, in education, public employment, and legislatures, is the best-known instrument of affirmative action in South Asia. Its foundations lay in the Constitution of 1950, which permitted the state to make special provisions for "socially and educationally backward classes" and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Articles 15(4), 16(4), and 46), and reserved legislative seats for Scheduled Castes and Tribes (Articles 330 and 332). Its early history is treated in Chapter 89. What is distinctive about the period after 1990 is the extension of reservation from the lowest-status groups to a much broader category of Other Backward Classes and the elaboration of its legal doctrine by the courts.

The Mandal Commission, appointed in 1979, reported in 1980 that backward classes constituted a majority of the population and recommended a 27 percent reservation for them in central government posts. When Prime Minister V. P. Singh announced implementation in August 1990, northern India witnessed violent protests, including self-immolations by some students, and the decision helped bring down the government (Chapter 99). In Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (November 16, 1992), a nine-judge bench upheld the 27 percent reservation but set rules that framed Indian law thereafter: reservations in general should not exceed fifty percent; "creamy layer" members of backward classes, defined by relative advantage, should be excluded; and reservation in appointment did not extend to promotion. Parliament responded by amending the Constitution to permit reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Tribes (the 77th Amendment, 1995). In 2005 the 93rd Amendment (Article 15(5)) authorized reservation in educational institutions, and the Court in Ashok Kumar Thakur v. Union of India (2008) upheld 27 percent OBC reservation in central institutions, with creamy-layer exclusion.

Reservation also operated in politics. Since 1992–93, the 73rd and 74th Amendments reserved seats in rural and urban local bodies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and women (Section 107.6). In 2019 the 103rd Amendment introduced a ten percent reservation for economically weaker sections outside existing reserved categories; the Supreme Court upheld it by a three-to-two majority in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (November 7, 2022). The measure showed that the logic of reservation was being contested from two directions: some argued that economic criteria were more appropriate than caste, while others noted that it introduced an economic test only for groups previously excluded from reservation and so departed from the principle of redressing social disadvantage.

On August 1, 2024, a seven-judge bench decided State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh, holding by a six-to-one majority that states may sub-classify Scheduled Castes in order to provide more precise benefits to groups within them, overruling an earlier ruling (E. V. Chinnaiah, 2004) that treated the Scheduled Castes as a single, homogeneous class. Justice Bela Trivedi dissented. The decision responded to claims that a few communities among the Scheduled Castes had captured disproportionate benefits, a claim pressed by groups such as the Madigas in Telangana and the Mazhabi Sikh and Valmiki communities in Punjab. Several opinions also discussed whether the "creamy layer" principle should apply to Scheduled Castes, though the Court did not impose such a rule. It is essential to distinguish the ruling from its implementation: the judgment authorized states to act on empirical data, and individual states, including Haryana and Telangana, moved at different speeds to enact sub-categorization, while other states and political groups opposed or questioned the idea. The ruling also illustrated the Court's role in arbitrating among disadvantaged groups, not merely between disadvantaged and privileged ones.

The ceiling of fifty percent remained a point of conflict. States such as Tamil Nadu maintained reservation above that level under special constitutional protection (the Ninth Schedule); other attempts, notably the Maratha reservation in Maharashtra, were struck down by the Supreme Court in 2021; and Bihar's 2023 increase to sixty-five percent was set aside by the Patna High Court in June 2024, with further litigation pending. Demands for reservation by dominant groups, from Jats and Patidars to Marathas, indicated how a policy designed to remedy the exclusion of the most disadvantaged had become a general vehicle for claims on scarce public positions, in a context in which public-sector employment grew slowly relative to the population. The question was partly one of supply: a reservation of a given percentage is of limited value if the number of government jobs and public university seats is small. That structural reality connects the topic to the employment trends analyzed in Chapter 106.

Comparison clarifies India's distinctiveness. Nepal's Constitution of 2015 adopted a principle of proportional inclusion, whereby women, Dalits, Indigenous peoples (Adivasi Janajati), Madhesis, Tharus, Muslims, persons with disabilities, and others have a right to participate in state bodies in accordance with that principle (Articles 38, 40, and 42; see the Primary Source Window in Section 107.5). It is closer to a rights-based system of representation than to India's quotas by category and ceiling, though implementation has been uneven and has favored better-organized groups. Pakistan reserved a share of federal government jobs for religious minorities in 2009 (five percent), a measure whose implementation was contested. Bangladesh maintained quotas in civil service recruitment for freedom fighters' descendants, women, districts, and ethnic minorities, which became a flashpoint in 2018 and 2024 (Section 107.18). Sri Lanka and Bhutan have not used comparable caste-based schemes, though Sri Lanka has had ethnic and district-based criteria in university admission.

Three distinct ideas have been attached to reservation, and the conflation of them explains much of the controversy. One is compensatory justice: redress for historical exclusion. A second is representation: ensuring that institutions reflect the society they serve. A third is distribution of opportunity: allocating scarce positions. Defenders of reservation have stressed the first two; critics have stressed the third, or argued that economic deprivation is a better criterion. The Indian Supreme Court has tended to treat reservation as an instrument of substantive equality within the Constitution rather than an exception to it. Whether reservation has transformed hierarchy or primarily increased representation within existing institutions is a matter of scholarly debate (see the Historical Debate in Section 107.3).

Historical Vocabulary
Reservation, Affirmative Action, Dalit, and Scheduled Caste

Reservation is the Indian term for a quota system that sets aside a share of seats or posts for designated groups. Affirmative action is the broader English term for policies favoring disadvantaged groups, used in different forms in the United States, South Africa, Malaysia, and elsewhere, and reservation differs from many of these in being group-based by caste and tribe, constitutionally authorized, and quantitatively fixed. Scheduled Caste is a legal term for communities listed in a presidential order under the Constitution; membership has religious restrictions (the 1950 order limited it to Hindus, later extended to Sikhs in 1956 and Buddhists in 1990), so Muslim and Christian Dalits are excluded from the list, a matter still disputed. Dalit, a Marathi and Sanskrit-derived word meaning "broken" or "oppressed," was adopted by activists from the 1970s as a term of self-assertion; it is a political and social identity, not a statutory category, and some individuals and officials prefer other terms.

These terms are not interchangeable. A person may be Dalit by self-identification, Scheduled Caste by law, and Buddhist or Christian by religion, with different legal consequences. Treating them as synonyms obscures why the boundaries of the category are themselves a political issue.

Section 107.3

Dalit Political, Intellectual, and Cultural Movements

Dalit movements after 1991 drew on a long intellectual tradition. B. R. Ambedkar, who led a mass conversion to Buddhism at Nagpur in October 1956 weeks before his death, left a legacy of constitutionalism, legal protection, education, and religious reorientation that later activists reinterpreted in varied ways (Chapter 80). His centenary in 1990–91 coincided with the Mandal controversy and gave a public focus to his thought: his portraits, statues, and birth anniversary became civic symbols across India, and the slogan "Jai Bhim" served as a greeting and a political call. For many Dalits, the memory of Ambedkar offered a national hero who had also been excluded, a figure outside the Congress and Hindu-nationalist pantheons.

Politically, the principal institutional vehicle was the Bahujan Samaj Party, founded in 1984 by Kanshi Ram, who sought to unite Dalits, backward classes, and religious minorities as the "bahujan," or majority of the people. Under Mayawati, the party led governments in Uttar Pradesh, India's most populous state, in 1995, 1997, 2002–03, and, with an outright majority, from 2007 to 2012. Her rule symbolized a Dalit woman exercising state power and used patronage, administrative appointments, and monuments to Dalit icons, though critics noted corruption, the party's dependence on one leader, and its alliances with other parties. The BSP's electoral strength then declined: it won a single seat in the Uttar Pradesh assembly in 2022 and no seats in the 2024 Lok Sabha election. In the same election, Chandrashekhar Azad, associated with the Bhim Army, won a seat in Uttar Pradesh as leader of the Azad Samaj Party, indicating the emergence of a new, more street-based style of Dalit politics. Other parties in Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, and elsewhere, such as the Viduthalai Chiruthaigal Katchi in Tamil Nadu, also organized Dalit constituencies, and the larger parties competed for Dalit votes with welfare schemes and symbolic gestures.

Legal protection was a major arena. The Atrocities Act of 1989 created special courts and harsher penalties for specified offenses against Scheduled Castes and Tribes. In March 2018, a Supreme Court ruling (Subhash Kashinath Mahajan) introduced safeguards against arrest that critics said weakened the Act; a nationwide Dalit protest on April 2, 2018, was followed by violence and deaths, and Parliament subsequently amended the Act to restore its provisions. The episode showed Dalit organizations' ability to mobilize across states and to influence legislation. Questions of access to justice remained: conviction rates in atrocity cases were low in many states, partly because of witness intimidation, and Dalit organizations pressed for stronger implementation.

Intellectual and cultural expression flourished. Dalit literature, which developed first in Marathi in the 1960s and 1970s with the Dalit Panther movement, spread to Hindi, Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Punjabi, Gujarati, and English, and included autobiographies such as Omprakash Valmiki's Joothan and works by writers including Bama, Sharankumar Limbale, and Baby Kamble, whose accounts of caste experience altered the broader literary landscape. Scholars such as Gopal Guru, Kancha Ilaiah, Anand Teltumbde, and Sukhadeo Thorat developed analyses of caste and inequality, while institutions such as the Dr. Ambedkar Foundation and independent presses created publishing channels. A distinctive religious development was the emergence among Punjab's Dalit communities of the Ravidassia tradition, which, after a 2009 attack on a Ravidassia preacher in Vienna, formally declared itself a separate religion centered on the teachings of the saint Ravidas. Detailed treatment of Dalit literature and media belongs to Chapter 108; here the point is that cultural assertion was part of political mobilization.

Women occupied a central, if often overlooked, place. Dalit women faced both caste and gender discrimination, a combination that scholars and activists described as "double marginalization" and that organizations such as the National Federation of Dalit Women, founded in 1995, addressed. Dalit women activists campaigned against sexual violence, as after the Hathras case in Uttar Pradesh in 2020, and against manual scavenging and for access to education and land. Their testimony was also a primary source for understanding caste as lived experience, since much of the formal record was produced by officials and elites.

Nepal's Dalit movement had its own trajectory. After the 2006 People's Movement, Dalit organizations pressed for constitutional recognition, an anti-untouchability law, and representation. Nepal's 2011 statute criminalized caste-based discrimination, and the 2015 Constitution included a dedicated Article 40 on Dalit rights, including proportional inclusion in state bodies, education, and land provisions. Yet reports of caste-based violence and exclusion continued, including cases involving inter-caste marriage, which suggested that legal recognition coexisted with social resistance. In Pakistan, Hindu Scheduled Caste communities campaigned for recognition within minority politics; in Bangladesh, Dalit and other occupational minorities such as the Rishi and Harijan communities formed rights groups to demand land, housing, and an antidiscrimination law; and in Sri Lanka, Tamil and Malaiyaha activists addressed caste and plantation-labor disadvantage. The global Dalit solidarity networks that emerged after the 2001 World Conference against Racism in Durban are treated only as context here; their international dimensions belong to Chapter 110.

Historical Debate

Have Reservations Transformed Social Hierarchy, or Primarily Increased Representation within Existing Institutions?

The question: India's reservation system has been in operation for decades. Did it change the structure of caste hierarchy, or did it mainly place members of disadvantaged groups into institutions whose underlying arrangements remained largely intact?

Interpretation A — Reservation as structural change: Scholars and advocates point to the growth of a Dalit and OBC educated middle class, the presence of Scheduled Caste and OBC officials, legislators, and local-government representatives, and the shifts in electoral politics. They argue that a generation of beneficiaries achieved educational and occupational mobility that would otherwise have been impossible, altering expectations, creating role models, and changing the composition of institutions that had been nearly exclusively dominated by upper castes.
Interpretation B — Reservation as limited, uneven redistribution: Others stress that reservation affects only the small share of the population that obtains public-sector positions or seats in selective institutions; that benefits are concentrated among relatively advantaged members of each group, a concern central to the 2024 sub-classification case; that vacancies, particularly in higher positions and faculty, often remain unfilled; and that private-sector employment, where much of the economy's growth has occurred, is largely unreserved. On this view, reservation increased representation but did not alter landholding, wealth, or everyday discrimination.

Evidence: Both views draw on census, employment, and educational data; on the National Sample Survey and other surveys comparing group outcomes; on studies of beneficiaries' careers; and on testimony. Scholarly studies generally find measurable gains in education and public employment for Scheduled Castes and OBCs, together with persistent gaps in income, wealth, and landholding, and substantial variation across states. The two interpretations are therefore not wholly opposed: evidence supports both significant gains for beneficiaries and clear limits to the policy's reach.

Why it matters: If reservations are judged by whether they transform hierarchy, they may appear insufficient; if judged by whether they open institutions to groups previously excluded, they have had measurable effects. The choice of criterion shapes arguments about extending, narrowing, sub-classifying, or supplementing the policy with measures such as scholarships, land access, and private-sector initiatives. This debate concerns the policy's effects and design; it does not make the defense of caste discrimination itself an equally supportable position, since discrimination on grounds of caste is prohibited by the constitutions of India and Nepal and by statute.

Section 107.4

Indigenous Communities, Ethnic Recognition, and Social Exclusion

Adivasi and Janajati communities occupy a different position in South Asian social history from Dalits, though activists and scholars sometimes treat the two together. Caste discrimination concerns a hierarchy within settled, usually Hindu-influenced, society that assigns graded status and stigma. Indigenous and ethnic claims, by contrast, concern recognition of distinct peoples, with their own languages, customary institutions, territories, and relationships to forests and land. The categories overlap, as when a tribal community is also poor or a Dalit community is also territorially concentrated, but they raise different questions. Chapter 107 therefore keeps them separate, along with the further distinction, drawn in Section 107.16, between indigenous and ethnic claims and broader questions of language policy.

In India, Scheduled Tribes made up about 8.6 percent of the population in 2011, more than a hundred million people, concentrated in central and eastern India, the Northeast, and parts of the west and south. The Constitution's Fifth and Sixth Schedules provided for special administration in tribal areas, but mineral extraction, dam construction, and forest conservation displaced many communities. The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996 gave village assemblies authority over local resources, and the Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognized individual and community rights of forest dwellers. In 2013 the Supreme Court's ruling on the Niyamgiri hills in Odisha upheld the role of village assemblies in deciding on a bauxite mining project by the Vedanta company, a case frequently cited as a victory for Adivasi rights. Implementation of the Forest Rights Act was uneven, with many claims rejected and states slow to recognize community rights, and in 2019 a Supreme Court order to evict people whose claims had been rejected, later stayed, showed how contested the issue was.

Conflict in central India further complicated the picture. The Maoist insurgency (Naxalism), which was strongest in Adivasi areas of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, and neighboring states, placed Adivasi communities between insurgents and security forces. In 2011 the Supreme Court declared the state-sponsored Salwa Judum militia in Chhattisgarh unconstitutional. The Union government announced a goal of eliminating the insurgency by March 2026 and reported sharp declines in its territorial reach, but these are official statements and independent assessment of the human and social costs remained limited at the chapter's research date. In the Northeast, the ethnic and religious violence in Manipur that began in May 2023 between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities killed more than two hundred people and displaced tens of thousands, and showed how competing claims over Scheduled Tribe status, land, and political representation could escalate into sustained conflict; its political history cannot be treated fully here.

Nepal's Janajati (Adhivasi Janajati) movement, which comprises dozens of officially recognized Indigenous nationalities such as the Tharu, Magar, Tamang, Gurung, Rai, Limbu, and Newar, accounted for more than a third of the population in the 2011 census. Janajati activism gained force in the 1990s and was a central force in the 2006 People's Movement, demanding secularism, a federal structure, proportional representation, and recognition of mother-tongue languages. The 2015 Constitution included many of these elements, but also provoked protest from Madhesi and Tharu communities who objected to the drawing of provincial boundaries and citizenship rules, leading to months of violence and an Indian border blockade (see Chapter 104). The Nepali case showed that inclusion in principle did not settle disputes over territory and representation.

In Bangladesh, indigenous peoples in the Chittagong Hill Tracts, including the Chakma, Marma, and Tripura, had been drawn into conflict since the 1970s after Bengali settlement and military deployment. The Peace Accord of December 2, 1997, established regional councils, but key provisions on land commissions, withdrawal of military camps, and the transfer of powers remained unimplemented or partial in later years, according to indigenous organizations, while the government cited other achievements. Plains indigenous communities such as the Santal faced land dispossession, and a violent eviction in Gaibandha in 2016 became a national controversy. A constitutional amendment of 2011 referred to "tribes, minor races, ethnic sects and communities" and not to indigenous peoples, a terminology disputed by activists. Elsewhere, Sri Lanka's Up-country (Malaiyaha) Tamils, descendants of plantation laborers brought from South India, sought citizenship and rights after independence, and the Vedda community sought recognition of land and culture; in Pakistan, small communities such as the Kalasha and the Baloch and Pashtun movements raised claims of ethnic discrimination and regional marginalization; and in Bhutan, the expulsion in the early 1990s of many ethnic Nepali Lhotshampa, who then lived in camps in eastern Nepal until a large resettlement program to Western countries after 2007, remains the most dramatic example of an ethnic dispute over citizenship in the region.

Across these cases, the main issues were similar: recognition by the state, rights to land and resources, education in mother tongues, political participation, and the effect of development projects on local communities. The state's categories, such as Scheduled Tribe in India or janajati in Nepal, were not simply labels imposed from above, since communities mobilized to be included in or defined through them. Colonial-era classification had shaped these categories (Chapter 73), but postcolonial politics gave them new meanings as claims to rights and resources.

Section 107.5

Gender Equality, Constitutional Rights, and Legal Reform

The legal status of women in South Asia since 1991 illustrates, perhaps more clearly than any other subject, the difference between constitutional guarantee, legislation, judicial interpretation, and enforcement. Every South Asian constitution guaranteed equality of some kind, and by the 2000s every country had ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, though several, including Bangladesh and Pakistan in different forms, entered reservations. The decisive changes came from legislatures, courts, and organized women's movements.

In India, judicial activism and legislation expanded protection. In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), the Supreme Court, responding to a petition by women's groups after the gang rape of a social worker in Rajasthan, laid down binding guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace, later codified in the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act of 2013. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act of 2005 created civil remedies, including residence rights, going beyond the criminal provision on cruelty by husbands and in-laws (Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code). The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act of 2005 made daughters equal coparceners in joint Hindu family property, and the Supreme Court in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (August 2020) clarified that this right did not depend on whether the father was alive in 2005. The December 2012 gang rape and murder of a young woman in Delhi, widely called the Nirbhaya case, brought large protests and the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 2013, which expanded definitions of sexual offences. Yet exceptions remained: Indian law continued to exempt most marital rape from the definition of rape, a position retained in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita of 2023 and challenged in the courts. In other areas, the Supreme Court decriminalized adultery in 2018 (Joseph Shine), arguing that the old provision treated wives as property, and ruled in the 2017 Shayara Bano case that instant triple talaq was unconstitutional, a decision followed by a 2019 statute criminalizing the practice that Muslim women's organizations and others debated, with some welcoming it and others objecting to its criminal penalties.

Sabarimala illustrated the tension between gender equality and religious custom. In September 2018 the Supreme Court held that excluding women of menstruating age from the Sabarimala temple in Kerala violated their rights; the decision provoked mass protests, including by many women who defended the custom, and the case was referred to a larger bench, and the broader legal question had not, as far as could be verified for this chapter, been finally settled. The episode showed that women were not a single constituency and that arguments about religious freedom, custom, and equality intersected.

Pakistan's legal history was marked by the legacy of Zia-ul-Haq's Islamization (Chapter 95). The Hudood Ordinances of 1979 had treated rape complaints as potential evidence of adultery, imprisoning many women; the Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act of 2006 removed rape from their scope. Honour killings, long treated as private or settled through victim-family "forgiveness," were addressed by the 2016 amendment that restricted such pardon. The murder of the social-media figure Qandeel Baloch by her brother in July 2016 prompted the legislation. Provincial laws, including Sindh's 2013 child-marriage act setting the age at eighteen, were more progressive than federal law in some respects. Women's organizations, including the Women's Action Forum (founded 1981) and Aurat Foundation, together with the annual Aurat March from 2018, pressed for further change, and the marches provoked organized religious and political opposition.

Bangladesh combined strong gender-related development outcomes with persistent legal and social limitations. Its Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act of 2010 provided civil remedies, and the Dowry Prohibition Act of 2018 revised an older statute. A 2017 child marriage law retained the legal minimum age of eighteen for women but allowed marriage of younger girls in "special cases" with parental consent and court approval, a provision criticized by rights groups. Personal laws remained religion-specific; Hindu women, for example, lacked equal inheritance rights under their community's law as in many places, and the 1980s discussion of a uniform family code did not advance. Sri Lanka had a long tradition of female suffrage and education but retained community-based family laws, including the Muslim Marriage and Divorce Act, whose provisions on marriage age and the role of quazi courts were debated by Muslim women's groups. Bhutan's Constitution of 2008 guaranteed equal rights, and some regional inheritance customs favored daughters; Maldives' laws combined statutory provisions with interpretation based on Islamic law.

Nepal's reforms illustrate how a legal system can change through litigation and constitution-making. A Supreme Court decision in 2002 and the Country Code amendment of the same year legalized abortion under conditions, ending a regime in which many women were imprisoned for it; the practice of confining menstruating women in sheds (chhaupadi) was banned by the Court in 2005 and criminalized by the Civil Code of 2017, though it persisted in some western districts. The Constitution of 2015 guaranteed women equal ancestral property rights and reserved at least one-third of seats in the federal legislature, as described in the source below.

Section 107.6

Women's Public Participation, Leadership, and Social Transformation

Women's participation in public life expanded in the decades after 1991, though unevenly and through different institutions. In India, the most substantial change occurred at the local level. The 73rd and 74th Amendments of 1992–93 required that one-third of seats and of chairperson positions in rural and urban local bodies be reserved for women, and many states later raised the share to one-half. Within a generation, millions of women had served as village council members and heads, perhaps the largest experiment in political inclusion in the region. Studies found that women representatives sometimes shifted public spending toward drinking water and other priorities identified by women, and that the experience changed norms about women's political roles. Studies also identified the phenomenon of "proxy" representation, in which male relatives (the sarpanch-pati phenomenon) exercised the authority nominally held by elected women, and the government itself acknowledged the problem. Women from Dalit and Adivasi communities faced particular obstacles, including violence in some cases after contesting elections.

At the national level, the picture was slower. Women remained well below one-third of the Lok Sabha for decades. After repeated failed attempts since 1996, Parliament passed the Constitution (106th Amendment), the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, in September 2023, reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women, but making its operation conditional on the next census and the delimitation of constituencies that would follow, so that it could not take effect before the elections after those processes are complete. This sequence, with caste enumeration included in the forthcoming population census, linked questions of gender and caste representation: critics demanded sub-quotas for OBC women, and supporters stressed that the measure could not be delayed indefinitely.

The implementation timetable became a concrete parliamentary controversy in April 2026. The government proposed the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill to accelerate women's reservation through constituency delimitation based on earlier census data and changes to parliamentary representation. On April 17, 2026, the Lok Sabha failed to approve that constitutional amendment by the required special majority; the related delimitation and Union Territories bills were not advanced. The failure did not repeal the 2023 women's reservation constitutional amendment, but it left the actual allocation of one-third of seats unresolved. The Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs records the failed bill and the withdrawal of related legislation. Arguments over representation, the size of the Lok Sabha, regional population change, and the rights of historically disadvantaged women remained entwined.

Women's leadership in other countries followed distinct paths. In Pakistan, Benazir Bhutto became the first woman to head the government of a Muslim-majority state in 1988, and the 2002 reforms and later laws maintained reserved seats for women in national and provincial assemblies, with a large number of women legislators and, after 2000, local-body reservations. Bangladesh's politics was dominated for decades by two women, Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia, whose rivalry (Chapter 102) rested on dynastic legitimacy as daughters or widows of national leaders, which complicated the interpretation of their rise as an indicator of broader gender equality, even as the country had reserved seats for women in parliament and a strong presence of women in local government. Sri Lanka had elected the world's first woman prime minister, Sirimavo Bandaranaike, in 1960; Chandrika Kumaratunga served as president from 1994 to 2005; a 2016 amendment mandated that twenty-five percent of local-government candidates be women; and Harini Amarasuriya became prime minister in September 2024. Yet Sri Lanka's female representation in national parliament remained low in comparison with its education and health indicators, showing that achievement in one area did not automatically translate into others.

Nepal's constitution mandates that at least one-third of each party's elected members in the federal legislature be women, and women held the presidency (Bidya Devi Bhandari, 2015–2023) and a large share of local posts after the 2017 elections, in which the legal requirement that mayors or deputy mayors be of different sexes produced many women deputy mayors. Following the youth-led uprising of September 2025, Sushila Karki, a former chief justice, became Nepal's first woman prime minister on September 12, 2025, heading an interim government that arranged the election of March 5, 2026 (see Chapter 104 for these political events). In Bhutan, democratic transition produced slow growth in women's parliamentary representation, and in Maldives women's participation in politics remained limited. Women also led movements: the Chipko environmental protests of the 1970s, the anti-liquor campaigns in Andhra Pradesh in the 1990s, the Self-Employed Women's Association, which since 1972 organized informal workers, the Shaheen Bagh sit-in against the citizenship law in Delhi in 2019–20, and Pakistani women's marches show a continuous history of collective action.

Restrictions and burdens continued alongside these gains. Women performed most unpaid household and care work, a burden measured in time-use surveys and examined in relation to employment in Chapter 106. Fear of violence and norms about honor limited women's mobility in public space, affecting education, work, and political participation. Women in professions faced unequal advancement, and in many communities families controlled women's choices about marriage and movement. The contrast between women's presence in representative bodies and their limited authority within institutions such as political parties, panchayats dominated by male networks, courts, and religious bodies remained a central theme. Women's movements also differed in their goals and strategies, with some emphasizing legal equality, others social welfare and livelihoods, and others religious reinterpretation, as in Muslim women's organizations that argued for gender-just readings of Islamic law.

Section 107.7

Marriage, Family, Household Authority, and Changing Social Expectations

The household remained the central institution of social life, and its transformation after 1991 was gradual and uneven. Marriage in most South Asian communities continued to be arranged or negotiated by families, but the meaning of "arranged" shifted. Survey and ethnographic evidence suggested a spectrum: families arranged marriages with the consent of the couple; couples chose each other and obtained parental approval; and a minority married against family wishes. Educated urban youth increasingly expected some say in the choice of spouse, and matrimonial advertisements, later online matchmaking sites, reproduced caste and community preferences even while they enabled new forms of selection. Age at marriage rose with female education, and the national family health surveys documented declines in child marriage in India, though the National Family Health Survey of 2019–21 still found about a quarter of women aged 20 to 24 had been married before eighteen. Rates remained higher in Bangladesh and in parts of Nepal and Pakistan, with strong regional and class variation.

Dowry, the transfer of money and goods from a bride's family to the groom's, was prohibited in India in 1961 and in Bangladesh and Pakistan by later statutes, yet continued and in many places expanded as marriage markets commercialized. Dowry-related violence and deaths remained a persistent concern, recorded in thousands of cases annually in India, and prompted legislation including Section 498A. The institution's spread to communities and regions where bride-price or other transfers had been more common showed that it was not simply an ancient survival but a practice responding to modern consumption and status competition. Scholars also noted that the misuse of the anti-dowry cruelty provision became a subject of political and judicial debate, with courts urging safeguards while women's organizations warned against weakening a protection for victims.

Inter-caste and interreligious relationships became flashpoints. Couples who married across lines of caste or religion could face family violence or collective community sanctions. In Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, caste councils (khap panchayats) condemned same-gotra and inter-caste marriages, and the 2007 killing of the couple Manoj and Babli led to a high-profile trial. In 2018 the Supreme Court (Shakti Vahini v. Union of India) issued guidelines directing authorities to protect couples and to treat such group interference as unlawful. In Pakistan, honour killings of women, and sometimes men, for choosing partners continued to be reported in the hundreds annually in rights-group counts, though counting was uncertain. In India, the Special Marriage Act of 1954 allowed civil marriage but required a notice period, criticized as exposing couples to family and community pressure. From 2020, several states enacted laws regulating religious conversion through marriage, with supporters claiming they protected women from coercion and opponents arguing that they targeted interfaith couples (Section 107.10). In the Hadiya case of 2018, the Supreme Court affirmed the right of an adult woman to choose her faith and spouse.

The structure of households also changed. Total fertility fell substantially: India's rate reached about two children per woman in the 2019–21 survey, around or below replacement level, and was similar in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Nepal, though higher in Pakistan, whose rate remained above three. Smaller families altered the allocation of resources among children and elders. Households were more commonly nuclear in structure in cities, though many families maintained multigenerational ties, through shared property, remittances, and care. Migration, including labor migration to the Gulf and to cities (Chapters 97 and 106), meant that many households were organized across distance: women managed farms and finances in the absence of husbands, children were raised by grandparents, and remittances funded schooling and weddings. These conditions both increased some women's autonomy in decision-making and imposed new burdens on them. The family also remained a source of care, credit, and security in the absence of strong public welfare, which helps explain why individuals who criticized family authority continued to depend on kin.

Legal reform and community practice interacted with considerable complexity. Hindu, Muslim, Christian, and Parsi communities in India were governed by different personal laws; in Pakistan and Bangladesh, Hindu marriage and family matters were regulated by community law, with Pakistan passing a Hindu Marriage Act in 2017 that for the first time provided a federal framework for registration and divorce among Hindus. Child marriage remained prevalent in some rural districts despite law, and domestic violence was widely underreported. Generalizations about "South Asian family values" thus obscured considerable variety, including matrilineal communities such as the Nairs of Kerala (historically) and the Khasi of Meghalaya, regions of Bhutan where inheritance often passed through women, and differences among urban professionals, rural laborers, and migrant workers.

Everyday Life and Social History

Marriage, Education, Household Authority, and Generational Change

Consider how a family in a mid-sized South Asian town might have experienced the decades after 1991. A grandmother may have married in her mid-teens to a husband selected by elders, never attended school, and spent decades within her husband's household. Her daughter might have finished secondary school, married in her early twenties after meeting prospective grooms arranged by relatives, and worked in a clinic or school. Her granddaughter might be completing a college degree, using a smartphone to communicate with peers, and expecting to take part in choosing a partner, though perhaps still seeking family approval and still conscious of caste, religion, and income as factors.

This composite illustration, drawn from patterns documented in surveys and ethnographies and not from any particular family, shows change without implying uniform progress. Education and employment expanded the younger women's choices, but caste and class boundaries often continued to shape whom they could marry, dowry expectations could persist, and the burden of housework and care remained heavy. Brothers and fathers might support a sister's schooling and yet resist her marriage outside the community. In migrant households, the same women might manage finances and farms while husbands worked abroad and still defer to in-laws in public. Historians must treat such experiences as varied, emphasizing the range of household strategies rather than a single trajectory from "tradition" to "modernity."

Section 107.8

Education, Literacy, and Intergenerational Social Mobility

Education expanded more rapidly after 1991 than in any previous period, and it became the principal route through which families sought mobility. Literacy rose across the region. India's literacy rate was about 74 percent in the 2011 census, with female literacy 65 percent, and recent national surveys placed overall literacy near or above the high seventies. Bangladesh and Nepal reached literacy rates in the mid-seventies; Pakistan's rate was lower, in the low sixties in recent official estimates; and Sri Lanka, with free education since the 1940s, exceeded ninety percent. Figures vary by survey and definition, and literacy as measured by simple tests says little about the quality of learning.

Government programs shaped enrollment. India's constitutional amendment of 2002 made elementary education a fundamental right, and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act of 2009 required schools to be available and mandated that private schools reserve 25 percent of entry-level places for children from disadvantaged groups. Midday meal schemes expanded enrollment, particularly of girls and poor children. Bangladesh's stipend programs for secondary-school girls, supported by NGOs such as BRAC, were credited with raising female enrollment to parity or above, a notable achievement given the country's income level. Nepal's constitution established education as a right, and Dalit scholarship schemes aimed to reduce disparities. Pakistan faced very high numbers of out-of-school children, estimated at over twenty million, with severe gender and regional gaps, especially in Balochistan and parts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and violent opposition to girls' schooling by militants was dramatized by the attack on the teenage activist Malala Yousafzai in October 2012, who with Kailash Satyarthi received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014. Sri Lanka's civil war disrupted education in the north and east, and Bhutan expanded schooling under a policy of free education. Higher education also grew: India's gross enrollment ratio in higher education reached the high twenties by the early 2020s, with rapid growth in private universities.

Inequalities of quality and access accompanied expansion. Large numbers of children moved to private schools, including inexpensive "low-fee" schools, because families doubted the quality of public schools. Learning surveys, such as the Annual Status of Education Report in India and similar citizen-led assessments in Pakistan, repeatedly found that many children in the upper primary grades could not read at their grade level. Language of instruction mattered: English-medium schooling carried prestige and economic returns, while mother-tongue instruction was often better for early learning but associated in the public mind with less opportunity (Section 107.16). In India, a coaching industry, centered in cities like Kota, prepared students for competitive entrance examinations, with high costs and pressures. Dalit, Adivasi, and Muslim students lagged on average in higher education, and universities reported discrimination, as in the case of Rohith Vemula. Religious education also played a role: madrasa networks in Pakistan and Bangladesh (including the Qawmi and government-recognized Alia streams) educated millions, while missionary and community-run schools in many countries educated children of varying faiths.

Did expansion weaken hierarchy? Evidence suggests that educational gaps between castes and between boys and girls narrowed in some respects, especially at primary and secondary levels, but that disparities persisted in higher education, in the quality of institutions attended, and in labor-market outcomes. Studies of employment found that even among graduates, upper-caste applicants often had advantages through networks, language skills, and information, and that caste and religion could influence hiring. Families with more resources invested in private tutoring and elite institutions, so that new educational opportunities could reproduce advantage. At the same time, first-generation graduates changed the expectations of their families and communities, and many drew on education to enter government service, teaching, politics, and law.

Cause and Effect

Education, Political Participation, Legal Reform, and Changing Social Status

Long-term causes

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century reform and anti-caste movements; constitutional commitments to equality; the expansion of state and community schooling after independence.

Short-term causes

Liberalization and rising demand for skilled labor; school-access legislation; scholarship, stipend, and reservation programs; political competition for lower-status voters.

Immediate effects

Higher enrollment and literacy, especially among girls and previously excluded groups; growth of educated Dalit, Adivasi, and female cohorts; expansion of private schooling.

Long-term consequences

Changing expectations about marriage, work, and political participation; persistent gaps in school quality and labor-market outcomes; new forms of stratification between private and public institutions.

Historical complexity: Education did not act alone. Its effects depended on whether jobs were available (Chapter 106), whether families could afford quality schools, whether law was enforced, and whether political movements gave educated people channels for action. A single-cause explanation of social change, whether "education" or "law" or "democracy," would therefore be inadequate.

Historical Debate

Have Legal Reforms Produced Corresponding Changes in Gender Relations and Everyday Social Practice?

The question: Since the 1990s, South Asian states passed laws on women's property, domestic violence, harassment, political representation, and marriage. Did these laws change social practice, or did social practice change independently, or not at all?

Interpretation A — Law as catalyst: Scholars and legal advocates argue that reforms such as local-body reservations, the 2005 inheritance amendment, and workplace and domestic-violence laws created new resources and norms. They changed what women could claim in courts, gave activists instruments for campaigns, and altered public expectations about acceptable behavior, even when implementation was imperfect. The 73rd Amendment, for example, produced a generation of women with experience in public office.
Interpretation B — Law constrained by social structure: Others emphasize that many women did not know of or could not use new rights; that family pressure discouraged daughters from claiming inheritance; that courts and police were often inaccessible; that dowry, child marriage, and violence persisted despite prohibition; and that economic and social changes, such as education, migration, and falling fertility, may have had greater effects than statutes. On this view, law followed social change as often as it led it.

Evidence: Both views draw on survey data on inheritance, domestic violence, and marriage; case studies of reservation in local government; court records; and ethnographic work. Studies of inheritance reform found that many daughters did not claim property, partly to preserve family relations, but also found rising claims in some regions where legal awareness and women's organizations were active. Such findings suggest that legal reform interacted with social conditions and that effects varied by region and community.

Why it matters: The answer shapes policy priorities: whether to invest in legal reform, in enforcement and legal aid, or in education and economic opportunity. It also shapes how historians evaluate reform movements, which can be seen either as agents or as symptoms of change.

Division III · Sections 107.9–107.12

Religion, Minority Rights, and Communal Relations

Section 107.9

Religious Majorities, Minorities, and the Changing Social Landscape

The religious composition of South Asian states differs sharply, and this difference shapes how "majority" and "minority" operate in each. In India, the 2011 census recorded about 80 percent Hindus, 14 percent Muslims, and smaller shares of Christians (2.3 percent), Sikhs (1.7 percent), Buddhists, and Jains, with the Muslim population among the world's largest in absolute numbers. Pakistan is overwhelmingly Muslim, with Hindus and Christians each under two percent in the census of 2017, and Ahmadis, who identify as Muslims but are legally declared non-Muslim, constituting a further minority. Bangladesh's 2022 census counted roughly 91 percent Muslims and 8 percent Hindus, with smaller Buddhist and Christian communities concentrated among indigenous peoples. Sri Lanka's 2024 population census, with results published in 2026, recorded approximately 69.8 percent Buddhists, 12.6 percent Hindus, 10.7 percent Muslims, 5.6 percent Roman Catholics, and 1.3 percent other Christians. The Buddhist majority was predominantly Sinhalese, while Tamil communities were represented among Hindus and Christians; these general patterns do not erase religious and ethnic diversity. Nepal's 2021 census recorded about 81 percent Hindus, with Buddhists, Muslims, Kirat followers, and Christians as significant minorities. Bhutan's population is predominantly Vajrayana Buddhist with a Hindu minority, and Maldives' citizens are, by law, Muslim.

Statistical source: Sri Lanka Department of Census and Statistics, Census of Population and Housing 2024, final report (results available in 2026).

These figures conceal internal diversity. Hinduism includes Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta, and numerous local, caste-linked, and reformist traditions, and "Hindu" as a political category was itself shaped by nineteenth- and twentieth-century developments (Chapter 74). Islam in South Asia includes Sunni Barelvi, Deobandi, and Ahl-e-Hadith traditions, Shia communities, Ismailis, Bohras, and Ahmadis, together with devotional Sufi traditions centered on shrines. Christianity encompasses ancient Syrian Christians of Kerala, Catholic communities along the coasts, Protestant missions, and fast-growing Pentecostal churches, with many converts from Dalit and Adivasi communities. Buddhism includes the Theravada of Sri Lanka, the Vajrayana of Bhutan and the Himalayas, and the neo-Buddhism of Ambedkar's followers. Sikhs and Jains form communities with distinctive institutions. Class, language, region, and caste cut across these categories, so that a Muslim weaver in Uttar Pradesh, a Muslim industrialist in Mumbai, and a Muslim fisher in Bangladesh share a religion but not an experience.

Religious institutions performed social functions beyond worship. Temples, mosques, gurdwaras, churches, monasteries, and shrines ran schools, hospitals, and charitable kitchens (the Sikh langar being the best known); religious endowments held substantial land and property; and religious networks organized pilgrimage, funerals, and welfare. Madrasas and missionary schools educated millions. In Pakistan, Bangladesh, and India, groups such as the Tablighi Jamaat and the Jamaat-e-Islami, in India, groups of the Ramakrishna Mission, and in Sri Lanka, Buddhist temple networks, provided education and community services. Political parties also used religious organizations as bases, and states regulated religious endowments to different degrees: South Indian state governments controlled Hindu temples to a degree that became a political issue, while the management of Muslim endowments (waqf) was governed by separate statutes, which Parliament amended in 2025, a change contested in the courts and politically.

The relationship between religion and the state differs. India's Constitution declares the country a "sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic" (the words "socialist" and "secular" were added to the Preamble in 1976), guarantees freedom of religion, and permits state regulation of religious practice in the interest of public order and social reform. Pakistan's Constitution declares Islam the state religion, requires laws to conform to Islamic injunctions, and, since the second amendment of 1974, defines Ahmadis as non-Muslims. Bangladesh's Constitution began in 1972 with secularism as a principle, was amended in 1977 and 1988 to remove it and establish Islam as the state religion, and was amended again in 2011 to restore secularism while retaining Islam as the state religion. Sri Lanka's Constitution gives Buddhism "the foremost place" and obliges the state to protect it while guaranteeing freedom of religion. Bhutan's Constitution of 2008 describes Buddhism as the country's spiritual heritage. Maldives' Constitution of 2008 makes Islam the religion of the state and denies citizenship to non-Muslims. Nepal declared itself a secular state in 2006, a decision confirmed in the 2015 Constitution, whose definition of secularism includes protection of religion and culture "handed down from ancient times," a formula that Hindu nationalists and others construed differently, and that coexisted with a prohibition on proselytizing. These variations show that no single model of church–state relations or minority protection applies across the region.

Section 107.10

Religious Freedom, Citizenship, and Minority Protections

India's constitutional approach to religion, often described as "principled distance," is a distinctive form of secularism, different from the strict separation associated with France or the United States. It guarantees equal freedom of conscience and the right to practice and propagate religion (Article 25), protects the rights of religious minorities to establish and administer educational institutions (Article 30), and allows the state to reform Hindu institutions and regulate secular activities connected with religion. The state does not establish a religion, but it supports and regulates religious institutions in various ways, and its personal law system applies different family laws to different communities. The Constitution's directive principle on a uniform civil code (Article 44) expressed a long-term aspiration that remained contested: advocates argue it would secure gender equality and national integration, while opponents, including many minority organizations and some women's groups, warn that it could impose majority norms or ignore diversity. Goa has retained a civil code from Portuguese rule, and Uttarakhand enacted a state uniform civil code in 2024, in force from January 2025; whether such state-level measures are constitutionally valid and how they should be assessed remain subjects of debate.

The history of personal law shows how family law became a symbol of identity. In the 1985 Shah Bano case, the Supreme Court granted a divorced Muslim woman maintenance under the general criminal law; after protests by Muslim leaders, Parliament passed the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act in 1986, limiting the ruling's reach. This episode, often cited by Hindu nationalists as evidence of "appeasement" and by others as evidence of political compromise, shaped debates for decades. In 2017 the Court, in Shayara Bano, declared instant triple talaq unconstitutional, and Parliament in 2019 made it a criminal offence. Similar debates concerned Christian and Hindu law: the Hindu Code reforms of the 1950s (Chapter 89), the 2005 inheritance amendment, and Christian divorce laws reformed in 2001.

Citizenship became a central issue in India after 2019. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act of December 2019 created an expedited route to citizenship for members of six religious minority communities (Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians) from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India before the end of 2014, excluding Muslims. The government presented it as a humanitarian measure for persecuted minorities; critics argued that it introduced a religious criterion into citizenship and, combined with a proposed national register of citizens, could endanger Muslims lacking documents. In Assam, a National Register of Citizens process, supervised by the Supreme Court, published a final list on August 31, 2019, that excluded about 1.9 million of those who applied; the exclusion affected people of various religions and was contested by both those who thought the list excluded too many and those who thought it excluded too few. Mass protests against the Act, including the Shaheen Bagh sit-in led by women in Delhi, preceded the violence of February 2020 (Section 107.11). The government notified the rules to implement the Act in March 2024; legal challenges were pending before the Supreme Court as far as could be verified for this chapter. A distinction is necessary between what the statute provides, which is documentary, what its critics allege about its possible consequences, and what government has said about its intentions.

Religious conversion has been regulated in several jurisdictions. Several Indian states passed "Freedom of Religion" Acts beginning with Odisha (1967) and Madhya Pradesh (1968), which prohibit conversion by force, fraud, or inducement, and further states, including Uttar Pradesh and others, enacted stricter laws with provisions on interfaith marriage in the 2020s. Supporters argue that these laws protect vulnerable people from coercion; opponents argue that they are vague, place burdens on converts, and are used against Christians and Muslims, citing reported prosecutions and vigilante incidents. In 1977 the Supreme Court (Rev. Stainislaus) held that the right to "propagate" did not include a right to convert others. Nepal's 2015 Constitution prohibited conversion, and the Penal Code of 2017 criminalized it, which affected Christian communities; Bhutan restricted proselytism, and Maldives prohibits the practice of other religions publicly. Pakistan's blasphemy provisions (Sections 295-B and 295-C, carrying severe penalties, introduced in the 1980s) and the anti-Ahmadi ordinance of 1984 restricted religious expression, and false or exaggerated accusations led to mob violence, as in the 2011 killing of the Punjab governor Salmaan Taseer, the lynching of the student Mashal Khan in 2017, the Sialkot lynching of a Sri Lankan factory manager in December 2021, and attacks on Christian neighborhoods in Jaranwala in August 2023. In Bangladesh, allegations of insulting religion, including social-media posts, sometimes led to prosecution or mob attacks, as in the Cumilla and Durga Puja violence of October 2021, and reports of attacks on Hindu homes and temples followed the political upheaval of August 2024, though the scale, causes, and attribution of those attacks were disputed by official and non-governmental sources.

Access to public institutions also differs by religion. In India, Scheduled Caste status is limited to Hindus, Sikhs, and Buddhists, so Dalit Muslims and Christians cannot claim benefits meant for Scheduled Castes, even though their social disadvantages are often similar; commissions, including the Ranganath Misra Commission (2007) and a later one appointed in 2022, examined the issue, and the Supreme Court has considered petitions. The Sachar Committee report of 2006 documented the relatively disadvantaged position of Muslims in education, employment, and credit, and led to a variety of programs, though their effect has been disputed. Minority educational institutions and the Aligarh Muslim University's minority status have also been the subject of litigation. Such issues show that minority protection involved questions of social and economic status as well as freedom of worship.

Historical Vocabulary
Personal Law, Secularism, Communalism, and Minority Rights

Personal law refers to family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption) that applies according to a person's religious community, a system inherited from colonial governance and retained in several states. Secularism has different meanings: in India, equal respect for all religions and state neutrality with some regulation; in Bangladesh, a constitutional principle that coexists with a state religion; in Nepal, a concept defined together with protection of ancient traditions. Communalism, in South Asian usage, refers to the political mobilization of religious identity in opposition to other communities, and is distinct from the English sense of "communal" meaning "shared"; scholars debate its use because it can seem to treat communities as natural units. Minority rights encompass protections for religious, linguistic, and ethnic minorities, including rights to maintain institutions, equal citizenship, and, in some contexts, special representation.

These terms are not neutral. Different political actors define "secularism" and "communalism" in ways that serve their positions, and historians have to identify who is using the terms and for what purposes.

Section 107.11

Communal Politics, Religious Mobilization, and Social Conflict

Communal violence in post-1991 South Asia was not a continuous, ancient conflict but a series of episodes with specific political and social circumstances, organized by identifiable actors, shaped by local conditions, and variously handled by states. Scholars such as Ashutosh Varshney, Paul Brass, and others have debated the causes, with Brass emphasizing "institutionalized riot systems" in which local political entrepreneurs organize violence, and Varshney emphasizing the role of civic associations that cross communities in preventing it. This section examines major episodes without attributing responsibility to whole religious communities.

The Ayodhya dispute was central. The Ram Janmabhoomi movement of the 1980s and early 1990s, led by the Vishva Hindu Parishad and supported by the Bharatiya Janata Party (Chapter 99), demanded a temple at a site in Ayodhya occupied by a sixteenth-century mosque, the Babri Masjid. Mass mobilization culminated on December 6, 1992, when a crowd of activists demolished the mosque in the presence of security forces, despite assurances given to the Supreme Court. Violence followed in several cities, with Mumbai in December 1992 and January 1993 experiencing riots that killed roughly nine hundred people by the count of the Srikrishna Commission, most of them Muslims, followed by serial bombings in March 1993 that killed many. The Liberhan Commission, which reported in 2009, assigned blame to leaders of the movement; a special court in 2020 acquitted all surviving accused in the criminal case on the demolition for lack of conclusive evidence. In November 2019 the Supreme Court, in a unanimous ruling, awarded the disputed land to a trust for building a Ram temple, directed that an alternative five acres be allotted to the Sunni Central Waqf Board, and described the 1992 demolition as a violation of law; the temple's consecration took place on January 22, 2024. Supporters saw the outcome as the fulfillment of a long-standing devotional aspiration and the resolution of a legal dispute; critics argued that it rewarded a movement that had broken the law. Historians treat the factual sequence, the court's reasoning, and political interpretations as distinct matters.

Gujarat in 2002 was another. On February 27, 2002, a coach of the Sabarmati Express was set on fire at Godhra, killing 59 people, mostly Hindu pilgrims returning from Ayodhya. Over the following weeks, large-scale violence occurred in many parts of Gujarat, in which, according to official figures presented to Parliament in 2005, 1,044 people died (790 Muslims and 254 Hindus), with other estimates higher; many thousands were displaced. The National Human Rights Commission and later scholarly and non-governmental investigations criticized the state government and police for failure to protect victims, and some alleged that the violence was organized, while the state government and others contested these characterizations and emphasized the provocation of Godhra. The origin of the Godhra fire itself was investigated and disputed by different commissions. A Special Investigation Team appointed by the Supreme Court reported to the courts; in June 2022 the Court dismissed a petition challenging its conclusion that there was no larger conspiracy at the top. Trials produced convictions in several cases, including one involving the Bilkis Bano gang rape, in which the Supreme Court in January 2024 set aside the premature release of eleven convicts. Other major episodes included the anti-Sikh violence of 1984 (Chapter 94), the anti-Christian violence in Kandhamal, Odisha, in 2008, the violence in Muzaffarnagar in 2013, and the Delhi violence of February 2020, which killed at least fifty people, a majority of them Muslims, amid protests over the citizenship law.

Sri Lanka's conflicts combined religion with ethnicity and language. After the civil war ended in 2009 (Chapter 103), organizations of Buddhist nationalist monks such as the Bodu Bala Sena (founded 2012) campaigned against Muslim practices and businesses, and anti-Muslim riots occurred at Aluthgama in June 2014 and in Kandy district in 2018. After the Easter bombings of April 2019, carried out by a small group of Islamist extremists and killing more than 250 people, anti-Muslim attacks occurred in parts of the country, and in 2020–21 the compulsory cremation of those who died of COVID-19, including Muslims whose faith prescribes burial, was reversed after protest. The treatment of Tamil communities and the legacy of the war are discussed in Chapters 96 and 103.

Pakistan experienced sectarian violence among Muslim communities and against minorities. Attacks on Shia communities, including the Hazara of Quetta in 2013, attacks on Ahmadi mosques in Lahore in May 2010, and bombings of Sufi shrines in Lahore (2010) and Sehwan (2017) were carried out by militant groups, some of which, such as Lashkar-e-Jhangvi, drew on sectarian ideologies that scholars link to the Afghan wars and Zia-era policies (Chapter 95). Christian and Hindu communities suffered from blasphemy accusations and from forced-conversion allegations, which provincial and national legislatures considered with limited success. Bangladesh's minority concerns involved attacks on Hindus, Buddhists, and Ahmadis, often connected with land disputes and local politics as well as religious animosity, as in the Ramu violence against Buddhists in 2012. In Bhutan and Maldives, the main religious tensions were over state restrictions on non-dominant religions. Nepal's conflicts over secularism, including the movement for a Hindu state, were largely political and constitutional, but the conversion law and incidents against Christians reflected tension.

Interpretation of these episodes requires attention to the roles of organized groups, police and government, electoral competition, local grievances, and media. Studies often found that violence clustered at moments of political competition, and that it was not simply spontaneous. The responses of states varied: in some cases commissions and courts investigated, convicted, and compensated, while in others, impunity prevailed. The following section turns to the other side of this history.

Section 107.12

Interreligious Coexistence, Local Institutions, and Social Repair

A history that recorded only violence would misrepresent the daily lives of most South Asians, who lived in religiously mixed neighborhoods, worked in shared markets, and took part in each other's festivals without conflict. Everyday coexistence was not a romantic myth, but a documented feature of social life, as also an unequal and sometimes fragile one, shaped by power and by background hierarchies of caste, class, and religion. Understanding it matters because it shows that polarization was produced by identifiable processes rather than being the natural condition of a diverse society.

Shared sacred spaces and practices were widespread. Sufi shrines such as the dargah of Moinuddin Chishti at Ajmer drew Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and Christian devotees, as did the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi and shrines across Punjab and Sindh. In Sri Lanka, the Kataragama complex attracted Buddhists, Hindus, and Muslims, and Sri Pada (Adam's Peak) was a pilgrimage site for several religions. In Kerala, the Sabarimala pilgrimage includes a visit to a shrine associated with a Muslim figure, Vavar. In Bengal, Baul and Fakir traditions blended Vaishnava and Sufi themes, and in Bangladesh and eastern India, neighbors across communities participated in each other's festivals, including Durga Puja and Eid. The Sikh langar fed people of all backgrounds, and the Kartarpur Corridor, opened on November 9, 2019, allowed Indian pilgrims to visit a major Sikh shrine in Pakistan without a visa, despite broader tensions between the two states. Commercial networks also crossed religious lines: Muslim weavers and Hindu traders, Hindu moneylenders and Muslim customers, and mixed guilds in towns such as Varanasi, where Muslim weavers produced saris sold by Hindu merchants.

Civic associations and local institutions played a part in repairing relations after violence. After the Mumbai riots of 1992–93, the police commissioner promoted "mohalla committees," neighborhood peace committees including members of different communities, which were credited in some areas, such as Bhiwandi, with helping to prevent the recurrence of violence. Varshney's comparative study of Indian cities found that those with strong associations spanning religious communities, such as business groups, trade unions, and professional bodies, were less likely to experience riots than those where associational life was separated by community, a conclusion debated by other scholars who emphasize political and police factors. After Gujarat 2002, lawyers, activists, and survivors pursued justice in the courts over many years, and relief and rehabilitation by community groups, including Muslim organizations and some Hindu volunteers, supplemented inadequate state provision. Citizens' groups also documented violence and sought accountability. After the Easter bombings in Sri Lanka, Buddhist, Christian, Hindu, and Muslim leaders held joint events. In Pakistan, the Edhi Foundation, founded by Abdul Sattar Edhi, ran ambulances and shelters that served people of all faiths, and individuals and groups defended persecuted minorities, sometimes at great risk, as in the case of Salmaan Taseer. Interfaith dialogue organizations, peace education programs, and joint initiatives by artists and musicians also developed, though their effect is hard to measure.

Political change could alter local relationships. Longstanding mixed neighborhoods became more segregated after violence, with people moving to areas where their community predominated; in Ahmedabad, for example, the proportion of residents living in single-community neighborhoods rose after 2002, and many Muslim families moved to the Juhapura area, a pattern documented by researchers. Social boycotts, the labeling of businesses by religion, and online mobilization changed the climate for coexistence. Conversely, local solidarity could persist despite national polarization; during public crises such as floods and the COVID-19 pandemic, community groups, mosques, gurdwaras, and temples provided relief to neighbors regardless of faith, even as other episodes of stigmatization of particular groups occurred.

The overall lesson is that communal relations were historically produced and contested. Coexistence and conflict coexisted in the same societies, and which one prevailed in a given place and moment depended on political competition, state action, local institutions, economic relationships, and the choices of individuals. It would be wrong to infer either that diversity inevitably caused conflict or that conflict was unimportant. These are matters of historical analysis, and the next division turns to categories of social inclusion that raised different issues of rights and recognition.

Division IV · Sections 107.13–107.16

Sexuality, Identity, Disability, and the Boundaries of Social Inclusion

Section 107.13

LGBTQ+ Rights, Law, and Changing Social Attitudes

One important strand of the legal history of sexual minorities in South Asia begins with a colonial statute. Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, drafted in the 1830s and enacted in 1860, criminalized "carnal intercourse against the order of nature," and influenced criminal law, with variations, in British-ruled India and territories including those that became Pakistan and Bangladesh; Sri Lanka developed related colonial-era provisions. Maldives followed a different legal trajectory: its modern criminal provisions concerning same-sex conduct are found in the 2014 Penal Code, notably Sections 411 and 412, and must be understood in their own constitutional and Islamic-law setting rather than as a direct transplantation of Section 377. Historians such as Ruth Vanita and Saleem Kidwai have shown that precolonial South Asian cultures included varied traditions of same-sex love and gender variance in literature, courts, and devotional life, and that the criminalizing law was a colonial import, though this should not be taken to mean that earlier societies were free of stigma. The modern movement for sexual-minority rights emerged in the 1990s, when activists in Delhi and Mumbai responded to the AIDS crisis and police harassment, publishing the report Less Than Gay (1991) and forming groups such as the Naz Foundation (India) Trust, which in 2001 filed the case that eventually reached the Supreme Court.

The Indian litigation unfolded over seventeen years. In July 2009, the Delhi High Court in Naz Foundation read down Section 377 to exclude consensual adult conduct; in December 2013 the Supreme Court in Suresh Kumar Koushal reversed that decision, saying that only a "minuscule fraction" of the population was affected and that change was a matter for Parliament. After a 2017 judgment recognizing a fundamental right to privacy (Puttaswamy), a five-judge Constitution Bench decided Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India on September 6, 2018, unanimously holding that Section 377, to the extent that it criminalized consensual sexual conduct between adults, violated the Constitution's guarantees of equality, non-discrimination, expression, and personal liberty, and overruling Koushal. The judgment emphasized "constitutional morality" in preference to prevailing public morality, and treated sexual orientation as an intrinsic part of identity. The Court did not address marriage, adoption, or antidiscrimination law, and the provision continued to apply to non-consensual acts.

Paraphrase of the holding and reasoning: The Court held Section 377 unconstitutional as it applied to consensual conduct between adults, concluding that it violated Articles 14, 15, 19, and 21. It reasoned that sexual orientation is a natural and immutable aspect of identity, that discrimination on this basis violates equality, that the Constitution's protection of individual dignity prevailed over majoritarian social morality, and that the earlier Koushal decision was wrongly reasoned. Several opinions acknowledged that members of the community had long faced stigma, blackmail, and harassment.

Analysis: The judgment shows how courts recast a social question as one of constitutional rights, drawing on comparative jurisprudence and on petitioners' testimony. It documents the legal change but not the social one: it cannot show how police behaved afterward, how families reacted, or how people experienced everyday discrimination. It also reflects the perspective of judges, and the opinions differ on reasoning. For social consequences historians need surveys, testimony from activists, and records of discrimination cases.

Marriage equality was a separate matter. In Supriyo Chakraborty v. Union of India, decided on October 17, 2023, a five-judge bench unanimously held that there is no fundamental right to marry under the Constitution, and, by majority, declined to recognize same-sex marriage or civil unions or to read the Special Marriage Act as including same-sex couples, leaving the matter to Parliament. The Chief Justice, in a minority opinion, would have recognized a right to civil union, a view shared in part by one other judge; all judges noted the government's statement that it would form a committee to examine entitlements for same-sex couples. Review petitions were dismissed in January 2025. The two decisions thus illustrate the distinction between decriminalization and recognition of relationships.

Nepal followed a different path. A Supreme Court decision in December 2007 (Sunil Babu Pant v. Nepal Government) directed the government to end discrimination against sexual and gender minorities and to study same-sex marriage, and the 2015 Constitution prohibited discrimination on grounds including gender and sexual orientation and listed sexual and gender minorities among groups with a right to social justice. Nepal's courts issued interim orders enabling the registration of same-sex marriages, and in November 2023 a same-sex couple's marriage was registered in Lamjung district, an event widely reported as the first in South Asia, although registrations were provisional and lacked comprehensive statutory backing. Bhutan decriminalized same-sex conduct in 2021 by amending its Penal Code, with little public controversy. In Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, colonial-era prohibitions or their statutory successors continued to constrain consensual same-sex conduct. Maldives separately criminalized same-sex sexual intercourse and sexual contact under Sections 411 and 412 of its 2014 Penal Code. The incidence of prosecution varied between states, and criminal law operated alongside stigma, vulnerability to harassment, and restricted access to legal protection; Sri Lanka considered repeal proposals but had not completed decriminalization by the research cutoff.

Distinguishing legal developments is essential. Decriminalization removes criminal penalties; antidiscrimination protection guarantees equality in employment, housing, and services; gender recognition allows legal identity to reflect gender identity; partnership recognition covers marriage or civil union; and social acceptance concerns family and community attitudes. A society can change on one dimension and not others. In India, urban activism, literature, film, and the activity of pride marches expanded visibility after 2018, while family rejection, workplace discrimination, and violence persisted; corporate diversity policies, some universities, and some state governments introduced measures, but a national antidiscrimination law did not exist. Rural and non-English-speaking queer people often faced the greatest difficulties and had less access to the networks and legal resources of urban activists. Public opinion surveys indicated increasing but varying acceptance, with religious and political organizations taking opposing positions.

Historical Vocabulary
Gender Identity, Legal Recognition, and Social Inclusion

Gender identity refers to a person's internal sense of their gender, which may or may not correspond to the sex assigned at birth. Legal recognition is the state's acknowledgment of a person's gender identity in documents such as identity cards and passports, and may be based on self-declaration, medical certification, or judicial or administrative approval. Social inclusion concerns access to education, work, health care, housing, and community life.

These categories are modern, and historians use them cautiously for earlier institutions such as the hijra communities of South Asia, which have their own histories, kinship structures, and religious roles and do not map neatly onto contemporary Western categories such as "transgender." Nor do the terms "gay," "lesbian," or "queer" capture the diversity of South Asian experiences of sexuality, which are expressed in many languages and regional idioms.

Section 107.14

Transgender and Gender-Diverse Communities: Recognition and Rights

Hijra communities in South Asia have a long documented history. Persons described as hijras, a term associated with people assigned male at birth who adopt feminine dress and roles, lived in organized communities with guru–disciple kinship structures (gharanas), earned income through ritual blessings at births and weddings (badhai), and in some periods held court roles under Mughal and princely rulers. In Pakistan the term khwaja sira, with connotations of court service, is widely used by community activists. Colonial law treated these communities as suspect: the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 targeted hijras in northern India for registration and surveillance, and Section 377 was applied against them. Postcolonial communities remained among the most marginalized, often excluded from family and employment, though they retained a religious and ritual significance in popular culture that sometimes coexisted with ridicule and violence. Scholars such as Gayatri Reddy, Serena Nanda, and Aniruddha Dutta have shown the diversity of these communities, and activists have often resisted the use of a single category to describe all gender-variant people.

India's legal turning point came on April 15, 2014, in National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (NALSA), in which the Supreme Court recognized transgender persons, including hijras, as a "third gender," held that gender identity is a matter of self-determination protected by the Constitution, directed governments to extend legal recognition and to treat them as socially and educationally backward classes for reservation purposes, and ordered measures in health care and education. Parliament subsequently passed the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act of 2019, which prohibited discrimination and created institutions, but it was criticized by many activists because it required certification by a district magistrate for a person's identity to be recognized, which they argued contradicted the self-identification principle of NALSA, and because its penalties for abuse were lighter than for comparable offences against others. Some states, including Tamil Nadu (which established a Transgender Welfare Board in 2008) and Kerala, adopted welfare schemes earlier. The practical effects were mixed: some individuals obtained identity documents and public posts, such as in the police, as in a few highly publicized appointments, while many others reported difficulties with bureaucracy and persistent discrimination in jobs, housing, and health care.

In Pakistan, the Supreme Court recognized a third category for identity cards from 2009 (the Dr. Mohammad Aslam Khaki case), and the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act was passed in May 2018, defining gender identity in terms of self-perception, prohibiting discrimination, and providing for rights to inheritance, education, and health care. The Act, noted internationally as one of the first in the region to incorporate self-perceived identity, drew opposition from some religious parties, who argued that it contravened Islamic injunctions and might enable same-sex marriage. In May 2023 the Federal Shariat Court held several provisions, notably those based on self-perceived identity, to be contrary to Islam; the decision was challenged before the Supreme Court, and its consequences for the Act were uncertain at the research date. The 2018 legislation and the 2023 judgment must be distinguished: the former was an act of parliament, the latter a judicial ruling on its compatibility with Islamic injunctions, and each has had distinct consequences. Activists also faced violence, including killings and attacks in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and elsewhere, which community organizations documented.

Bangladesh's government recognized hijras as a third gender in a 2013 decision, and in 2014 announced employment for some hijras in government service; hijra candidates stood for local office, and the Election Commission added a category for hijra voters in 2018. A madrasa for transgender students opened in Dhaka in 2020. Yet critics argued that formal recognition was incomplete, since the 2013 measure used the term "hijra" to describe a broad range of gender-diverse people and since legal protection from discrimination remained limited. Nepal's Supreme Court in 2007 directed the state to recognize a third gender, citizenship certificates and passports with a gender marker "other" were issued from 2015, and the Constitution addressed gender and sexual minorities in provisions on rights and inclusion, though implementation fell short of constitutional promise. In Sri Lanka, a system of gender recognition certificates based on medical certification was introduced in 2016, and in Maldives and Bhutan, public discussion remained limited.

Across these cases, the difference between formal recognition and lived experience was pronounced. Many transgender people faced exclusion from schools and families at young ages, which limited education and employment opportunities and led some to rely on traditional occupations such as ritual collection and sex work, which exposed them to police harassment and violence. Community organizations, including hijra gharanas, health NGOs, and rights groups, provided support, and transgender writers and activists such as A. Revathi, Laxmi Narayan Tripathi, and Living Smile Vidya wrote about their lives and campaigned for rights. Educated transgender professionals were increasingly visible, though their numbers were small. The recognition of a "third gender" also raised the question of whether categories created by states fit the diverse self-understandings of those they described: some activists preferred recognition of male, female, and other identities by self-declaration, while others valued the specific cultural identity of hijra communities.

Section 107.15

Disability, Accessibility, and the Expansion of Social Citizenship

Disability entered public policy in South Asia first as a matter of charity and medical care, and during the period of this chapter increasingly as a matter of rights. Traditional attitudes combined compassion with stigma, and some religious and cultural ideas associated impairment with karma or misfortune, while families bore most of the care. State provisions after independence were limited, with institutions for particular categories such as blindness and polio. Large numbers of people lived with disabilities arising from poverty, malnutrition, conflict, accidents, and inadequate health care, and decades of war in Sri Lanka and Kashmir and landmines and other conflict injuries added to those numbers. India's 2011 census recorded about 26.8 million people with disabilities, about 2.2 percent of the population, a figure many experts considered an undercount because of definition and enumeration; global estimates suggest a much higher proportion, around fifteen percent, and the new census of 2027 was scheduled to include disability questions.

Legislation expanded rapidly with the international movement for disability rights. India enacted the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act in 1995, and, after ratifying the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2007, replaced it with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act of 2016, which expanded the recognized categories to twenty-one, provided for reservations in government jobs (four percent) and higher education (five percent), and mandated accessibility. Nepal enacted a Disability Rights Act in 2017 and referred to the rights of persons with disabilities in its Constitution; Bangladesh passed the Rights and Protection of Persons with Disabilities Act in 2013; Sri Lanka passed a Protection of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act in 1996 and ratified the Convention in 2016; Pakistan's Islamabad Capital Territory Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act of 2020 followed earlier ordinances from 1981. All of these countries ratified the Convention, with dates varying, though ratification did not ensure enforcement.

Courts played an active role in India. In Jeeja Ghosh v. Union of India (2016), the Supreme Court ruled on the right of a woman with cerebral palsy to be treated with dignity by an airline; in Vikash Kumar v. UPSC (2021), it addressed the right to a scribe in examinations; and in Rajive Raturi v. Union of India (2024), it directed the government to frame mandatory accessibility rules after finding that its existing rules were not binding. Such cases indicated both the legal advance and the persistence of inaccessible public spaces, transport, websites, and schools. Governments also launched programs such as India's Accessible India Campaign (2015) with limited achievement of targets.

Inclusive education was a major issue. Many children with disabilities remained out of school, in the absence of trained teachers, accessible buildings, or transport, while special schools segregated others. Families faced costs, stigma, and limited support. Employment likewise lagged: reserved government posts were often unfilled, private-sector hiring was low, and the cost of assistive technology was high. Disability intersected with poverty, gender, and caste; women with disabilities faced particular risks of violence and neglect, and rural people had less access to services. Disability movements, including the National Platform for the Rights of the Disabled in India, Bangladesh's disability federations, and organizations of deaf people campaigning for recognition of sign languages, provided leadership, and the slogan "nothing about us without us" signaled a shift from charity to participation. Persons with disabilities themselves, including writers, athletes in the Paralympics, and legislators, challenged stereotypes.

The history of disability rights thus belongs to the broader history of citizenship. It extended the idea of equal participation to people whose exclusion had been treated as natural or medical, and it raised practical questions of design and public spending that went beyond constitutional equality. It also showed how legal frameworks adopted from international norms could be used by domestic movements, and how the gap between legal promise and physical access might remain wide.

Section 107.16

Language, Cultural Recognition, and the Politics of Belonging

Language has been a central issue in South Asian politics since the nineteenth century, and its institutional dimensions after 1991 concern which languages are used in schools, administration, courts, and the media, and thus who has access to opportunity. This section addresses language and cultural recognition as general institutional questions; the specific claims of historically marginalized indigenous communities are addressed in Section 107.4. The two overlap but are not identical: a language movement may be led by a majority group within a region, as in the Bengali movement of 1952, or may concern a minority within a state.

India's language policy is complex. The Constitution recognizes Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, with English continuing for official purposes, and lists scheduled languages in the Eighth Schedule, which grew from fourteen in 1950 to twenty-two after the addition of Konkani, Manipuri, and Nepali (1992), and Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, and Santali (2003). Linguistic states were created from the 1950s (Chapter 89), and a "three-language formula" was intended to promote multilingualism in schools. The National Education Policy of 2020 recommended that the medium of instruction be the mother tongue at least through the primary years where possible and continued the three-language approach; Tamil Nadu opposed what it saw as pressure toward Hindi, a position rooted in the anti-Hindi agitations of 1937–39 and 1965, and disputes over education funding and language policy resurfaced in 2025. In October 2024 the Union Cabinet extended "classical language" status to Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, and Bengali, which already included Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, and Odia, showing how language recognition had become a matter of cultural prestige and resources.

Nepal's 2015 Constitution declares all mother tongues spoken in the country to be national languages and Nepali in the Devanagari script the official language, while allowing provinces to adopt additional official languages; Maithili, Bhojpuri, and others are used in provincial administration. This reflected a shift away from the monolingual policy of the Panchayat era (Chapter 96), under which Nepali was promoted as the language of national unity, and the demands of Janajati and Madhesi movements. Implementation challenges included the production of textbooks in numerous languages and the use of Nepali as the language of employment and government.

In Pakistan, Urdu is the national language and English remains the language of the elite, courts, and many offices, and the Constitution of 1973 contemplated English being replaced by Urdu within fifteen years, a provision the Supreme Court in 2015 ordered the government to implement, with limited effect. Punjabi, the most widely spoken language, has had little official use in education, while Sindhi, Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki, and others have varying recognition; the Sindhi language riots of 1972 and the Bengali language movement before 1971 (Chapters 90 and 92) shaped awareness of the issue. Debates continued over provincial languages in schools and over medium of instruction in a country in which education was divided between Urdu-medium, English-medium, and madrasa systems, with a hierarchy of access to jobs and prestige. In Bangladesh, where the Language Movement of 1952 is a founding memory and February 21 was declared International Mother Language Day by UNESCO in 1999, Bangla is the state language, and indigenous groups such as the Chakma, Marma, and Garo asked for education in their own languages; the government introduced primary textbooks in several indigenous languages from 2017, which activists welcomed but described as limited.

Sri Lanka's history provides the clearest case of how language policy can generate conflict. The Sinhala Only Act of 1956 made Sinhala the sole official language, a measure that, together with the effects on public employment and university admission, fueled Tamil grievance (Chapter 91); the Thirteenth Amendment in 1987 made Tamil an official language and English a link language, but implementation was partial, and many Tamil speakers continued to have difficulty obtaining services in their language. Post-war efforts to implement a trilingual policy had mixed outcomes. In Bhutan, Dzongkha is the national language and promoted through education and the "One Nation, One People" policy of the 1980s and 1990s, which included a national dress code (driglam namzha), while in the south, Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa suffered under measures that restricted their cultural practices and citizenship (Section 107.4). In Maldives, Dhivehi is the national language.

These policies influence the distribution of opportunity and the sense of belonging. If a child must learn in a language different from that spoken at home, learning may be slower, and if government jobs require proficiency in a particular language, those who speak it have an advantage. English occupies a distinctive position across the region as a language of higher education, commerce, and elite status, and the contest between English and mother tongues reflects tensions between access to global opportunity and the preservation of local cultures. Language politics also shapes ideas of nationhood: policies that equate national unity with one language risk excluding others, while multilingual recognition can accommodate diversity but demands resources. The study of language, therefore, connects the social history of this chapter to the cultural history of Chapter 108.

Section 107.17

Civil Society, Voluntary Organizations, and Community Institutions

The social changes described in earlier sections were largely produced through organizations that stood between households and the state. Civil society, in the sense used here, includes voluntary associations, trade unions, professional bodies, women's groups, religious charities, community organizations, advocacy networks, and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs). South Asia had a long history of such institutions, from caste associations and reform societies of the colonial period (Chapter 74) to cooperatives and labor unions, but the period after 1991 saw expansion in their number, scale, and international connections, driven by state withdrawal from some services, foreign and domestic funding, and the spread of rights-based advocacy.

It is important to distinguish types. Grassroots associations, such as village women's savings groups, caste or community welfare societies, youth clubs, and neighborhood committees, are rooted in local membership and often unregistered. Larger professionalized NGOs employ staff, manage budgets, and often depend on external funding. Some became very large: BRAC, founded in Bangladesh in 1972 by F. H. Abed, grew into one of the world's largest nongovernmental development organizations, running schools, microfinance, health programs, and enterprises, and Grameen Bank, founded by Muhammad Yunus, pioneered microcredit and shared the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize. India's Self-Employed Women's Association (SEWA), established in Ahmedabad in 1972 by Ela Bhatt, organized women in the informal economy as a trade union and cooperative, while Kerala's Kudumbashree (1998) linked neighborhood groups of women to local government. Self-help groups, which pooled savings and provided credit, enrolled many millions of women across India and became a major channel of policy. In Pakistan, the Edhi Foundation, the Aga Khan networks, and the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (1987) occupied distinct spaces; in Sri Lanka, Sarvodaya (founded 1958) mobilized villages for development; in Nepal, community forestry user groups managed forests under legislation, and Dalit and Janajati federations pressed for inclusion.

Civil society organizations contributed to the legal and social changes examined in this chapter. The Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan in Rajasthan, led by activists including Aruna Roy, pioneered public hearings that helped produce India's Right to Information Act of 2005, which gave citizens a legal tool for demanding records, and campaigns by labor and rights groups contributed to the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act of 2005. Women's organizations drafted and lobbied for legislation on domestic violence and workplace harassment, as in the Vishaka litigation; Dalit organizations pressed for the Atrocities Act and its enforcement; legal aid groups and public-interest lawyers brought cases that shaped constitutional doctrine. In health, NGOs worked in HIV prevention, maternal care, and community health; in education, they provided schooling for the excluded, as in BRAC's non-formal schools. Religious charities, including mosque, temple, and gurdwara networks and organizations such as Edhi in Pakistan, provided disaster relief and welfare, and community organizations in cities provided mutual aid for migrants.

Civil society also raised questions of accountability and independence. Critics noted that professionalized NGOs could be accountable more to donors than to the communities they served, that some organizations were created to capture funds, and that service delivery by NGOs could allow states to avoid their responsibilities. Governments regulated the sector with varying degrees of suspicion. India's Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act of 2010, amended in 2020, restricted foreign funding, and licenses of a number of organizations, including prominent advocacy and research groups, were cancelled or suspended, while Amnesty International India ceased operations in 2020 after its bank accounts were frozen; the government argued that regulation was necessary to prevent misuse of funds and foreign interference, while the organizations and others described it as constraint on legitimate advocacy. Bangladesh required NGO registration and oversight through its NGO Affairs Bureau; Pakistan imposed registration rules for international NGOs from 2015 to 2018, and Sri Lanka and Nepal also regulated the sector. The history of civil society thus involved both expansion and contention over its legitimate role, and the quality of these institutions varied considerably by region, leadership, and political context.

Section 107.18

Student Movements, Popular Protest, and Collective Action

Collective protest was a continuous feature of South Asian public life after 1991, and it took forms ranging from student agitations to women's campaigns, Dalit mobilizations, minority-rights protests, and peasant and labor movements. This section compares several episodes, focusing on who participated, how they organized, what they demanded, and what the movements show about citizenship and generational change. The detailed political histories of the Bangladeshi and Nepali cases belong to Chapters 102 and 104.

In India, university campuses were prominent arenas. After the death of Rohith Vemula in January 2016, student groups in Hyderabad and elsewhere, led by Ambedkarite organizations, protested against caste discrimination in higher education and demanded legislation; at Jawaharlal Nehru University in Delhi, a 2016 controversy over alleged anti-national slogans led to a sedition case against the student union president, Kanhaiya Kumar, and a national debate on dissent. In December 2019 and the following months, protests against the Citizenship (Amendment) Act began at Jamia Millia Islamia and Aligarh Muslim University and spread across the country, with the Shaheen Bagh sit-in, led by women of all ages, becoming a symbol; some protesters read the preamble of the Constitution and used the national flag, framing their action as constitutional. In 2012–13 women's and student protests after the Delhi gang rape created pressure for legal reform, and in 2018 the #MeToo movement led to allegations against prominent men in media and politics. The 2018 Dalit protests (Section 107.3) and the farmers' agitation of 2020–21, which was not a student movement, showed the capacity of organized groups to influence national policy, as the government repealed three agricultural laws in 2021.

Bangladesh's student movement of 2018 and 2024 illustrates a protest over public-sector quotas that became a mass uprising. In 2018, students demanded reform of the civil-service quota system, which reserved a large share of posts, including a substantial share for descendants of freedom fighters of 1971; the government abolished the quotas in 2018. In June 2024 the High Court reinstated the quota, prompting new protests organized by "Students Against Discrimination," which met a violent response from police and from supporters of the ruling party. After the Supreme Court reduced the quota in July 2024, protests continued with demands for accountability for the killings; the government's use of force and the imposition of curfews and internet shutdowns turned a dispute over employment into a challenge to the government itself. Sheikh Hasina resigned and left the country on August 5, 2024. The United Nations human rights office later estimated that as many as about 1,400 people may have been killed in the unrest, a figure that, like others, was disputed and under investigation. The protests involved students from public and private universities, school pupils, and many women, and an interim government was formed under Muhammad Yunus (Chapter 102).

Nepal's youth mobilization of September 2025 followed a government order on September 4 to block a number of unregistered social media platforms. Protests, called by young people, many of them students, on September 8 in Kathmandu, directed against the ban and against corruption and political privilege, were met with police firing that killed at least nineteen people; the following day violence escalated, government buildings including the parliament were burned, and Prime Minister K. P. Sharma Oli resigned. Reports counted more than seventy deaths overall. Sushila Karki, a former chief justice, became interim prime minister on September 12, and elections were held on March 5, 2026, which the Rastriya Swatantra Party (RSP) won by a large margin. On March 26 the RSP elected Balendra Shah its parliamentary party leader, while Rabi Lamichhane remained party chairperson; Shah was sworn in as prime minister on March 27, 2026 (Chapter 104). The movement, sometimes called a "Gen Z" protest, was notable for the use of social media and online platforms to organize, and for a generational critique of an established political class, although the social composition of participants, including their caste, ethnicity, region, and class, was only beginning to be studied by the research date.

Other movements deserve comparison. In Pakistan, the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (founded 2018) mobilized against enforced disappearances and military abuses in Pashtun areas, the Aurat March since 2018 campaigned for women's rights, and the lawyers' movement of 2007–09 (Chapter 101) showed the political role of professional groups. In Sri Lanka, the protests of 2022, the Aragalaya, which brought together students, professionals, workers, and others during the economic crisis, led to the president's resignation (Chapter 103). In Maldives, the Reformasi movement of 2003–08 preceded the 2008 Constitution. Across these cases, the social composition of protests varied: student groups provided organizational capacity and symbolic legitimacy; women's participation was often prominent; marginalized groups mobilized around their own issues; and religious and political parties sometimes sought to channel or oppose movements.

Several patterns can be drawn. Protests often began with a specific grievance, such as quotas, a ban on social media, or a discriminatory law, and expanded to broader claims about corruption, accountability, or citizenship. They relied on digital media, but also on older networks of student unions, local organizations, and neighborhood solidarity. State responses combined concessions, repression, and efforts to delegitimize protesters, and consequences ranged from policy reversals to the fall of governments. Their long-term effects depended on whether participants created durable organizations, how political parties responded, and whether institutions changed. They also revealed a generation with different expectations, more educated than its parents, more connected, and less willing to accept hierarchy in politics and family.

Section 107.19

Social Change and the Unfinished Struggle for Equality through 2026

Assessing the cumulative effect of three and a half decades of change requires multiple measures, and no single index can capture them. Measurable outcomes show real improvement. Literacy rose and gender gaps in school enrollment narrowed in India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka; fertility fell to about replacement level or below in most of the region; child marriage declined, though not everywhere; millions of women served in local government; Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe, and OBC representation in public institutions grew; and new legal protections were introduced on domestic violence, harassment, disability, gender identity, and caste atrocities. Educated Dalit, Adivasi, Muslim, and women professionals became more visible in public life, and rights language moved from the margins into mainstream politics.

Persistent disparities were equally evident. Caste violence and discrimination continued; Dalit and Adivasi people were overrepresented among the poor and landless; Muslims in India lagged in education and public employment according to the Sachar Committee and later studies; women's labor-force participation and share of unpaid work remained unequal (Chapter 106); sexual violence and domestic violence persisted; religious minorities in several countries faced threats and restrictions; sexual and gender minorities faced discrimination; and people with disabilities encountered inaccessible institutions. Access to justice was constrained by cost, delay, and fear. Many of these disparities interacted: a Dalit woman with a disability in a poor rural district faced overlapping disadvantages.

Comparison among the seven countries warns against a single scale of "modernization." Sri Lanka achieved high literacy and long-standing women's education and health indicators while experiencing deep ethnic and religious conflict and low female representation in the national legislature. Bangladesh made striking gains in girls' schooling and women's economic participation while struggling with political polarization, minority security, and the legal status of personal law. Nepal adopted the most explicit constitutional scheme of inclusion in the region, but faced continuing disputes about federalism, representation, and the effectiveness of institutions, and a political upheaval in 2025. India combined a large and active judiciary, reservation system, and plural civil society with persistent caste violence, communal tension, and contested citizenship rules. Pakistan's reforms in women's protection, minority rights, and transgender recognition were limited by religious and judicial challenge and security problems. Bhutan and Maldives, small states with distinct religious and political arrangements, are less documented, and their social histories are comparatively less studied; the evidence available to this chapter on their civil society, minorities, and gender relations is correspondingly thinner. Social change did not move in the same direction everywhere, and gains in one area did not predict gains in another.

Distinguishing established outcomes from promises and controversies is particularly important at the end of this chapter. As of November 20, 2026, India's Census 2027 had progressed through staggered houselisting in 2026, designated snowbound population-enumeration operations in September, and new online self-enumeration in selected jurisdictions beginning November 16. Population enumeration in many other jurisdictions remained scheduled for 2027, and the nationwide caste results and their implications for reservations were still unknown. India's 2023 constitutional amendment concerning women's legislative reservation had not yet yielded the envisaged allocation of parliamentary and assembly seats: an April 17, 2026 attempt to accelerate implementation through the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill failed to obtain the required special majority in the Lok Sabha, and dependent delimitation legislation did not proceed. The consequences of the 2024 sub-classification judgment were being worked out in individual states; the uniform civil code, the Waqf amendment, citizenship-law rules, and conversion laws were subjects of continuing litigation and political dispute; Pakistan's transgender legislation was subject to judicial challenge; and Nepal's and Bangladesh's post-protest governments had not yet produced a stable record. These are developments in progress, and historians cannot yet judge their results.

Social Hierarchy, Rights, and Institutional Transformation, 1991–2026

What Changed

  • Legal recognition expanded to groups previously excluded or criminalized, including sexual and gender minorities, persons with disabilities, and women claiming property
  • Education, literacy, and women's presence in local government and professions increased substantially
  • Caste moved from a mainly ritual and local hierarchy toward a field of electoral politics, law, and statistics, including the return of caste enumeration to India's census
  • Movements, courts, and civil society organizations became principal means of claiming rights, and young people used digital tools to mobilize
  • Households became smaller and marriage practices more varied, with migration reshaping family organization

What Stayed the Same

  • Endogamy and caste-linked social networks continued to structure marriage, employment, and residence
  • Dalits and Adivasis remained overrepresented among the poor and exposed to violence
  • Family authority, dowry, and unequal care burdens persisted, despite legal prohibitions
  • Religious identity remained a basis for political mobilization and, in some cases, violence
  • The gap between legal guarantees and enforcement remained wide

Who experienced change most directly? Urban, educated, and mobile people, first-generation graduates, women in local politics, and groups that gained new legal recognition often experienced the sharpest change. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural poor, landless laborers, residents of remote regions, and those facing overlapping disadvantages often encountered greater continuity. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that change was selective: institutions adapted, and benefits were unevenly distributed, which is why historians should avoid describing the period either as one of uniform progress or of unchanging tradition.

Review and Historical Synthesis

The history of social change in South Asia between 1991 and 2026 resists both of the narratives most often applied to it. It is not the story of a society whose ancient hierarchies remained fixed, and it is not the story of a steady movement from tradition to modernity. The institutions examined in this chapter (caste, gender relations, religion, language, and the boundaries of inclusion) were historical products that changed under pressure from law, politics, economics, and the actions of ordinary people. Caste in 2026 was not the Vedic varna scheme, nor the jati order of the early modern period, nor the colonial census category; it was a modern, legally defined, electorally significant, and socially lived reality. Gender relations were reshaped by schooling, migration, falling fertility, and legal reform, and also by backlash and persistence. Religion was reconfigured by electoral politics, constitutional definitions, and violence, and also by everyday coexistence.

Several processes interacted. Law provided frameworks and resources: constitutions that prohibited discrimination, statutes that expanded rights, and courts that interpreted them in cases brought by ordinary litigants and organizations. Democratic institutions, including elections, reserved seats, and local governments, made numerically large groups politically important and gave previously excluded people experience of office. Education created new aspirations and capacities. Collective action, from Dalit mobilization to women's campaigns, from indigenous struggles to student movements, translated grievances into public claims. These processes reinforced one another, but they did not always align. Education without jobs produced frustration; legal rights without enforcement produced skepticism; political representation without redistribution left deep inequalities; and mobilization sometimes provoked countermobilization.

That is why formal equality and social equality diverged. A guarantee in a constitution does not alter who owns land, who has access to a good school, or whose family has networks that provide employment. Inherited institutions adapted rather than disappeared: caste associations became political organizations, family networks provided support in the absence of public welfare, religious institutions provided education and charity, and personal laws continued to organize marriage and inheritance. In many cases, new categories such as Scheduled Caste, OBC, Janajati, "third gender," or "minority" were created by the state, and communities used them strategically, with the effect that law and identity shaped each other. Moreover, change did not benefit all members of disadvantaged groups equally, as the debates over creamy layer and sub-classification illustrate, and the interests of groups sometimes conflicted.

The comparative evidence shows that each country followed its own course. India developed an extensive judicial and legislative apparatus of group-based remedies, alongside intense contention over citizenship and religion. Nepal wrote inclusion into its constitution after a period of insurgency and movement politics. Bangladesh achieved notable educational and economic gains for women and then experienced a student-led political rupture. Pakistan's legal reforms often met religious and judicial resistance, and Sri Lanka's conflicts demonstrated how language and religion could be used for nation-building and exclusion. Bhutan and Maldives defined national identity through particular religious traditions. Because of these differences, historians must avoid using a single country as the standard.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized agency. The most significant changes did not result from a predetermined progression nor solely from state initiative. They were produced by struggles among households, communities, state institutions, reformers, political movements, and ordinary citizens: a Dalit woman who stood for village council; a litigant who challenged a colonial-era statute; a family that decided to send a daughter to school; a neighborhood committee that kept the peace; a student who joined a protest. The effects of these actions were uneven and sometimes reversible. The history of social change since 1991 is therefore a history of continuing contestation. Chapter 108 turns to the media and cultural institutions through which these contests were represented and amplified, and Chapter 111 returns, at the end of the book, to how South Asians remember and argue about the history this chapter has traced.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how South Asians challenged, defended, reformed, and reinterpreted inherited social institutions between 1991 and 2026, and why expanded legal rights and educational opportunities did not always produce equal social outcomes. Its central argument is that caste, gender relations, religious identity, language, and other categories were historical products, continually remade by law, politics, economics, and collective action, and not unchanged survivals from the ancient past.

Division I addressed caste and inclusion. Varna, jati, and legal categories such as Scheduled Caste are distinct, and caste in the period was an institution reshaped by democracy, urbanization, and statistics. Endogamy and caste-linked networks persisted, and violence against Dalits continued, while education, migration, and electoral politics altered everyday practice. India's reservation system developed through the Mandal controversy, the Indra Sawhney judgment, constitutional amendments, and the 2024 sub-classification ruling, and its effects are debated between those who see structural change and those who see limited redistribution. Nepal's proportional-inclusion scheme offered a different model.

Dalit movements drew on Ambedkar's legacy and produced political parties, legal campaigns, literature, and new activist organizations, with Dalit women playing a central role, while Adivasi, Janajati, and other indigenous and ethnic communities pursued claims about recognition, land, and political participation that differ from caste claims and were shaped by conflicts over forests, mining, federal boundaries, and citizenship.

Division II examined gender, family, and education. Women's legal status advanced through legislation and judicial decisions in India, reforms in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, and Nepal's constitutional and litigation-driven changes, but enforcement and social norms limited their effect. Women's public participation expanded most dramatically in local government, with national representation lagging. Marriage and household life changed with schooling, migration, and falling fertility, while dowry, family control, and violence over inter-caste and interreligious relationships continued. Education expanded access, particularly for girls, but inequalities of quality and language, and the privatization of schooling, meant that opportunity remained unequal.

Division III turned to religion. The seven countries have different religious demographics and constitutional relationships between religion and the state, including different meanings of secularism. Controversies over personal law, citizenship, conversion, and blasphemy produced disputes about equality and minority protection, and major episodes of violence, including Ayodhya and the Mumbai riots, Gujarat 2002, Sri Lanka's anti-Muslim violence, and sectarian attacks in Pakistan, were shaped by organized political actors and state responses, not by ancient hatred or the collective responsibility of any community. Everyday coexistence, shared shrines, civic associations, and local peace efforts show that polarization was historically produced and contested.

Division IV addressed boundaries of inclusion. Decriminalization of same-sex conduct in India (2018) and Bhutan (2021) was distinct from marriage equality, which the Indian Supreme Court declined to recognize in 2023, while Nepal advanced registrations on a provisional basis. Transgender recognition developed through the NALSA judgment, Pakistan's 2018 Act and its 2023 judicial challenge, and measures in Bangladesh and Nepal, with continuing gaps between recognition and lived experience. Disability rights moved from charity to citizenship, and language policy shaped access to opportunity and belonging in Nepal, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka.

Division V examined civil society and collective action. NGOs, women's groups, unions, and community institutions contributed to legislation and services while confronting questions of accountability and state regulation. Student and youth movements in India, Bangladesh, and Nepal showed how grievances over quotas, discrimination, corruption, and online freedom could become broader claims about citizenship, with the 2024 Bangladesh and 2025 Nepal cases illustrating generational change.

Taken together, the evidence shows measurable gains in literacy, women's representation in local office, legal recognition, and the presence of historically excluded groups in public life, alongside persistent disparities, discrimination, and violence. The seven countries followed different paths, and gains in one dimension did not predict gains in another. Several developments remained unresolved on November 20, 2026: India's staggered census had begun but its nationwide caste results were not yet available; the April 2026 attempt to accelerate the implementation of women's legislative reservation had failed; and litigation over personal law and citizenship continued.

The chapter has emphasized that formal and social equality diverged because law does not by itself redistribute land, schooling, or networks, and that inherited institutions adapted rather than disappeared. It has also emphasized agency: social change resulted from struggles among households, communities, state institutions, reformers, political movements, and ordinary citizens, rather than from a predetermined progression from tradition to modernity.

Key Terms

Varna
A fourfold classification of society in Sanskrit normative texts, distinct from the thousands of local jati communities through which social life was organized.
Jati
A regional, usually endogamous community associated with occupation, kinship, and a position in local status hierarchies.
Endogamy
The practice of marrying within one's own community, a principal mechanism by which caste boundaries persisted.
Dalit
A term of political self-identification, popularized from the 1970s, for people placed at the bottom or outside the hierarchical order; not a statutory category.
Scheduled Caste / Scheduled Tribe
Communities listed under India's Constitution as eligible for special protections and representation; Scheduled Caste status is limited by religion.
Other Backward Classes (OBCs)
Socially and educationally backward communities, other than Scheduled Castes and Tribes, eligible for reservation following the Mandal Commission and Indra Sawhney.
Reservation
India's system of quotas in education, public employment, and legislatures for designated groups.
Creamy layer
The relatively advantaged section of a backward class excluded from reservation benefits under Indra Sawhney.
Sub-classification
The division of a reserved category into subgroups for differentiated benefits, permitted for Scheduled Castes by the 2024 Davinder Singh judgment.
Proportional inclusion
Nepal's constitutional principle that designated groups have a right to participation in state bodies in proportion to their presence.
Adivasi / Janajati
Indigenous or tribal communities, termed Adivasi or Scheduled Tribes in India and Janajati in Nepal, with distinct legal status and political claims.
Forest Rights Act (2006)
Indian law recognizing individual and community rights of forest dwellers, unevenly implemented.
Pasmanda
A term for lower-status and marginalized Muslim groups, associated with a movement that criticized upper-status dominance of Muslim leadership.
Atrocities Act (1989)
Indian statute establishing special penalties and courts for specified crimes against Scheduled Castes and Tribes.
Personal law
Family law applied according to religious community, as in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh.
Secularism
A constitutional principle with varying meanings: equal respect for religions in India, a principle coexisting with a state religion in Bangladesh, and a definition including protection of ancient traditions in Nepal.
Communalism
In South Asian usage, the political mobilization of religious identity against other communities.
Citizenship (Amendment) Act (2019)
Indian law providing expedited naturalization for certain religious minorities from three neighboring countries, excluding Muslims; rules notified in 2024.
Dowry
Transfers of money and goods from a bride's family at marriage, prohibited by statute but widespread, and linked to violence.
Panchayat reservation
The 1992–93 constitutional requirement of reserved seats for women, Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes in local government.
Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (2023)
The 106th Amendment reserving one-third of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats for women, to take effect after the next census and delimitation.
Hijra / khwaja sira
Historically established gender-diverse communities with their own kinship institutions, whose names are used in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh.
Section 377
A colonial-era provision criminalizing "carnal intercourse against the order of nature," read down in India in 2018.
Constitutional morality
The principle, invoked in Navtej Singh Johar, that constitutional values of dignity and equality take precedence over prevailing social morality.
Third gender
A legal category recognized in several South Asian states for people not classified as male or female, whose scope and meaning are debated by activists.
Social citizenship
The idea that citizenship includes access to education, health, and participation, not only legal and political rights.
Civil society
Voluntary associations, unions, NGOs, and community institutions that act between the household and the state.
Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act
Indian law regulating foreign funding of organizations, amended in 2020 and a subject of dispute over civil-society freedom.
Census 2027
India's first digital census, including caste enumeration for all communities, with population enumeration scheduled for January–February 2027.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how inherited institutions are made and remade. Students who encounter caste, patriarchy, or religious division in simplified forms may imagine them as unchanging features of South Asian civilization. The evidence presented here shows instead that each was shaped by the law, the economy, and the politics of particular moments, and that people acted on them, sometimes to preserve them and often to change them.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that run through the book. The constitutional settlements of Chapter 88, the reform and anti-caste movements of Chapters 74 and 80, the colonial categories of Chapter 73, and the political shifts of Chapters 94 and 99 all appear here as living influences. The social history of 1991 to 2026 is where those earlier developments meet the democratic competition, education, migration, and digital communication that characterize the present, and it shows the long reach of decisions made in earlier periods.

It connects regions as well. Struggles over recognition in Nepal, Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka had different outcomes, but they drew on shared languages of rights, shared legal inheritances from the colonial period, and transnational exchange of ideas. The comparison guards against the habit of treating India as the template for the region, and it underscores that smaller states and minority communities have histories that merit equal attention.

Chapter 108 now turns to the media, cinema, literature, and public sphere in which these social conflicts have been represented, and Chapter 111 will ask how South Asians remember and contest the longer past. The social changes traced here remained in progress on the chapter's research date of November 20, 2026, and students should treat them accordingly: as a history still being made, with outcomes that cannot yet be known.
