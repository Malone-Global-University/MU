Civil Disobedience and Constitutional Crisis

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the morning of 6 April 1930, on a flat stretch of the Gujarat coast near the village of Dandi, a frail man of sixty stooped at the edge of the Arabian Sea and picked up a crust of salt left by the waves. Behind him stood the volunteers who had walked with him for twenty-four days, and beyond them a gathering of villagers, nationalist workers, and journalists. By the letter of the law, the act was a crime. Salt, the most ordinary of household necessities, was subject to a government monopoly and a government tax, and gathering it without authority was an offense.

The man was Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, and the act was small by design. Nobody was injured and no building or institution fell. Yet within weeks, in coastal villages from Gujarat to Tamil country, Bengal, and Odisha, and in towns and cities well inland of the sea, thousands of people had followed the example by making salt, picketing shops, refusing taxes, or accepting arrest. The British Indian government, which had spent a decade learning how to answer nationalist agitation, found itself confronting a movement that appeared to turn the state's own laws into the instrument of its embarrassment.

This chapter is about that movement, but it is not only about that movement. The years between 1927 and 1935 produced two connected histories. One was a history of the street, the shore, and the countryside: boycott demonstrations against a British commission, the declaration of complete independence at Lahore, the Salt March, mass picketing, the repression of 1930 and 1932, and the slow exhaustion of the campaign by 1934. The other was a history of rooms and documents: the Simon Commission, the Nehru Report, Jinnah's Fourteen Points, three Round Table Conferences in London, the Gandhi–Irwin Pact, the Communal Award, the Poona Pact, and finally the Government of India Act of 1935. Neither history can be understood without the other. Constitutional frustration pushed Congress toward the demand for Purna Swaraj, or complete independence; mass resistance in turn forced the British government to the negotiating table; and the negotiations in turn exposed disagreements among Indians that the shared desire for self-government could not conceal.

That last point is the chapter's central problem. By 1930 it had become possible, for the first time on a mass scale, to agree that British rule should end. It had not become possible to agree on what should replace it. Should an independent or self-governing India be a unitary state with a strong center, or a federation with powerful provinces? Should minorities be protected by separate electorates, reserved seats in joint electorates, or other safeguards? Who could speak for Muslims, for Sikhs, for the communities then called the Depressed Classes, or for the rulers and subjects of the princely states? These were not marginal quarrels attached to an otherwise united struggle. They were questions about the distribution of political power in a future state, and they were argued with equal seriousness by people who all opposed colonial domination.

The historian's evidence for this period is unusually abundant and unusually partisan. There are statutes, command papers, and conference proceedings; viceregal correspondence preserved in British archives; Congress resolutions and the voluminous writings and letters of Gandhi; the newspapers of a lively Indian press operating under censorship and ordinance; memoirs by participants of every political persuasion; police and intelligence reports that watched crowds more attentively than they understood them; and eyewitness journalism, most famously Webb Miller's account of Dharasana. Each kind of source has its own vantage point. Official reports tend to count disorder rather than motive. Nationalist accounts tend to emphasize discipline and sacrifice. Ambedkar's later writings reflect the bitterness of a leader who believed that the Poona Pact had cost his community a hard-won safeguard. This chapter asks students to read all of these as evidence, not as verdicts.

The chapter also continues arguments begun in earlier chapters. Chapter 79 traced how Gandhi converted the Rowlatt agitation, the Khilafat cause, and Non-Cooperation into the first mass politics of the nationalist movement, and Chapter 80 examined peasant, labor, anti-caste, and women's movements that had their own organizations and aims. Civil Disobedience drew on both, but it also exposed the strain between them and the leadership of Congress. Here, too, the chapter should be read alongside Chapter 77, which explained how separate electorates entered Indian constitutional politics in 1909, and Chapter 78, which traced the Lucknow Pact and the reforms that followed the First World War.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, the declaration of independence at Lahore on the last night of 1929 was a political objective and a pledge, not an accomplished fact; India would not become sovereign for another seventeen years, and the route to that outcome would pass through war, negotiation, and Partition. Second, the Government of India Act of 1935, the most elaborate constitutional text produced under the Raj, should not be read backward as a blueprint for the independent states of 1947. It was a British statute with British safeguards, and most Indian political parties regarded it as inadequate even when they chose to work within parts of it. Chapter 82 will examine that choice, beginning with the provincial elections of 1937.

By the end of this chapter students should be able to explain how constitutional dissatisfaction, the demand for complete independence, mass civil disobedience, and competing claims to political representation interacted between 1927 and 1935; why the movement's moral force exceeded its immediate political gains; why negotiations in London and in India repeatedly failed to produce a settlement; and why the Poona Pact, the Communal Award, and the 1935 Act belong at the center of the history of Indian self-government rather than at its margins.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the appointment of an all-British Indian Statutory Commission in 1927 provoked a nationwide political reaction and how Indian groups divided over boycott and cooperation.
  2. Reconstruct the Simon boycott, the Lahore lathi charge of 30 October 1928, and the death of Lala Lajpat Rai, distinguishing established facts from contested claims.
  3. Analyze the proposals of the Nehru Report of 1928 and the objections raised by Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, and other political constituencies.
  4. Interpret Jinnah's Fourteen Points of 1929 as part of a wider debate over federalism and minority safeguards, and avoid treating Indian Muslims as a single political bloc.
  5. Distinguish dominion status from Purna Swaraj, and a political declaration of independence from the attainment of sovereignty.
  6. Evaluate why Gandhi chose salt as the issue for civil disobedience and how the Salt March of 1930 related to the larger campaign.
  7. Compare civil disobedience in Gujarat, the Tamil country, Malabar, Bengal, Odisha, and the North-West Frontier Province, and assess the varied social interests that sustained it.
  8. Assess how the colonial state responded to a movement that welcomed arrest, and why repression had limits as well as effects.
  9. Evaluate the First and Second Round Table Conferences and the Gandhi–Irwin Pact as attempts at settlement, including what the pact did and did not concede.
  10. Interpret the disagreement between Gandhi and Ambedkar over representation of the Depressed Classes, and explain the differences among separate electorates, reserved seats, and joint electorates.
  11. Analyze the Communal Award and the Poona Pact, using differing historical interpretations of their consequences.
  12. Explain the structure and limits of the Government of India Act of 1935, distinguishing the provincial autonomy that took effect in 1937 from the all-India federation that never did.

Historical Context

The constitutional framework against which Indian politicians argued in the late 1920s had been fixed by the Government of India Act of 1919, which implemented the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms discussed in Chapter 78. That Act introduced dyarchy in the provinces. Certain "transferred" subjects such as education, public health, and local self-government were placed under Indian ministers responsible to elected provincial councils, while "reserved" subjects including law and order, finance, and land revenue remained with the governor and his executive council. Ministers often controlled little money. Governors could override them in several ways. The electorate was drawn from property, tax, and education qualifications and included only a small fraction of the adult population, and separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, and other communities, first introduced in 1909 and extended in 1916, remained in force. At the center, the Governor-General retained decisive authority and an elected Legislative Assembly could be overridden through certification.

The 1919 Act contained a promise: a statutory commission would review its working after ten years. That promise explains the timing of the Simon Commission, which was appointed two years ahead of schedule. Indian politicians would later argue that the real issue was not the date but the commission's composition and the assumption that Britain alone would decide the pace of Indian self-government.

The nationalist movement entered this decade in an exhausted and divided condition. Non-Cooperation, examined in Chapter 79, had been suspended in February 1922 after the violence at Chauri Chaura, and Gandhi's imprisonment, the collapse of the Khilafat cause after the Turkish abolition of the caliphate, and the Hindu–Muslim riots of the mid-1920s had left Congress without a unified program. Within Congress, the Swarajists contested legislatures with a policy of obstruction, while many "no-changers" carried on constructive work in villages. Local peasant campaigns, such as Vallabhbhai Patel's land-revenue campaign at Bardoli in 1928, showed that mass mobilization could be revived around concrete grievances. Chapter 80 has shown that peasant organizations, trade unions, non-Brahmin and Dalit movements, and women's associations were developing programs of their own, sometimes aligned with Congress and sometimes critical of it.

The wider environment shaped these developments. A generation of Indian politicians had watched the Dominions in the British Empire gain greater autonomy, while India remained governed on different principles. Economic conditions were deteriorating. The rupee-sterling ratio fixed in 1927 was widely seen as favoring British interests, agricultural prices were falling, and the world depression that began in 1929 would soon sharply depress farm incomes, increasing the weight of fixed land-revenue demands. Meanwhile the Russian Revolution, the Chinese nationalist movement, and the example of Ireland provided models of resistance and of state-building that younger Indian activists studied closely. Within India, younger leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose urged complete independence and a social program, while older figures such as Motilal Nehru preferred a constitutional settlement within the Empire.

This chapter therefore begins where constitutional hope and nationalist impatience meet. Its time frame is 1927 to 1935, from the appointment of the Simon Commission to the enactment of the Government of India Act. Chapter 82 will take up the result: provincial elections, Congress ministries, the reorganization of the Muslim League, and the competing visions that shaped the road to independence and Partition.

Section 81.1

The Simon Commission, 1927–1928: Constitutional Review without Indian Representation

In November 1927 the British government announced the membership of the Indian Statutory Commission, soon known universally by the name of its chairman, the Liberal lawyer Sir John Simon. The commission was the body promised by the Government of India Act of 1919 to review the working of the new constitution and to recommend whether responsible government should be extended, modified, or restricted. Its seven members were all members of the British Parliament, drawn from the Conservative, Liberal, and Labour parties. Among them was Clement Attlee, who would be Prime Minister when India became independent two decades later. No Indian was appointed.

The government's reasons were not obviously malicious. The Conservative cabinet under Stanley Baldwin and the Secretary of State for India, Lord Birkenhead, wanted a body that Parliament itself would regard as authoritative, and they feared that any Indian members would be treated as representatives of a single faction and would split the commission. The timing also mattered. A general election was due in Britain in 1929, and the Conservatives, who distrusted Labour's likely approach to India, preferred that the review be completed under their own supervision. Birkenhead was moreover openly skeptical about the capacity of Indian politicians to agree on any constitution, and some historians argue that the commission was designed partly to demonstrate that failure.

To Indian politicians of almost every persuasion, however, the exclusion was a statement of principle. A body that would examine India's fitness for self-government without a single Indian member appeared to treat Indians as subjects of inquiry rather than participants in constitution-making. The sense of insult cut across the divisions of the 1920s. It touched Congress members who wanted complete independence and also liberals who had loyally worked the 1919 reforms. It also touched Indians who feared that the British would exploit communal disagreements. For many, the issue was not what the commission would say but who had the right to say it.

The reaction was not uniform. Congress, meeting at Madras in December 1927, resolved to boycott the commission at every stage and in every form. The Hindu Mahasabha, many liberals under Tej Bahadur Sapru, and a section of the Muslim League led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah also decided to boycott it. But a different section of the Muslim League, led in Punjab by Sir Muhammad Shafi, chose to cooperate, as did the Justice Party in Madras, the Unionist leadership in Punjab, many Sikh and princely-state representatives, and some organizations speaking for the Depressed Classes. B. R. Ambedkar, whose own career is traced in Chapter 80, submitted evidence on behalf of Depressed Class organizations and served on a provincial committee that worked with the commission. The pattern of cooperation shows that groups with doubts about a Congress-led constitutional future could see advantages in engaging with the British directly.

The commission also operated through a structure that was intended to soften the exclusion. Each provincial legislature was invited to appoint a committee to work with the commission, and a Central Committee, chaired by Sir Shankaran Nair, was set up to take evidence from the Indian Legislative Assembly and to cooperate with the commissioners. The commissioners made two tours of India, from February to March 1928 and again from October 1928 to April 1929. Boycott advocates dismissed these committees as window dressing, since the commissioners themselves were British and the final report would be theirs.

The difficulties of the 1919 constitution gave the commission its subject. Dyarchy had produced governments in which ministers answerable to elected councils had little control over finance, police, or land revenue; ministers could be overruled or bypassed; and the division of "transferred" and "reserved" subjects hindered coherent administration. The franchise remained narrow. Many urban professionals, merchants, and landholders felt that the reforms offered the semblance of power. Others, especially from groups previously excluded from public life, valued the opportunity for representation precisely because it offered some access to the political system.

The commission's report, published in two volumes in 1930, would propose the replacement of provincial dyarchy by provincial responsibility under safeguards, and an eventual federal structure in which the princely states would be brought into relation with British India. But it did not use the phrase "dominion status," and it did not propose responsible government at the center. By then, as later sections show, events had overtaken it. The report mattered less for its contents than for the history of the controversy that preceded it, and for the way parts of its analysis fed into the later Round Table Conferences and the 1935 Act. For the present, the important point is that the commission was boycotted by the dominant strands of organized nationalism, and the boycott itself became a nationwide campaign.

Primary Source Window

The Simon Commission and the Boycott Resolutions

Documents: Announcement of the Indian Statutory Commission (November 1927); resolutions of the Madras Congress (December 1927); Report of the Indian Statutory Commission, Cmd. 3568–3569 (1930) · Source type: Official government statement and report; political resolutions

Context: The announcement and the later report were written by British officials and parliamentarians for the British Parliament and the Government of India. The boycott resolutions were drafted by Indian political organizations to explain a refusal. The documents therefore sit on opposite sides of a dispute about authority.

Paraphrase (not original wording): The government statement presents the commission as the review required by the 1919 Act and as a body of parliamentarians able to report to Parliament. The Congress resolution declares that the commission's composition is an insult to Indian self-respect and calls for non-cooperation with it. The later report, in its recommendations, treats provincial autonomy and a federal framework as proper directions of reform while omitting any commitment on dominion status.

Analysis: These sources reveal a disagreement about legitimacy more than about technical constitutional design. The commission's report is valuable for its detailed survey of Indian administration, but it was prepared without Indian members and cannot be treated as a neutral summary of Indian opinion. The boycott resolutions show the objections of organized nationalism but do not show the views of those who chose to cooperate.

Section 81.2

The Simon Boycott, Nationwide Protest, and the Death of Lala Lajpat Rai

The commission's first party landed in Bombay on 3 February 1928, and the day was marked by a general strike, or hartal, in the city. Black flags were displayed and crowds shouted the slogan that became the emblem of the campaign: "Simon Go Back." Similar demonstrations took place in Calcutta, Madras, Delhi, Lahore, Lucknow, Patna, and many smaller towns as the commissioners traveled. Because Congress, the Hindu Mahasabha, and a large section of the Muslim League had agreed to the boycott, the demonstrations drew on a wide range of organizations, and the protests allowed leaders to display a unity that Indian politics otherwise lacked.

Students and young people were especially visible. Student associations, youth leagues, and the newly active provincial Congress committees organized processions and meetings. Lawyers who had practiced in courts, journalists, municipal councilors, and small traders joined hartals. The campaign also benefited from the networks of the earlier Non-Cooperation movement, and from the presence of a younger generation, including Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, who had recently been touring the country to build youth organizations. In regions with strong communist or labor organizations, such as Bombay, unions participated in the strikes.

The official response was to protect the commission and permit most protests so long as they remained peaceful, though police lathi charges occurred in several places. In Lucknow, where demonstrators confronted the commission in November 1928, Jawaharlal Nehru and Govind Ballabh Pant were both beaten in a police charge; Pant suffered injuries that troubled him for the remainder of his life. The violence was largely limited, but it had political effects, since it allowed nationalists to present the movement as peaceful and the police as aggressors.

The most consequential confrontation occurred in Lahore. The commission reached the city on 30 October 1928, and Lala Lajpat Rai, a veteran nationalist who had been prominent in the Arya Samaj, in Congress, and later in the Hindu Mahasabha, led a procession to the railway station carrying black flags. Police under the supervision of Superintendent James Scott, who was assisted by a young deputy, J. P. Saunders, cleared the crowd with a baton charge. Lajpat Rai was struck, and photographs and accounts of an elderly leader being beaten spread quickly. He addressed a large public meeting that evening and is reported to have said that the blows would prove to be the nails in the coffin of British rule; the exact wording of the reported remark varies between accounts, which is a reminder of how tradition shapes testimony.

Lajpat Rai died on 17 November 1928, about eighteen days after the incident. The causal relationship between the baton blows and his death is disputed. Nationalist leaders attributed his death directly to the injuries and treated him as a martyr. Government spokesmen, including some in Parliament, argued that the evidence did not establish a causal link, noting that he had a prior history of heart disease and that his death was attributed to heart failure. Historians generally accept that he was struck, that the incident affected his health, and that the contemporary political significance of his death does not depend on a settled medical judgment. What is clear is that his funeral attracted very large crowds in Lahore and that the death hardened feeling against the commission and the police.

The event also had a radical sequel. A small group of revolutionaries associated with the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association, among them Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru, and Chandrashekhar Azad, decided to avenge Lajpat Rai. On 17 December 1928 they shot and killed J. P. Saunders, mistaking him for Scott. The incident, and the subsequent bombing of the Central Legislative Assembly in April 1929 by Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt, led to a major conspiracy trial. Their execution in March 1931, discussed in §81.14, would complicate the negotiations of that year. Revolutionary nationalism was not the principal current of the Simon boycott, but the boycott helped create the emotional conditions in which it gained recruits.

The boycott accomplished several things. It displayed the breadth of opposition to the commission. It provided a platform for young leaders to assume visible roles. It gave Congress an opportunity to reorganize without a single contentious program. And it put pressure on Indian leaders to respond to the challenge issued, in 1927, by Birkenhead: that Indians should produce their own constitutional proposal acceptable to all communities. The result of that challenge was the committee whose report forms the subject of the next section.

Section 81.3

The Nehru Report of 1928: An Indian Constitutional Proposal

Lord Birkenhead's taunt, delivered in the House of Lords, that Indians were unable to agree on a constitution of their own, offered Indian politicians an opening. An All Parties Conference met in Delhi in February 1928 and again in Bombay in May. In the latter meeting a committee was appointed under the chairmanship of Motilal Nehru to determine the principles of a constitution for India. The committee's members included Tej Bahadur Sapru, Subhas Chandra Bose, Sir Ali Imam, Shuaib Qureshi, M. S. Aney, Mangal Singh, and others. Jawaharlal Nehru served as secretary to the committee, though he was among the younger figures uneasy about the moderation of its conclusions. The committee's report, published in August 1928, was the first comprehensive Indian attempt to draft a constitutional framework for the whole of British India.

The central proposal was that India should attain dominion status, equivalent to that of Canada or Australia, with a constitution established by statute of the British Parliament. The report envisaged a responsible government at the center and in the provinces, a bicameral federal parliament, and a cabinet answerable to the lower house. Residuary powers, those not assigned to either federal or provincial lists, were to rest with the center. The report proposed the separation of Sind from the Bombay Presidency, subject to financial viability, and full provincial status for the North-West Frontier Province. It recommended linguistic provinces in principle, and it included a declaration of nineteen fundamental rights, including equality before the law, freedom of conscience, freedom of association, and the right to free elementary education. This declaration would be an important antecedent for the later fundamental rights discussed in Chapter 88.

The hardest question was representation. Since 1909, Muslims had voted through separate electorates, and the Lucknow Pact of 1916, discussed in Chapter 78, had confirmed this arrangement as part of a Congress–League agreement. The Nehru Report rejected separate electorates and proposed instead joint electorates, in which all voters on a common roll chose representatives, with seats reserved for Muslims in provinces where they were a minority, in proportion to population and for a limited period of ten years. In provinces where Muslims formed a majority, notably Punjab and Bengal, no reservation was recommended, on the argument that majorities do not need protection. In the central legislature, a fixed one-quarter of seats was proposed for Muslims. Reservation for Sikhs was not recommended in Punjab, which dismayed Sikh leaders.

The committee defended its approach on grounds of principle and of pragmatism. In principle, it regarded separate electorates as an obstacle to a common national citizenship and the habits of compromise that a democratic polity required. In practice, it believed that a lasting settlement could not be built on arrangements that appeared to institutionalize division. Its critics would answer that for minorities who feared domination, the issue was not abstract principle but a concrete mechanism that guaranteed a share of power.

Reaction was mixed. Congress initially welcomed the report, and the All Parties Convention at Calcutta in December 1928 considered its adoption. Liberals such as Sapru, who had helped to draft it, were among its warmest supporters. The Hindu Mahasabha objected in part, with leaders such as M. R. Jayakar and B. S. Moonje cautious about concessions to Muslims and the proposed separation of Sind. The Sikh League and Sikh representatives demanded protections for their community in Punjab. Many Muslim leaders, as the next section shows, regarded the report as inadequate. Meanwhile Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, with S. Srinivasa Iyengar, formed the Independence for India League in late 1928 to protest that dominion status was an unacceptably limited objective, an argument that would shape the Congress debates of §81.5.

The report therefore performed a double function. It showed that Indians could draft a coherent constitutional scheme and answered Birkenhead's challenge in a formal sense. At the same time it demonstrated, in its failure to command general agreement, how difficult it was to reconcile competing communal, regional, and ideological claims. It deserves to be read not as a failure but as the opening of a constitutional conversation in which many of the topics later settled by British decision, or by the Constituent Assembly after 1946, were first set out in organized form. Students should also distinguish the report's proposal of dominion status, which was a settlement within the British Empire, from the demand for complete independence adopted at Lahore a year later.

Section 81.4

Muslim Political Demands, Jinnah's Fourteen Points, and the Limits of Constitutional Agreement

It would be a serious mistake to speak of a single Muslim response to the Nehru Report, since Muslim political opinion in the late 1920s was divided by region, class, ideology, and strategy. Some Muslim politicians, among them Dr. M. A. Ansari and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, were closely allied with Congress and favored joint electorates. The Khilafat leaders, the Ali brothers, remained influential but had been shaken by the loss of the cause that had earlier united them with Gandhi. The Punjab Muslim leadership, represented by Sir Muhammad Shafi, Sir Fazl-i-Husain, and the Unionist Party, was primarily concerned with preserving Muslim and landholding interests in a province where Muslims formed a narrow majority. The Muslim League itself was split between the Shafi faction, which cooperated with the Simon Commission, and the faction led by Jinnah, which boycotted it. Each approached the constitutional question differently.

Jinnah's own position in this period is often misunderstood if it is read through the later history of Pakistan. In the late 1920s he was a lawyer with a record of seeking Hindu–Muslim agreement, associated with the Lucknow Pact and with Congress. His proposals aimed to adapt the Nehru Report so that Muslim leaders might support it. In late 1927 and early 1928 he and a group of Muslim leaders had offered to accept joint electorates with reserved seats if certain conditions were met, namely the separation of Sind, reforms in the North-West Frontier Province and Baluchistan, and fixed Muslim representation in Punjab and Bengal in proportion to population. These proposals, sometimes called the Delhi Muslim Proposals, formed the background of the Nehru Report's attempt at accommodation.

At the Calcutta Convention in December 1928, Jinnah moved amendments that would have secured one-third of seats for Muslims in the central legislature and placed residuary powers in the provinces. The proposals were rejected, in part because of the opposition of the Hindu Mahasabha and Sikh representatives and in part because the Nehru committee considered them inconsistent with its approach. Jinnah's attempt at compromise failed, and he left Calcutta convinced that the Congress and its allies were not prepared to meet minority concerns. Later accounts describe the episode as a parting of ways, though historians debate how definitive the break was. In the early 1930s he would continue to attend the Round Table Conferences, then spend several years largely in England before returning to Indian politics in 1934.

In March 1929, at a session of the Muslim League in Delhi, Jinnah put forward fourteen points that stated the constitutional conditions on which he and his supporters would accept a future constitution. The principal demands included a federal constitution with residuary powers vested in the provinces; uniform provincial autonomy; protection of the Muslim majority in Punjab, Bengal, and the North-West Frontier Province so that it would not be reduced to a minority or even equality; a one-third share for Muslims in the central legislature; the retention of separate electorates, with the right of any community to opt for joint electorates later; the separation of Sind from Bombay; reforms in the North-West Frontier Province and Baluchistan equal to those in other provinces; safeguards for Muslim education, language, culture, and religion; adequate Muslim representation in services and government bodies; and the requirement that no cabinet at the center or in a province should be formed without at least one-third Muslim ministers. A final point proposed that no constitutional change should be made without the concurrence of the states composing the Indian federation.

These points should be read carefully. They were not a demand for a separate state. They were an effort to specify how a united federal India could protect the interests of a minority concentrated differently in different provinces. Their emphasis on provincial autonomy reflected the fact that Muslims were a majority in some provinces but a minority in the center and in others, so that strong provinces would protect their position while a strong center dominated by a Hindu majority would not. For critics, the points showed communal demands that would hinder a unified national government. For supporters, they identified minimal conditions for security under majoritarian democracy. Both views were held at the time, and both continued to shape debates through the 1930s and 1940s.

The wider disagreements were not limited to Hindus and Muslims. Sikh leaders, concentrated in Punjab, feared that neither Congress nor the Muslim League would protect their position as a minority in a province where Muslims were a plurality. They demanded reserved seats and weightage, and some groups, in their opposition to the Nehru Report, argued for protections that matched those of Muslims. Depressed Class leaders, including Ambedkar, were developing their own demands for representation, as Chapter 80 explained. Landholders, industrialists, workers, and women's organizations also had positions on the franchise and constituencies. Thus the decisive problem of the period was not simply Hindu–Muslim disagreement but a more complex set of claims about the proper balance between majority rule and minority protection.

The political lesson of 1928–1929 is therefore that shared opposition to the Simon Commission did not create a shared constitutional program. Agreement on the objective of self-government did not establish agreement on how political power should be distributed among communities, provinces, and the center. This problem would shape every negotiation discussed in the rest of the chapter.

Section 81.5

From Dominion Status to Complete Independence, 1928–1929

When the Congress met at Calcutta in December 1928 under the presidency of Motilal Nehru, the party faced a choice that the Nehru Report had sharpened. The report proposed dominion status, which many senior leaders regarded as realistic and attainable. A younger group, led by Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, regarded dominion status as incompatible with the dignity and the interests of a nation of India's size, and they wanted Congress to declare complete independence as its goal. The disagreement was in part generational and in part ideological, since Jawaharlal Nehru and Bose were influenced by socialist and anti-imperialist currents that the older leadership distrusted.

Gandhi, who had largely withdrawn from active politics after his release from prison in 1924, returned to the central stage at Calcutta and brokered a compromise. His resolution declared that Congress would adopt the Nehru Report's constitution if the British government accepted it by the end of 1929; if it did not, Congress would launch a campaign of nonviolent non-cooperation, including refusal to pay taxes. Originally Gandhi proposed a two-year period. Under pressure from Nehru and Bose, who proposed an amendment for immediate independence that was defeated by a substantial margin, the period was shortened to one year. The compromise held the party together while committing it to action if the deadline passed without result.

This episode is useful for understanding how Congress made decisions. It was not a disciplined party with a single line but a coalition of regional organizations, lawyers, merchants, landholders, peasants' representatives, and young radicals, each with different priorities. Gandhi's authority lay partly in his ability to craft formulas that allowed disagreeing factions to remain together. The year 1929 also saw new regional campaigns, including Bardoli's follow-up discussions, labor agitation in Bombay and Bengal, and the Meerut Conspiracy Case, in which the government arrested many communist and trade-union organizers in March 1929. The political environment was becoming more radical and more repressive at the same time.

The British government's response came on 31 October 1929, when the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, announced, with the approval of the Labour government that had come to power in Britain in June, that the natural issue of the 1917 declaration of responsible government was the attainment of dominion status. He also stated that a Round Table Conference would be held in London, after the Simon Commission's report, to consider the views of British India and the princely states. The announcement was cautiously worded. It offered no timetable and no assurance that the conference would accept the Nehru Report. In Britain, it drew sharp criticism from Conservatives, including Lord Birkenhead and Winston Churchill, who accused the government of weakening British authority.

Indian political leaders issued the Delhi Manifesto on 1 November 1929. Reported signatories included Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, Madan Mohan Malaviya, and Tej Bahadur Sapru. It cautiously welcomed Irwin's announcement but sought assurances that the Round Table Conference would frame a dominion constitution rather than debate whether to grant one, that Congress would have a leading role, and that political prisoners would receive a general amnesty. No satisfactory assurance followed. On 23 December, a bomb exploded beneath the viceroy's special train near Delhi, damaging it but leaving Irwin unharmed. Later that day Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, Sapru, and Jinnah met Irwin. Gandhi asked whether the conference would draft a dominion constitution; Irwin declined to guarantee it. The meeting ended without agreement.

The failure of these exchanges mattered because it supported the argument of those who maintained that negotiation unaccompanied by pressure was fruitless. Gandhi himself, who was not committed to independence as a slogan in 1928, concluded that the Congress should be prepared to act. His reasoning is worth noting: the question was no longer simply the constitutional label but whether the British government would acknowledge a right of Indians to determine their own constitution. Jawaharlal Nehru, elected president for the Lahore session after Gandhi declined the office, was in a position to give the party the clear declaration that he and Bose had demanded.

Section 81.6

The Lahore Congress of December 1929 and the Declaration of Purna Swaraj

The Lahore session of Congress, held at the end of December 1929 on the banks of the Ravi, was staged as the moment of resolution. Jawaharlal Nehru, then forty, arrived on horseback in a procession through the city, a deliberate display of youth and energy. Lahore, the capital of Punjab and a city of large Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh communities, had been shaken a year earlier by the death of Lajpat Rai and the killing of Saunders, and the session took place in an atmosphere of high feeling and tight police security.

The central resolution declared that the word swaraj in the Congress constitution meant complete independence, that the Nehru Report's scheme had lapsed with the expiry of the deadline, and that Congress should boycott the legislatures and instruct its members to resign their seats. It authorized the All-India Congress Committee to launch a program of civil disobedience, including the non-payment of taxes, whenever it should see fit. The resolution was adopted on 31 December. At midnight, as the year ended, the tricolor flag of the Congress was hoisted on the bank of the Ravi, and Nehru presided over what contemporaries regarded as a solemn and joyful ceremony.

The Congress also decided that 26 January 1930 should be observed throughout India as Independence Day, with public meetings at which a pledge would be read. The pledge, drafted by Gandhi, stated that it was a crime against humanity to submit to a rule that had deprived Indians of their freedom and had ruined them economically, politically, culturally, and spiritually, and that Indians should therefore prepare for civil disobedience, including non-payment of taxes. The day was observed in many towns and villages, often with processions, flag hoisting, spinning, and the reading of the pledge. The reading of a common text by large numbers of people in different languages was itself a political act, and the observance of 26 January would later be revived: in 1950 it became the date on which the Constitution of India came into force, an example of how nationalist symbolism carried forward into the postcolonial state.

Students should distinguish carefully what Lahore did and did not accomplish. It defined the objective of Congress as complete independence. It did not create an independent state, nor did it specify the constitution of an independent India or how its minorities, provinces, and princely states would be treated. The Lahore resolution also did not commit Congress to a single method of attaining independence. It authorized civil disobedience but left the timing and form to be decided. Within weeks Gandhi would be asked to take responsibility for that decision.

The declaration also had limits in its political reach. Many Muslim leaders and organizations did not attend or support the Lahore resolutions. The Muslim League, Sikh organizations, and liberal groups had their own positions on the future constitution, and the Congress resolution to boycott the legislatures meant that, for the time being, Congress members would not use the constitutional forum, even as other groups did. The Round Table Conferences would later be held without Congress, a consequence of this choice. Moreover, the Lahore session was dominated by delegates from Hindu-majority provinces and by urban professionals, though it was also attended by peasants and workers.

The symbolic power of Lahore nevertheless lay in its timing. It converted the goal of independence from the position of a radical minority into the avowed goal of the largest nationalist organization. After Lahore, even moderate leaders who preferred negotiation had to explain why they were not demanding independence. And the mass observance of 26 January 1930 showed that the party could mobilize participation across regions on short notice, a capacity it would need in the following months.

Historical Vocabulary
Purna Swaraj and Dominion Status

Swaraj means, literally, self-rule, and had been used by Tilak, Gandhi, and others in several senses: self-government, moral self-discipline, and village autonomy. Purna Swaraj adds purna, "complete," and in the Congress resolution of 1929 meant full independence from British control. Dominion status described the position of self-governing parts of the British Empire such as Canada, Australia, and South Africa, which controlled their own domestic and increasingly their foreign affairs while remaining within the Empire under the Crown. After the Balfour Declaration of 1926 and the Statute of Westminster of 1931, dominions had substantial autonomy.

The distinction mattered politically. Supporters of dominion status argued that it was a practical route to self-government with minimal conflict. Advocates of Purna Swaraj argued that anything short of complete independence would leave India subordinate. In 1947 India and Pakistan would in fact become dominions first, with the British monarch as head of state, until their constitutions were completed, a reminder that the two categories were not always mutually exclusive in practice.

Section 81.7

Why Salt? Gandhi's Strategy and the Preparation for Civil Disobedience

In February 1930 the Congress Working Committee, meeting at Sabarmati, authorized Gandhi to plan and lead civil disobedience. For several weeks he gave little public indication of his intentions, and many leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru, were uncertain what form the campaign would take. Gandhi's choice, when it came, surprised many observers: he would break the salt laws.

Salt had been a government monopoly and a source of revenue in various forms since the early years of Company rule. The Salt Act of 1882 and related regulations prohibited the manufacture of salt except under government license or by the government's own works and imposed an excise duty on salt produced or imported. In the 1920s the duty had been doubled by executive action, raising its burden. Because salt was a necessity for every household, and because poorer families spent a larger proportion of their small income on it, the tax weighed more heavily on the poor than on the rich. Salt also had strong associations in people's minds with daily labor, food, and the livelihood of coastal communities who had once made it freely.

Gandhi's reasoning combined moral and practical considerations. He needed an issue that was intelligible to villagers, did not depend on specialist knowledge, and allowed anyone to participate by a simple act. At the same time it had to be a clear violation of a law that nationalists could present as unjust. Salt met these criteria. It linked rich and poor, urban and rural, and cut across religious lines. It also allowed Gandhi to distinguish between peaceful lawbreaking and violence: breaking the salt law harmed no person, yet the state could not ignore it without conceding its authority. The tactic was an application of satyagraha, the method he had developed in South Africa and used in Champaran, Kheda, and Ahmedabad, and refined through the failures of Non-Cooperation, as discussed in Chapter 79.

On 30 January 1930, Gandhi published in his journal Young India a list of eleven demands, a kind of ultimatum to the government. They included the abolition of the salt tax, a reduction of the land revenue by half, a reduction of military expenditure and of the high salaries of senior officials, the introduction of prohibition, protection for Indian textiles, the reservation of coastal shipping for Indians, the release of political prisoners other than those convicted of violence, the abolition of the Criminal Investigation Department's political wing, changes in the arms law, and a lower rupee-sterling exchange ratio. The list shows that the salt campaign was meant to carry a wider agenda, appealing to peasants, merchants, and the middle classes, and that it did not rest on a single grievance.

On 2 March 1930 Gandhi wrote a letter to Irwin and sent it through a young Englishman, Reginald Reynolds, who was associated with his ashram. The letter began "Dear Friend," a salutation that surprised many. It set out Gandhi's view that British rule was an injury to India, described the burdens of land revenue, military expenditure, and the salt tax, and stated that unless the viceroy removed the evils he listed, he would begin a civil disobedience campaign on 11 March, with the help of ashram colleagues, to break the salt laws. Irwin did not respond substantively; a secretary replied that he regretted that Gandhi was planning to violate the law. The exchange allowed Gandhi to say that he had given the government fair notice and an opportunity to avoid confrontation.

Preparation for the march was careful. Gandhi chose seventy-eight volunteers from the ashram and from various parts of India, so that the group represented different regions and social backgrounds, and included Hindus and Muslims, though most were from Gujarat. He insisted on discipline: participants were to abstain from violence, accept arrest without resistance, and observe prayer and spinning during the journey. The route was planned in advance, with stops in villages where meetings would be held. Congress workers in Gujarat, many of them experienced from the Bardoli campaign, arranged food, lodging, and publicity. Gandhi also drew on his understanding of the press, inviting journalists, allowing filming, and sending dispatches that attracted international attention.

Two further points should be noted. First, Gandhi's choice reflected an awareness of the limits of earlier mass campaigns. Non-Cooperation had ended in violence; he wished to begin Civil Disobedience with a carefully controlled act that could be extended gradually and withdrawn if disorder threatened. Second, the choice of salt reveals a strategic judgment about the state: that an administration that rested on revenue and law would be more vulnerable to the moral exposure of an unjust law than to a direct physical challenge.

Historical Vocabulary
Civil Disobedience

Civil disobedience means the deliberate, public, and nonviolent breach of specific laws that the protesters regard as unjust, accompanied by willingness to accept the legal penalty. The phrase was made famous by Henry David Thoreau, though Gandhi preferred to describe his method as satyagraha, "holding to truth." It differs from non-cooperation, which involves withdrawal of support from the government, such as boycotts of courts, schools, and councils, without necessarily breaking any particular law.

In India, the Non-Cooperation campaign of 1920–1922 emphasized withdrawal, while the Civil Disobedience campaign of 1930–1934 emphasized lawbreaking and the refusal of obedience to specific regulations. The two overlapped, since Civil Disobedience also used boycotts and picketing. Chapter 79 examined the earlier campaign; this chapter shows how its successor changed in method and scope.

Section 81.8

The Salt March, March 12–April 6, 1930: Sabarmati to Dandi

On the morning of 12 March 1930, after a prayer meeting, Gandhi left Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad with seventy-eight chosen companions. He carried a staff and walked at the front. The march ran roughly 240 miles, or about 390 kilometers, through the districts of Kheda, Surat, and Navsari, and ended at the coastal village of Dandi. The group walked about ten to fifteen miles a day, usually in the early morning and evening to avoid the heat, and halted in villages for meetings, prayers, and the collection of funds.

The villages along the route had been prepared. Local Congress workers arranged food, water, and places to sleep, and local volunteers cleaned roads and decorated entrances. Gandhi spoke each day about the evils of the salt tax, the need for nonviolence, the importance of spinning and the boycott of foreign cloth, and the sins of untouchability and liquor. He also appealed to village officials, many of whom held hereditary posts such as headmen or accountants, to resign. In Gujarat during these weeks, hundreds of village officials did resign, a significant sign of political pressure on a colonial administration that relied heavily on local functionaries. The campaign thus began to hollow out the state at its lowest level while the march itself was still under way.

The scale of the movement grew along the way. More volunteers joined, and crowds in the villages grew. Press coverage expanded as Indian-language and English newspapers sent correspondents, and foreign journalists reported the march to audiences in Britain and the United States. The government's attitude was at first uncertain. Officials debated whether to arrest Gandhi at once, and they decided to wait, partly because they thought the march might fizzle out and partly because they feared that arresting him would increase its force. Irwin's reports to London show that the government underestimated the potential of the movement in these early weeks.

Gandhi reached Dandi on 5 April. After spending the night in prayer, he walked into the sea on the morning of 6 April, bathed, and then picked up a lump of natural salt left on the beach by the tide. By accepted accounts he is said to have remarked that with this act he was shaking the foundations of the British Empire; the wording preserved in different reports varies, and the saying has probably acquired some of its force in retrospect. The act itself was less a manufacture of salt than a symbolic breach of the law, since the crust was a natural deposit. Gandhi and his followers then began to evaporate seawater to produce salt, and he asked supporters across India to do likewise.

The Salt March should be understood in several ways. It was a religious pilgrimage in the idiom of Indian ascetic practice, with Gandhi walking like a pilgrim. It was a media event that used press, photograph, and newsreel to reach distant audiences. It was a political organizing tour that reached rural Gujarat and strengthened Congress networks. And it was a deliberate challenge to the state that tested what the government would do. Its ability to combine these roles explains why it produced such a powerful effect.

Care is needed, however, about what the march was not. It was not the entire Civil Disobedience Movement. It was the opening act of a campaign that spread to many regions, took many forms, and lasted for years, as the following sections show. Its immediate participants were few, and the majority of those who later broke the salt law did so far from Dandi. Nor was it the only possible beginning. Some leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, were initially unsure about the emphasis on salt. Historians have also noted that the march's symbolism worked partly because of Gujarat's strong Congress organization and because Gandhi's own prestige as a leader was exceptional. Later campaigns lacked both advantages.

After Dandi Gandhi moved slowly down the coast, continuing to speak and to prepare a raid on the Dharasana Salt Works, a government factory, as the next stage. The government, uncertain how to respond, began arresting local leaders in other regions while leaving Gandhi at liberty until early May. The summoning of the people to civil disobedience had been successful. The question now was what shape the movement would take as it spread.

Section 81.9

Civil Disobedience Spreads: Salt Making, Boycotts, and Regional Campaigns

After 6 April 1930, people across India began to violate the salt laws in many ways. The simplest form was symbolic: a nationalist worker would publicly collect a small quantity of natural salt, often at a mass meeting, and the police would arrest him or her or allow the act to pass. A second form was the organized manufacture of illicit salt by evaporating seawater in pans, a practice that could be sustained for weeks and, in coastal areas, provided small amounts of marketable salt. A third was the sale of illegal salt in towns, sometimes as a ritual of defiance, with packets carried through streets by volunteers. A fourth was the raid on government salt works, which involved crowds attempting to enter the premises, and brought direct confrontation with guards and police.

In Gujarat, the home of the campaign, the movement drew on the networks created during earlier satyagrahas. Volunteers manufactured salt along the Surat and Bharuch coasts, and Congress workers organized village councils and picketing. In Bombay city, crowds made salt on the beaches of Chowpatty and Worli, and the Congress organized a salt satyagraha at Wadala. Merchants, Parsi and Gujarati businesspeople, and professionals participated alongside students.

In the Tamil country, C. Rajagopalachari, who had been a leading Congress organizer in Madras and was a close associate of Gandhi, led a march of about a hundred volunteers from Tiruchirappalli to Vedaranyam, on the Coromandel coast, beginning on 13 April. They walked about 150 miles in some twelve days, passing through Thanjavur district, where local Congress committees offered hospitality. At Vedaranyam on 28 April the group made salt and were arrested. The march was explicitly modeled on Dandi and illustrates how a leader in another linguistic region adapted the strategy to local organization.

On the Malabar coast, K. Kelappan led a march from Calicut to Payyannur in April, and volunteers made salt on the northern Kerala beach. In Andhra, salt-making camps were established along the coast and in the delta districts. In Bengal, the movement spread unevenly. In Midnapore district, particularly at Contai and Tamluk, peasants and local Congress workers made salt and refused payment of the local chaukidari tax, a levy for the village watchmen, creating one of the most sustained regional campaigns. In Odisha, Gopabandhu Choudhury and others led salt satyagraha at coastal sites such as Inchudi near Balasore. In Karachi, Sind, and other coastal areas, smaller groups participated.

The regional differences were significant. In Gujarat and in Tamil country the campaign followed the Gandhian model most closely, with disciplined volunteers and an emphasis on nonviolence. In Bengal it intersected with a tradition of revolutionary activism. On 18 April 1930, a group led by the schoolteacher Surya Sen seized the police and auxiliary-force armories in Chittagong, an action that was explicitly not Gandhian, though it occurred in the same weeks and was sometimes confused by the authorities with the Congress campaign. The government's actions against Chittagong and its response to civil disobedience influenced one another. In the Central Provinces and Maharashtra, the movement drew more on forest-law violations and on resistance to local taxes than on salt, since these regions lacked coasts.

The shift from the single march to regional campaigns also changed the nature of leadership. Gandhi remained the symbolic center, but local organizers, many of them lawyers, schoolteachers, or merchants with experience in Congress committees, decided how and where to act. The Congress Working Committee issued general guidance, which was later supplemented by ordinances from the government that outlawed Congress committees, so local activists increasingly improvised. The campaign thus acquired the uneven but wide-ranging character that sets Civil Disobedience apart from the more centrally directed Non-Cooperation. The movement also drew participants whose reasons for joining were not identical to Gandhi's. A coastal salt maker might be seeking the restoration of a livelihood; a student might be pursuing national honor; a trader might be reacting to the economic slump; a peasant might be resisting the revenue collector.

Section 81.10

Economic Resistance: Revenue Refusal, Foreign-Cloth Boycotts, and Local Political Demands

The Civil Disobedience Movement was not only about salt. From April 1930 it incorporated a range of economic actions through which Indians resisted the colonial state and its commercial partners. The most visible was the boycott of foreign cloth and the picketing of shops that sold it, a campaign that combined nationalist and economic aims, since it promoted Indian mill and handloom production and punished British textile exporters. In cities such as Bombay, Ahmedabad, Calcutta, and Delhi, Indian merchants' associations supported the boycott and pledged not to import or sell foreign cloth. Imports of British cotton piece goods fell significantly in 1930–1931, though the fall was also affected by the world depression and by tariff changes.

Picketing of liquor shops was a second major form. Women volunteers in particular stood outside shops selling alcohol and toddy, urging customers not to buy. Prohibition was one of Gandhi's eleven demands and attracted women's organizations, temperance advocates, and some religious reformers. The excise on liquor was an important source of provincial revenue, so picketing attacked government finances as well as drinking.

A third form was resistance to land revenue and local taxes. In Gujarat, particularly in the Bardoli and Kheda areas where earlier campaigns had been conducted, cultivators refused to pay the revenue, and many suffered confiscation of land and property as a result. In parts of Bengal, the chaukidari tax was refused. In Maharashtra, the Central Provinces, and Karnataka, forest-law violations occurred, in which villagers cut grass or wood from reserved forests in defiance of the Forest Act, a protest that connected to long-standing grievances about restricted access to forest resources. In parts of the United Provinces, a "no-rent" campaign grew during the depression and was associated with the Congress leadership, in particular with Jawaharlal Nehru.

The social base of these actions was varied, and the interests of the participants did not always coincide. Merchants and industrialists, notably in Bombay and Ahmedabad, supported the boycott and gave funds to Congress, in part because they benefited from the protection of Indian cloth. But their support was conditional, and many became uneasy when picketing and no-tax campaigns threatened to disrupt business or when radical ideas gained influence. Industrial workers took part in some strikes and demonstrations, though trade unions had their own organizational history, discussed in Chapter 80, and Congress did not command their loyalty. Landowning cultivators, particularly the dominant peasant castes such as the Patidars of Gujarat, were among the most active supporters of revenue refusal, since they paid the largest land-revenue demand and had experience of Congress organization. Tenants, sharecroppers, and landless laborers, who often paid rent to landlords rather than revenue to the state, had different interests, and the depression intensified tensions between tenants and landlords. Congress leaders were often reluctant to endorse rent refusal against Indian landlords, a position that sometimes left poorer rural people feeling that the nationalist movement did not fully address their problems.

The distinction between disputes with the British state and disputes with Indian landlords or employers is crucial. When villagers refused to pay revenue to the government, the Congress could readily support them as part of a campaign against colonial taxation. When tenants refused rent to a zamindar or workers struck against an Indian mill owner, the situation was more complicated, since the Congress included landlords and industrialists among its supporters and donors. The movement's capacity to accommodate these tensions varied by region and over time. These issues connect to the independent agrarian and labor movements examined in Chapter 80, which were developing demands that went beyond the immediate aims of Civil Disobedience.

Government revenue and trade were both affected. Land-revenue collection fell in some districts, imports of foreign cloth declined, and the government's excise income from liquor decreased. Yet these effects should not be exaggerated. Most districts continued to pay revenue, and the government's finances were not threatened with collapse. The economic dimension of Civil Disobedience was significant mainly because it linked the nationalist cause to the daily economic interests of many groups, and because it showed the difficulty of maintaining that link when interests diverged.

Everyday Life and Social History

Salt, Cloth, and the Household Budget

For a poor household in rural India in 1930, salt was a small but unavoidable purchase, and a tax on it was a tax on every meal. Households also bought cloth, usually coarse and often imported or mill-made, and many consumed liquor or toddy. Each purchase became politically charged during the campaign. A family that refused foreign cloth might turn to khadi, which was costlier and coarser; a family that participated in picketing might lose a day's labor; a family whose men were imprisoned might lose its main income and face a debt that persisted long after the campaign.

The costs and risks fell unevenly. Wealthier households could absorb a fine or the confiscation of property; poorer households could not. Women who joined picketing often did so while continuing to manage the household, and many, especially from middle-class and professional families, experienced public political action for the first time. The campaign therefore changed domestic life as well as national politics, though the evidence for such changes survives mainly in memoirs and police files and requires careful interpretation.

Section 81.11

Women, the Frontier, and Regional Political Leadership in Civil Disobedience

One of the most striking features of Civil Disobedience was the participation of women. In earlier campaigns, women had supported nationalist causes as spinners, fundraisers, and members of associations, as Chapter 80 explained. In 1930 they marched, made salt, picketed shops, addressed meetings, led processions, and accepted imprisonment in large numbers. Gandhi encouraged this, partly because he considered women suited to nonviolent resistance, and many women organizers carried out their own initiatives. Participation was strongest in urban areas of Gujarat, Bombay, Bengal, Madras, and the United Provinces, though women from villages also took part.

Sarojini Naidu, a poet and former Congress president, was a major leader and, after Gandhi's arrest, was chosen to lead the Dharasana action. Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay organized women volunteers in Bombay and was arrested after taking part in an attempt to sell contraband salt. Kasturba Gandhi, Mithuben Petit, Avantikabai Gokhale, Hansa Mehta, and many others organized marches and meetings. In Bengal and the United Provinces, women from families of Congress leaders, including Kamala Nehru, Swarup Rani Nehru, and Vijayalakshmi Pandit, took leading roles in Allahabad, where women's committees organized picketing and demonstrations. Women activists were imprisoned in hundreds, and in some provinces jailed women outnumbered what officials had expected.

The meaning of this participation was debated then and since. For some leaders, women's activism extended their domestic roles into the public sphere and was compatible with traditional ideas of female virtue. For others, it created a basis for demands for greater legal and political rights, and by 1931 women's organizations were pressing for equal franchise and rights, as the Karachi resolutions would show. Historians also note that most participants came from middle-class and upper-caste backgrounds, and that the movement's gender politics did not eliminate patriarchal assumptions within Congress or the family.

Developments in the North-West Frontier Province demonstrate that the movement was not merely an extension of decisions made by Gandhi. Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a Pashtun reformer from Utmanzai, had spent years establishing schools and promoting social reform among Pashtun communities. In 1929 he founded the Khudai Khidmatgar, "Servants of God," a volunteer organization whose members wore red uniforms and were popularly called Red Shirts. They took a pledge of nonviolence, an ethic that Ghaffar Khan, a devout Muslim, derived in part from Islamic notions of service and patience, and they worked for social reform, opposition to feuding, and resistance to colonial rule. The movement drew on Pashtun grievances against heavy land revenue, the colonial frontier regulations, and the power of khans and landlords, and it built an organization in the settled districts of Peshawar, Mardan, Charsadda, and Bannu.

The scholarship of Sayed Wiqar Ali Shah and other historians of the Frontier shows that Pashtun politics had its own social and regional logic, with its own divisions among landlords, peasants, clerics, and urban groups, and that Ghaffar Khan's movement allied with Congress as a matter of strategy rather than subordination. The alliance became formal in 1931. Historians have also stressed that the Khudai Khidmatgar's nonviolence should be seen in the setting of Pashtun customs and Islamic ideas, not merely as Gandhi's influence transplanted.

The confrontation of 23 April 1930 in Peshawar, known as the Qissa Khwani Bazaar massacre after the city's “Storytellers' Bazaar,” was the Frontier's most consequential episode of the campaign. Following arrests of Congress and Khudai Khidmatgar leaders, demonstrators gathered in the city; armored vehicles drove into crowds and troops fired on civilians. The scale of the deaths is disputed. Colonial authorities reported approximately forty people killed, whereas historian Sayed Wiqar Ali Shah, drawing on additional published and unpublished evidence, estimates about 230 killed and more than 500 injured. Neither figure is a verified final count. The discrepancy reflects incomplete records, contested investigations, and the politics of colonial repression; the deaths of unarmed demonstrators themselves are not in doubt.

The Royal Garhwal Rifles episode became a lasting symbol of military dissent. Nationalist accounts describe soldiers associated with Chandra Singh Garhwali refusing to fire on a predominantly Muslim crowd. Yet in the House of Commons on 30 June 1930, the Secretary of State for India said the men were formally court-martialed for refusing orders to proceed from their lines into the city—not for disobeying a direct order to shoot. Seventeen men received sentences: one transportation for life, one for fifteen years, and fifteen others ranging from ten years' transportation to three years' rigorous imprisonment. Accounts differ on whether the episode should be dated 23 or 24 April. The later interpretation of a refusal to fire must therefore be distinguished from the official charges, without discounting the soldiers' defiance. Colonial control of Peshawar weakened temporarily before the government reasserted authority and intensified repression against Khudai Khidmatgar activists.

Section 81.12

Dharasana, Imprisonment, and the Colonial Repression of 1930

The government's response developed in stages. In the first weeks it arrested local leaders and salt makers while avoiding a confrontation with Gandhi. By early May it concluded that the movement was spreading too rapidly. In the night of 4–5 May, Gandhi was arrested at Karadi, near Dandi, under a regulation of 1827 that permitted detention without trial, and lodged in Yeravda Jail near Poona. Other leaders followed: Jawaharlal Nehru had been arrested in April, and in the following months the Congress Working Committee, provincial committees, and thousands of local workers were arrested. In June the government declared the Working Committee unlawful, and in the autumn the Congress itself was banned in many provinces.

The government drew on a broad set of legal powers. It issued ordinances prohibiting meetings and processions, restricting the press, and suppressing "unlawful associations," and it used the Indian Press Act and other legislation to punish newspapers that published nationalist news; many editors were prosecuted and presses were closed or required to deposit security. Police used batons against crowds, and in some places firing occurred. Property, including land, houses, and goods, was seized from defaulters in revenue campaigns, and fines were collected from villages. Estimates of the number imprisoned in 1930–1931 are usually given as upward of sixty thousand, though figures vary with the definition of imprisonment and the source. Conditions in jail were harsh and varied widely, and political prisoners did not always receive the better treatment that some leaders obtained.

The most famous confrontation occurred at Dharasana, a government salt factory south of Dandi. After Gandhi's arrest the leadership of the planned raid passed to Abbas Tyabji, then to Sarojini Naidu, with Gandhi's son Manilal and the Muslim leader Imam Abdul Kadir Bawazeer among the organizers. On 21 May 1930, columns of volunteers approached the works, which were surrounded by barbed wire and a ditch and guarded by local police under British officers. The volunteers, who had been trained to accept blows without retaliation or defense, advanced in groups, and were struck down by police using steel-tipped lathis. Wave after wave of volunteers continued to approach, and the injured were taken away to a makeshift hospital.

International attention came through the reporting of Webb Miller, an American correspondent for the United Press, who witnessed the event and sent a dispatch that was published in many newspapers in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere. Miller's account, which described the volunteers' discipline and the police violence in vivid terms, was in part obstructed by British censorship in India, which he evaded by sending his story through other channels. His dispatch gave figures of two killed and over three hundred injured, figures that other sources do not wholly confirm; he was not able to count the casualties himself, and the figure should be treated as an estimate relayed from the organizers' sources. The report had effects beyond India. It influenced opinion in the United States and in Britain, where some members of Parliament raised the matter, and it shaped later images of Indian nonviolent resistance. A reader should note that Miller's account was written for a Western audience and reflects the concerns of that audience, and that the Indian experience of the event comes through other sources as well.

Repression had mixed effects. It imprisoned many leaders and activists, disrupted organization, and instilled fear. It also made martyrs, kept the issue in the press, and gave participants a moral claim that the state could not easily answer. The government faced the problem of a movement that deliberately sought arrest. Mass imprisonment strained the jail system, and the authorities could not easily imprison tens of thousands indefinitely. But the repression also had limits on the nationalist side. Not all regions maintained nonviolent discipline. In May 1930 in Sholapur, a textile town in Maharashtra, rioting after the arrest of Congress leaders led to attacks on police and government buildings and a brief period of local self-rule before martial law was imposed and several people were executed. Gandhi and many Congress leaders regretted such violence, and the episode illustrates the tension between a leadership committed to nonviolence and crowds whose responses were less disciplined.

The principal question of this section, how the colonial state sought to preserve political authority against a movement that invited arrest and punishment, has more than one answer. The government used coercion but also sought allies among Indians willing to cooperate, including princes, landholders, loyalist politicians, and some minority organizations, and it sought to retain the initiative by promising a Round Table Conference. Repression and negotiation were thus complementary parts of a single strategy, a point that helps explain why the two halves of this chapter's history cannot be separated.

Everyday Life and Social History

Picketing, Prison, and Family Life

Participation in Civil Disobedience had consequences for families. A man or woman who was arrested might spend months in prison, during which the household had to manage without income or labor. Women who joined picketing sometimes did so against the wishes of relatives, and some experienced both new freedoms and new risks. Prison memoirs describe overcrowded barracks, poor food, and the use of the jail as a political school in which prisoners from different backgrounds read, debated, and organized. Children of activists grew up in households whose members were often absent, and the experience formed part of the memory of a generation.

For many poor participants, the costs were heavier and the records thinner. Villagers whose land was confiscated for non-payment of revenue sometimes lost it permanently, and debts incurred during the campaign lingered. Their stories are less well documented than those of leaders, which is a reminder that nationalist memory tends to highlight the more visible participants.

Section 81.13

The First Round Table Conference, 1930–1931: Federation without Congress

The Round Table Conference promised in Irwin's declaration of October 1929 opened in London on 12 November 1930, inaugurated by King George V in the House of Lords and presided over by the Labour Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald. By then the Simon Commission's report had been published, Civil Disobedience was at its height, and the Congress leadership was in jail. The conference lasted until 19 January 1931. About eighty-nine delegates took part: representatives of the three British parties, sixteen delegates from the princely states, and some fifty-eight from British India, selected by the government to represent communities and interests rather than elected by any Indian body.

The composition of the Indian delegation shows how the government understood Indian politics. Muslim delegates were led by the Aga Khan and included Jinnah, Sir Muhammad Shafi, Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar, and Maulvi Fazlul Haq. The Hindu Mahasabha was represented by B. S. Moonje and M. R. Jayakar; Liberals by Tej Bahadur Sapru, V. S. Srinivasa Sastri, and C. Y. Chintamani; Sikhs by Sardar Ujjal Singh and others; and the Depressed Classes by B. R. Ambedkar and R. Srinivasan. Landholders, business interests, labor, women, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans also had delegates. The Indian National Congress was absent. It had declared that it would attend only a conference to frame a constitution for complete independence, and its leaders were in prison.

Without Congress, the conference nevertheless made significant progress on constitutional principles. The most important development was the willingness of the princely states' representatives, led by the Maharaja of Bikaner and the Nawab of Bhopal, to participate in an all-India federation that would link the states to British India. The princes' position reflected their own interests: they hoped a federal structure would protect their autonomy and their treaty relationship with the Crown, known as paramountcy, from interference by an Indian-controlled central government, and they feared that an advance in British India alone would leave them exposed. Their acceptance changed the constitutional discussion, because federation had not featured prominently in the Nehru Report and, with the states at the table, it became the core of British planning.

The conference agreed in principle on several propositions. The central government would be federal, with responsible government at the center subject to safeguards in the spheres of defense, external affairs, and finance and for the protection of minorities, and the provinces would be autonomous. The Federal Structure Committee, chaired by Lord Sankey, explored the allocation of powers and the composition of a federal legislature. MacDonald's concluding statement of 19 January 1931 declared that the government's policy was to seek a federal constitution with responsibility at the center, subject to necessary safeguards. This was a significant statement, but it was formulated in conditional terms and left unresolved how responsibility would be reconciled with reserved powers.

Minority representation remained unresolved. The Muslim delegates insisted on separate electorates and on protections in provinces where they were a majority or a minority, while Hindu delegates were divided. Sikhs pressed for protection in Punjab. Ambedkar argued that the Depressed Classes should be recognized as a distinct minority entitled to political safeguards, a position he set out in a memorandum and in speeches, and he gave unusually forceful testimony to the conference about the historical exclusion of his community. The delegates failed to reach agreement, and MacDonald indicated that the British government would determine the question if Indians could not, a statement that foreshadowed the Communal Award discussed in §81.17.

The first conference therefore produced proposals and a direction rather than a settlement. Its significance lay in establishing federation and provincial autonomy as the framework of British policy, in drawing the princes into the constitutional process, and in showing that no settlement could be considered legitimate without Congress. The British government, recognizing that the absence of Congress limited the conference's authority, began to look for a way to bring Congress into the discussions. That effort led to the contacts between Irwin and Gandhi in early 1931.

Section 81.14

The Gandhi–Irwin Pact of March 1931 and the Karachi Congress

Gandhi and other members of the Working Committee were released unconditionally on 25 January 1931, shortly after the close of the first conference, as a gesture of conciliation. After consultation with Congress leaders, Gandhi sought a meeting with the viceroy. Between 17 February and 5 March 1931, Gandhi and Irwin held a series of discussions at the viceroy's house in Delhi, eight meetings in all. The talks were controversial in Britain, where Winston Churchill, then out of government, publicly condemned the spectacle of a viceroy negotiating on equal terms with a leader he had imprisoned, whom he described in disparaging terms. For nationalists the meetings were significant because the head of the Raj treated Gandhi as a negotiating partner.

The agreement, signed on 5 March 1931 and often called the Delhi Pact, was a compromise. Congress agreed to suspend civil disobedience, to participate in the next Round Table Conference, and to abandon boycotts directed specifically against British goods. The government agreed to withdraw the ordinances that had been issued to deal with the movement, to release political prisoners who had not been convicted of violence, to permit peaceful picketing in certain circumstances, to allow the residents of villages along the coast to collect or make salt for their own use and for sale within the village, and to restore, in some circumstances, property that had been seized, though not where it had been sold to third parties. The government refused to hold an inquiry into police excesses, a Congress demand on which Gandhi yielded.

Students should be careful about what the pact did not do. It did not abolish the salt tax; the salt monopoly and tax remained in force, with limited exceptions for local, domestic use. It did not concede independence, nor did it commit the government to any specific constitutional outcome. The agreement mentioned federation with Indian responsibility, safeguards in the interests of India, and the concept of reserved subjects, which represented the government's position. Gandhi regarded the pact as a truce that preserved the principle of Congress's right to resist and allowed it to enter the discussion on constitutional questions with prestige restored.

The agreement divided nationalist opinion. Gandhi and his supporters argued that it recognized Congress as an equal party and brought relief to thousands of prisoners and villagers. Critics, including many younger radicals, argued that it gave away the movement's leverage for too little, since the salt tax remained and the status of those convicted of violent offenses was left unresolved. Jawaharlal Nehru, though he accepted Gandhi's judgment, was dismayed at the compromises. Some of Congress's supporters in business, who suffered from the disruptions of the boycott and the depression, welcomed the end of the campaign.

The controversy over the executions of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Sukhdev made matters worse. The three had been convicted in the Lahore Conspiracy Case for the killing of J. P. Saunders. A prominent section of public opinion demanded that their sentences be commuted as part of the settlement, and Gandhi raised the issue with Irwin, who refused to intervene and said he could not justify commutation. The three were hanged in Lahore on 23 March 1931, earlier than expected and in secrecy. Congress leaders condemned the execution but could not reverse it. When Gandhi arrived in Karachi for the annual Congress session, he was greeted with black-flag demonstrations by young activists who accused him of failing to save the three men. Historians debate how much leverage Gandhi actually had: some argue that he could have made commutation a condition of the pact, while others argue that Irwin and the government would not have accepted it and that the pact would have collapsed. Either way, the episode illustrates the gap between the nonviolent mainstream of Congress and revolutionary currents that had gained popularity.

The Karachi session of the Congress, held from 29 to 31 March 1931 under the presidency of Vallabhbhai Patel, endorsed the Gandhi–Irwin Pact and authorized Gandhi to represent Congress at the next Round Table Conference. It also adopted a resolution on fundamental rights and economic policy, largely drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru with Gandhi's approval. The resolution committed Congress, in a future constitution, to guarantee civil liberties and religious freedom, equality before the law regardless of caste, creed, or sex, universal adult franchise, free primary education, and the protection of minority languages and cultures. Its economic provisions called for a reduction in land revenue and rent, relief for peasants from debt, state ownership or control of key industries, labor protections, and minimum wages. The resolution was a landmark: it showed Congress taking up the concerns of workers and peasants, and many of its provisions would resurface in the Constituent Assembly debates of the 1940s, discussed in Chapter 88. It also provided a means of reconciling the party's radical and moderate wings without committing it to a particular social revolution.

The pact therefore gave the government a respite, gave Congress a platform, and set the stage for Gandhi's departure for London. But it did not eliminate underlying disagreements, either between the government and Congress or among Indians. The question of constitutional design, above all the representation of minorities, remained.

Section 81.15

The Second Round Table Conference, 1931: Gandhi, Ambedkar, and Competing Constitutional Claims

Gandhi sailed from Bombay in late August 1931 and reached London in mid-September, after the conference had opened on 7 September. He was the sole official representative of Congress, a decision that gave him a position of exceptional prominence but also exposed him to the disadvantage of speaking alone. He was accompanied by Sarojini Naidu, Madan Mohan Malaviya (who attended in a personal capacity), his secretary Mahadev Desai, and others. In Britain a National Government under MacDonald, formed in August in response to the financial crisis, held office, with a Conservative majority, and the Secretary of State for India was Sir Samuel Hoare. Gandhi stayed in the working-class district of Bow, in East London, at Kingsley Hall, and visited textile workers in Lancashire, who had suffered from the Indian boycott of cloth, an act that drew great public attention.

At the conference, Gandhi insisted that Congress represented the whole of India, including Muslims, Depressed Classes, and other minorities, in the sense that its goal was a free India in which all communities would have equal rights. He proposed that the central subjects of defense and external affairs should be controlled by India, and he demanded that the future constitution should be established with complete responsibility at the center, subject to safeguards that would be defined in India's interest. These positions placed him in conflict with the government and with other delegates, who treated Congress as one party among several. The difficulty was compounded by the fact that other communities rejected his claim to speak for them.

The dominant subject became the representation of minorities. The Muslim delegation, with the Aga Khan, Jinnah, Shafi, and others, demanded separate electorates and the protection of Muslim majorities in the provinces. Sikhs demanded similar safeguards in Punjab. Gandhi sought to negotiate an agreement with Muslim leaders, offering to accept their demands in return for their support of complete independence, but the discussions failed, in part because of divisions among the Muslim delegates and the opposition of other minorities and of Hindu groups. In November 1931, Muslims, Depressed Classes, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans concluded a joint memorandum, sometimes called the Minorities Pact, to coordinate their demands for safeguards. Sikhs were not party to the pact and viewed its implications for Punjab with concern.

The confrontation between Gandhi and Ambedkar was one of the most important of the conference. Ambedkar argued that the Depressed Classes constituted a distinct political minority, a group of about 40 to 50 million people who had been subjected to social exclusion by the caste system and who could not depend on caste Hindus, even sympathetic ones, to protect their interests. He demanded separate electorates for the Depressed Classes, initially for a transitional period, together with universal adult franchise, and representation in services and legislatures proportionate to their numbers. His position rested on his experience that formal equality would not suffice without political power, and he insisted on the community's right to elect its own representatives.

Gandhi opposed separate electorates for the Depressed Classes. He maintained that Congress represented them as well, that untouchability was a wrong within Hindu society which Hindus themselves were bound to remove, and that separate electorates would perpetuate and institutionalize the division between caste Hindus and the Depressed Classes. He is reported to have stated, in a heated exchange and in the Minorities Committee, that he would resist the claim with his life, and he also dismissed the claim that Ambedkar represented the communities in question. Ambedkar replied that Congress had not shown itself an effective defender of the Depressed Classes, that other organizations such as his own represented them better, and that safeguards had to be political, not merely moral. Delegates such as Rettamalai Srinivasan, who also represented the Depressed Classes, sometimes took positions that differed from Ambedkar's, which showed that the community was not politically uniform.

This exchange deserves close study because it shows two sincere opponents of untouchability disagreeing about method. Gandhi believed that the solution lay in transforming Hindu society and in keeping the Depressed Classes within a common electorate; Ambedkar believed that safeguards of political power were needed because social reform had proved too slow and too dependent on the goodwill of the dominant castes. The disagreement also reflected different views of representation: whether a leader or movement could legitimately speak for a community because of moral authority, or whether representation required a distinct electoral mandate. The difference between them would reach a crisis in 1932.

The conference ended on 1 December 1931 without agreement on minorities. MacDonald announced that the government would, if necessary, settle the question itself, a statement that Gandhi, who feared communal division in the constitution, criticized. Gandhi returned to India convinced that Congress had been outmaneuvered by the combination of British policy and communal divisions, and that the government was not offering a real transfer of power.

Section 81.16

The Return to Civil Disobedience, 1932–1934, and the Third Round Table Conference

Gandhi landed in Bombay on 28 December 1931 to find that the political situation had deteriorated. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact had been undermined by disputes over its implementation. In Bengal, new ordinances had been issued after terrorist attacks; in the United Provinces, the depression had intensified conflict between tenants and landlords, and the "no-rent" campaign was spreading; in the North-West Frontier Province, authorities had arrested Khudai Khidmatgar leaders. Lord Willingdon, who had replaced Irwin in April 1931, was a more hard-line viceroy, and the government, having concluded that Congress could be defeated by firm action, was prepared to resist further concessions. Gandhi asked for an interview with the viceroy, who refused it.

On 4 January 1932, Gandhi and Vallabhbhai Patel were arrested. Emergency ordinances made Congress organizations unlawful; offices and funds were seized, picketing suppressed, newspapers censored, and activists detained. In a report dated 2 May, Madan Mohan Malaviya was said to have estimated 80,000 cumulative arrests during the first four months of 1932. This was a contemporary political estimate, not a verified count of convictions or of prisoners held at one time. In a parliamentary return covering the period to 31 March, British officials reported 27,375 convictions under ordinary law and 12,142 under emergency ordinances—a combined 39,517 convictions—and 31,114 convicted people still imprisoned at that date. The two sets of figures measure different categories and time periods, and cannot be compared as if they were rival counts of the same population. The government had prepared a more systematic response than in 1930.

The movement of 1932–1934 differed from that of 1930. The new campaign took place under harsher conditions, with a less sympathetic government and fewer opportunities for mass demonstrations. It featured boycotts, picketing, and individual lawbreaking, but with weaker central direction and less access to the press. Participation remained substantial in some regions, particularly in Bombay, Gujarat, the United Provinces, and Bengal, but business groups, worn down by the depression and by the earlier campaign, were less willing to fund it, and many peasants were exhausted by the confiscations and by falling crop prices. Several of the economic issues that had energized 1930 were now more difficult to sustain. The movement also became more dispersed and more dependent on local initiative.

While the confrontation with the government continued, the constitutional process moved ahead without Congress. The Third Round Table Conference met in London between 17 November and 24 December 1932. It was a smaller gathering than its predecessors, with about forty-six delegates, and the Labour Party declined to participate. Congress did not attend, since its leaders were imprisoned or banned. The conference focused on practical questions of federal finance, the franchise, and the structure of the new federation. Its results were incorporated into a White Paper published in March 1933, which laid out the government's proposals, and were then examined by a Joint Select Committee of both Houses of Parliament, chaired by Lord Linlithgow, which met from 1933 to 1934. Indian delegates were invited to give evidence but many Congress politicians and nationalists considered the committee unrepresentative.

In May 1933 Gandhi began a twenty-one-day fast, principally connected to his campaign against untouchability, and was released from prison as it began. He suspended mass civil disobedience for six weeks; the suspension was later extended. In July Congress shifted toward individual civil disobedience. Gandhi withdrew the campaign in April 1934, judging that diminishing participation could no longer sustain effective mass pressure. Congress prepared to contest the November 1934 Central Legislative Assembly elections and later provincial contests. Gandhi also withdrew from active Congress membership that year to concentrate on constructive work against untouchability through the Harijan Sevak Sangh and his journal Harijan.

The campaign's end did not extinguish nationalist opposition. Congress remained the largest nationalist organization and retained mass sympathy. Shifting toward elections signaled a decision to use the emerging constitutional framework. The late 1920s and early 1930s had also strengthened Congress's left wing: its socialists established the Congress Socialist Party in 1934, a subject of Chapter 82. Meanwhile a major constitutional dispute over representation had sharpened in 1932, as the next division explains.

Historical Debate

Was the Civil Disobedience Movement a Political Success?

The question: Did Civil Disobedience advance the cause of Indian independence, or did it fail to achieve its immediate objectives and leave the constitutional problem unresolved?

Interpretation A — A significant political success: Supporters argue that the movement mobilized millions across regions and classes, delegitimized the claim that British rule rested on consent, brought women into public politics, drew international attention, forced the government to negotiate with Gandhi as an equal, and strengthened Congress's organization and prestige. On this view, the lack of immediate independence is a poor measure, since the campaign changed the terms on which Indians and Britons understood the Raj.
Interpretation B — A limited and costly outcome: Critics argue that the movement did not achieve its demands: the salt tax remained, the Gandhi–Irwin Pact conceded little, the repression of 1932 broke the organization, the campaign was withdrawn in 1934 without gains, and the poorest participants bore heavy costs. They add that the movement's dependence on Gandhi's leadership, on propertied supporters, and on Hindu-majority regions left many Muslims, Dalits, and workers outside its core, and that the constitutional outcome was determined by the British government.

Evidence: Historians draw on government records, Congress papers, press coverage, statistics of arrests, revenue and trade, memoirs, and regional studies. Some, such as Judith Brown, have emphasized the importance of the movement's alliances with local interests and its constraints; others have stressed its social breadth or its limits in particular regions.

Why it matters: The assessment affects how students understand the relationship between mass movements and negotiation, and how they interpret the road to 1947: as a story in which pressure steadily advanced independence, or one in which it set the conditions for later bargaining.

Section 81.17

The Communal Award of 1932: Minority Representation and the Constitutional Problem

The Communal Award, announced by Ramsay MacDonald on 16 August 1932, was the British government's decision on a question that Indian delegates had been unable to settle. It was called the Communal Decision in official papers and the Communal Award in popular usage, and it dealt with the allocation of seats in the provincial legislatures of British India and the manner in which members would be elected. It did not address the structure of the federation or the central legislature in detail, which were left to later discussion.

Its background was the long history of separate electorates, which had been introduced for Muslims by the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909, as Chapter 77 explained, and extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans by 1919. Muslim leaders regarded separate electorates as essential to protect their representation; Congress, in the Nehru Report, had rejected them; and the Lucknow Pact of 1916, discussed in Chapter 78, had accepted them as part of a Hindu–Muslim bargain. The delegates to the Round Table Conferences had attempted to reach an agreed settlement, but the Minorities Pact, the Muslim demands, the Sikh position, and the divisions among Hindus prevented agreement. The government decided to act in the absence of consensus, a decision that critics later interpreted as a use of communal divisions to retain control, while the government maintained that it was compelled by the absence of Indian agreement.

The Award retained separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans and allocated seats to them in the provincial legislatures. In Bengal and Punjab, where Muslims were a majority or near-majority of the population, the Award allocated them a number of seats that reflected a narrow lead rather than a large majority, and in provinces where they were a minority it gave weightage, that is, a share exceeding their proportion of the population. It gave Sikhs separate electorates in Punjab, though without the proportion they had demanded. It also provided special seats for women, labor, landholders, commerce and industry, and universities.

The most controversial provision concerned the Depressed Classes, the term then used officially for those later called Scheduled Castes. The Award granted them a number of seats in the provincial legislatures, to be filled by election from special constituencies in which only members of the Depressed Classes could vote, for an initial period of twenty years. They would also be entitled to vote in the general constituencies, which meant, in effect, a second vote. The total number of seats allotted to the Depressed Classes in the provincial councils was seventy-one. This provision represented a substantial victory for Ambedkar's demand for separate representation, but it was exactly what Gandhi had said he would resist.

Students should be careful to distinguish the mechanisms involved. A separate electorate is one in which only voters of a particular community vote for the candidates for the seats assigned to that community. A reserved seat in a joint electorate is one in which the seat is set aside for candidates of a particular community, but all voters in the constituency participate in the election. These arrangements produce different incentives: in a separate electorate, candidates must win the support of their own community; in a joint electorate with reservation, they may need to appeal to the broader electorate, including members of the dominant communities. Supporters of separate electorates argued that they ensured authentic representation; opponents argued that they entrenched division. Neither mechanism was a neutral technical device.

Reactions varied. Many Muslim leaders accepted the Award, although some were dissatisfied with aspects of its application to Bengal and Punjab. Sikh leaders were dissatisfied with the proportions allotted in Punjab. Many Hindu leaders, particularly in Bengal, objected that the Award disadvantaged Hindus in that province, where it set the Hindu share below their proportion of the population, and the Hindu Mahasabha opposed it. Congress, which had not accepted it and formally declined to reject or accept it, said it would not oppose the Muslim and Sikh provisions but disapproved of the arrangement as a whole. Ambedkar and the Depressed Class representatives welcomed the separate electorates. Gandhi, imprisoned at Yeravda, had told the British government in March and again in August 1932 that he would fast unto death if separate electorates were provided for the Depressed Classes. His intervention, and the response it provoked, belong to the next section.

Section 81.18

Gandhi, Ambedkar, and the Poona Pact, September 1932

On 20 September 1932, Gandhi began a fast unto death in Yeravda Jail, stating that he was protesting against the separate electorates for the Depressed Classes. His reasoning was religious and political. He regarded untouchability as a blot on Hinduism that Hindus themselves must remove, and he believed that separate electorates would divide the Depressed Classes from the rest of Hindu society permanently, preventing the social reconciliation he sought. He also regarded the Award as an instance of the British policy of dividing Indians. He did not oppose reserved seats for the Depressed Classes in a joint electorate, and he indicated that he was willing to give them a larger share of seats.

For Ambedkar, the fast posed a painful dilemma. He had worked for years to secure political recognition for the Depressed Classes, regarded separate electorates as the best available means of guaranteeing a voice independent of caste Hindu influence, and saw the Award as a rare concession. Gandhi's fast now confronted him with a choice: insist on the Award and risk responsibility for Gandhi's death, with the possibility of retaliation against Depressed Class communities, or concede and give up what he considered an important safeguard. Ambedkar later described the fast as coercive, and he said that he had acted under duress, while Gandhi's supporters regarded it as an act of moral witness. How one assesses the fast depends in part on how one assesses the legitimacy of a fast as a political method, a question that students should consider critically.

Negotiations began in Poona and involved a range of leaders: Ambedkar and other Depressed Class representatives such as M. C. Rajah and Rettamalai Srinivasan; Madan Mohan Malaviya, who played a significant role as an intermediary; Tej Bahadur Sapru and M. R. Jayakar, who came to mediate; and Congress leaders including C. Rajagopalachari, Rajendra Prasad, and the industrialist G. D. Birla. Gandhi was consulted in prison. The talks were conducted under extreme time pressure, with Gandhi's health deteriorating. On 24 September, an agreement, afterwards called the Poona Pact or Yeravda Pact, was concluded. It was signed by Ambedkar, Malaviya, and other representatives, and was approved by Gandhi.

The pact abandoned separate electorates for the Depressed Classes and substituted joint electorates with reserved seats. The number of reserved seats in the provincial legislatures was increased from seventy-one under the Communal Award to 148. A system of primary elections was devised: in each reserved constituency, the Depressed Class voters in an electoral college would elect a panel of four candidates by a single vote, and these four would then stand in the general election, in which all voters would participate. Reserved seats were also set aside for the Depressed Classes in the central legislature, in the proportion of 18 percent of the seats allotted to the general electorate. The agreement included provisions for the representation of the Depressed Classes in public services and for educational grants. The system of primary elections was to continue for ten years, unless terminated earlier by mutual agreement. The British government accepted the pact on 25 September, and Gandhi ended his fast on 26 September, in the presence of Rabindranath Tagore, who had come to Poona.

The effect of Gandhi's fast on public opinion was dramatic. In many parts of India, temples were opened to Depressed Classes, and public meetings declared that untouchability should be removed. The momentum led to the founding of the All-India Anti-Untouchability League, later renamed the Harijan Sevak Sangh, to promote the welfare of the Depressed Classes. Yet historians note that the immediate public gestures did not produce lasting changes in social practice in many places, and that Dalit activists remained skeptical of the movement's sincerity. The term "Harijan," meaning "children of God," which Gandhi used for the Depressed Classes, was criticized by many Dalit leaders, including Ambedkar, as patronizing, and the term "Scheduled Castes" would soon enter official usage through the Government of India Act of 1935.

The meaning of the agreement was contested at once. For supporters, it was a compromise that increased the number of reserved seats, avoided a permanent split, and gave the Depressed Classes substantial representation in a form acceptable to caste Hindus. For Ambedkar and his followers, it substituted a system in which candidates would be acceptable to caste Hindu voters for one in which the Depressed Classes would elect their own representatives, and so weakened the independence of Depressed Class politics. Ambedkar later criticized the Pact severely in writings published in the 1940s, and the evaluation of it remained a central issue in Dalit politics. Chapter 88 and Chapter 107 consider how the question of reservation and political representation continued into independent India. The next feature examines the debate directly.

Historical Vocabulary
Separate Electorates, Joint Electorates, and Reserved Seats

In a separate electorate, only members of a designated community vote for the representatives of that community, usually from constituencies drawn for that purpose. In a joint electorate, all eligible voters in a constituency vote together. A reserved seat is a seat set aside for candidates from a designated community; it can be contested in a joint electorate, where all voters participate, or, as in the Communal Award, in a separate one.

The terms are often confused, but the political consequences differ. Separate electorates were introduced for Muslims in 1909 and later extended; Congress and many nationalists criticized them as divisive, while many minority leaders defended them. The Poona Pact replaced separate electorates for the Depressed Classes with reserved seats and primary elections. A form of reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes would continue in independent India, discussed in Chapter 107, while separate electorates were abolished.

Historical Debate

Did the Poona Pact Strengthen or Weaken Dalit Political Representation?

The question: Did the replacement of separate electorates by reserved seats in joint electorates strengthen the political position of the Depressed Classes, or did it weaken their independent voice?

Interpretation A — The Pact strengthened representation: Supporters note that the Pact more than doubled the number of reserved seats, secured representation in the central legislature and in public services, avoided the risk of backlash and social conflict, and kept the Depressed Classes within a common electorate where candidates would need broader support. On this view, the larger number of seats and the integration into the general electorate gave a more durable base for later reservation policies.
Interpretation B — The Pact weakened independent political power: Ambedkar and many Dalit scholars argue that the Pact gave the Depressed Classes more seats but less genuine representation. Because the final election was decided by a general electorate dominated by caste Hindus, successful candidates were often those acceptable to the dominant parties, especially Congress, and not necessarily those preferred by Depressed Class voters. The panel system reduced the effect of the separate vote, and the Pact was signed under the pressure of a fast.

Evidence: The debate rests on the text of the Award and the Pact, the results of the 1937 elections and later contests, Ambedkar's writings, Congress records, and studies of Dalit politics in the provinces. Historians differ over how far the primary election system protected independent candidates, and over how to compare seat numbers with the quality of representation.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand the origins of reservation in independent India and the relationship between Congress and Dalit politics. It also bears on a general problem in democratic theory: whether representation of a minority is best secured through separate or joint institutions.

Section 81.19

The Government of India Act of 1935: Provincial Autonomy and the Limits of Constitutional Reform

The Government of India Act of 1935 emerged from the Simon Commission, the Round Table Conferences, the 1933 White Paper, and the Joint Select Committee. Introduced in Parliament in December 1934, it faced opposition both from Conservatives around Winston Churchill, who thought it conceded too much, and from Indian nationalists, who considered it inadequate. It received Royal Assent on 2 August 1935. Comprising 321 sections and ten schedules, the Act provided the constitutional framework in British India until independence; elements later entered the constitutions of India and Pakistan in modified form.

The Act had two principal parts: provincial autonomy and an all-India federation. In the provinces, it abolished dyarchy and made all provincial subjects, including law and order, the responsibility of ministers answerable to elected legislatures. Eleven provinces were recognized: Madras, Bombay, Bengal, the United Provinces, Punjab, Bihar, the Central Provinces and Berar, Assam, the North-West Frontier Province, Orissa, and Sind. Sind was separated from Bombay and Orissa from the former Bihar and Orissa Province, changes that reflected long-standing regional demands. Burma was separated from India altogether. The franchise was expanded considerably, to enfranchise about 30 million people, or about 14 percent of the population, with a larger proportion of women than before, though property, tax, and educational qualifications remained. Separate electorates were retained for Muslims, Sikhs, and other groups; seats were reserved for the Scheduled Castes in accordance with the Poona Pact, using the new term that was defined in a schedule.

Provincial autonomy was subject to significant limitations. Governors retained special responsibilities, including the protection of minorities, the prevention of threats to peace and tranquility, the safeguarding of the rights of the princes, and the protection of the legitimate interests of the services, and they could act on their own judgment in these matters. They could also dismiss ministries or suspend constitutional government in the event of a breakdown. Governors appointed ministers, and could, in certain circumstances, act without ministerial advice. Many provincial legislatures were bicameral. These powers meant that provincial government was responsible but not fully sovereign.

At the center, the Act proposed a federation of British Indian provinces and princely states, with a bicameral federal legislature, consisting of a Council of State of 260 members and a Federal Assembly of 375 members. The princely states were to receive a considerable share of seats, 104 in the Council of State and 125 in the Federal Assembly, and the rulers would nominate their representatives. The federal executive would operate under a form of dyarchy: defense, external affairs, and ecclesiastical affairs would be reserved to the Governor-General, who would administer them with counselors, while the remaining subjects would be transferred to ministers. The Governor-General would hold special responsibilities and wide powers to act on his own authority, and the Act made provision for financial safeguards, including a Reserve Bank, an independent railway authority, and a Federal Court. The Federal Court, created in 1937, was an important institution, the forerunner of the Supreme Court.

The federation, however, never took effect. It required that rulers of states accounting for at least half the population and half the seats allotted to the states accede before it could be brought into operation. The princes, who had accepted federation in principle in 1930, became hesitant as they saw the terms, including the prospect of an Indian-dominated central government and the erosion of their autonomy, and negotiations dragged on until the outbreak of the Second World War in 1939 led to the postponement of the scheme. The Indian National Congress rejected the federal provisions, since they would give the princes a heavy share of representation and would leave key powers with the Governor-General. The Muslim League also criticized the federal scheme for different reasons, with Jinnah regarding the scheme as unacceptable. Students must therefore distinguish carefully between the federation, which was legislated but never implemented, and provincial autonomy, which went into effect on 1 April 1937 and which shaped Indian politics in the last decade of British rule.

Congress rejected the Act and demanded a constituent assembly elected on adult franchise. In a statement of 27 March 1936, Jawaharlal Nehru characterized it as “a charter of slavery.” Jinnah condemned the federal scheme but saw opportunities in the provincial provisions; liberals regarded it as limited progress, while British ministers presented it as substantial devolution. The Act afforded Indian parties unprecedented provincial executive responsibility but left central defense, external affairs, and key financial safeguards under British control.

The Act's longer-term significance was considerable. Provincial autonomy gave the parties administrative experience, created a political arena in which elections were contested, and enlarged the electorate. The separation of Sind, the creation of Orissa, and the detachment of Burma altered the political map. The retention of separate electorates and of safeguards, and the princely states' role in the federal scheme, embedded communal and princely interests in the constitutional order. Finally, many features of the Act were carried into the later constitutions: the Federal Court, the distribution of powers in three lists, emergency provisions, and the use of governors. This legacy is examined in Chapter 88. Yet the Act was a statute passed by the British Parliament without the consent of Indian political parties, and it did not settle the question of sovereignty. The provincial elections of 1937, to which Chapter 82 turns, would show how Indian parties used the framework it created.

Section 81.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Mass Resistance and the Unresolved Constitutional Question

The years from 1927 to 1935 witnessed an intensification of both resistance and negotiation, and the central argument of this chapter is that the two were bound together. The Simon Commission controversy began as a dispute over who had the right to examine India's constitutional future. It provoked a boycott that revealed the breadth of opposition to British control and, through Lord Birkenhead's challenge, led Indian politicians to produce the Nehru Report. The report's failure to win support from Muslim, Sikh, and other leaders, and the fourteen points that followed, showed that agreement on the principle of self-government did not entail agreement on how power should be divided. The Lahore Congress, by adopting Purna Swaraj, redefined the nationalist objective and made mass action the logical next step.

The Salt March and the Civil Disobedience Movement that followed converted that objective into practice. The movement drew millions into action by taking a simple, widely shared grievance and turning it into a public act of defiance, and it allowed participants with different aims to take part: coastal salt makers, merchants, students, women's organizations, peasant landholders, and Pashtun reformers each brought distinct social concerns. The government's response combined repression with an offer of negotiation, and the movement's moral force, together with the international attention that Dharasana attracted, made it difficult for the government to rely on coercion alone. But the movement also had limits: it did not bring independence, it depended heavily on Gandhi's leadership and on regions with strong Congress organization, it imposed heavy costs on participants, and it faded under the pressure of the depression, repression, and exhaustion.

The negotiations that accompanied it were no more conclusive. The First Round Table Conference, without Congress, established federation as the framework of British policy and drew in the princes. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact gave Congress recognition and relief but conceded neither independence nor the abolition of the salt tax. The Second Conference exposed the deadlock over minority representation and brought Gandhi and Ambedkar into open disagreement. The failure to reach an Indian settlement led to the Communal Award, which in turn provoked the Poona Pact. These events were not a distraction from the story of Indian self-government but integral to it, because they raised the question of who would exercise power in a self-governing India and how minorities, including communities subjected to caste oppression, would be protected within it.

The Government of India Act of 1935 was the institutional result. It ended dyarchy, introduced provincial autonomy, enlarged the electorate, and created new provinces, and it carried forward institutional features, such as the Federal Court and the distribution of legislative powers, that would be inherited by independent states. At the same time, it retained separate electorates and extensive safeguards, left defense and external affairs in British hands, and proposed a federation that depended on the consent of the princes and never took effect. It marked a significant shift in the machinery of government, yet it left the fundamental question of sovereignty unanswered. In this sense the Act expresses both the possibilities and limits of colonial constitutional reform.

Chapter 82 takes up the story from here. The provincial elections of 1937 and the formation of Congress ministries would test the Act in practice, the Muslim League would reorganize under Jinnah's leadership, and socialist, communist, Hindu nationalist, and regional movements would develop their own programs. The unresolved disputes over federation, minority representation, and sovereignty, which this chapter has traced, would enter a new political setting in which they would become more urgent and, ultimately, more dangerous.

Cause and Effect

From Constitutional Dissatisfaction to Mass Resistance and Negotiation

Long-term causes

The limits of dyarchy and the narrow franchise under the 1919 Act; the legacy of Non-Cooperation; growing economic strain; Congress's wish to be recognized as a negotiating power; and unresolved communal and regional questions about representation.

Short-term causes

The exclusion of Indians from the Simon Commission; the failure of the Nehru Report to win agreement; the unsatisfactory response of the viceroy in 1929; the world depression; and the expiry of the one-year deadline set at Calcutta.

Event or transformation

Adoption of Purna Swaraj at Lahore and the launch of Civil Disobedience with the Salt March, followed by negotiation in London and in India.

Immediate effects

Mass participation, mass arrests, the Gandhi–Irwin Pact, three conferences, the Communal Award, the Poona Pact, and the revival of civil disobedience in 1932.

Long-term consequences

A constitutional framework in the 1935 Act, a Congress prepared to contest elections, deepened communal and caste disputes over representation, and the setting for the politics of 1937 and the road to Partition.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. British policy, Indian divisions, the depression, Gandhi's strategy, regional conditions, and the princes' calculations all shaped events, and the results were not inevitable.
Multiple Perspectives

Civil Disobedience and Constitutional Reform: Different Experiences and Aims

Congress leaders: They saw the movement as a means of asserting that India's future would be determined by Indians, and they combined pressure with negotiation, while differing among themselves over whether the goal was dominion status, independence, or a social transformation.
British authorities: They treated the movement as a threat to law and order and tried to isolate Congress through repression, while seeking cooperating partners among princes, minorities, and moderates and offering a constitutional framework that preserved key powers.
Women activists: Many saw the movement as an opportunity to enter public life, accepting imprisonment and organizing picketing, while confronting continuing limits on their social and political position.
Muslim political leaders: They were divided, with some allied with Congress, others cooperating with the government, and many concerned with provincial autonomy and guaranteed representation, as the Fourteen Points indicate.
Sikh representatives: They sought protection as a minority in Punjab and objected to proposals that gave them neither a distinct share nor assurance against domination by larger communities.
Dalit leaders: Ambedkar argued that political safeguards independent of caste Hindu control were needed, while others accepted Gandhi's approach or sought different forms of representation.
Princely-state representatives: They joined the federal discussions to protect their treaty relationship with the Crown and later hesitated as the terms of federation became clear.
Regional activists: In Midnapore, Malabar, Tamil country, Odisha, and the Frontier, local leaders adapted the campaign to local grievances and organizations, sometimes diverging from the priorities of the national leadership.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience; the Reforms of 1919 and 1935

What Changed

  • The nationalist goal moved from self-government within the Empire to complete independence.
  • The method moved from withdrawal from institutions (Non-Cooperation) to deliberate lawbreaking (Civil Disobedience), with stronger participation by women and by regional leaders.
  • Provincial dyarchy was replaced by responsible ministries, and the electorate expanded substantially.
  • The princely states were drawn into the constitutional design through the federal scheme.

What Stayed the Same

  • Gandhi's commitment to nonviolence and his central place in Congress strategy.
  • British retention of control over defense, external affairs, and finance, and of reserve powers.
  • Separate electorates for several communities and an electorate limited by property and education.
  • Disagreement among Indian parties over representation and the structure of the state.

Who experienced change most directly? Activists, women who entered public politics, peasants and traders affected by boycott and repression, and voters newly enfranchised. Who experienced greater continuity? The landless poor, many tenants, and those outside the expanded franchise, as well as the princes whose authority was preserved by treaty. Why the pattern matters: It shows how a major transformation in political method and institutions could coexist with the persistence of colonial control and of unresolved questions, which shaped what followed.

Chapter Summary

Between 1927 and 1935 the struggle for Indian self-government took two connected forms: mass civil disobedience and constitutional negotiation. This chapter has argued that each shaped the other and that neither resolved the question that lay beneath both, namely who would hold power in a self-governing India and how that power would be distributed among communities, provinces, and the center.

The chapter began with the Simon Commission, appointed in November 1927 to review the constitution created by the Government of India Act of 1919. Its all-British membership turned a routine review into a controversy about political legitimacy. Congress, the Hindu Mahasabha, liberals, and a section of the Muslim League boycotted it, while other groups, including a rival League faction, the Justice Party, the Punjab Unionists, and some Depressed Class organizations, cooperated. The boycott demonstrations of 1928 culminated in the Lahore lathi charge of 30 October and the death of Lala Lajpat Rai on 17 November, an episode whose medical causation is disputed but whose political effect was large.

Lord Birkenhead's challenge that Indians could not agree on a constitution prompted the Nehru Report of 1928, which proposed dominion status, responsible government, a strong center, joint electorates with limited reservation, and fundamental rights. It failed to win general agreement. Jinnah's amendments were rejected at Calcutta, and in March 1929 he formulated the Fourteen Points, calling for a federation with provincial residual powers, guaranteed Muslim shares, and continued separate electorates. Sikh, Hindu, and Dalit leaders raised their own demands, so that the debate showed the plurality of Indian political interests and the difficulty of reconciling majority rule with minority protection.

Within Congress, younger leaders pressed for complete independence. The Calcutta compromise of December 1928 set a one-year deadline for dominion status. Irwin's declaration of October 1929 offered no timetable, and the failure of the December meeting with Gandhi and other leaders led to the Lahore Congress, which on 31 December 1929 adopted Purna Swaraj and authorized civil disobedience. The independence pledge of 26 January 1930 was a declaration of an objective, not the attainment of sovereignty.

Gandhi's choice of salt as the issue reflected his strategy of using a concrete, widely felt grievance and a simple, nonviolent act of lawbreaking. After his letter to Irwin of 2 March 1930, he led seventy-eight companions from Sabarmati on 12 March and broke the salt law at Dandi on 6 April. The march launched a campaign, not the whole movement. Civil disobedience spread to Bombay, the Tamil country, Malabar, Andhra, Bengal, Odisha, and the Frontier, took many forms, and drew on many social groups: merchants supporting the cloth boycott, landowning peasants refusing revenue, women picketing liquor and cloth shops and making salt, and Pashtun volunteers in the Khudai Khidmatgar. The Qissa Khwani Bazaar massacre of 23 April and the Dharasana raid of 21 May illustrated both the movement's moral force and the violence of repression.

The government responded with arrests, ordinances, press controls, and seizure of property, imprisoning large numbers of people, while also promising a Round Table Conference. The first conference, in London from November 1930 to January 1931, proceeded without Congress and established federation, provincial autonomy, and a conditional form of responsibility at the center as the framework of British policy, with the princes accepting federation in principle. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact of 5 March 1931 suspended civil disobedience and secured the release of prisoners and limited salt-making rights, but it did not abolish the salt tax or concede independence. The execution of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Sukhdev and the Karachi Congress showed the gap between negotiation and radical nationalism, and its resolution on rights and economic policy defined a social program.

The Second Round Table Conference of 1931, which Gandhi attended as Congress's sole representative, exposed the deadlock over minority representation. Gandhi claimed that Congress spoke for all Indians, but Muslim, Sikh, and Depressed Class delegates rejected that claim, and Ambedkar's demand for separate political safeguards brought him into direct conflict with Gandhi. Civil disobedience resumed in January 1932 under harsher repression, the Third Round Table Conference met without Congress, and the campaign was withdrawn by 1934, when Congress turned toward electoral politics.

The British government's Communal Award of August 1932 retained separate electorates for several communities, including the Depressed Classes, who were allotted seventy-one seats. Gandhi's fast beginning on 20 September led to the Poona Pact of 24 September, which replaced separate electorates with reserved seats in joint electorates, increased the number to 148, and introduced a system of primary elections. Historians disagree about whether this strengthened or weakened Dalit political representation, and the debate remains central to understanding the history of reservation and Dalit politics.

The Government of India Act of 1935, the legislative result of the conferences, ended dyarchy, introduced provincial autonomy effective in 1937, enlarged the electorate to roughly 14 percent of the population, created Sind and Orissa as provinces, separated Burma, and proposed an all-India federation of provinces and princely states. The federation never came into operation, and governors and the Governor-General retained extensive powers. The Act marked a major change in the structure of government but did not resolve the question of sovereignty.

The chapter's main lesson is that agreement on the goal of ending colonial rule did not produce agreement on the constitutional shape of an independent India. The disputes over federation, minority representation, caste, and the status of princely states, which Civil Disobedience and the Round Table Conferences made visible, entered a new phase with the provincial elections of 1937, the subject of Chapter 82.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a mass movement and a constitutional process unfolded together and how each exposed the limits of the other. Civil disobedience demonstrated that colonial authority depended on cooperation and that moral and symbolic action could challenge it. The Round Table Conferences demonstrated that the colonial government could not negotiate a settlement without Congress and that Congress could not impose a settlement on other political actors.

The chapter also reveals the processes that shaped the end of British rule. The questions of federation, minority safeguards, caste representation, and the position of the princes, which were first systematically debated in these years, did not disappear in 1935. They returned in the elections of 1937, the Cabinet Mission of 1946, the Partition of 1947, the integration of the princely states, and the work of the Constituent Assembly. Features of the 1935 Act, from the Federal Court to the division of powers and emergency provisions, entered the constitutions of independent states.

In the context of the six-volume series, the period belongs to the long history of how South Asians sought to define political community. It links the earlier history of reform and nationalism in Chapters 74 to 80 with the final negotiations of Chapters 83 to 87. It also reminds readers that the history of this period cannot be reduced to a single narrative of unity or division: many groups sought freedom and many disagreed about what it should mean.

Chapter 82 now turns to the elections of 1937, the Congress ministries, and the political realignments that followed the new constitutional order.
