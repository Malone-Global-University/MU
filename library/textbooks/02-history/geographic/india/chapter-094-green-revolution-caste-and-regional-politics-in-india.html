Part XVII — South Asia in an Age of Upheaval, 1971–1991

Green Revolution, Caste, and Regional Politics in India

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the grain markets of Punjab's agricultural districts in the 1970s, wheat arrived in quantities that would have seemed unimaginable to policymakers a decade earlier, when India had depended on shiploads of imported grain to avert famine. The abundance was real, and it was concentrated: in particular districts, among particular farmers, in a particular band of the northwest. Meanwhile, elsewhere in the same decade, political conflicts over caste, land, language, regional identity, and who counted as a citizen were intensifying. Both stories belong to the same history.

This chapter asks how India's agricultural transformation and its expanding democratic politics shaped one another between the 1960s and 1991, with the greatest concentration on the years from 1971 to 1985. Chapter 93 examined the concentration of power under Indira Gandhi, the Emergency of 1975–77, the election of 1977, and the democratic recovery that followed. Those events were visible at the summit of the political system. This chapter turns to processes operating beneath and alongside them: changes in rural production, in the balance of power within villages, in the political organization of caste communities, in the growth of parties rooted in particular regions, and in violent conflicts in Assam and Punjab that tested the limits of the federal Republic.

The chapter's central argument is that economic modernization and expanding political participation together strengthened democratic representation, altered established hierarchies, and intensified conflicts over resources, identity, regional autonomy, and state authority. Neither the Green Revolution nor the rise of backward-caste, Dalit, and regional politics produced a uniform result. The new agricultural technology raised grain output dramatically, secured national food supplies, and enriched many farmers, yet its benefits were unevenly distributed across regions and social classes and brought environmental costs. The same decades saw communities long excluded from power organize politically and win representation, and saw the Congress party, which had dominated the national and most state governments since 1947, lose the near-monopoly it once enjoyed.

The chapter also takes seriously the darker side of this transformation. Competition among groups for land, jobs, political voice, and recognition did not always take peaceful forms. In Assam, a mass movement against the presence of undocumented migrants culminated in the massacre of Nellie in 1983. In Punjab, a dispute over federal powers, resources, and religious identity escalated into armed militancy, the army's operation at the Golden Temple in June 1984, the assassination of the Prime Minister in October of that year, and the organized killing of Sikhs, especially in Delhi, in the days that followed. These events were not departures from India's democratic history so much as moments when democratic competition, security policy, and communal identity collided with tragic results.

Historians draw on a wide range of evidence for these subjects: agricultural and population censuses, production statistics, Election Commission returns, commission reports such as that of the Mandal Commission, party manifestos and activist writings, legal texts such as the Assam Accord, government white papers, parliamentary debates, press reporting, civil-liberties investigations, judicial commissions, and oral testimony. These sources vary in reliability and perspective. Official statistics may undercount conditions that governments find embarrassing; commission reports completed decades after an event reflect later evidence and later politics; activist manifestos express the aims of movements rather than neutral description. This chapter therefore attributes figures to their sources and distinguishes contemporary documents from later investigations.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I examines the Green Revolution and the transformation of rural India. Division II traces changes in rural political power, farmers' movements, the Other Backward Classes, and the Mandal Commission. Division III examines Dalit political assertion, the growth of regional parties, and the changing federal system. Division IV turns to the Assam Movement and Punjab's political crisis. Division V treats Operation Blue Star, the assassination of Indira Gandhi, the anti-Sikh violence of 1984, and the synthesis of the chapter's themes. Chapter 95 turns to Pakistan, and Chapter 96 to Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and the smaller states; this chapter remains focused on India and refers to neighboring countries only where needed.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why a technological and economic success in agriculture, an expanding democracy, and an increasingly competitive and fragmented party system were connected, and why the same decades produced both greater political participation and profound conflicts over identity, resources, and the authority of the state.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why food insecurity and import dependence in the 1950s and 1960s led Indian policymakers to adopt the strategy now called the Green Revolution.
  2. Describe the components of the Green Revolution package and distinguish scientific innovation from the public infrastructure needed for its adoption.
  3. Compare agricultural regions that adopted high-yielding varieties quickly with rainfed and less-developed regions.
  4. Assess how the Green Revolution affected large landowners, smallholders, tenants, laborers, women, and the environment, avoiding uniform claims of benefit or harm.
  5. Analyze how land reform, commercialization, and electoral competition changed the balance of rural political power, and why prosperous farmers organized as an interest group.
  6. Distinguish among Other Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and individual caste identities, and trace the development of the OBC category.
  7. Explain the appointment, findings, and political consequences of the Mandal Commission through the decision of August 1990.
  8. Trace Dalit political mobilization from Ambedkar's legacy through the Dalit Panthers and Kanshi Ram's organizations to the founding of the Bahujan Samaj Party.
  9. Evaluate the rise of regional parties and the changing federal relationship, including Article 356, without equating regionalism with separatism.
  10. Compare the causes, leadership, and state responses in the Assam Movement and Punjab's crisis, distinguishing demands, organizations, and acts of violence.
  11. Reconstruct, with attributed evidence, Operation Blue Star, the assassination of Indira Gandhi, and the anti-Sikh violence of 1984, and evaluate questions of responsibility.
  12. Synthesize how agricultural change, caste representation, regional movements, and federal conflict transformed the Congress-dominated system into a more competitive order by 1991.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a country committed to industrial planning in the 1950s redirect so much policy attention to grain production in the 1960s?
  2. Why did the Green Revolution succeed so unevenly across India's regions, and what does that unevenness reveal about the relationship between technology and public investment?
  3. Did agricultural modernization reduce rural inequality, reinforce existing advantages, or do both at once?
  4. How did the political significance of landholding groups differ from their position in ritual caste hierarchies?
  5. Why did reservation policies for Other Backward Classes become one of the most contested issues in Indian politics?
  6. In what ways did Dalit political movements differ from one another in strategy, leadership, and regional setting?
  7. How did federal parliamentary institutions create space for regional parties, and why were most regional parties not separatist?
  8. How did long-run migration, language politics, and citizenship questions shape the Assam Movement, and why must its demands be distinguished from the violence committed during it?
  9. How did disputes over autonomy, resources, and religious identity in Punjab escalate into militancy and military intervention?
  10. What does the transformation of Indian politics between 1971 and 1991 show about the relationship between democratic participation and state coercion?

Historical Context

Independent India inherited an agrarian economy in which most people lived in villages and farmed small holdings with low yields. Partition in 1947 had divided the irrigated lands of the Punjab and the jute-growing and rice-growing regions of Bengal, and the new Republic's population grew rapidly, from roughly 361 million in the census of 1951 to roughly 548 million in 1971. The early Five-Year Plans, discussed in Chapter 89, emphasized heavy industry, irrigation projects, and institutional land reform, while the zamindari system was abolished in most states. Yet agricultural productivity grew slowly, and by the early 1960s India relied on grain imports, largely under American food-aid arrangements.

The political context was equally important. The Congress party had dominated the first three general elections and most state governments, organizing support through a network of local notables, landholding castes, and patronage connections described by scholars as the Congress system. The party's dominance began to weaken after Nehru's death in 1964. In the elections of 1967, Congress lost control of several state governments and saw its Lok Sabha majority sharply reduced. The party split in 1969, and Indira Gandhi then built a new base through direct appeals to the poor, the electoral successes of 1971, and the centralizing political style that culminated in the Emergency, as Chapter 93 explained. Constitutional democracy survived that crisis, and the electorate that voted out Congress in 1977 and returned it in 1980 was increasingly politically conscious and organized along lines of region, caste, and economic interest.

Chapter 88 described the constitutional framework: universal adult franchise, a federal parliamentary structure with strong central powers, reserved seats and reserved positions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and a commitment to social equality. Chapter 89 described the efforts at planning, land reform, and linguistic reorganization of states. The present chapter examines how these institutions interacted with agricultural change and political mobilization after the 1960s. Students should keep in mind that the groups discussed here, such as Jat farmers, Yadavs, Dalits, Assamese-speaking communities, and Sikhs, are not unified blocs. Each was divided by region, class, language, and political outlook.

A note on terminology. Contemporary Indian usage distinguishes the Scheduled Castes (the constitutional category for communities historically subjected to untouchability, widely called Dalits), the Scheduled Tribes (Adivasi communities listed in the schedule), and the Other Backward Classes (a broader administrative category of socially and educationally disadvantaged groups). These categories overlap with, but are not identical to, caste identities, economic class, and religious community. They are explained further in Section 94.7.

Division I

The Green Revolution and the Transformation of Rural India

Section 94.1

Food Insecurity and the Origins of India's Green Revolution

The Green Revolution began as a response to fear. In the 1950s and early 1960s India's population was growing by roughly two percent a year, while grain production grew more slowly and unevenly. Policymakers in the first three Five-Year Plans had given priority to heavy industry, irrigation works, and institutional reform of landholding, expecting that agricultural output would respond to land reform, cooperatives, and community development. Output did rise, particularly through the expansion of cultivated area, but yields per hectare remained low and monsoon failure could still produce acute scarcity. From 1956 India imported large quantities of wheat under American food-aid arrangements, and by the mid-1960s imports had become a structural feature of national food policy.

The crisis came in 1965–67. Two successive monsoon failures sharply reduced the harvest, and foodgrain production in 1965–66 fell well below the level of the preceding years. Imports rose to roughly ten million tonnes in 1966, and parts of Bihar experienced severe scarcity in 1966–67. The dependence was politically painful. Food aid came amid Cold War diplomacy, and Indian leaders were acutely aware that the supply of grain could become a tool of foreign pressure, a concern that accounts of the period commonly attribute to the shipments being arranged on a short-term basis during the war in Vietnam. Whatever the precise diplomacy, the experience convinced much of the political leadership that food self-sufficiency was a matter of national security as well as welfare.

The new strategy did not appear from nowhere. In 1959 a Ford Foundation team had urged India to concentrate scarce inputs in districts with assured water and good soils rather than spreading them thinly. The Intensive Agricultural District Programme (1960) and the Intensive Agricultural Areas Programme (1964) put this advice into practice. Indian institutions were also expanding: the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) in Delhi, new agricultural universities modeled on American land-grant colleges, including Pantnagar (1960) and Punjab Agricultural University at Ludhiana (1962), and the National Seeds Corporation (1963). These institutions trained scientists and provided the extension networks through which new techniques would reach farmers.

International research supplied the seeds. At the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center's predecessor program in Mexico, supported by the Rockefeller Foundation, Norman Borlaug and his colleagues bred short-strawed wheat varieties that responded to fertilizer without falling over. Indian wheat scientists, among them M. S. Swaminathan at IARI, were in contact with the program from the early 1960s, and Borlaug visited India in 1963. After field trials, India imported Mexican seed in large quantity in 1966, reported at around 18,000 tonnes, and Indian breeders went on to develop locally adapted varieties such as Kalyan Sona and Sonalika. For rice, the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines released the semi-dwarf IR8 in 1966, and Indian research stations soon produced their own improved varieties.

The decisive political decisions belonged to the Indian government. C. Subramaniam, as Minister for Food and Agriculture from 1964, together with officials such as B. Sivaraman, argued that farmers would adopt new technology only if they were assured of inputs and remunerative prices. The Agricultural Prices Commission and the Food Corporation of India were established in 1965 to recommend support prices and to purchase and store grain. The strategy thus combined foreign-bred genetic material, Indian scientific and administrative capacity, and a deliberate commitment to price incentives. It also carried a clear choice: to concentrate on regions where returns would be highest, accepting that other areas would be left behind in the short run. That choice, made under the pressure of famine, shaped everything that followed.

A further distinction concerns the language of famine and self-sufficiency. India's planners were not reacting only to the total amount of food in the country; they were confronting failures of purchasing power, regional transport, stock management, and the credibility of government response. Seed policy therefore became a political project with different beneficiaries: cultivators expected better yields, consumers hoped for stable supplies, and national officials wanted less diplomatic dependence on food shipments. Scientific success could not substitute for access by families with little income. When using import statistics, historians should distinguish shipments authorized, shipments received, and the proportion of consumption met from abroad; these measures tell different stories about vulnerability. Policies developed under emergency conditions also tended to prioritize rapid increases in output rather than a balanced strategy for the driest districts.

Section 94.2

High-Yielding Seeds, Irrigation, Fertilizers, and the New Agricultural Economy

The seeds were only one part of what scholars call the Green Revolution package. High-yielding varieties (HYVs) produced large increases in output only when grown with reliable water, chemical fertilizer, and, increasingly, pesticides and timely cultivation. Traditional tall varieties, if given heavy doses of fertilizer, grew top-heavy and collapsed; the semi-dwarf varieties converted nutrients into grain. But the seed needed controlled irrigation, because the plants were sensitive to water stress, and in much of India the monsoon alone could not guarantee it. The technology was therefore scale-neutral in principle, since a small farm could plant the same seed as a large one, yet dependent in practice on resources not equally available to all.

Public infrastructure explains much of the pattern. Punjab had benefited from extensive canal irrigation inherited from the colonial period and expanded after independence, including the Bhakra-Nangal system, while the spread of electric and diesel tubewells in the 1960s and 1970s gave farmers individual control over groundwater. Rural electrification, all-weather roads, regulated markets, and assured government purchase at announced prices made investment in tubewells, seed, and fertilizer profitable. Cooperative and commercial credit expanded, and the nationalization of the major banks in 1969 (discussed in Chapter 93) was followed by efforts to direct lending toward agriculture. Fertilizer consumption rose sharply from very low levels in the 1960s, supported by government subsidies and domestic production.

Procurement and price support completed the system. The government announced minimum support prices for wheat and rice and purchased grain in the main producing states through the Food Corporation of India and state agencies, building buffer stocks. Farmers in Punjab and Haryana thus faced an exceptionally secure market, which encouraged them to shift toward wheat and, later, paddy. This assured market was not a natural feature of the technology; it was a political and administrative choice. Mechanization followed in the more prosperous areas, with tractors, threshers, and later combine harvesters changing the labor requirements of cultivation.

The geography of adoption was sharply focused. Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh moved first and furthest in wheat; Punjab, in particular, became the country's emblematic Green Revolution state and a principal supplier of grain to the central pool. Parts of coastal Andhra Pradesh, the Cauvery delta and other irrigated areas of Tamil Nadu adopted high-yielding rice varieties, and later decades saw wider spread. Large areas of rainfed agriculture, in central and western India, in much of the eastern plains, and in dryland regions of the peninsula, saw much slower change. The Green Revolution was thus never a nationwide event.

The new input economy produced powerful intermediaries. Seed dealers, fertilizer suppliers, private moneylenders, cooperative societies, and procurement agents influenced a farmer's ability to use the package and to receive payment. A household might be technically capable of growing a high-yielding variety yet still avoid it because a failed monsoon would leave an unpayable debt. Irrigation reduced risks for some but depended on access to a canal outlet or the capital to sink a well. Women contributed indispensable agricultural labor while often lacking ownership of land, formal access to loans, or control over proceeds. Consequently the unit of analysis should not be an undifferentiated 'farmer': the size and title of holdings, crop choice, household labor, and local bargaining relationships determined who could turn a public technology into private gain.

Historical Vocabulary
The Green Revolution "Package"

The phrase Green Revolution was popularized in the late 1960s to describe the rapid rise in cereal yields associated with high-yielding varieties. Historians use the word "package" because the seeds required complementary inputs: irrigation, fertilizer, pest control, credit, and assured markets. The term also reminds students that the change depended on public institutions as much as on laboratory science.

The word "revolution" can mislead. The transformation was substantial but regionally concentrated, extended over decades, and left many farmers and crops outside its reach. This chapter uses the term as a conventional label rather than as a claim of uniform change.

Context: After the droughts of 1965–67, the Indian government issued policy statements, price recommendations, and plan documents describing a new agricultural strategy built on improved seeds, inputs, and price support. Paraphrase of the documentary content: These documents argue that national food security requires raising yields in areas with assured water, that farmers must be offered prices high enough to justify new inputs, that seed and fertilizer supplies must be organized by public agencies, and that stocks must be held to stabilize supply. (This is a paraphrase of the policy argument, not a quotation; students should consult the cited institutional archives for original wording.)

Section 94.3

Agricultural Productivity, Food Security, and Regional Disparities

The aggregate results were striking. Indian wheat production, roughly 11 million tonnes around 1960–61, rose to about 24 million tonnes by 1970–71, about 36 million by 1980–81, and about 55 million by 1990–91 (figures here are rounded from official series and should be treated as approximate). Total foodgrain production, about 82 million tonnes around 1960–61, reached roughly 108 million in 1970–71 and about 176 million in 1990–91. Rice output grew more slowly in the early years but expanded steadily from the 1970s. Wheat yields per hectare in Punjab and Haryana rose to levels far above the national average. The quantitative study below summarizes these changes.

Food security improved in a specific sense: the country largely ceased to depend on large-scale imports of food aid after the early 1970s, and the government accumulated substantial public stocks. This achievement had a political meaning, because it removed a source of external leverage and reinforced the legitimacy of state planning. At the same time, national self-sufficiency in grain was not the same as nutritional security for every household. People who lacked purchasing power could remain hungry even when granaries were full, a distinction that later scholars, including the economist Amartya Sen in his work on famines, emphasized in general terms.

The Public Distribution System (PDS) linked procurement to consumption. Grain purchased by the Food Corporation of India was distributed through fair-price shops to consumers at subsidized prices, with the state governments managing distribution. The PDS expanded from an emergency measure to a permanent institution, although its coverage and quality varied widely: it reached urban populations and some states, notably Kerala and, from the 1980s, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, far more effectively than others. Procurement itself was concentrated in a few states, which meant that the farmers of Punjab and Haryana supplied a very large part of the central pool while the consumers of other regions relied on it.

Regional disparities widened. Where assured irrigation, roads, markets, and credit existed, farmers adopted the new varieties and intensified production. In rainfed regions, in much of eastern India (including Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, and Odisha), and in many tribal and hill areas, the package was harder to adopt: water control was weaker, flood and drought risks higher, tenancy and landholding patterns less favorable to investment, and public services thinner. Pulses and coarse cereals, important to the diets of poorer households and to dryland farming, received less research attention and saw slower yield growth. The Green Revolution thus altered India's agricultural geography, widening gaps between advanced and lagging regions even as it raised the national average.

National grain totals are important but incomplete measures. Output rises when more hectares are cultivated, when each hectare yields more, or when farmers grow additional crops during a year. Historians should therefore compare area, yield, irrigation coverage, and cropping intensity rather than attribute every added tonne to seed varieties. They should also consider state procurement and marketable surplus: a district producing more grain did not automatically supply the central pool if local consumption and infrastructure differed. Similarly, a fall in food imports may reflect stronger domestic production, changes in prices or exchange constraints, or altered policy choices. Comparing multiple indicators helps distinguish the Green Revolution's genuine productivity gains from broader changes in India's food economy. Section 94.4

Rural Inequality, Agricultural Labor, and Environmental Consequences

Who benefited from the Green Revolution? The answer depends on which question is asked. Large and medium landowners with access to water and capital were generally best placed to adopt the new inputs and earn the largest absolute gains. Yet many smallholders also adopted high-yielding varieties, because the seed itself was divisible, and some gained substantially, particularly where irrigation, credit, and cooperative or market services were accessible. Studies conducted at the time and later disagree about the net effect on inequality, and distinctions are essential: a rise in absolute incomes for most cultivators is compatible with a widening of the gap between richer and poorer households. This chapter's Historical Debate returns to this question.

Tenants faced mixed prospects. In some areas, landowners who saw rising profits from cultivation evicted tenants or resumed direct cultivation, while others continued leasing under new terms. Land reform legislation, discussed in Chapter 89, had in many states weakened the position of intermediaries but often left tenants' rights poorly enforced, and tenancy was frequently informal and unrecorded, which makes its measurement difficult. Agricultural laborers, the landless households who worked for wages, depended on employment, wage rates, and the cost of food. In regions of intensive cultivation, additional cropping, harvesting, and threshing created more demand for labor, and in Punjab and Haryana the demand drew migrant laborers from eastern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. At the same time, mechanization of tasks such as threshing and harvesting displaced some labor, and wage gains varied by region and season. Women, who performed much agricultural work, from transplanting and weeding to processing grain, experienced changes in workload and, in some cases, in the loss of tasks to machines, while receiving lower wages than men; evidence on their experience is thinner than for male cultivators.

Agricultural modernization also changed the economics of farming. Farmers who invested in tubewells, tractors, seed, and fertilizer needed cash and credit, and rising input costs made cultivation more dependent on markets and on institutional lenders, though informal moneylenders remained significant. Debts could be manageable in good years and crushing when harvests failed, prices fell, or input costs rose. Indebtedness became an important political grievance, as Section 94.6 explains.

The environmental consequences became clearer over time. Intensive irrigation with tubewells lowered water tables in parts of Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh, and the shift to paddy in regions of limited rainfall intensified groundwater withdrawal. Canal irrigation without adequate drainage produced waterlogging and salinity in some tracts. Heavy fertilizer use affected soil structure and nutrient balance, and growing dependence on pesticides raised concerns about health and ecology. The replacement of diverse traditional varieties and mixed cropping by a narrow set of high-yielding cultivars reduced genetic diversity, and the cost of electricity and diesel subsidies fell on public budgets. These problems did not erase the gains in food production, but they showed that the benefits were not cost-free, and many of the concerns became prominent in later decades.

The Green Revolution therefore cannot be summarized as success or failure. It averted dependence on food aid, raised incomes for large numbers of cultivators, and transformed the northwest, while leaving inequality, ecological stress, and regional disparity as long-term problems. Its most important political effect, explored in Division II, was to create a prosperous farming class with a distinct set of economic interests and new capacities for organization.

Environmental change was gradual rather than uniform. Tubewell expansion initially reduced farmers' dependence on erratic rainfall, but pumping rates later exceeded recharge in parts of the northwest. Fertilizer and pesticide use could raise output while creating localized risks for soil, water, and farm workers. The balance depended on what crops were grown, whether irrigation water was managed effectively, and what extension advice was available. Many effects became much more visible after the first phase of rapid growth and should not be presented as if fully known to decision-makers in 1966. Equity also differed within households: an increase in farm income did not prove that women or hired laborers controlled more resources. The social history of production requires wage records, household studies, and testimony alongside national crop statistics.

Historical Debate 1

Did the Green Revolution Reduce Rural Inequality or Reinforce Existing Advantages?

The question: Did high-yielding varieties and associated policies narrow the gap between rich and poor rural households, or widen it?

Interpretation A — Reinforcement of advantage: Because adoption required capital, water, and credit, larger landowners gained most, tenants were sometimes displaced, mechanization reduced demand for some labor, and regional disparities widened. On this view the Green Revolution strengthened a class of prosperous cultivators and sharpened inequality.
Interpretation B — Broad but unequal gains: Because seeds were divisible and the new crops were labor-intensive, many smallholders adopted them and benefited, employment and wages rose in some regions, and food prices were lower than they would otherwise have been, which helped the poor. On this view the technology was scale-neutral in its effects on the poor, and the main inequalities arose from preexisting differences in access to water and infrastructure.

Evidence: Both views rely on village surveys, National Sample Survey data, and regional studies, which differ by place and period. Distinguishing changes in average income from changes in distribution, and absolute poverty from relative inequality, is crucial.

Why it matters: The answer shapes judgments about development strategy, about the Green Revolution's political legacy, and about whether agricultural growth can reduce poverty without institutional reform.

Division II

Rural Political Power and the Rise of Backward-Caste Mobilization

Section 94.5

Landholding Groups, Rural Elites, and the Redistribution of Political Influence

Rural political power in the early decades of independence rested on a combination of landholding, social status, and control of local institutions. In many regions, the Congress party relied on landowning notables and on the votes they could mobilize from dependents, tenants, and caste networks. Land reform changed the foundations of this arrangement without abolishing it. The abolition of zamindari and other intermediary tenures, carried out state by state in the 1950s, removed the largest landlords' legal claims over villages and brought many tenants into direct relationship with the state. In practice, the main beneficiaries were often the middle-ranking cultivating groups, who had held substantial rights as tenants or occupants and now became owner-cultivators. Ceiling laws, which limited the size of holdings, were widely evaded, and redistribution to landless people remained limited in most states, with notable exceptions such as Kerala and West Bengal after the Left Front's operation barga tenancy registration from 1977.

The Green Revolution and the commercialization of agriculture strengthened these middle-ranking cultivators. Groups such as the Jats of Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh, the Yadavs and Kurmis of parts of the Gangetic plain, the Patidars of Gujarat, the Marathas of Maharashtra, the Vokkaligas and Lingayats of Karnataka, and the Reddys and Kammas of Andhra Pradesh combined economic weight with numerical presence. The anthropologist M. N. Srinivas used the term "dominant caste" for groups that were numerous, owned substantial land, and held local power, even where their ritual rank was not the highest. The concept helps explain why the political influence of such groups did not correspond to the classical hierarchy in which Brahmins held the highest ritual position.

Electoral competition added another dimension. Universal adult franchise meant that numerical strength mattered, and parties had to build coalitions among communities. As the monopoly of older notables faded, leaders from cultivating castes entered the legislatures and ministries, first in state politics and then nationally. Their power often rested on control of cooperative sugar mills, marketing committees, credit societies, and local government bodies, institutions through which rural patronage flowed. In Maharashtra, for example, cooperative sugar factories provided an institutional base for Maratha political leadership. These networks tied rural prosperity to political organization.

It is important to distinguish economic dominance, caste status, and electoral influence. A group could be economically dominant in a region but ritually ranked below others, or numerically large but economically divided. Many members of landowning castes were small or marginal farmers; many members of supposedly backward castes were prosperous cultivators. Subordinate communities, including Dalits and many landless laborers, usually lacked land and often depended on dominant groups for employment, which constrained their political freedom and exposed them to violence when they asserted claims for wages or dignity. Rural politics therefore combined cooperation and conflict, not only among castes but within them.

By the 1970s, the result was a shift in the social base of political leadership. Congress retained strength by accommodating these groups in many states, but in others, regional and opposition parties drew on the same constituencies. The prosperous cultivators who had gained from agricultural modernization began to press for policies favorable to agriculture, and in some states for recognition as backward classes entitled to reservation, as the next sections show.

The concept of the dominant caste helps connect these processes without identifying any single community as invariably powerful. A locally influential group might hold substantial land, command numbers at the ballot box, and control cooperative banks or market committees, even when ritual hierarchy placed it below Brahmins. Economic and political dominance could reinforce one another, yet there were important regional variations and conflicts inside such groups. Landless workers and minority cultivators encountered the state's programs through institutions often managed by their wealthier neighbors. Conversely, electoral competition sometimes gave disadvantaged groups leverage over local elites. Studying panchayat decisions, agricultural-credit records, and the distribution of irrigation connections reveals more about rural power than an abstract hierarchy alone. The Green Revolution changed the rewards attached to local authority and encouraged new claims to represent the countryside.

Section 94.6

Farmers' Movements, Agricultural Interests, and State Policy

Why would prosperous farmers criticize a government whose policies had enriched them? The answer lies in the structure of agricultural markets and public finance. Procurement prices were set by the state, and cultivators argued that the prices they received were kept low to subsidize urban consumers and industry. Input costs, particularly for electricity, diesel, fertilizer, and water, were set partly by governments, and farmers sought subsidies and lower tariffs. Because the output of the Green Revolution regions entered national markets and policy debates, the economic interests of farmers became directly political, and agricultural price policy became a subject of organized agitation.

The most influential political exponent of this view was Charan Singh. A Jat leader from western Uttar Pradesh, he had served as Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh, founded the Bharatiya Kranti Dal in 1967, and later led the Bharatiya Lok Dal, which merged into the Janata Party in 1977. He had opposed Nehru's advocacy of cooperative farming and argued for a policy centered on the independent peasant proprietor, and he maintained that rural India was systematically disadvantaged relative to cities in prices and public investment. He served as Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister in the Janata government and became Prime Minister in July 1979 after the Janata coalition collapsed, but his government resigned in January 1980 without facing Parliament after Congress withdrew its support. Though his tenure was brief, he gave institutional form to a political constituency of cultivating castes in northern India, and his party, later the Lok Dal, was an important base for subsequent leaders.

Beyond party politics, new farmers' organizations emerged from the late 1970s. In Maharashtra, the Shetkari Sanghatana led by Sharad Joshi mobilized onion, sugarcane, and cotton growers from 1979 on the argument that low procurement prices constituted exploitation of rural India by an urban-industrial order. In Karnataka, the Karnataka Rajya Raitha Sangha, associated with M. D. Nanjundaswamy, emerged from agitations beginning around 1980 over levies and prices. In Tamil Nadu, agriculturists' organizations pressed demands over electricity and prices in the early 1980s. In Punjab, farmers' unions pressed on procurement, input prices, and water. These movements differed in ideology, but shared a language of "farmers versus the urban sector."

The most prominent was the Bharatiya Kisan Union (BKU), which became powerful in western Uttar Pradesh under Mahendra Singh Tikait, a Jat leader from the Baliyan khap region of Muzaffarnagar district. In early 1987, protests over electricity tariffs at Shamli, in which police firing killed protesters, galvanized the movement, and in January 1988 the BKU besieged the divisional commissioner's office in Meerut for weeks, demanding lower tariffs, higher sugarcane prices, and loan relief. The BKU combined caste and community networks with economic demands; its mass base was primarily among Jat cultivators, though it also claimed to represent farmers generally. Its protest repertoire, including dharnas, road blockades, and large rallies, demonstrated the capacity of organized cultivators to pressure state governments.

Farmers' movements had ambiguous political meanings. They represented real grievances over prices, indebtedness, and arbitrary administration, and they showed that rural India could mobilize effectively within a democracy. Critics argued that they largely represented relatively well-off cultivators, with limited attention to landless laborers, and that demands for subsidies and loan waivers strained public budgets and groundwater. Movements often sought to transcend caste with the idea of "kisan," yet their leadership and base frequently coincided with particular dominant castes. Students should therefore treat farmer politics as both an expression of democratic interest representation and an example of how economic interest, caste identity, and regional grievance could overlap.

Farmers' organizations should also be distinguished from agricultural labor unions. A demand for higher procurement prices or lower electricity charges benefits certain cultivators directly, but may not improve the position of households that must buy grain or sell their labor. The political language of 'the farmer' could unite large and small landholders against the state while concealing disagreements over wages, tenancy, and debt. Regional movements differed in ideology: some worked through established parties, others emphasized nonpartisan protest, and still others entered electoral bargaining after mass demonstrations. Comparing their programs with actual social composition prevents the assumption that all rural protest represented the poorest villagers. The broader result was the emergence of agriculture as a specialized interest that national governments had to negotiate with, rather than merely an area they planned from above.

Section 94.7

Other Backward Classes: Caste Hierarchy, Political Organization, and Social Justice

The category "Other Backward Classes" has an administrative and constitutional history. The Constitution of 1950 reserved seats and posts for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and, in Article 340, directed the President to appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of "socially and educationally backward classes." Article 15(4), added by the First Amendment in 1951 after the Supreme Court's Champakam Dorairajan decision on Madras communal reservations, allowed the state to make special provision for their advancement, and Article 16(4) permitted reservation of public posts for backward classes inadequately represented in state services. The term OBC came into use for groups that were neither Scheduled Castes nor Scheduled Tribes but were considered disadvantaged by caste-based social and educational backwardness.

The politics of backwardness had older roots, especially in southern and western India. In the princely state of Kolhapur, Shahu Maharaj reserved a large proportion of posts for non-Brahmin communities in 1902. In Mysore, the Miller Committee of 1918 recommended preferences for non-Brahmins. In Madras Presidency, the Justice Party and later the Self-Respect Movement led by Periyar (Chapter 80) built a political culture of non-Brahmin assertion, producing a system of communal representation in appointments. Where Brahmin and other upper-caste groups had dominated education, administration, and the professions, non-Brahmin movements demanded access. In the south and west these movements had achieved significant institutional gains before independence and after it; Tamil Nadu's reservation percentages for backward classes rose in 1971 and again in 1980, and Karnataka appointed commissions, including the Havanur Commission of 1975, to define eligible groups.

The northern pattern was different. There, the upper castes had been dominant in administration, but large cultivating and artisan groups such as Yadavs, Kurmis, Koeris, and many others had a more complex relationship with the land hierarchy, and caste politics took a longer time to be openly organized around backwardness. In Bihar, the Triveni Sangh of the 1930s had united Yadavs, Kurmis, and Koeris; the socialist leader Ram Manohar Lohia later advanced a political formula calling for substantial representation of backward castes, Dalits, women, and minorities in leadership. Karpoori Thakur, Chief Minister of Bihar from 1977 to 1979, introduced a reservation system in 1978 that divided beneficiaries into categories of backward and more backward groups, with provisions also for women and economically poor members of upper castes. The measure provoked protests and illustrated how reservation could split as well as unify backward constituencies. In Uttar Pradesh, the Chhedi Lal Sathi Commission of 1977 examined backwardness, and leaders such as Ram Naresh Yadav rose in the Janata period.

The first national effort to identify backward classes was the Kaka Kalelkar Commission, appointed in 1953, which reported in 1955 and listed more than two thousand groups, using criteria that included ritual status. The central government did not implement its recommendations, citing difficulties of definition, and in 1961 left the identification of backward classes largely to states. The result was an uneven landscape: some states had extensive reservation, others little, and no national OBC list existed. This is why the Mandal Commission, discussed in the next section, was so consequential.

Students must distinguish OBCs from other categories. Scheduled Castes are listed by presidential order and correspond to groups historically subjected to untouchability; Scheduled Tribes are Adivasi communities listed for their social and geographic conditions. OBCs are a heterogeneous category, including relatively prosperous cultivating castes and poor artisan and service castes, and boundaries vary by state. A single caste might be on the OBC list in one state and not in another, and OBC identity is therefore a political and administrative construction as well as a social one, produced through commissions, lists, and negotiation rather than through a single traditional hierarchy.

Backward-class classification involved methodological and political choices. The term referred to social and educational disadvantage, but the practical identification of communities varied across states and over time. Commissions examined occupations, schooling, representation, and inherited exclusion; critics challenged whether particular criteria adequately distinguished beneficiaries. Women within OBC groups faced disadvantages not captured by a single community label, while poorer people from groups outside specified lists could also have weak access to education. Such complexity did not negate discrimination, but it made policy design contentious. Students should distinguish the statutory or administrative listing of a group from a researcher's description of its economic condition. The political rise of OBC leaders reflected long-standing organizing and electoral change, not the mere administrative creation of a category in 1979.

Historical Vocabulary
Other Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes

Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) are groups listed in schedules to the Constitution (by presidential order) and entitled to reserved legislative seats, public employment, and educational places. Other Backward Classes (OBC) is an administrative category of socially and educationally disadvantaged groups outside those schedules, identified by commissions and state and central lists. Dalit, meaning "broken" or "oppressed" in several Indian languages, is a self-chosen political term widely used for communities historically subjected to untouchability, largely overlapping with the Scheduled Castes.

These categories are not identical to individual caste identities (jatis), to economic class, or to religion. Treating them as interchangeable obscures the very differences that shaped political conflict.

Section 94.8

The Mandal Commission and the Transformation of Caste Politics, 1979–1990

The Janata government under Morarji Desai announced in December 1978 that it would appoint a second national commission on backward classes, in line with Article 340, and the commission was constituted in January 1979 under B. P. Mandal, a Yadav politician from Bihar who had briefly been Chief Minister of that state in 1968. Its mandate was to determine criteria for identifying socially and educationally backward classes and to recommend measures for their advancement. The commission worked through a changing political landscape; the Janata government fell in 1979, and the commission submitted its report in December 1980 to a Congress government under Indira Gandhi, which did not act on it.

The commission used a combination of social, educational, and economic indicators, supplemented by surveys and by the 1931 census, the last to enumerate caste comprehensively, as a base for estimating population. It identified 3,743 castes and communities as socially and educationally backward and estimated them, together with non-Hindu backward communities, at about 52 percent of the population. Because the Supreme Court had indicated that reservations should not generally exceed fifty percent, and since Scheduled Castes and Tribes already had 22.5 percent, the commission recommended a reservation of 27 percent for OBCs in central government services and public-sector undertakings, along with provisions in educational institutions, promotions, and measures of land reform, vocational training, and other social support. The commission's recommendations went beyond reservation; later debate focused almost entirely on the quota for jobs.

Throughout the 1980s the report remained a subject of political debate. It was laid before Parliament in 1982, and opposition leaders and backward-caste politicians demanded its implementation. In the meantime, states continued their own policies, and the northern parties that emerged from the Lohiaite and Charan Singh traditions, later the Janata Dal and related parties, treated the report as a political program. The Congress governments of the 1980s did not implement it.

The decisive moment came under the National Front government of V. P. Singh, formed in December 1989 after the elections discussed in Section 94.12. On August 7, 1990, the Prime Minister announced in Parliament that the government would implement the commission's recommendation of 27 percent reservation in central government jobs, and an official memorandum followed on August 13. Contemporary commentators and later historians have debated the motives. Some stress the government's ideological commitment to social justice; others emphasize political calculation amid a rivalry between V. P. Singh and Devi Lal, the Jat leader who sought to mobilize farmers, and the desire to build an OBC political base. Both elements were likely present, and the sources do not permit a single settled account.

The decision produced the largest wave of protest over reservation in India's history. Students, particularly in northern India, mounted demonstrations and strikes against the measure, and some students attempted self-immolation, including a widely reported attempt in Delhi in September 1990. Supporters, especially among OBC communities, mobilized in favor. The Supreme Court stayed the order, and in November 1992, after the period of this chapter, its decision in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India upheld the 27 percent reservation while excluding the "creamy layer" of better-off OBCs and affirming a general ceiling of fifty percent. The Mandal controversy ended the National Front government's coalition in November 1990 when other political conflicts, including those over the Ramjanmabhoomi movement (Chapter 99), led the BJP to withdraw support.

It is vital to understand that reservation was not an invention of 1990. Several states had implemented reservation for backward classes decades earlier, and the Constitution already provided for SC and ST reservations. What changed in 1990 was the national scope, the policy's visibility, and the politicization of OBC identity across northern India, where parties built on backward-caste constituencies were soon to contest and win power. Detailed treatment of the post-1991 consequences belongs to Chapter 99.

The Mandal debate was also a dispute over institutions: who would identify the eligible communities, what evidence should be used, how vacancies would be counted, and how individual applicants would be treated. The report's population estimates were contested, and its recommended 27-percent share for central public employment did not itself establish a complete system of implementation. Decisions announced in August 1990 prompted demonstrations and counter-mobilization; judicial review and later administrative changes followed beyond the principal period of this chapter. Presenting the 1990 announcement as if it immediately resolved every legal question would be misleading. Its deeper importance lies in how social-justice demands moved from many state-level arenas to the center of national coalition politics, changing the program and constituencies of parties that hoped to govern India.

Dalit and Adivasi communities: Already beneficiaries of reservation, they had varied responses; some saw potential allies, others worried about competition with dominant OBC groups or about the dilution of their own claims.
The central government and political parties: Calculated electoral advantage, ideological commitment, and the threat of rival mobilization; opposition parties, including those that favored Hindu unity, feared the fragmentation of their constituencies.

These groups held unequal power and did not bear equal responsibility for the violence of the period. The point is the complexity of how a single policy was understood.

Division III

Dalit Political Assertion and the Transformation of India's Party System

Section 94.9

Dalit Politics after Independence: Constitutional Rights, Social Exclusion, and Political Mobilization

The Constitution of 1950 abolished untouchability in Article 17, guaranteed equality before the law, and reserved seats in legislatures and posts in government service for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, at levels of roughly 15 and 7.5 percent. These provisions reflected the work of B. R. Ambedkar, who had chaired the Drafting Committee (Chapter 88). Parliament passed the Untouchability (Offences) Act in 1955, later strengthened and renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976, and in 1989 enacted the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act. Yet the gap between legal equality and social reality remained wide. Dalit communities continued to face exclusion from temples, wells, and village spaces, discrimination in employment and housing, and in many regions economic dependence on dominant landholders.

Reservations opened real opportunities in education and government employment, and created a small but growing educated Dalit middle class, particularly in cities and in regions such as Maharashtra and Punjab. But reserved posts were often unfilled, particularly at senior levels; access to quality schooling remained unequal; and most Dalits lived in rural areas as agricultural laborers or small cultivators with little land. Land reform rarely reached them in significant measure. Because the Green Revolution increased the economic stakes of rural production and wage bargaining, some demands for higher wages and basic dignity brought violent responses from landowning groups.

Episodes of violence became markers of the era. In December 1968 in Kilvenmani, in the Thanjavur delta of Tamil Nadu, 44 Dalit laborers, largely women and children, were burned to death in a hut during a conflict between agricultural workers organized under Communist leadership and local landowners; the accused landowners were ultimately acquitted on appeal, a result that became a symbol of impunity. In 1977 in Belchi, Bihar, eleven people, most of them Dalits, were killed, and Indira Gandhi's visit to the village after her electoral defeat became a political event. Between 1978 and the early 1980s, the proposal to rename Marathwada University in Maharashtra after Ambedkar provoked violence against Dalits in the region. These episodes show that assertion could meet coercion, and they fed the sense that constitutional promises required political organization to be realized.

Ambedkar's legacy took several forms. His conversion to Buddhism in October 1956, shortly before his death, inspired the large Neo-Buddhist movement among Mahars in Maharashtra and shaped a cultural identity centered on rejection of caste hierarchy. His political legacy was institutionalized, at first, in the Republican Party of India, founded in 1957 after his death, which won substantial support in parts of Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh but soon fragmented through factionalism and the incorporation of its leaders by Congress. Many Dalits voted for Congress, which in various states offered reserved positions, welfare programs, and the legacy of the national movement; the party's dependence on Dalit votes, along with those of Muslims and upper castes, was a pillar of its coalition.

Dalits were not a politically uniform community. They belonged to many jatis, including Mahars in Maharashtra, Chamars and Jatavs in the north, Malas and Madigas in Andhra Pradesh, Paraiyars and Pallars in Tamil Nadu, Pulayas in Kerala, and Balmikis, Mazhabi Sikhs, and Ramdasias in Punjab, and they differed in language, region, religion, occupation, and in access to education and reserved posts. Disputes over the distribution of reservation benefits among Scheduled Caste subgroups, such as Punjab's 1975 preference for some groups, show the internal divisions. Treating Dalit politics as a single movement obscures that diversity.

Legal reform also had uneven reach. In some places officials implemented reserved seats and anti-discrimination provisions more actively than in others; access to a police station or court could depend on local status and resources. The ability to record an atrocity or to obtain relief was itself a form of unequal power. Dalit women often faced overlapping forms of caste, gender, and economic coercion that aggregate accounts hide. Equally, educated Dalit professionals and public employees were not simply removed from community politics; their salaries, associations, and networks helped finance new forms of organizing. This connection between state employment and independent political mobilization explains part of the transition from welfare administered by established parties toward demands for representative power. It also helps make sense of the different organizational strategies examined in the following section.

Section 94.10

Dalit Panthers, Kanshi Ram, and the Emergence of New Political Organizations

The Dalit Panthers emerged in Bombay in 1972, led by young writers and activists, including Namdeo Dhasal, J. V. Pawar, and Raja Dhale, many of them from Neo-Buddhist and urban working-class backgrounds. The name recalled the Black Panther movement in the United States, and the group's style combined militant rhetoric with literary creativity: the Marathi Dalit literature movement, including Dhasal's poetry, brought the language of the slums and of caste humiliation into public culture. The immediate spark was violence against Dalits in rural Maharashtra and the perceived failure of the older Republican Party leadership, and the group's manifesto of 1973 presented a program that linked caste oppression to class exploitation, calling for the unity of Dalits and other oppressed groups, including workers and landless peasants.

The Panthers were significant for their politics of dignity and confrontation, for the creative output they inspired, and for organizing on matters from atrocities to access to public services. They did not become a lasting electoral force. Internal disagreements over ideology, between a more class-based Marxist orientation and an Ambedkarite Buddhist emphasis, and over relations with political parties split the organization in the later 1970s. Its legacy lay chiefly in culture and in the demonstration that Dalit assertion could take militant forms.

A different strategy emerged in northern India through Kanshi Ram. Born in 1934 in Punjab, he worked as a government scientist in Pune, where he became involved in conflicts over the recognition of Ambedkar's birthday and was drawn into organizing among employees from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, OBCs, and religious minorities. In 1978 he established the Backward and Minority Communities Employees Federation (BAMCEF), a network of educated government employees committed to the idea that the "bahujan," the majority of the population excluded from power, should be united. He argued that dependence on upper-caste-led parties would never deliver representation and that Dalits and other bahujan groups must build their own political organization.

In 1981 he founded the Dalit Shoshit Samaj Sangharsh Samiti, known as DS4, which moved from employee networks to mass mobilization, using cycle marches, public meetings, and slogans, in states including Punjab, Haryana, and Uttar Pradesh. In April 1984, on Ambedkar's birth anniversary, he launched the Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP). The BSP's initial electoral performance was modest: it won only a few seats in 1989, with about two percent of the vote nationally, but it was built on an explicit strategy of capturing political power as a precondition for social change, and on careful organization at the grassroots, particularly in Uttar Pradesh. Mayawati, a teacher from Delhi who became one of Kanshi Ram's closest associates in the early 1980s, contested early elections and entered the Lok Sabha in 1989; her later career as Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh belongs to subsequent chapters.

The differences among these strategies are instructive. The Dalit Panthers emphasized militant cultural and political assertion, often with a left orientation; the Republican Party sought an independent Ambedkarite party but fragmented; Kanshi Ram's organizations stressed building a wider bahujan identity and capturing power electorally, and were willing to enter alliances. The first major Dalit party to build a durable electoral base was thus a product of the era's convergence of rural change, reservation-created employee networks, and the weakening of Congress's hold on Dalit voters.

Manifestos and movement memories require particular care. The Dalit Panthers' writings deliberately adopted expansive and confrontational language, while later accounts of Kanshi Ram's networks sometimes read their eventual electoral successes back into their beginnings. To understand what an organization actually achieved at a given date, compare membership claims with election returns, contemporary reports, and local studies. The term bahujan offered a broad political identity, but assembling Dalit, OBC, minority, and other voters into one durable coalition involved significant bargaining. Local caste rivalries could obstruct cooperation even when leaders used a common emancipatory vocabulary. The significance of the BSP by 1990 is therefore organizational and programmatic as well as electoral: its project challenged the assumption that marginalized communities must remain permanent subordinate partners within Congress or another established party.

Section 94.11

Regional Parties and the Decline of Congress's Electoral Monopoly

Regional parties were not a novelty of the 1980s. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK), founded in 1949 by C. N. Annadurai, emerged from the Dravidian movement, whose themes of non-Brahmin identity, Tamil language, and opposition to the imposition of Hindi had been developed in earlier decades (Chapter 80). It campaigned against Hindi in the agitations of 1965 and in 1967 won a majority in the Tamil Nadu assembly, ending Congress rule there; Congress has not governed the state alone since. After Annadurai's death in 1969, M. Karunanidhi led the party. In 1972 the film star M. G. Ramachandran, who had left the DMK, founded the All India Anna DMK (AIADMK), which won the 1977 assembly election; he served as Chief Minister until his death in 1987, building a popular base through welfare programs, notably the noon meal scheme for schoolchildren, and a film-based political appeal.

Elsewhere, parties rooted in regional identity and social coalitions emerged. The Akali Dal, discussed in Division IV, represented a Sikh political tradition in Punjab. The National Conference dominated Jammu and Kashmir under Sheikh Abdullah and later Farooq Abdullah. The Shiv Sena, founded in Bombay in 1966, mobilized Marathi speakers around claims about jobs and language. The Left Front, dominated by the Communist Party of India (Marxist), won power in West Bengal in 1977 under Jyoti Basu and remained there for decades, combining organization with land-reform measures. In Karnataka, the Janata Party and its leader Ramakrishna Hegde formed a non-Congress government in 1983. In Andhra Pradesh, the actor N. T. Rama Rao founded the Telugu Desam Party (TDP) in March 1982 on a platform of Telugu self-respect and welfare, and won a majority of the assembly in January 1983, ending Congress rule in the state; in the Lok Sabha election of 1984, the TDP won about thirty seats, making it the largest opposition party.

Why were regional parties not necessarily separatist? Most operated within the constitutional framework, contested elections, participated in coalitions, and demanded greater autonomy, resources, or recognition for language and culture, not secession. The Constitution's structure of states reorganized on linguistic lines (Chapter 89) had given regional identities institutional form, and federal parliamentary politics created incentives for regional parties: they could win state governments, bargain at the center, and use Rajya Sabha representation. Even the DMK, which in the early 1960s had flirted with a Dravidian secessionist demand, abandoned it after the 1963 constitutional amendment penalizing advocacy of secession and chose electoral politics. Distinguishing regionalism from separatism is essential, because the difference shaped how governments responded and how conflicts developed.

The expansion of regional parties had several causes. The state-level electorate grew more politically conscious and differentiated, and agricultural prosperity and the spread of media and literacy strengthened regional public spheres. Congress's centralization under Indira Gandhi reduced the authority of strong regional leaders in her own party, leaving space for opposition forces to claim regional identity. Control of state governments gave regional parties patronage and resources. The party system thus changed from one dominant national party with factional competition inside it to a multi-party system in which Congress faced strong competitors in many states.

The meaning of a 'regional party' also depended on context. Some organizations promoted linguistic recognition and state development, others formed around a charismatic leader, and still others emphasized caste coalitions or demands for autonomy. Their policies could change after they entered government. Strong regional electoral support did not always translate into large national seat totals, because parliamentary representation depended on where votes were concentrated. Nor was every non-Congress party automatically a regional movement. Comparing national and assembly results reveals how a party could dominate one state's elections and possess limited influence in another. Such contrasts are essential for understanding why coalition politics became viable: national governments increasingly required partners with concentrated local legitimacy and different policy priorities, even when those partners agreed mainly on opposing the incumbent.

Section 94.12

Federalism, State Autonomy, and Coalition Politics in a Changing Republic

India's Constitution created a federal structure with a strong center. The Union government controls defense, foreign affairs, and major financial powers; state governments exercise authority over agriculture, policing, and other subjects; Governors are appointed by the President on the advice of the central government. Article 356 allows the President to take over a state government if its constitutional machinery fails, and this power, used frequently, became one of the most controversial aspects of federalism. When the same party governed at the center and in most states, the constitutional tensions remained muted. As opposition parties won state governments, they came to the surface.

The use of Article 356 reflected partisan competition. Opposition governments were dismissed repeatedly: the DMK government of Tamil Nadu in 1976 during the Emergency; nine Congress-ruled state governments dismissed by the Janata government in 1977 after its victory; and nine opposition-ruled state governments dismissed by Indira Gandhi's government in 1980 after her return. In such cases, the central government argued that electoral verdicts at the center undermined the legitimacy of state governments, while critics regarded it as an abuse. The role of Governors also generated controversy. In July 1984 the Governor of Jammu and Kashmir, Jagmohan, dismissed the National Conference government of Farooq Abdullah, who had been supported by a majority, after defections. In August 1984 the Governor of Andhra Pradesh, Ram Lal, dismissed the Chief Minister N. T. Rama Rao and installed his rival; large protests, pressure from opposition leaders, and national controversy led to his restoration in September. These episodes helped define a debate on the Governor's neutrality.

The central government responded to the debate in 1983 by appointing the Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State relations, which reported in 1988 and urged, among other things, restraint in the use of Article 356 and greater consultation in the appointment of Governors. Opposition-ruled states had meanwhile convened meetings in 1983 to coordinate positions. Regional demands for greater financial resources, for control over resources such as river waters, and for the revision of Centre-state arrangements, including the Anandpur Sahib Resolution in Punjab and demands from Tamil Nadu and West Bengal, formed part of a broader contestation over federal arrangements. The Supreme Court's judgment in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India in 1994, a later development, imposed judicial review on the use of Article 356.

Opposition cooperation developed in stages. Janata in 1977 combined the Congress (O), Jan Sangh, Bharatiya Lok Dal, and socialists, but fell in 1979 through factionalism, as Chapter 93 explained. In the later 1980s, a new alignment formed around V. P. Singh, who had left Congress in 1987 after a dispute with Rajiv Gandhi over corruption allegations. The Janata Dal was formed in 1988, and with the TDP, the DMK, the Assam Gana Parishad, and other regional groups it launched the National Front. The elections of November 1989 produced a hung Parliament: Congress, which had won over 400 seats in 1984, fell to 197; the Janata Dal and allies won about 143 for the Janata Dal itself; the Bharatiya Janata Party rose from two seats to 85; the Left parties won around 45. V. P. Singh formed a minority National Front government with outside support from both the BJP and the Left, two forces that were ideological opponents. The result showed that no single party could now command a majority, that regional parties were integral to national coalitions, and that caste and regional mobilization had transformed the electoral landscape.

Federal conflicts were not always constitutional crises in the narrow legal sense. Many emerged as disputes over budget allocations, procurement, education, policing, irrigation, and the appointment of local officials. A state government could accept the Union's sovereignty while arguing that central agencies ignored regional priorities. Governors and Article 356 became contentious partly because they offered visible ways for national actors to replace or constrain elected state ministries. Equally, regional leaders sometimes used central intervention to mobilize support or explain failures of their own administrations. Historians should compare the formal text of the Constitution with the actual bargains required to govern a diverse federation. The emerging coalition system made these bargains more public, and the crises in Assam and Punjab showed what could happen when negotiation lost credibility.

Historical Vocabulary
Federalism and Article 356

Federalism divides governmental powers between a national government and state governments. India's version is sometimes called "quasi-federal" because of its strong central powers, including Governors and emergency provisions. Article 356 allows the President to assume the functions of a state government when its constitutional machinery has failed; the resulting period is commonly called President's Rule.

The importance of the article lies in its frequent use for political purposes. Students should note that "autonomy" in Indian political debate usually refers to a rebalancing within the federal system, not secession.

Historical Debate 2

Democratic Deepening, Fragmentation, or Both?

The question: Did the growth of regional and caste-based politics deepen Indian democracy, fragment the political order, or do both?

Interpretation A — Democratic deepening: Political participation spread to groups previously marginalized, as backward-caste, Dalit, and regional leaders entered legislatures and governments, turnout among poorer and lower-caste voters rose, and parties had to address new constituencies. Scholars such as Christophe Jaffrelot have described a "silent revolution" in which lower castes gained power through the ballot.
Interpretation B — Fragmentation and instability: The decline of a unifying party increased governmental instability, encouraged identity-based competition, strengthened clientelism, and in some cases intensified violence; central responses to regional demands often relied on coercion rather than negotiation.

Evidence: Election data, studies of legislators' social backgrounds, state-level case studies, and analyses of violence support different parts of each view, which are not mutually exclusive.

Division IV

The Assam Movement and Punjab's Crisis of Regional Autonomy

Section 94.13

Assam's Historical Background: Migration, Language, Identity, and Political Representation

The Assam Movement arose from a long history of state-making and population movement, not from a sudden discovery of migration in 1979. Colonial rule had reorganized the Brahmaputra valley around tea plantations, oil extraction, administration, and agricultural expansion. British employers recruited plantation labor from other Indian regions, while cultivators from overcrowded districts of eastern Bengal settled on river islands and lowlands. Political boundaries moved around populations whose livelihoods, languages, and family networks had already crossed them. The 1947 Partition turned one set of internal routes into international boundaries; the creation of Bangladesh in 1971 transformed the legal and humanitarian circumstances again. Such shifts do not determine citizenship by themselves, but they explain why documentary status became difficult to disentangle from historical movement.

Assam was not a society of two homogeneous communities. Assamese speakers, Bengali speakers, Bodo and other Indigenous peoples, hill groups, tea-garden labor communities, Nepali speakers, and Muslims and Hindus of different origins inhabited overlapping political geographies. In the Brahmaputra valley, campaigns to make Assamese the official language reflected a desire to protect local public life after decades of demographic change. Yet the language measures of 1960 and disputes over schools and universities could also make Bengali-speaking residents feel excluded. The linguistic reorganization and separation of several hill territories changed what 'Assamese' and 'regional majority' meant in practice. Students should resist mapping every religious identity onto a particular language or migration history.

Land was central to conflict. Erosion and flooding repeatedly displaced villagers along the Brahmaputra, and new settlements in forest, grazing, and char areas raised disputes about customary rights and administrative tenure. Census categories described language and birthplace, but they were not reliable proxies for the citizenship of every individual. A family might have lived in the valley for generations yet have incomplete paperwork, while somebody crossing a border recently might appear in other records. Political claims about 'foreigners' often treated these very different circumstances together. For historians, the crucial distinction is between migration estimates, documented individual legal status, and rhetoric deployed during political campaigns.

The immediate political trigger was an electoral-roll dispute. In 1979, during preparations for a parliamentary by-election in the Mangaldoi constituency, allegations arose that names of noncitizens had entered electoral lists. Student leaders argued that electoral competition would be distorted unless unauthorized entrants were identified and removed. Their concern linked questions of belonging with the distribution of seats and offices in a democracy organized by universal adult franchise. Electoral rolls are therefore more than administrative lists: they can become instruments through which rivals imagine either political inclusion or dispossession. Yet an allegation against a roll is not proof concerning all named voters, and a fair legal process must distinguish evidence about individuals from group stereotypes.

Earlier political institutions helped explain why students became influential. The All Assam Students' Union, or AASU, had developed networks across colleges and towns and could coordinate strikes, processions, and boycotts beyond the capacities of conventional parties. The All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad broadened the movement's organizational reach. Their leaders drew support from many citizens who believed that successive governments had ignored regional grievances; other residents feared that proposed exclusions could endanger their safety and citizenship. Both realities must be retained. The movement was a major episode in popular political mobilization, while its rhetoric and effects could also expose vulnerable neighbors to threats.

The federal context matters as much as demography. Assam's government depended on central decisions about borders, citizenship law, elections, and security, but rural land and daily administration involved state and district authorities. The central government sought to maintain constitutional elections; movement leaders demanded safeguards before polling. Neither an election nor a boycott could by itself settle underlying disagreements over identity. The conflicts showed that India's federal system provided arenas for demands but could also sharpen the stakes of administrative categorization. Section 94.14 follows what happened as negotiations repeatedly failed and civil disobedience intersected with mass violence.

Historical analysis must also leave room for residents who did not fit movement categories: women whose names appeared differently across records, riverine households whose documents were destroyed by floods, labor migrants recruited during earlier decades, and Indigenous communities who feared both settlement expansion and a debate dominated by Assamese-Bengali rivalry. Their experiences complicate a story told solely through student leaders or central ministers. To understand why Assam became a crisis, students need the history of agrarian change, the administrative making of boundaries, and the unequal capacity to prove belonging.

Section 94.14

The Assam Movement, 1979–1985: Mobilization, Violence, and the Assam Accord

From 1979, AASU and the allied AAGSP pressed for the detection and removal of people they regarded as foreign entrants, along with corrections to electoral rolls before elections were held. Protest methods included marches, blockades, shutdowns, petitions, and campaigns of noncooperation. These actions displayed organizational capacity and articulated a widely felt anxiety over representation, but their reach also created pressure on those who did not join. The central government negotiated intermittently with student leaders under Indira Gandhi, and the state passed through unstable ministries and periods of direct rule. Disagreement over the applicable cutoff date made compromise difficult: a date near Partition and one associated with the Bangladesh War implied very different categories of people affected.

The proposed 1983 elections became a point of rupture. Movement organizations called for a boycott, while the central government proceeded with polling under extraordinary security conditions. Electoral participation varied sharply from locality to locality. Boycotts did not remove the constitutional requirement to protect those who wished to vote, and holding elections did not absolve authorities of responsibility for foreseeable danger. Violence spread in several districts, involving attacks on civilians identified by religion, language, or perceived origin. The category 'foreign' frequently reflected an assailant's accusation rather than any adjudicated status. Official decisions and mobilization tactics must be considered together with local conflicts over land and power.

On 18 February 1983, in and around Nellie in present-day Morigaon district, attackers massacred large numbers of villagers, predominantly Bengali-origin Muslims, including women and children. Contemporary and retrospective death counts diverge substantially: the widely cited official figure is approximately 1,800, while other accounts give higher estimates. The precise total should be reported with attribution rather than manufactured certainty. What is not disputed is the occurrence of mass civilian killing. Accounts from survivors describe flight, loss of relatives, and failures of protection. Explanation of perpetrators' motives must not be confused with justification, and neither the victims nor all members of any larger community should be treated as one political actor.

Other communities also suffered during the wider unrest. The destruction of property, attacks on settlements, disruption of schooling and commerce, and fear of further violence extended far beyond the best-known massacre. Families already coping with river erosion and poverty faced a sudden loss of security. Official investigations were conducted, but their availability, publication, and political consequences were limited; subsequent prosecutions and compensation were widely criticized as inadequate. An institutional history cannot close at the point of a negotiated settlement without assessing these failures. The fact that a movement had mass support cannot reduce the significance of violence against minorities.

The Memorandum of Settlement signed on 15 August 1985 by the Union and Assam governments with AASU and AAGSP leaders became known as the Assam Accord. Its citizenship provisions specified different treatment for those who entered before 1 January 1966, those arriving from that date through 24 March 1971, and those arriving on or after 25 March 1971. The middle group was subject to detection and registration and temporary deletion from electoral rolls under the Accord's framework; people arriving on or after the later cutoff were to be dealt with under applicable law. This was a negotiated administrative arrangement, not a declaration that every person of a particular language or religion was an outsider.

The Accord also promised safeguards for the Assamese people's cultural, social, and linguistic identity, economic-development measures, and border-management efforts. These clauses reveal that the movement was about more than electoral lists: it joined concerns over demographic change with employment, cultural institutions, and public investment. Implementation proved difficult because individual status had to be established through law and records. The Assam Accord's provisions later generated further litigation and contested politics; those developments can be acknowledged briefly without projecting their outcomes backward into 1985. A historical account should separate the text agreed in 1985 from later disputes about execution.

Movement leaders entered electoral politics through the Assam Gana Parishad, formed in 1985, which won that year's assembly election; Prafulla Kumar Mahanta became chief minister. This was a striking conversion of student mobilization into a governing party and an example of how regional protest could reshape India's electoral map. It did not resolve tensions among Assamese speakers, Bengali-origin communities, and Indigenous groups, nor did it erase the losses of 1983. Democratic representation and the protection of minorities remained parallel tests of the political settlement. The closing lesson is not that identity claims can never be reconciled, but that reconciliation requires institutions capable of applying law impartially and protecting people during political contention.

Section 94.15

Punjab's Political Crisis: Sikh Identity, Akali Dal, and Demands for Autonomy

Punjab's political crisis grew out of distinctive histories of religious institutions, language, territorial reorganization, and federal bargaining. Sikhism emerged in the Punjab region and developed institutions of worship, community service, and religious authority, including the Harmandir Sahib or Golden Temple and the Akal Takht in Amritsar. Sikh identity cannot be reduced to a single electoral preference, and Sikhs did not share one attitude toward the central government. Political competition also involved Hindu and other communities, agricultural class divisions, urban-rural distinctions, and multiple Sikh organizations. This complexity is fundamental to explaining why arguments about autonomy could become associated with a wider confrontation.

After Partition divided Punjab in 1947, proponents of a Punjabi-speaking state pursued the Punjabi Suba demand, while opponents feared that linguistic organization would have religious consequences. In 1966, India reorganized the former Punjab into Punjab and Haryana, with several hill areas placed in Himachal Pradesh. The city of Chandigarh became a Union Territory serving as the capital of both Punjab and Haryana. The territorial arrangement did not settle disagreements over Punjabi-speaking areas, the allocation of river waters, or the relation between state and central authority. The creation of a state could therefore satisfy one long-standing demand while creating new disputes.

The Shiromani Akali Dal was an important political vehicle for Sikh and Punjabi regional demands; the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee administered major gurdwaras under a separate institutional framework. Their roles overlapped but were not identical. The Akali Dal contested elections, negotiated with national parties, and sometimes entered state government, while religious activists and authorities had their own influence. Congress remained a powerful competitor and at times relied on alliances or factional strategies to limit Akali authority. The balance among these actors was unstable: policy disagreements could be framed through faith, but political demands were not necessarily demands for an independent state.

The Anandpur Sahib Resolution first adopted in 1973, with a related set of formulations endorsed in 1978, became the best-known statement of Akali aspirations. It advocated substantial state autonomy, shifts in the distribution of central and state powers, and measures addressing Punjabi language, Chandigarh, and other territorial and resource claims. Interpretations differed. Supporters emphasized a genuinely federal constitutional order; opponents regarded portions of the program as threatening national unity. It is misleading either to treat every demand for decentralization as secessionism or to ignore how separatist organizations later adopted a different political vocabulary around Khalistan. The resolution must be read in its changing political context and in its particular versions.

Water politics connected the conflict to Division I. Punjab's agricultural success depended on irrigation and electricity, making disputes over Ravi-Beas river allocations and canals especially consequential. Neighboring Haryana and Rajasthan had claims and needs of their own; environmental and constitutional arguments about riparian rights intersected with parties' attempts to claim regional legitimacy. The proposed Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal became a political symbol. Chandigarh and transfer of Punjabi-speaking areas likewise acquired meaning beyond administrative boundaries because communities saw them as tests of whether negotiations delivered their promises.

The federal structure created both possibilities for compromise and reasons for distrust. Article 356 permitted central intervention when a state's constitutional machinery was said to have failed; frequent partisan uses elsewhere fed suspicion that elected regional governments were vulnerable. The Akali Dal's protest politics drew upon familiar constitutional and nonviolent techniques, including demonstrations and arrests, even as more militant groups developed outside or alongside party channels. Historians should not retroactively attribute later violence to all who endorsed earlier autonomy demands. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the central question was how the Indian state would negotiate with multiple Sikh political currents without equating them.

The 1978 confrontation in Amritsar involving Sikh groups and the Sant Nirankari mission, in which people were killed, became a focal point for religious mobilization and helped raise the public profile of preacher Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale. But no single clash explains the crisis. Competition within Punjab's parties, police decisions, perceived sacrilege, socioeconomic aspirations, and campaigns for greater autonomy created a volatile field. The next section traces how shootings, assassinations, arrests, and reciprocal mistrust turned a contest over governance into an expanding security conflict. The distinction between political demands and attacks on civilians remains indispensable throughout.

Section 94.16

Militancy, Communal Tensions, and the Escalation of Violence in Punjab, 1978–1984

Punjab's worsening conflict was neither a spontaneous eruption of religious antagonism nor a straight line from a resolution about federalism to armed rebellion. Political leaders made choices within an increasingly polarized setting. After the 1978 clash in Amritsar, Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale's preaching and organizing attracted followers who believed that Sikh religious rights and dignity were endangered. His influence developed alongside older Akali institutions and was never identical to them. The media, rival parties, police, and central authorities sometimes described distinct actors with the same label, obscuring differences between religious revivalism, constitutional agitation, political intimidation, and separatist violence.

The 1981 murder of newspaper proprietor Lala Jagat Narain intensified the crisis. Bhindranwale was arrested and later released when authorities said sufficient evidence for prosecution was lacking. Whether particular killings can be connected to specific leaders requires case-specific evidence; suspicion should not be converted into conviction by narrative repetition. Other murders, assaults, and threats nonetheless created an atmosphere in which journalists, officials, moderate political figures, and ordinary civilians faced growing danger. Violence was also directed at Hindus and at Sikhs who opposed militants. Such attacks undermined the social relationships on which peaceful bargaining depended and encouraged arguments for extraordinary security measures.

The Akali Dal's Dharam Yudh Morcha, launched in 1982 under Sant Harchand Singh Longowal's leadership in cooperation with Bhindranwale, sought to press political and religious demands through mass mobilization. Protesters courted arrest, and the agitation intersected with nationwide visibility during the Asian Games in Delhi. Participants held varied goals: some emphasized state autonomy and concrete settlement terms; others adopted militant or separatist positions. Confusing their objectives makes it impossible to explain why negotiations repeatedly stalled. The movement operated in a context of mistrust over police treatment, access to religious places, and the perceived use of central power for partisan ends.

Violence escalated in 1983. The killing of Deputy Inspector General A. S. Atwal near the Golden Temple complex in April symbolized the weakness of enforcement in areas of intense religious and political significance. Punjab came under President's Rule in October after the collapse of state government and continuing attacks. Armed men, weapons, and fortifications accumulated within or near the Golden Temple complex, including the Akal Takht area. The religious importance of the shrine, its ordinary pilgrims, and its crowded urban surroundings made the consequences of a military assault especially grave. The presence of militants did not erase civilian protections or the significance of the site to Sikhs worldwide.

Central and state authorities faced a genuine security challenge, but debates remain about alternatives, timing, intelligence, and the effects of earlier political maneuvering. Critics have argued that national parties sometimes exploited factional conflict and delayed negotiation; supporters of force emphasized the severity of shootings, intimidation, and the buildup of arms. Documentary evidence establishes the growth of violence but does not conclusively resolve every later claim of secret patronage. A responsible account attributes disputed allegations and examines the constraints, interests, and options known to decision-makers at each stage rather than reading the outcome as inevitable.

Negotiations on river waters, Chandigarh, territorial adjustments, and the distribution of governmental power failed repeatedly. A proposed settlement was not simply a technical question: leaders feared appearing to surrender to threats, while agitators doubted the credibility of promises and suspected that concessions would be postponed. Escalation changed the bargaining environment. Armed violence intimidated potential moderates; coercive policing alienated other residents; religious spaces became sites of political and military calculation. These feedback loops explain why an institutional disagreement could turn into a crisis affecting the entire Republic.

By the first days of June 1984, the government chose a major Army operation aimed at armed militants in the Golden Temple complex and other gurdwaras. This decision did not extinguish the history of constitutional Sikh demands, nor did those demands justify attacks on civilians. The immediate decision created a dilemma for historical judgment: a government may invoke the duty to restore security, but it remains obliged to account for the proportionality of force, protection of civilians, and the meaning of an assault on a major religious center. Section 94.17 reconstructs the operation and distinguishes official counts, independent claims, and lasting consequences.

Division V

Section 94.17

Operation Blue Star: The Golden Temple, Military Intervention, and Political Consequences

In early June 1984 the Indian government ordered a military assault on armed militants occupying positions in and around the Golden Temple complex in Amritsar. The operation, generally known as Operation Blue Star, took place in the midst of a constitutional dispute over autonomy and an expanding campaign of murders and intimidation. The authorities presented the action as necessary to restore law and order and remove heavily armed militants from a religious site. Critics questioned whether political negotiations, a longer containment strategy, or different operational choices might have avoided the scale of destruction and trauma. To examine those competing arguments, one must first reconstruct the sequence rather than treat the military decision as either self-evidently justified or self-evidently inexplicable.

The complex was one of Sikhism's holiest places and a functioning space of worship and pilgrimage. Its central sanctuary, the Harmandir Sahib, is distinct from the Akal Takht, the institution of Sikh temporal authority, and from surrounding buildings and courtyards. Armed followers of Bhindranwale and other militants had strengthened positions in and near the complex; retired military officer Shabeg Singh is often identified in accounts of defensive preparations. Pilgrims, religious staff, families, and other civilians also remained present. The government imposed curfews, communication restrictions, and other security controls. The timing near a major religious observance contributed to the risk that worshippers would be caught in the fighting.

The main assault unfolded during the first week of June, particularly the nights of 5–6 June, with clashes and follow-on operations beyond the central buildings. Army units encountered determined armed resistance, and heavy weapons were employed in fighting that seriously damaged the Akal Takht and other structures. Bhindranwale and several associates were killed. The sanctity of the site and the deaths of civilians transformed the operation's meaning: what the government described as an action against armed militants was experienced by many Sikhs as an assault on a sacred institution and on their community. These perceptions were neither uniform nor reducible to one political program.

Casualty accounting remains contested. Official statements counted several hundred deaths among militants and civilians and reported significant losses among security forces, while independent observers and Sikh organizations have questioned the completeness of those counts and offered much higher estimates. Restrictions on journalists and communications, uncertainty about those present, and the dispersal of evidence complicate later reconstruction. The chapter therefore should not give a single allegedly definitive civilian toll. It should distinguish military personnel, armed militants, pilgrims, and other civilian deaths wherever a source permits, and acknowledge that some lists aggregate categories that cannot be separated reliably.

Damage to the Akal Takht was materially and symbolically consequential. Photographs, government accounts, community testimony, and later restoration debates show that destruction of built heritage can deepen a political crisis even when a government asserts a security imperative. Reports of injuries to religious personnel, detention, and disruption of worship intensified alienation. The Army's actions at other gurdwaras during the broader operations also require distinction from the battle at Amritsar. A careful history does not assume every Sikh soldier, civil servant, or villager reacted alike; documented protests, desertions, grief, and dissent nonetheless revealed a widening rupture.

The immediate political effects included mistrust of the government, anger at leaders regarded as responsible for the operation, and intensified separatist sentiment among some groups. Yet a demand for accountability or protection of a shrine was not equivalent to endorsement of armed violence. Sikh communities around India and overseas expressed a range of responses from mourning and political protest to more radical demands. The operation also complicated relations among the Akali Dal, the SGPC, and militant networks. A settlement would eventually require renewed political negotiation as well as policing, because the underlying disagreements over territory, water, and constitutional powers had not vanished.

For the chapter's central argument, Operation Blue Star demonstrates the dangerous boundary between federal contestation and coercive central intervention. The state had legitimate obligations to protect civilians from militant violence, while also being accountable for its choice and application of force. Historical inquiry should evaluate the evidence on both duties. The June operation did not conclude Punjab's crisis. It was followed in October by the prime minister's assassination and, most tragically, by attacks on Sikh civilians who had no responsibility for the assassins' actions. Those events demand separate treatment rather than being folded into a narrative in which violence appears to follow automatically from grievance.

Section 94.18

The Assassination of Indira Gandhi, 31 October 1984

On the morning of 31 October 1984, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was shot at her residence in New Delhi by two members of her Sikh security detail, Beant Singh and Satwant Singh. The shooting followed the crisis created by Operation Blue Star and was widely understood in the context of anger over the military action at the Golden Temple. It was a deliberate act by identified individuals, not an act for which Sikh citizens collectively bore responsibility. That distinction is central to an accurate account because the hours and days that followed saw retaliatory language used to target people who had no connection to the murder.

Indira Gandhi had returned to office in 1980 after the collapse of the Janata government. Chapter 93 has already analyzed her political rise, the Emergency, and her return; the present section must stay within its designated purpose. Her assassination changed the immediate leadership of the Union government and triggered a crisis of public security amid the unresolved Punjab conflict. It is not necessary to retell her career or equate her leadership with every policy of the preceding two decades. An event so symbolically powerful can easily overshadow the structural history of agricultural change, caste politics, and federal bargaining that gives this chapter its wider meaning.

President Zail Singh returned to Delhi and Rajiv Gandhi, Indira's son and a Congress politician, was sworn in as prime minister later on 31 October. The rapid transition preserved formal continuity under the Constitution and reflected support among senior Congress figures for a single successor. Constitutional succession, however, did not guarantee the protection of civilians on the streets. Rumors and inflammatory narratives circulated, and groups gathered around public institutions and neighborhoods. The government's capacity to maintain order became an immediate test of whether electoral and administrative institutions could protect minority citizens in a moment of national shock.

The assassins' individual histories and the exact security arrangements at the residence have been the subjects of official and journalistic investigation. The important factual distinction is between the perpetrators and the broader Sikh population, among whom responses to the killing included condemnation, fear, grief, and demands for safety. Many Sikhs had opposed violence before and after June 1984; some had protested the Army operation without advocating assassination or separatism. The attempt to make a religious community answer for the actions of two guards inverted the principle of individual responsibility upon which law depends.

Public mourning and national anxiety were genuine. Indira Gandhi had been the dominant national political figure for much of the period since 1966, and her killing left uncertainty about leadership, the security services, Punjab policy, and future elections. Yet the speed of Rajiv Gandhi's succession also demonstrated that institutions could act decisively when political incentives aligned. The contrasting failure to intervene effectively against organized anti-Sikh attacks, examined in the next section, exposes a profound asymmetry between protecting the continuity of the central government and protecting citizens in vulnerable neighborhoods.

In the weeks after the assassination, Rajiv Gandhi's government moved toward a general election that would be held largely in December 1984, with delayed polling in some constituencies. Congress gained an overwhelming parliamentary victory amid sympathy for the assassinated leader, concern over stability, and the fragmented opposition. This result gave the new government strong electoral authority. It did not settle historical questions about the conduct of officials during the anti-Sikh violence or the root causes of Punjab's crisis. A large majority, as Chapter 93 showed for an earlier moment, is not equivalent to unlimited legitimacy for state action.

The assassination therefore occupies a pivotal but bounded place in Chapter 94. It did not begin the transformations explored in Divisions I–III, nor did it provide an explanation for earlier conflicts in Assam. It instead marked the moment when a prolonged federal and security dispute collided with the highest level of national government. The consequences were shaped not only by the fact of the killing but also by the responses of political leaders, police, party workers, journalists, and neighbors. Those choices made the difference between collective mourning and collective punishment.

Section 94.19

The Anti-Sikh Violence of 1984: Mass Killing, Displacement, Accountability, and Political Consequences

Following Indira Gandhi's assassination, Sikh men, women, and children were attacked in Delhi and other Indian cities. The most concentrated killing occurred from 1 to 4 November 1984. Organized mobs assaulted homes, shops, vehicles, and gurdwaras, killed civilians, and destroyed livelihoods. Survivors described attackers using electoral lists or local knowledge to identify Sikh households, and cases of sexual violence, displacement, and lasting trauma have been documented. The violence is frequently described as a pogrom because victims were selected primarily for perceived communal identity and attacks in numerous neighborhoods displayed coordinated features. This was not a battle between armed forces, and the victims must not be rhetorically associated with the two assassins.

Official tallies vary by investigation and geographical frame. The Ahuja Committee determined a Delhi death toll of 2,733, a figure later recorded by the Nanavati Commission. A Ministry of Home Affairs statement gave 3,325 deaths nationwide, including Delhi. These are not rival estimates of the same geographic population and should never be placed next to one another without their different coverage. Other survivor groups, journalists, and researchers reported additional missing people or higher estimates. A scholarly text can explain competing claims, but should not manufacture a consolidated number or treat unverified totals as equal to documented fatalities.

Delhi localities such as Trilokpuri, Sultanpuri, and parts of east Delhi became emblematic of the attacks. In some places, crowds were transported or armed with materials used to set buildings and people on fire. Families sought shelter in gurdwaras, schools, neighbors' homes, and relief camps. Non-Sikh neighbors and volunteers sometimes intervened to protect threatened households, while others joined violence or remained silent. These contrasted choices make clear that the events were not an inevitable reaction of an entire religious community to national tragedy. The violence was enabled by concrete decisions about organization, incitement, transport, and protection.

Police failures were a major issue in subsequent inquiries. Survivors reported refusal to register complaints, officers failing to disperse attackers, and delays in relief and investigation. These allegations varied by locality and require particular evidence, but the breadth of testimony and official findings rules out a simple account of isolated spontaneous rioting. The 2005 Justice Nanavati Commission examined affidavits and earlier inquiries, discussed the role of some local Congress politicians and police conduct, and assessed patterns of organized attack. It did not conclude that every Congress leader planned the violence; responsibility must be assigned to individuals and institutions according to evidence, not party membership alone.

The human consequences extended far beyond the days of killing. Widows and children lost income, survivors were relocated, businesses were destroyed, and many families struggled for compensation or recognition of deaths. Fear and humiliation altered relations with neighbors and with state institutions. Women's testimony is especially important because sexual violence and the costs of supporting displaced families have often received less attention than male death counts. The experience of surviving a pogrom cannot be reduced to a statistic, although carefully sourced statistics are necessary to describe scale and assess official performance.

Accountability unfolded slowly. The Ranganath Misra Commission and other inquiries were followed by the Nanavati Commission, later criminal cases, and continuing campaigns by survivors' organizations and human-rights advocates. Different proceedings addressed distinct questions: the cause of violence, administrative responsibility, criminal culpability, and compensation. Historians should distinguish findings of an inquiry from convictions in a court, allegations from established facts, and failures to prosecute from findings of innocence. Subsequent developments beyond 1991 may be noted in source annotations but should not dominate this chapter's chronological account.

The immediate political settlement was paradoxical. Congress won a huge majority in the December 1984 parliamentary elections, and Rajiv Gandhi pursued new policies while seeking to negotiate the Punjab dispute. The Rajiv–Longowal Accord of July 1985 attempted to address Chandigarh, territorial issues, and other grievances, but implementation faltered and Longowal was assassinated the following month. Electoral victory did not mend the breach created by the mass violence or end militancy. The crisis illustrates a basic tension: a state can conduct elections and maintain a constitutional executive while failing disastrously to secure equal protection for a minority.

The events of November 1984 must be narrated plainly as violence against Sikh civilians, with accountability and loss at the center. The larger historical lesson connects this section to Chapters 93 and 94: democratic forms do not automatically prevent coercion, whether under emergency law or through the breakdown of ordinary policing. The meaningful measure of citizenship is not simply the right to vote but whether officials protect people when powerful groups target them. Division V concludes by examining how those failures coexisted with the broadening of electoral participation in a transformed political system.

Section 94.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Agricultural Change, Caste Assertion, Federalism, and the Transformation of Indian Politics

Between the 1960s and 1991, India experienced transformations that cannot be explained by the political biography of any single prime minister. New seeds, irrigation, and public purchasing systems helped transform food production, while rising education, migration, caste-based organizing, and regional party competition altered the social foundations of authority. A state able to build grain stocks and administer mass elections was also a state in which citizens fought over land, public employment, language, and the meaning of federal autonomy. The chapter has shown that these developments were connected, though not reducible to one linear modernization story.

The Green Revolution demonstrates how scientific change depends on institutions and distribution. Indian and international agricultural research supplied improved plant varieties, but canals, electric pumps, fertilizer, credit, roads, assured prices, and state procurement helped determine where they succeeded. Grain output grew substantially, easing dependence on large food-aid shipments. Yet farmers in less-irrigated areas faced different opportunities, while tenants, laborers, and women often had less control over capital and land. Increased aggregate production did not automatically ensure household nutrition. Moreover, intensive irrigation and chemical inputs created longer-term environmental pressures, some of which became more apparent after the initial productivity gains.

Agricultural growth also generated political constituencies. Prosperous cultivating groups demanded favorable prices, electricity, roads, and recognition. Farmers' movements organized outside conventional party channels, and electoral leaders used the language of rural interests to challenge city-centered policy. These developments were not simply a revolt of the poor: some relatively advantaged cultivators became influential while landless workers sought wages and dignity. Economic change altered the stakes of caste and class conflict rather than abolishing them. The location of power within a village mattered for who could capture public benefits and who had to negotiate for access.

Other Backward Classes mobilization and the Mandal controversy made this shift visible at the national level. The state developed categories intended to remedy social and educational disadvantage, and movements demanded a greater share of public employment and political representation. The 1990 decision to implement central reservations for OBCs was not the creation of caste politics from nothing; it built on state-level policies, earlier commissions, and the growing power of leaders who organized communities excluded from upper-level administration. The conflict over reservations exposed disagreement about merit, historical deprivation, and the legitimate purposes of government, but no national category erased the diversity of its constituent groups.

Dalit political movements likewise broadened the repertoire of democratic action. From Ambedkar's constitutional and organizational legacy to the Dalit Panthers' cultural militancy and Kanshi Ram's long-term electoral strategy, organizers challenged both overt discrimination and the dependence of marginalized voters on parties led by others. These strategies produced different results and internal disagreements. The growth of the BSP did not end caste violence or guarantee equal access to land and schooling, but it demonstrated that independent political organization could change bargaining power. Democracy deepened insofar as people previously treated chiefly as dependents could claim office and shape public priorities.

Regional parties made the same point from a different direction. The DMK, AIADMK, Telugu Desam, Assam Gana Parishad, and other organizations showed that voters could pursue linguistic, cultural, and regional interests through constitutional elections. Their goals differed, and regionalism should never be equated with separatism. Yet the strong powers of the Union, including Article 356 and the appointment of governors, generated repeated tensions when state governments challenged the center. By 1989, the National Front's dependence on multiple parties and outside supporters showed how far national politics had moved from Congress's earlier dominance. Competition became broader without necessarily becoming easier to govern.

Assam exposes the danger of political categorization when law and social identity are confused. A movement could legitimately seek accurate electoral rolls and recognition of regional concerns, while still producing intimidation and catastrophic attacks on civilians. Nellie in 1983 must be understood as a massacre, not an administrative by-product of a policy dispute. The 1985 Accord represented a negotiated attempt to distinguish legal categories and provide safeguards, but its existence did not restore the lives lost or instantly resolve the challenges of documentation, migration, and belonging. Constitutional agreements require fair implementation and protection for minorities if they are to serve democratic ends.

Punjab reveals another path from regional grievance to violence. The Akali Dal's demands over federal authority, Chandigarh, and river waters emerged within India's constitutional politics, but prolonged confrontation unfolded alongside armed militancy, targeted killings, escalating security measures, and grave distrust. Operation Blue Star in June 1984 and its damage to a sacred complex deepened that distrust. Indira Gandhi's assassination on 31 October was the act of identified individuals, not of the Sikh community. The ensuing organized anti-Sikh attacks showed how destructive collective blame becomes when police and political authorities fail to protect citizens.

The two crises should be compared, not conflated. Assam raised specific questions about migration, citizenship documentation, and electoral representation; Punjab involved autonomy, resources, religious institutions, and militant violence. Both highlighted the limits of a federal state that sometimes answered regional demands through central coercion. Both also demonstrated the power of negotiation: the Assam Accord and the Rajiv–Longowal Accord were efforts to restore political avenues. The mere signing of an agreement could not substitute for public trust, administrative competence, protection of civilians, or sustained commitment by participants.

The chapter's central answer is therefore deliberately double. India's democracy became more representative and competitive as cultivators, OBCs, Dalits, and regional communities created new political organizations. Simultaneously, competition for scarce resources, unequal institutions, and the actions of states and militants could generate coercion and mass violence. These were not interchangeable outcomes, and progress in one field did not excuse abuses in another. By 1991 India was neither the unchallenged Congress system of the 1950s nor a failed democracy. It was an increasingly plural political order facing the challenge of converting mobilization into equal rights, accountable institutions, and nonviolent federal bargaining.

Chapter Summary

By the early 1970s the Indian government had substantially strengthened its ability to produce and procure grain. The Green Revolution combined scientific research, high-yielding varieties, irrigation, energy, fertilizer, and a network of public incentives. Productivity growth was significant, particularly in the northwest, but regional disparities, unequal access to land and capital, and environmental pressures also became defining features of agricultural change. Greater grain output did not mean that every household was nutritionally secure.

Economic change reshaped rural power. Prosperous cultivators gained political leverage through cooperatives and farmers’ associations, while agricultural laborers and tenants pursued different interests. The rise of OBC politics, the 1980 Mandal Commission report, and the 1990 reservation decision grew from earlier efforts to remedy social disadvantage. Dalit activists pursued cultural resistance, legal reform, independent association, and electoral organization; the Dalit Panthers, BAMCEF, DS4, and the BSP illustrate contrasting strategies. These movements altered the basis of political representation without removing discrimination.

Regional parties grew within the Indian federal order. Their expansion was not synonymous with separatism: many demanded more equitable resources, recognition of language, and state autonomy while seeking office through elections. Yet federal contestation sometimes coincided with communal and security crises. The Assam Movement raised questions about migration and electoral rolls, and the 1983 Nellie massacre exposed catastrophic failure to protect civilians. The 1985 Assam Accord addressed specific legal dates and regional safeguards, but did not erase those losses.

Punjab’s dispute over autonomy, territory, and resources escalated amid armed attacks and countermeasures. Operation Blue Star in June 1984 damaged an important Sikh sacred complex and deepened political alienation. Indira Gandhi was assassinated in October by two security personnel; the organized anti-Sikh violence that followed targeted civilians as a community and involved severe failures of policing and accountability. These events reveal that a state can maintain elections and nevertheless fail in its core obligation to protect equal citizenship.

The final political transformation was a more competitive party system. Congress remained an important national force but lost its majority in 1989, as regional, caste-based, and national opposition organizations reshaped coalition possibilities. The chapter’s principal conclusion is not that modernization necessarily created either democratic improvement or violence: it created new resources, constituencies, and political stakes, while the consequences depended upon institutions, decisions, and the protection or violation of rights.

Key Terms and Definitions

Green Revolution
A technologically and institutionally supported expansion of crop yields, especially wheat and rice, beginning in the 1960s.
High-yielding variety (HYV)
Crop variety responsive to fertilizer and water under appropriate growing conditions.
Minimum support price
Government-announced procurement price meant to reduce marketing uncertainty for certain crops.
Procurement
Purchase of crops by public agencies for distribution or buffer stocks.
Public Distribution System
Network distributing subsidized food staples through designated outlets.
Operational holding
Land cultivated as a single management unit, whether or not the operator owns it.
Tenant
Cultivator using land held under an arrangement with an owner.
Agricultural laborer
Person employed for wages or other compensation to work on farms.
Groundwater depletion
Withdrawal of groundwater faster than replenishment in a given location.
Dominant caste
Locally influential caste group whose land, numbers, or political leverage gives it social power.
Kisan politics
Organized advocacy and electoral mobilization conducted in the name of cultivators and farmers.
Other Backward Classes (OBCs)
Administrative category of socially and educationally disadvantaged communities, distinct from Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
Scheduled Caste
Constitutionally notified category of communities historically subjected to caste exclusion, including many identifying as Dalit.
Scheduled Tribe
Constitutionally notified category of tribal communities; not interchangeable with all uses of Adivasi identity.
Reservation
Policy setting aside specified representation or opportunities for eligible groups.
Creamy layer
Later administrative concept excluding more socially advanced members of OBC groups from certain benefits; distinguish its post-1990 legal development.
Dalit
Self-affirming identity used by many communities historically targeted by caste exclusion.
Bahujan
Political idea of a numerical majority composed of multiple marginalized communities.
Regional party
Political party whose electoral and organizational base is concentrated in a state or region.
Federalism
Constitutional distribution of authority between Union and state governments.
Article 356
Provision authorizing President’s Rule when constitutional government in a state is deemed to have failed.
President’s Rule
Union administration of a state under Article 356 following a constitutional proclamation.
Coalition
Cooperation among multiple parties to form or support a government.
Electoral roll
Official list of registered voters for an election.
Citizenship
Legal membership in a political community, to be established under governing law rather than inferred from communal identity.
Anandpur Sahib Resolution
Akali political program on autonomy and other demands, articulated in 1973 and reformulated or endorsed in 1978.
Dharam Yudh Morcha
Akali protest campaign begun in 1982 in the Punjab political crisis.
Militancy
Use or threat of organized armed force to advance political aims, distinct from peaceful political mobilization.
Pogrom
Mass attack directed at a particular communal group, often involving coordination and failures of protection.
Judicial commission
Official investigatory body collecting evidence and reporting findings; it does not itself issue criminal convictions.
Assam Accord
Memorandum of Settlement signed on 15 August 1985 addressing migration, electoral rolls, and regional safeguards.
AASU
All Assam Students’ Union, a leading organization during the 1979–1985 Assam Movement.
Operation Blue Star
June 1984 Indian Army operation against armed militants at the Golden Temple complex and other sites.
Akal Takht
Historic institution of Sikh temporal authority in the Golden Temple complex, badly damaged in June 1984.
Nanavati Commission
Commission of inquiry whose 2005 report investigated the 1984 anti-Sikh violence.

Chapter Reflection

Modernization changed the material conditions of India's democracy but did not dictate its moral direction. Grain harvests rose and more citizens entered public life as organized claimants rather than silent beneficiaries. The institutions that enabled these changes—public investment, electoral law, and federal government—could create opportunity and also preserve unequal access to influence.

The history of Assam and Punjab requires especially careful language. It is possible to take regional demands seriously without excusing attacks on civilians. It is possible to analyze the state’s responsibility to maintain security while holding public authorities accountable for excessive force or failures to protect minority citizens. Neither mass mobilization nor a parliamentary mandate suspends those obligations.

Chapter 94 closes as India approaches the 1990s with a more plural party system and unresolved questions about social justice, regional authority, and equal citizenship. Its final question is not simply who won power, but whose rights and physical safety political institutions were able—or unable—to secure when competition intensified.
