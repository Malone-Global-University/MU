The Birth of Organized Indian Nationalism

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On December 28, 1885, seventy-two delegates gathered at the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College in Bombay for the first session of a body that called itself the Indian National Congress. Lawyers, journalists, teachers, and public figures had travelled by rail from Bengal, Madras, the Bombay Presidency, and northern India. Their meeting lasted only a few days and passed a modest set of resolutions. Yet it began an institution that would meet every year, claim to articulate common political interests across a subcontinent not previously represented through a national association of this form, and eventually lead the movement that ended British rule.

The meeting had not been planned for Bombay. It was first arranged for Poona, and an outbreak of disease there led the organizers to move it. That detail is a useful warning against reading the founding as a grand, preordained event. The Congress was assembled by people who were drawing on associations, newspapers, and personal networks that had been growing for more than a decade, and its first session was an act of coordination as much as of creation.

This chapter asks how educated Indian political leaders turned regional grievances into an organized nationalist movement, and what the early Congress achieved and failed to achieve through constitutional agitation. It rejects two common oversimplifications. The first is that Indian nationalism began suddenly in 1885. The second is that the early Congress was politically insignificant because it favored petitions, resolutions, and appeals to British liberal principles over confrontation.

The principal period is 1870 to 1905, with necessary background from 1858 onward. Chapters 71 to 75 examined the structures of Crown rule, the colonial economy, the classification of society, reform movements, and the growth of cities and labor. Those developments created a class of English-educated professionals, a communications network of railways, telegraphs, and presses, and a set of economic and racial grievances. Chapter 76 shows how these conditions became organized political opposition.

The early leaders were not seeking independence in the later sense. Most demanded representation, administrative equality, financial accountability, and the fulfillment of what they regarded as British promises. They were nonetheless sharply critical of the foundations of colonial government. Dadabhai Naoroji and Romesh Chunder Dutt built a systematic economic critique of imperial finance that remained central to nationalist argument for decades, and the constitutional campaigns of these years established the arguments that later movements would extend or challenge.

The evidence for this chapter comes from Congress reports and presidential addresses, the writings of Naoroji and Dutt, the Indian Councils Act of 1892, parliamentary debates, newspapers, and the correspondence of officials. Each has limitations. Congress records show what leaders chose to say publicly, colonial records often reflect official hostility or condescension, and few sources record how the vast rural majority regarded these politics. The chapter treats these gaps as part of the history rather than silences to be filled with assumption.

The early Congress developed within an empire where policy could be debated publicly but effective executive control remained beyond Indian voters. Throughout the chapter, a distinction is maintained among a proposal made by political associations, a vote cast in the British Parliament, a rule adopted by colonial administrators, and a change actually experienced by people governed under that rule. This distinction is essential for measuring the practical effects of nineteenth-century constitutional nationalism.

The chapter also connects to the larger series. Chapter 77 turns to the Swadeshi movement, the diversification of nationalist politics, and the founding of the Muslim League. Chapter 76 is the threshold: it explains the institutions and ideas that the next generation inherited, and why a growing number of them concluded those institutions were not enough.

Historical Context

The rebellion of 1857 ended Company rule. Under the Government of India Act of 1858 and Queen Victoria's Proclamation, Crown rule promised equal treatment and respect for Indian customs and religions, and the new structure of Secretary of State, Viceroy, and Indian Civil Service is the subject of Chapter 71. The promises of the Proclamation were broad and the practice narrower, and educated Indians would repeatedly cite the distance between them.

Meanwhile, the universities of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, founded in 1857, began producing graduates who entered law, teaching, journalism, and the lower ranks of government service. The railways, telegraph, and postal system, discussed in Chapter 72, allowed these people to meet and correspond across regions. Printing presses multiplied newspapers in English and in Indian languages, a development introduced in Chapter 73. Cities such as Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, and Poona gathered professionals into a new public, and the legal profession in particular trained people in argument, procedure, and the language of rights.

Grievances accumulated alongside these capacities. The reservation of senior posts for Europeans, the lowering of the entrance age for the Indian Civil Service in 1878, the Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act of the same year, the costs of Afghan wars, and famine in the 1870s all gave concrete material to criticism. The Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883 revealed to many Indians how strongly European residents resisted equality before the law. Political life before 1885 was therefore not empty. What it lacked was durable national coordination, and the chapter traces how that was built.

Division I

The Emergence of Organized Indian Politics

Division I · Section 76.1

Political Awakening after 1857: The Origins of Organized Nationalism

Organized nationalism did not grow directly out of the rebellion of 1857. The rebellion was suppressed, many of its leading military and court networks were disrupted, and its aftermath produced a cautious and often suspicious colonial state. The new generation of political actors came instead from a different source: the institutions that Crown rule expanded in the decades that followed. Government schools, colleges, and universities taught English, British history, and constitutional ideas to a small but growing number of students. Many of these graduates entered the professions of law, journalism, teaching, and clerical service, and they formed a public with shared experiences and shared grievances.

Three features of their situation mattered. First, they were educated in a political vocabulary that included liberty, representation, and equal treatment before the law, and they could see how far colonial practice fell short. Second, the colonial state was racially stratified. Senior positions were largely reserved for Europeans, Indians in government service were often paid less and promoted more slowly, and European residents had legal privileges that Indians did not. Third, new communications allowed those with similar complaints to discover each other. A lawyer in Madras could read a Calcutta newspaper, and a delegate could cross the subcontinent by rail.

It is important to distinguish these early political attitudes from the demand for independence. Most public figures before 1900 did not ask for the end of British rule. They argued for a fuller admission of Indians to government, for control of expenditure, and for the application of the principles Britain itself professed. Some were already deeply critical of the economic effects of empire, but their remedies lay inside the imperial framework. Treating them as failed revolutionaries, or as loyalists with no critical edge, misses what was distinctive about their politics.

The educated public was also small and unevenly distributed. Literacy in English reached only a tiny fraction of the population, concentrated in major cities and in particular castes and communities, notably Brahmans, Kayasthas, Parsis, and certain Muslim and trading groups, with strong regional differences. That limited base shaped both the achievements and the weaknesses of what followed.

The geography of this political public deserves attention. Institutions created during Company rule and enlarged under the Crown did not distribute educational opportunities evenly. A student graduating from the University of Calcutta could enter a world of courts, newspapers, municipal appointments, and voluntary associations that had no close equivalent in many agricultural districts. In Bombay, networks linking merchants, lawyers, and municipal politics gave public argument an institutional home; in Madras, colleges and the press encouraged cooperation among lawyers and teachers. Connections across the presidency towns mattered, but the countryside was far from silent. Cultivators brought grievances about assessments and indebtedness to officials and intermediaries, and local protests could force elites to confront problems beyond their professional experience. The challenge for political leaders was converting very different claims into language they believed an imperial government would hear.

The Crown's public promises created an unusual political opening. Queen Victoria's 1858 proclamation referred to impartial treatment in public employment and to respect for Indian subjects' rights. Lawyers and journalists could cite those principles without repudiating the legitimacy of the Crown. This made the imperial government's own vocabulary a resource for criticism. Yet the practical relationship between an Indian petitioner and an executive officer remained unequal: no national electorate could dismiss a viceroy or secretary of state. The gap between the language of rights and the institutions of rule is more revealing than either the proclamations or the discrimination considered in isolation. It explains why leaders developed a politics of public evidence, constitutional analogy, and repeated petitions.

There were also inheritances from earlier resistance and reform. The rebellion of 1857, peasant mobilizations, religious debates, and campaigns over education belonged to different political traditions and should not be collapsed into a continuous nationalist march. Nevertheless, public arguments about discrimination and economic distress did not occur in a vacuum. Reform associations had already taught members to hold meetings, compose rules, solicit subscriptions, and correspond with newspapers. Municipal controversies accustomed some professionals to elections and the scrutiny of accounts. The novel development from the 1870s was not merely stronger indignation; it was a capacity to maintain organizations across provincial boundaries and to claim that similar grievances called for common remedies.

Cause and Effect

From Social and Economic Change to Organized Nationalist Demands

Long-term causes

Expansion of English-language education, professional employment, railways, telegraphs, and printing after 1858; racial hierarchy in government and law; heavy and rising fiscal burdens; recurrent famine.

Short-term causes

The measures of 1878; famine and war expenditure; the Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883; the imprisonment of Surendranath Banerjea in 1883; Lord Ripon's departure and the failure of reform.

Transformation

Presidency associations coordinated their campaigns, held national conferences, and in December 1885 convened the first Congress session.

Immediate effects

An annual forum, a recurring program of resolutions, networks of delegates, and a London committee.

Long-term consequences and complexity: The institution outlasted its first generation and became the vehicle of later mass movements. No single cause explains it: grievances alone did not create organization, and education alone did not create opposition. The combination of capacity, grievance, and leaders willing to coordinate produced the change.

Section 76.2

Regional Political Associations before the Indian National Congress

Political associations existed in India before 1870, notably in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, but many were dominated by landholders and wealthy merchants and concentrated on petitions concerning property, taxation, and the renewal of the East India Company's charter. The associations that emerged from 1870 differed because professionals and the educated middle classes were prominent in them, and their concerns extended to representation, the civil service, and public finance.

The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, founded in 1870, is the clearest early example. Associated with M. G. Ranade and Ganesh Vasudeo Joshi, it sought to represent the public of the Poona region and the Deccan in relations with government. It collected information on the condition of cultivators, submitted memorials, and organized public discussion of revenue policy and famine. Its structure of subscribing members and elected officers gave it a more public character than earlier bodies, and it was a model for others.

The Indian Association was founded in Calcutta in 1876, with Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose among its leaders. Banerjea passed the Indian Civil Service examination after a successful appeal over an age-related eligibility dispute, served as a magistrate, and was dismissed in 1873 following a separate alleged administrative irregularity. He subsequently became a teacher and powerful public speaker. The Association built branches in towns across Bengal and into northern India, held large meetings, and took up the issues of the civil-service entry age and of Indian representation. Its connections to the press, particularly through Banerjea's newspaper The Bengalee, made it effective at publicity.

In Madras, the Madras Mahajana Sabha was established in 1884, bringing together lawyers, journalists, and public figures from the presidency, with strong links to the newspaper The Hindu. In Bombay, the Bombay Presidency Association was founded in 1885 by Pherozeshah Mehta, K. T. Telang, and Badruddin Tyabji, whose members included prominent Parsi, Hindu, and Muslim lawyers and civic leaders. Mehta in particular was prominent in the municipal government of Bombay and brought a tradition of civic administration into politics.

These organizations differed in membership, finance, and style. Poona's was closely tied to an intellectual tradition of economic analysis; Calcutta's was oriented to mass meetings and agitation; Bombay's to law and municipal politics; Madras's to the press and to the provincial legal profession. They did, however, share an approach: they drew on subscriptions and volunteer labor, wrote memorials, cultivated newspapers, and addressed government through legal and constitutional channels. Their existence meant that when coordination became possible, there was an organizational structure ready to use.

Earlier bodies provide a useful comparison. The British Indian Association in Bengal, founded in 1851, emerged from a milieu of prominent landholders and had considerable experience in memorializing officials. The Bombay Association and Madras Native Association also petitioned the government in the early 1850s. These precedents contradict any claim that political association began in 1870, but they did not create the same expanding interregional machinery that developed later. Property-owning patrons could fund political action and secure access to officials, while their dependence on established interests restricted the range of grievances they emphasized. The important analytical shift was toward organizations whose spokespeople increasingly came from the legal profession, universities, and the newspaper world, although these too were socially selective.

The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha claimed to speak in the name of the public, but that claim requires examination. Its membership and its representative arrangements gave it a recognizable collective character without making it a democratic assembly of the Deccan's inhabitants. Ganesh Vasudeo Joshi and colleagues sought information about revenue assessments and rural distress and addressed memorials to higher authority. Their investigations during the difficult years of the 1870s show how an association might translate conditions in farming districts into arguments about imperial administration. Ranade's influence encouraged a political economy that linked fiscal policy, industry, and the development of Indian resources. This was a particular regional tradition, not an identical model copied everywhere.

The Indian Association brought a different strength: circulation. Surendranath Banerjea's speaking tours and the Association's campaigns against restrictions on civil-service access helped connect educated audiences in provincial towns with the Calcutta public. Banerjea's own career must be reported precisely. An age-related objection to his original civil-service candidacy was decided in his favor; he entered service, then was dismissed in 1873 following a separate alleged administrative irregularity. His later standing as a critic of racial inequality arose from this history but cannot be reduced to a failed age application. Ananda Mohan Bose's leadership likewise illustrates the importance of educational and civic networks rather than a politics built around one charismatic speaker.

The four prominent associations also reveal limitations in the adjective 'national.' Their principal correspondence passed among urban elites, often in English, and the needs of cultivating and wage-working households reached them indirectly. Regional allegiances were real; Poona, Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay had different legal cultures and social coalitions. National coordination required negotiation among these existing institutions, not their disappearance into a uniform organization. That tension—between the prestige of provincial leaders and the ambition to articulate common demands—remained visible in Congress annual sessions after 1885.

Section 76.3

Newspapers, Public Meetings, Political Campaigns, and the Indian National Conference

The associations learned to coordinate by campaigning. In 1878, Lord Lytton's government passed the Vernacular Press Act, restricting newspapers in Indian languages, and the Arms Act, regulating the possession of weapons in ways that discriminated between Europeans and Indians. Both provoked protests, and the Vernacular Press Act was repealed in 1882 under Lord Ripon. A decision made in 1876 to lower the maximum age for entry to the Indian Civil Service, reflected in the examination arrangements of 1878, made it harder for Indian candidates to travel to Britain and compete. The Indian Association took a leading part in the resulting campaign.

The Ilbert Bill of 1883 was a more consequential episode. The bill proposed to allow Indian judges and magistrates in the districts to try European defendants. European residents, particularly in Bengal, organized a vigorous opposition, and the government compromised by providing for trial by jury with a substantial European component. Many educated Indians drew a lesson from both the opposition and the compromise: that equality before the law could be contested by an organized racial interest, and that Indians needed organization of their own. The same year, Surendranath Banerjea was imprisoned for contempt of court after a newspaper comment on a judge, and the response to his imprisonment showed how widely a public cause could be taken up in cities across the north and west.

In December 1883, the Indian Association organized the first Indian National Conference in Calcutta, attended by delegates from various parts of Bengal and from other regions. A second Conference was held in Calcutta in December 1885, coinciding with the Congress session in Bombay. It is important to distinguish these Conferences from the Congress. They were organized by one association, with Bengal as their principal base, and they demonstrated that interregional gatherings were practicable. The Congress, in contrast, drew its first delegates from several presidencies and was organized by a coalition. By 1886 the two efforts converged and the Conference merged into the Congress.

The central development was therefore the creation of interregional political coordination before December 1885. The methods of the Congress, such as public meetings, memorials, fundraising, and newspaper publicity, were not invented in Bombay in 1885. They were adapted from associations that had spent a decade learning them.

Political journalism was an institution as well as a medium. A newspaper needed capital, editors, compositors, distribution arrangements, subscribers, and relationships with local correspondents. Its opinions circulated through reading rooms, public recitations, and conversations among people who did not purchase papers themselves. English facilitated communication between educated people from different regions, but Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu, and other languages helped make complaints intelligible within local political worlds. Translation was therefore a political process. An argument about representation might be expressed to a British audience as equality under law and to a provincial audience as protection against arbitrary officials. Neither formulation necessarily exhausted what participants thought.

The civil-service agitation illustrates how a seemingly technical matter became public politics. The decision in 1876 to lower the maximum entry age, reflected in examination arrangements from 1878, sharpened the disadvantage of candidates required to travel from India to Britain while still young. Associations organized meetings and memorials because the issue joined questions of education, public employment, racial access, and the credibility of British assurances. Disagreement about eligibility rules became an argument about who should administer the country. It also gave organizers practical experience in collecting signatures, coordinating provincial statements, and maintaining pressure over several years. Later Congress demands for simultaneous examinations inherited this repertoire.

The Ilbert Bill controversy exposed different limits. European protests against the possibility of certain Indian judges exercising criminal jurisdiction over European British subjects invoked fears about racial status and alleged threats to European women. The compromise revealed that formal legal qualifications did not settle questions of social hierarchy. At the same time, Indian mobilization against European opposition was not proof that all Indians shared one agenda. Newspapers and public meetings were dominated by those with access to education, money, and urban networks; other constituencies could view the contest through different priorities. The 1883 National Conference should be understood as the culmination of this organizational learning, not merely as a dress rehearsal for a more famous meeting two years later.

Section 76.4

The Founding of the Indian National Congress, December 1885

The immediate organizer of the first Congress was Allan Octavian Hume, a retired civil servant who had left the Indian Civil Service in 1882 and become convinced that Indians needed a national political organization. In 1883 he addressed an open letter to the graduates of Calcutta University, urging them to organize. He formed an Indian National Union in 1884 and, working with leaders in Bombay and Madras, planned a national meeting at Poona for December 1885. He also consulted with the Viceroy, Lord Dufferin, who reportedly approved the idea of a gathering that would air Indian opinion but wished it not to be primarily political, an element that later fed controversy.

The meeting was transferred from Poona to Bombay because of an outbreak of disease in Poona, and the first session opened on December 28, 1885, at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College with 72 delegates. W. C. Bonnerjee presided. Delegates included leaders of the Bombay Presidency Association such as Mehta and Telang, Dadabhai Naoroji, and representatives from Madras and elsewhere, along with a small number of Europeans. The second session followed in Calcutta in December 1886.

The organizers had a clear division of roles. Hume gave energy and contacts, but the regional leaders, who already commanded followings and had a record of political work, gave the movement its substance and legitimacy. The founding is best understood as a coalition in which an experienced British sympathizer cooperated with Indian leaders who had their own plans and their own organizations.

Historians have long debated why the founders did what they did. One account, associated with a later portrayal of Hume's conversations with Dufferin, holds that Congress was conceived as a "safety valve," a means of letting off political steam that might otherwise have erupted in violence, and that the Viceroy approved it on that basis. Critics argue that this account minimizes Indian agency, and that the evidence for the safety-valve motive is thin and retrospective. Indian leaders pursued their own objectives, and the institution that emerged was shaped by them. This chapter treats the safety-valve explanation as an interpretation to be weighed, not as established fact, and does not present Hume as the sole creator of Indian nationalism (see the Historical Debate in Section 76.19).

The setting of the first meeting makes the Congress's initial scale visible. Its delegates were not elected by universal suffrage, and there was no nationwide party organization capable of binding their home districts. They came because political associations and personal networks invited or sponsored them, and because they had the resources and social standing to travel. The choice of Bombay after reports of cholera in Poona was a practical response to danger, not evidence that the meeting lacked political importance. The Congress's institutional innovation was to make a gathering of people from multiple presidencies recurring, public, and programmatic. The act of claiming to deliberate on 'Indian' questions across provincial divisions was itself consequential.

The first session must be studied through its proceedings, not through later patriotic retellings. Its resolutions addressed a commission to investigate administration, wider participation in legislative councils, civil-service examinations in India as well as Britain, and the burden of military spending. These were concrete reforms, rather than a demand for immediate independence. A participant could profess fidelity to the Crown and still consider the colonial allocation of resources profoundly unjust. To some observers, this looked like prudent constitutionalism. To others, it revealed dependence on the goodwill of the very officials whose actions were being criticized. Both reactions belong to the movement's early history.

The 'safety-valve' account is a good exercise in historical method. Some later commentators interpreted Hume's motives as an attempt to provide a peaceful outlet for discontent; the evidence for a single official design imposed by Dufferin is insufficient for treating that explanation as established. It is also possible for an individual British supporter to have hoped for stability while Indian colleagues pursued representation and accountability. Motives need not be identical for political cooperation to occur. The more important question is institutional agency: who organized local constituencies, framed resolutions, raised funds, edited reports, and sustained sessions when the initial novelty passed? Indian associations performed these tasks, making a simple founder-centered explanation misleading.

Division II

Congress Organization and Moderate Political Leadership

Division II · Section 76.5

Congress Annual Sessions, Resolutions, and National Organization

The most important decision of the early Congress was to meet again. By holding annual sessions in different cities, the organization acquired a rhythm and a claim on attention. After Bombay in 1885 and Calcutta in 1886 came Madras in 1887, Allahabad in 1888, and Bombay again in 1889. Each session was hosted by a local reception committee, which raised funds, arranged accommodation, and assembled a delegation. The host city's elites thereby took a stake in the institution, and the movement of sessions across regions made it harder for any presidency to treat Congress as foreign.

Participation grew. Commonly cited figures place delegate numbers at several hundred by 1886 and well over a thousand by 1888, though counts are imperfect because delegates were chosen informally. Selection was by public meetings and local associations rather than by any systematic electoral process, and the organization had no legal power to compel any government action. Its authority was moral and political, derived from the claim to articulate common demands.

The characteristic product of a session was a set of resolutions, drafted in committee, debated, and passed. Presidential addresses set the tone, and reports were published and circulated. Over time, resolutions on repeated themes, such as expansion of legislative councils, Indian entry to the civil service, reduction of military expenditure, and equal legal treatment, gave the Congress a recurring program. Because the same demands returned year after year, they served as a standard against which the government's responses could be measured.

Holding sessions across regions with different languages, religions, and political traditions was itself an achievement. Debate was in English, which allowed delegates from different regions to communicate but excluded those who did not command it. The sessions were occasions for exchanging information, building friendships, and learning how issues looked in other provinces, so the institution was a school of national politics as well as a forum for demands.

Delegate counts illuminate both expansion and its limits. The first session's 72 participants can be compared with the hundreds who attended Calcutta in 1886 and the 1,248 delegates reported for the Allahabad meeting of 1888. Figures taken from session reports should always be identified as delegate counts, not population-wide votes or independently verified membership totals. The official Congress retrospective gives 434 for 1886, while some later compilations give 436; the discrepancy is small but instructive. What can be established without pretending to perfect precision is that the annual meeting rapidly acquired a larger constituency among the educated political public. The number of those capable of addressing delegates directly was much smaller than the number affected by the issues under discussion.

Local reception committees were vital institutions. A meeting in a new city required halls, transport, accommodation, hospitality, finances, police arrangements, printers, and a program acceptable to participants from distant provinces. Those tasks brought together regional political associations and local benefactors who might disagree on social or religious questions. Presidential addresses were especially important because a prominent public figure could offer a national interpretation of provincial concerns. Reports and printed resolutions carried the meeting back into newspapers and district associations. This infrastructure made an annual Congress possible even before a permanent professional secretariat developed on a scale associated with twentieth-century political parties.

The resolutions' repeated subject matter has sometimes been characterized as evidence of futility: a demand appears year after year because the government refuses it. That reading overlooks the educational function of repetition. The demand for simultaneous civil-service examinations, for example, kept a recognizable constitutional issue before distinct annual audiences and allowed organizers to compare ministerial promises with results. Repeated budget and council resolutions also established a common vocabulary for evaluating government practice. Their weakness was equally real. The Congress could not enforce its wishes, and a carefully worded resolution could leave a village household no better protected against coercion or rising taxation.

The institution also created arguments over procedure. Who was entitled to represent a town or province, and who was prepared to finance that representation? Would Congress take positions on socially divisive reform issues or restrict itself to demands on which delegates could agree? Should the organization cultivate British allies even if doing so required rhetorical restraint? Such choices were not merely technical: they shaped the movement's membership, credibility, and future divisions. The history of Congress administration therefore belongs at the center of a chapter on political nationalism, not in an appendix to the careers of famous leaders.

Section 76.6

The Moderate Leaders: Bonnerjee, Mehta, Tyabji, Ranade, and Gokhale

The leaders of the early Congress are often called Moderates, a label applied later in contrast with the more assertive critics who emerged by the 1890s. The term needs care. It describes a preference for constitutional methods and gradual reform, not an absence of conviction.

W. C. Bonnerjee, a Calcutta barrister, was the first president and presided again in 1892. Pherozeshah Mehta, a Bombay lawyer, was a dominant figure in the Bombay Presidency and the Bombay Municipal Corporation, known for forceful argument and a commanding presence in Congress councils. Badruddin Tyabji, also a Bombay lawyer, became the first Muslim president of Congress in 1887. M. G. Ranade, a judge in the Bombay judicial service, was not formally a Congress leader because of his official position, but his scholarship on economics and his work in Poona and in the National Social Conference, which he helped found in 1887, shaped much of the movement's thought. Surendranath Banerjea brought oratory and a network of Bengal followers. Gopal Krishna Gokhale, a younger teacher and a protégé of Ranade, rose to prominence in the 1890s for his command of financial detail.

What united them was a belief in constitutional methods and a faith that British political institutions could be pressed to extend their principles to India. What divided them was considerable. Ranade and Gokhale emphasized social reform alongside political demands; others held that political questions should come first, and that the Congress should not take up matters that divided communities. Mehta was skeptical of the claim that social reform was an essential companion; Banerjea and Bose concentrated on agitation; Ranade stressed careful economic analysis. These disagreements show that Moderate politics was a field of debate, not a single line.

Tyabji's presidency at Madras in 1887 is especially significant. It shows that Muslim professionals were among the early leaders and challenges any assumption that the movement belonged to one religious community. Tyabji also addressed directly the concern that Congress might neglect Muslim interests, a question taken up in Section 76.18.

Biographical comparison should begin with the leaders' different institutional bases. Bonnerjee's standing arose from legal practice in Calcutta, Mehta's from Bombay's municipal world, and Ranade's from scholarship, public associations, and judicial office. Tyabji also worked within Bombay's legal and civic networks but made the question of religious inclusion particularly prominent. Gokhale belonged to a younger generation shaped by education and the analytical traditions of Poona. These differences mattered because they shaped the skills each brought to politics: forensic argument, scrutiny of municipal finance, statistical analysis, or the ability to organize public meetings. 'Moderate' is a useful description of shared constitutional methods, but not a sufficient explanation of these varied careers.

A legal career had advantages for nationalist leadership. Lawyers knew how to frame an argument, read statutes, draft memorials, and challenge the consistency of administrative decisions. They also enjoyed professional networks in colonial towns. Their prominence introduced limits. The habit of treating political problems as questions to be remedied by recognized authority could undervalue agitation by agricultural laborers, tenant farmers, or industrial workers. Many Congress leaders sincerely considered themselves representatives of a wider public, but sincerity did not create representative accountability. Historians should ask how the leaders obtained evidence about people outside their professional milieu and how they responded when popular concerns challenged elite assumptions.

The National Social Conference, associated with Ranade and established in 1887 alongside the Congress context, makes a further distinction visible. Reformers disagreed about whether campaigns concerning caste and marriage should be publicly linked to constitutional agitation. Some feared divisive topics would weaken the fragile coalition; others feared that political liberty without social equality would reproduce injustice. This argument crossed the conventional Moderate/Extremist divide and shows why a single political label cannot explain the position of every leader. Even among those committed to constitutional practice, the proper relationship between social reform and political reform remained unresolved.

Tyabji's example is also more complex than a symbolic first. His 1887 presidency challenged assertions that Congress was necessarily a Hindu institution, while his insistence on avoiding divisive matters exposed a difficult organizational problem: an association claiming common political interests needed mechanisms for protecting those who feared domination by numerically or socially powerful groups. That problem was not settled by a single inclusive session. It shaped later debates about representation, separate constituencies, and the scope of national politics.

Historical Vocabulary
Moderate

"Moderate" designates leaders who preferred petitioning, persuasion, and parliamentary pressure to confrontation. The term was fixed in historians' usage largely by contrast with "Extremist" critics after 1900, and contemporaries did not always use it. It should not be read as meaning timid or loyalist without qualification. Many Moderates were severe critics of imperial finance and racial inequality. The label also obscures real differences among them, including their views of social reform, economic policy, and tactics. Its usefulness is in identifying a strategic orientation, not a fixed ideology.

Section 76.7

Moderate Political Methods: Petitions, Persuasion, and Constitutional Agitation

The Moderates' repertoire consisted of resolutions, memorials to the government and Parliament, public meetings, speeches, newspaper campaigns, delegations, and lobbying in Britain. They believed that informed British opinion and the pressure of Parliament could produce substantial reform, and they appealed to the Proclamation of 1858 and to British constitutional traditions as the standard to which the government should be held.

This strategy was shaped by the legal and political environment. Colonial law gave wide discretion to executive authority, and sedition and press laws limited what could safely be said. Open organization was legal; secret conspiracy was not. Leaders were professionals who risked their livelihoods and, in some cases, their liberty. A strategy of public, lawful agitation was therefore not only a matter of belief but also of prudence, and it allowed the movement to operate openly and to claim legitimacy.

The approach also rested on a belief that could be tested: that the British public, and Parliament, would respond if confronted with evidence of injustice. The evidence of the next two decades was mixed. There were real gains, such as the expansion of councils in 1892, and the Commons resolution of 1893. There were also repeated failures to translate sympathy into change, and Secretaries of State and Viceroys were generally unresponsive. That disappointment is central to Section 76.19.

Historians therefore distinguish strategic moderation from unconditional loyalty. Naoroji and Dutt used the language of loyalty while condemning the economic structure of empire. The early Congress was not passive: sustained organization, publication, and fundraising represented sustained political work, and evaluating it requires asking what alternatives were realistically available.

A petition was not merely a plea. Effective memorials assembled claims, specified legal or administrative remedies, invoked precedents, and produced a record to which governments might later be held. Nationalist organizations investigated expenditure, asked officials for explanations, and circulated statistical arguments precisely because such evidence could be addressed to British public opinion. They believed publicity might make arbitrary rule politically costly. This practice created a documentary archive of colonial criticism and trained a network of people to work with official records. It also assumed that the relevant institutions would eventually act upon reasoned evidence. Governments repeatedly frustrated that expectation, and the political meaning of constitutional agitation changed as disappointment accumulated.

Political restraint operated within a coercive order. The executive could supervise public meetings, prosecute writing alleged to be seditious, regulate newspapers, and shape appointments. Congress leaders consequently combined sharp criticism of expenditure or discriminatory arrangements with ritual professions of loyalty. These could reflect genuine attachment to constitutional ideals, tactical calculation, or both. A scholar should neither take expressions of loyalty as transparent evidence of support for every aspect of colonial rule nor dismiss all such language as empty. The test is to compare speeches with the leaders' actual proposals, persistent campaigns, and reactions when promised reforms did not arrive.

There was a practical problem of audience. A memorial addressed to the Secretary of State in London relied on an entirely different chain of influence from a vernacular report distributed among provincial readers. The former sought changes through recognized imperial authority, while the latter could build constituencies and political expectations among Indians. Congress leaders invested heavily in both, although their metropolitan efforts sometimes consumed resources without yielding results. Public political education and imperial lobbying were therefore complementary but not always equally successful. The contrast between the Commons vote of 1893 and the government's subsequent refusal to implement simultaneous examinations provides a particularly clear illustration of this tension.

Evaluating the Moderates requires an explicit standard of success. If success means achieving immediate control over administration, their record was slight. If it means creating institutions capable of maintaining campaigns and defining publicly contestable standards, it was more substantial. Neither conclusion removes the social limitation: most peasants and industrial workers did not select Congress delegates, and the movement had not developed the methods of popular mobilization later employed by nationalists. Constitutional agitation was a formative strategy whose accomplishments and weaknesses developed together.

Section 76.8

Constructing a National Political Public: Press, Language, and Provincial Networks

Congress sessions lasted days; politics continued the rest of the year through newspapers and local organization. English-language papers such as The Hindu, The Bengalee, Amrita Bazar Patrika, and The Mahratta and vernacular papers such as Kesari carried debates, reports, and editorials. Leaders corresponded with colleagues in other provinces, local lawyers organized meetings, and teachers and students attended and reported on them. This network transformed isolated provincial grievances into common issues.

English was both a bridge and a barrier. It allowed a Bengali lawyer, a Tamil journalist, and a Parsi merchant to share a platform. It also meant that most of the population could not take part directly. Vernacular newspapers and speeches extended the reach, and political ideas reached those who did not read English through readings, discussions, and translations, but the dependence on English reinforced the elite character of the movement.

A national organization could therefore exist before it had mass support. Its significance lay in coordination: the ability to formulate a common program, to speak to government in a single voice, and to create a recognizable national forum. This question of reach and representation is examined critically in Section 76.17.

Political communication was rarely a simple transmission from Congress headquarters to passive readers. Editors selected news, shortened speeches, emphasized local grievances, and translated unfamiliar constitutional terms into accessible idioms. Reports traveled by post and railway, appeared in newspapers, and were repeated at meetings or reading circles. A political issue in Bombay might become significant in Bengal because participants recognized in it a similar complaint about racial privilege or government spending. Conversely, local audiences could reject a provincial leader's attempt to depict his own interest as a national cause. Nationalism was built through this process of selection, translation, and disagreement; the process cannot be inferred merely from the number of newspapers in circulation.

Print also encouraged new forms of accountability within the movement. A leader's speech could be compared with an earlier address, and resolutions printed in one year with the government's reply in another. Editorial disagreements exposed differences between those seeking compromise and those favoring public pressure. Newspapers published letters and carried controversies between social reformers and political leaders. Women writers and people outside the most prominent associations took part in these wider printed debates, although not necessarily on equal terms. The press was thus a contested public arena rather than an instrument controlled by a single nationalist center.

Language remained a structural barrier. English allowed a Tamil-speaking lawyer and a Bengali journalist to debate without sharing another Indian language, but it privileged the educational system and classes that used English confidently. Translating terms such as 'representation,' 'public opinion,' or 'rights' was not a neutral exercise: each language carried established political and moral vocabularies. Local reading communities could understand government expenditure in terms of taxes, food prices, and district patronage rather than parliamentary budget procedure. Studying these differences prevents a misleading picture in which all Indians acquired an identical nationalist consciousness at once.

The geography of political circulation is equally revealing. Presidency capitals were connected by rail, post, newspapers, and personal visits, but a network on a map cannot prove that messages crossed every social boundary. Workers could listen to public discussion without joining Congress, and rural protest could proceed independently of elite campaigns. Evidence of nationalist penetration therefore requires reports of local meetings, correspondence, subscriptions, and reactions rather than assumptions based solely on communication technology. Later mass movements would depend on the expansion of these networks and on challenging their limits.

This reconstruction is a composite based on patterns in the evidence, not an account of a named individual. A delegate in the late 1880s was typically a lawyer, teacher, or journalist from a provincial town or presidency city. He travelled by rail, sometimes for several days, and was lodged by the host city's reception committee. On return, he would likely report to his association, write for a newspaper, and continue a professional career that sustained his politics.

The costs were real: travel, lost income, and, for those in government service, the risk of official displeasure. Most women, and the great majority of the population, were outside this world. Dadabhai Naoroji and Economic Nationalism

Division III · Section 76.9

Dadabhai Naoroji: Political Leadership across India and Britain

Dadabhai Naoroji (1825–1917) was the most prominent Indian political figure of the late nineteenth century to work in both India and Britain. Born into a Parsi priestly family in Bombay, he was educated at Elphinstone College and became a professor there. In the 1850s he entered commerce, with a firm in London, and began a life divided between the two countries. He edited a Gujarati paper, Rast Goftar, was involved in educational and reform societies in Bombay, and served briefly as an official in the princely state of Baroda.

In London he organized Indian opinion. He helped found the East India Association in 1866, which presented Indian questions to British audiences and the press, and he cultivated relations with liberal and radical politicians, trade-union and reform figures, and sympathetic journalists. His central purpose was to bring Indian grievances before the British public, which he regarded as the ultimate source of authority over Indian policy.

In India, Naoroji was a founder of the Bombay Presidency Association's circle and an early supporter of the Congress. He presided over the Calcutta session of 1886, and again at Lahore in 1893, and was thus one of the few leaders with a recognized position both in the national movement and in British politics.

In 1892 he was elected to the House of Commons as a Liberal for Finsbury Central, by a very narrow margin, becoming the first Indian elected to the British Parliament. He sat until losing his seat in 1895. His parliamentary career was short, but he used it to press for simultaneous civil-service examinations, for an inquiry into Indian finance, and for other reforms, and he was a member of the Royal Commission on Indian expenditure that was appointed in 1895. His career should be distinguished from the wider presence of South Asians in Britain, which included students, sailors, merchants, and domestic workers. Naoroji's achievement was exceptional and rested on his unusual social standing and connections.

Naoroji's political career joined several worlds that historians too readily separate: commercial experience, teaching, associational politics, statistical criticism, and British parliamentary activity. His movements between western India and London mattered because he could frame Indian economic grievances for audiences familiar with imperial budgets and with parliamentary claims to fiscal responsibility. His membership in the British Liberal Party and his electoral victory in 1892 made him visible in a political system in which Indians were normally subjects rather than lawmakers. Yet his position in Parliament remained exceptional and structurally limited. He represented Finsbury Central constituents, not an electorate in India, and his ability to change imperial policy depended on party alliances and official responses.

His election cannot be evaluated only as a biographical milestone. It demonstrated that an Indian political figure could engage British electoral politics directly and it gave the economic critique of empire a parliamentary platform. At the same time, the practical difficulty of representing India's demands from a marginal position in a large legislature exposed the weakness of metropolitan lobbying. Parliamentary debates reveal the difference between speeches sympathetic to India's grievances and executive decisions about administration. For Naoroji and his supporters, that distance strengthened the argument that Indians needed meaningful power within their own institutions.

Naoroji also helped define a style of political reasoning. He distinguished asserted imperial benefits from the financial channels through which resources left India, and he challenged official estimates on their own statistical terms. Such analysis required painstaking reading of revenue statements and knowledge of how salaries, pensions, remittances, and public charges entered the accounts. His influence therefore extended beyond any one election or presidency: he gave political organizers an evidentiary method that could be reproduced and challenged. The next section examines the structure of that argument rather than simply celebrating its author.

Section 76.10

Drain Theory: Wealth, Imperial Finance, and Colonial Poverty

Naoroji's most influential contribution was the theory of the "drain of wealth." He argued that a large part of India's annual production was transferred to Britain without an equivalent return, and that this transfer, rather than any native incapacity, explained Indian poverty. He pointed to the Home Charges, the payments made in Britain on behalf of the Indian government, including salaries and pensions of European officials, interest on public debt, dividends on railway investment guaranteed by the government, and military and administrative costs. These payments were met from Indian revenues and, he argued, took resources out of the Indian economy without a corresponding return flow.

He first set out the argument in lectures and papers in the 1860s and 1870s, including his 1876 paper on the poverty of India, and consolidated it in Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, published in London in 1901. He used official statistics, budget papers, and trade returns, and he estimated national income and per-capita earnings in order to show the scale of the poverty. In the title, "un-British" signaled a rhetorical strategy: he contended that the practice of the government contradicted the principles that Britain claimed for itself.

His estimates were contested then and have been since. Critics objected to his income calculations, to his treatment of services rendered by the colonial state and investments made in India, and to the claim that transfers necessarily impoverished the economy. Defenders note that his core insight, that a colonial state's fiscal structure could transfer resources abroad, was substantial and has been pursued by later economic historians in different forms. Students should distinguish his interpretations and estimates from uncontested economic measurements. Chapter 72 introduced the drain argument as an interpretation of colonial economics; what matters here is its transformation into a political argument that connected economic grievance to the demand for representation and control of finance.

Drain theory began with an accounting problem but made a larger claim about political power. Naoroji argued that certain payments were drawn from Indian resources without a corresponding inflow of Indian-controlled investment or reciprocal goods and services. Salaries and pensions remitted by European officials, private profits sent overseas, interest paid abroad, and the charges incurred by imperial administration could all become part of the question. Not every international payment has the same economic effect: interest may be associated with investment, and imports can provide value. Naoroji's analysis therefore requires attention to the distinction between payments for services, transfers, and the counterfactual possibility that Indians might have retained or deployed more of their income under different political arrangements.

The phrase 'home charges' described expenses incurred in Britain and financed through Indian revenues. These included aspects of debt service, administrative costs, pensions, and procurement, although the composition changed over time. A student studying these records should ask which items were paid from Indian taxation, who received them, whether the corresponding decisions were made in India or Britain, and how the amounts compare with the economic resources of the period. An aggregate number by itself cannot establish every causal link Naoroji asserted. Nevertheless, a large and recurring stream of payments under externally controlled administration raised a powerful question: why did the population bearing the fiscal burden lack effective control over the budget?

Naoroji's work was not a transparent set of definitive national-income accounts. The source material was fragmentary, official categories changed, and estimates of Indian income and poverty varied with their assumptions. His critics challenged both data and inference, while sympathizers sometimes repeated the largest numerical claims without examining methodology. Scholarly teaching should preserve this tension. The theory's significance lies in the disciplined attempt to transform economic statistics into a political indictment, not in the proposition that every figure was indisputably measured.

The argument also changed nationalist strategy. A complaint about an individual discriminatory appointment could be answered by appointing a qualified Indian; a critique of economic drain implicated the structure of administration, expenditure, trade, and control over public resources. Economic nationalism thus broadened the scope of Congress demands. It exposed a dilemma for Moderates who believed in appeals to British justice: if important financial burdens were built into imperial government, reform could require more than occasional concessions from officials. That dilemma connected early constitutional agitation to more assertive demands in the following period.

Historical Vocabulary
Drain of Wealth and Home Charges

"Drain of wealth" is the nationalist term for the outward transfer of resources from India to Britain through official remittances, pensions, interest, and profits. "Home Charges" was the official term for the expenditure incurred in Britain on India's account. The two are related but different: Home Charges are an item in the accounts, whereas the drain is an interpretation of their economic significance. Naoroji and Dutt argued the drain was harmful; colonial officials argued that the payments covered services and capital that benefited India. The debate shows how statistics can support rival claims.

Section 76.11

R. C. Dutt, Colonial Expenditure, and the Economic Critique of Empire

Romesh Chunder Dutt (1848–1909) brought a different background to economic criticism. A Bengali member of the Indian Civil Service from the early 1870s, he served as a district officer and later as a divisional commissioner, and he retired in 1897. His experience of revenue administration gave his arguments an authority that a purely political critic might lack. He presided over the Congress at Lucknow in 1899.

His writing addressed land revenue, famine, and the economic history of the previous century. In works on famines and on land revenue, including open letters addressed to Lord Curzon, he argued that heavy and inflexible land-revenue demands left cultivators with no reserves and made famine more severe. His major works, The Economic History of British India (1902) and India in the Victorian Age: An Economic History of the People (1904), offered a long historical account in which British policy had damaged Indian industry and agriculture and imposed a heavy fiscal burden.

Dutt and Naoroji shared a critique of fiscal transfers, but they should not be assimilated. Naoroji focused on the drain as a transfer of resources and on official statistics for national income; Dutt emphasized land revenue, the condition of the cultivator, and a historical narrative of industrial decline. Dutt also made specific policy proposals, such as moderate and fixed land-revenue assessments. Together, these writers gave the national movement a body of argument that went beyond complaint: it was systematic, based on government records, and addressed to policy.

Dutt brought a different professional history to nationalist economic criticism. Having served within the Indian Civil Service, he understood the routines through which revenue settlements, district administration, official reporting, and famine policy were justified. Retirement gave him greater opportunity to organize these experiences into a sustained historical account. This background should not make his interpretation immune from criticism: experience within a bureaucracy can illuminate procedures while also shaping which documents appear authoritative. Dutt wrote both as a critic of colonial economic choices and as someone trained in the language and records of the state he criticized. That combination helps explain why his work appealed to constitutional nationalists.

The two economic histories need bibliographic precision. The 1902 original was titled The Economic History of British India; the 1904 companion was India in the Victorian Age: An Economic History of the People. Later collected and revised editions have circulated under the shared title The Economic History of India with subtitles referring to early British rule and the Victorian age. It is incorrect to treat the later composite titles as if they were the exact title pages of both first editions. The chronological division mattered to Dutt's argument: he traced developments over an extended imperial period rather than attributing agricultural distress to a single viceroy or temporary crisis.

Dutt investigated land revenue, irrigation, public debt, tariffs, railways, and famine to ask whether colonial priorities advanced the welfare of cultivators. He criticized assessments that left households vulnerable to bad harvests and forms of expenditure that served imperial needs more readily than local economic resilience. His treatment of famine did not imply that climatic shocks were imaginary; instead, it contested explanations that stopped at rainfall and failed to examine household purchasing power, taxation, or administration. The relationship between government policy and famine mortality must still be evaluated through regional evidence and scholarship, not settled by citing one nationalist author.

Comparing Dutt with Naoroji reveals complementary methods. Naoroji concentrated attention on transfers of wealth and the political implications of fiscal dependence; Dutt supplied a historical narrative of policies affecting land and livelihoods over many decades. Both employed official records and wrote for audiences capable of challenging government claims. Their critics disagreed about causal attribution and the benefits of infrastructure or trade, which makes comparative analysis valuable. Economic nationalism matured not because it produced a single uncontested balance sheet, but because it made the costs and beneficiaries of empire a sustained public question.

Section 76.12

Economic Nationalism: Taxation, Tariffs, Industry, and Fiscal Accountability

The economic program of the early Congress combined demands about expenditure, taxation, and opportunity. Resolutions called for reducing military expenditure, which consumed a large share of revenue and funded campaigns beyond India's frontiers; for lower and more equitable taxation, including relief from the salt tax; for restraint in administrative costs; and for an end to discrimination in economic opportunity. Gokhale's later budget speeches were exemplary in this tradition. A Royal Commission on Indian expenditure, appointed in 1895 largely as a result of political pressure, gave Naoroji and others a platform and produced a large body of evidence, though its practical effect was limited.

Tariffs were more controversial. Indian cotton mill owners, concentrated in Bombay and Ahmedabad, resented the removal of import duties on Lancashire cotton goods, and the 1894 duties on specified Indian mill yarn, followed by the revised 1896 excise on woven cotton goods, were criticized as arrangements favoring Lancashire manufacturers. The measures differed in scope and were not enacted simultaneously. Nationalists took up this grievance, but they disagreed about protection: some emphasized revenue and free trade, others advocated protection for infant industry. Interests in the movement, including mill owners, landholders, and consumers, did not always coincide.

Economic arguments served a political purpose. By showing that fiscal decisions were made in London and Simla without Indian consent, leaders connected economic interests to the demand for participation in government. The movement for control of the budget was at once economic and constitutional. It is important to distinguish this argument-driven economic nationalism from the organized boycott of British goods and promotion of Indian-made goods that belongs to the Swadeshi movement of 1905 and after, treated in Chapter 77.

Fiscal demands were politically more difficult than they first appeared. A proposal to reduce military expenditure raised questions about frontier policy, imperial wars, and the allocation of costs between British and Indian taxpayers. Advocates of retrenchment asked why India should bear burdens connected to wider imperial strategic objectives when Indian representatives could not determine policy. Officials, in contrast, emphasized security and administrative obligations. The disagreement was not simply over whether armies were expensive; it concerned which governing body had authority to define necessity and determine who paid. Congress resolutions on expenditure thus connected financial criticism to representation.

Tariffs made the issue more concrete. Bombay's cotton mills competed with imported British textiles, while Lancashire manufacturers sought access to Indian markets. The government reintroduced import duties in the 1890s for revenue purposes, but the treatment of domestic Indian manufactures generated fierce controversy. In 1894 duties on certain imported cotton goods and yarn were accompanied by an excise on specified Indian mill yarn; in 1896 the structure was revised, with yarn duties removed and an excise on cotton piece goods alongside the lowered import duty. These successive changes must not be described as one measure enacted in 1896. Their political significance lay in the appearance that imperial fiscal policy protected influential British manufacturers at the expense of Indian industrial development.

Protection of industry was not always identical with the interests of workers or consumers. A tariff might benefit factory owners while raising prices for purchasers; a revenue measure might affect handloom weavers differently from power-loom mills. Indian producers were themselves divided by region, product, and access to capital. Congress could claim that industrial development served national economic independence, but the historian must investigate whose interests a specific proposal advanced. The presence of merchants and professionals within nationalist organizations made this especially important. Economic nationalism was a coalition of arguments, not a guarantee that every class would benefit equally from every policy.

The economic program joined administrative economy with proposals for development. Leaders discussed indigenous enterprise, technical instruction, public works, irrigation, and conditions for private investment, but they did not agree on one blueprint for state intervention. Ranade's support for industrial growth, Naoroji's critique of the drain, and Dutt's attention to agrarian distress could reinforce one another without being interchangeable. This section therefore establishes the conceptual roots of later Swadeshi economic initiatives while stopping short of the boycotts and mass campaigns that belong to Chapter 77.

Division IV

Constitutional Reform and Participation in Government

Division IV · Section 76.13

Legislative Councils and the Indian Councils Act of 1892

Representation was the Congress's constitutional centerpiece. The Indian Councils Act of 1861 had created legislative councils at the center and in the provinces, to which the government nominated additional members, some of them Indians, but those bodies were advisory and had no control over finance. The early Congress asked for larger councils, for a portion of members to be elected, for the right to discuss the budget, and to put questions to the government.

The Indian Councils Act of 1892 enlarged the councils and altered the procedure for appointing non-official members. It allowed members to discuss the annual financial statement and to ask questions of the executive, under restrictions, but not to vote on the budget. It did not provide for direct election; instead, some non-official members were nominated by the governor-general or governors on the recommendation of bodies such as municipalities, district boards, universities, and chambers of commerce, an indirect method which officials preferred to describe in terms other than election. Officials retained a majority.

The Act therefore expanded the opportunity for Indian participation without creating parliamentary government, universal or wide suffrage, or ministerial responsibility to elected Indians. Congress leaders considered the reforms inadequate, but they used the new opportunities. Pherozeshah Mehta and Gokhale in particular made councils into platforms for analysis of finance, and the experience created habits of parliamentary debate that outlasted the limits of the Act.

To understand the 1892 reform, students should distinguish a legislative council from a legislature with responsible government. Under the arrangements following the 1861 Act, councils included officials and nominated nonofficial members, but they were designed principally to assist legislation under executive supervision. Appointment did not amount to a mandate from Indian voters, and the executive retained decisive authority. When Congress called for wider membership and a representative element, it challenged both the numerical balance of the councils and the principle that government should not have to answer to those it governed. The demand acquired force because budgets and laws shaped the lives of people who could not remove the officials making the decisions.

The Indian Councils Act of 1892 enlarged the permissible number of additional members and allowed rules that incorporated recommendations from selected local bodies and associations. In some circumstances members could discuss the annual financial statement and put questions, within restrictive procedures. The law did not itself create a general direct electorate, require the executive to retain the confidence of the council, or transfer control over executive finance. It is better to describe the beginnings of limited indirect representation through nominations under rules than to claim that the Act established parliamentary democracy. The text of the statute, regulations issued under it, and subsequent council practice should be read together.

The difference between legal wording and actual operation is a teaching opportunity. If a rule allowed a municipal body to recommend a person whom the government might then nominate, who ultimately controlled entry? If a member could ask a question after notice, could that member compel a change in policy? A debating right might expose waste or inequality without providing authority to amend an expenditure item. By asking these questions, students can evaluate why nationalist critics welcomed small openings yet regarded the measure as inadequate. Constitutional reform had to be judged by powers as well as by titles.

Congress debates over the Act demonstrate an emerging political standard. Leaders who had once demanded recognition as informed advisers increasingly assessed reform by whether it gave Indians a meaningful share in governing. The 1892 councils made criticism audible in new institutional settings but left the framework of executive supremacy intact. Subsequent nationalist politics would inherit both the experience of working within councils and the argument that their limits were structural rather than accidental. The next chapter develops later constitutional controversies, but the conceptual groundwork belongs here.

Section 76.14

Indianization of the Civil Service and Administrative Equality

Access to the higher civil service was a long-standing grievance. The Indian Civil Service, which administered the districts and filled senior positions, was recruited by competitive examination held in London. Indian candidates had to travel to Britain, afford the costs, and, following the 1876 decision to reduce the maximum age to nineteen, which affected the examination arrangements from 1878, prepare within a very narrow window. A few Indians succeeded, among them Surendranath Banerjea, who was subsequently dismissed after a dispute, and the number of Indians in the service remained very small.

The Congress demanded that examinations be held simultaneously in India and England, and that the age limit be raised. These demands were attractive because they appealed to the principle of open competition, which the government had itself endorsed, and because they would greatly widen the pool of Indian candidates. They also bore on much larger questions of who ruled India and in whose interest.

The campaign reached the House of Commons. On June 2, 1893, the House passed a resolution favoring simultaneous examinations, on a motion by a Liberal member, and Naoroji, newly elected, supported it. But a parliamentary resolution was not a law or an administrative decision. The Secretary of State and the Government of India, who held that the proposal would endanger the character of the service, did not implement it. Simultaneous examinations were not introduced for many years. The episode illustrates the gap between sympathy and institutional change that Moderates repeatedly encountered.

Recruitment policy was a crucial test of the proclamation of equality. Entry to the elite civil service was formally open by competition, yet the competitive examination was held in Britain, where candidates from India faced expensive travel, unfamiliar living costs, and the need for training within a different educational environment. Age restrictions compounded these barriers. The decision to lower the maximum age was made in 1876, while the revised examinations affected candidates under the arrangements of 1878. Campaigners treated the change as part of a wider pattern in which neutral-sounding rules could operate unequally because the contestants faced radically different conditions. In this sense 'Indianization' was not simply a demand for jobs; it concerned the legitimacy of a government whose highest offices were overwhelmingly European.

The issue also exposed divisions between different official services. A few Indians might enter provincial or subordinate departments while the posts with greatest authority and remuneration remained largely insulated. Officials sometimes presented limited provincial appointments as evidence that the principle of equality had been honored. Nationalist leaders answered that access to lesser offices could not substitute for participation in decisions about revenue, policing, and district administration. They also criticized disparities in salary, pension, promotion, and the influence of European networks. These were questions of institutional design as well as personal prejudice.

The proposal for simultaneous competitive examinations was intended to create a common standard in India and Britain. Supporters argued that candidates should sit substantively equivalent tests and be ranked together rather than face a geographic obstacle unrelated to ability. Opponents invoked professional training, administrative cohesion, and perceived differences in educational preparation; some expressed explicit fears about weakening European control. The debates of June 2, 1893 show the government question plainly. The House of Commons ultimately adopted a resolution favoring simultaneous examinations, but the executive rejected implementation. A parliamentary resolution was politically significant yet did not by itself amend recruitment procedures.

That contrast continued after the vote. Parliamentary questions in 1894 recorded that ministers had considered the matter and cited objections to implementation. The dispute illustrates a general feature of colonial constitutional politics: sympathetic words in one part of the imperial system did not ensure action by its administrators. Indian nationalists could point to a public statement of principle, then demonstrate that the promised equality remained inaccessible. It is necessary to distinguish this earlier campaign from later systems of civil-service recruitment that evolved substantially in the twentieth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Simultaneous Examinations and Indianization

"Simultaneous examinations" refers to the proposal that the Indian Civil Service examination be held in India at the same time as in Britain, with a single merit list. "Indianization" is the broader process of appointing Indians to senior government positions. The first is a specific institutional demand; the second is a general aim. Opponents argued that senior officials must be British for security and standards; supporters argued that merit and the Proclamation's promises of equality demanded Indian admission. The question mattered because the civil service held real power.

Section 76.15

Administrative Accountability, Civil Rights, and Equality before the Law

Beyond councils and the civil service, early Congress raised wider constitutional demands. It called for the separation of judicial and executive functions, since district officers combined the roles of magistrate, revenue collector, and police supervisor. It demanded equal treatment of Indians and Europeans in law, as the Ilbert Bill had highlighted; freedom of the press; revision of the Arms Act; and scrutiny of public expenditure and of officials.

The strength of these arguments lay in their use of British principles. Leaders cited the rule of law, equality before the law, and the accountability of government to the governed, and then pointed to colonial practice. The contradiction between the claim of lawful and impartial administration and the racial privileges of European residents was an effective political theme.

Officials answered differently. Many defended the existing system as necessary for security and efficiency, argued that the educated minority did not represent the masses, and held that the Indian public lacked the experience needed for wider responsibility. Some, particularly in the districts, regarded Congress as the voice of an ambitious class seeking jobs. Comparing these positions shows that the debate concerned the nature of imperial authority, not only specific administrative changes.

The separation of executive and judicial functions illustrates why administrative structure mattered to ordinary people. In many districts the same general administrative hierarchy exercised powers associated with revenue collection, policing, and criminal justice. Critics argued that overlapping functions could weaken impartial adjudication because an officer charged with maintaining order might also influence the assessment of disputes arising from police or revenue action. Such concerns did not require proof that every individual officer behaved improperly. They were arguments about institutional safeguards: a legal right offers less protection if the body administering that right also has a stake in the contested decision.

Press freedom and the law of sedition raised related questions. The repeal of the Vernacular Press Act in 1882 did not remove all possible prosecutions or executive controls over political expression. Nationalist journalists operated in a legal environment in which criticism could be investigated as a threat to public order. Demands for open debate were therefore not merely demands for the convenience of educated editors. They addressed the ability to expose misconduct, examine budgets, and coordinate political campaigns without relying on official permission. At the same time, newspapers carried their own social prejudices and selective interests; freedom to publish did not make every published claim reliable.

The Arms Act controversy and the Ilbert Bill dispute both supplied memorable examples of unequal status. An apparently general law might exempt classes associated with European privilege, and the qualifications of Indian judges could be subordinated to racial objections. The lesson early political organizations drew was that impartiality required enforceable rules, not simply verbal assurances that officials would treat subjects fairly. This helps explain why Congress demands moved between administrative details and constitutional principles. A dispute over the allocation of magistrates could become an argument about citizenship, while a complaint about the expense of the army could become a question of representative control.

A useful exercise is to compare the perspective of a plaintiff in a district court, a journalist facing prosecution, and a member of an imperial council. Each encountered a different level of the colonial state. For the plaintiff, judicial independence mattered immediately; for the journalist, limits on expression affected public scrutiny; for the council member, budgetary procedure constrained the ability to change policy. These cases show why a movement dominated by lawyers could develop a broad constitutional critique while still leaving other urgent forms of popular politics insufficiently represented.

Section 76.16

Indian Nationalism in Britain: Parliamentary Lobbying and International Networks

London was an integral part of early nationalist politics because Parliament and the India Office controlled policy. The East India Association, founded by Naoroji in 1866, provided a forum for discussion of Indian affairs. In 1889 the British Committee of the Indian National Congress was established, with Hume, Sir William Wedderburn, and others, to represent Congress in Britain. It published a journal, India, organized meetings, and kept contact with members of Parliament, journalists, and reform groups. Wedderburn, a former Indian official who sympathized with the movement, presided over Congress sessions in 1889 and 1910.

Indian leaders tried to influence British elections, public opinion, and parliamentary debate. They gave evidence, sent delegations, and funded publications. Naoroji's election was an example of success, and the Commons resolution of 1893 another. The approach had clear limits. British political parties had little interest in Indian questions, and sympathetic members were a small minority. Dependence on metropolitan allies meant that nationalists were reliant on people whose priorities were British and whose attention was intermittent.

The chapter's point is that early Indian nationalism operated through both Indian and British institutions. Understanding it as a movement confined to India misses a significant component of its method and helps explain the disappointment that followed.

London organizations supplied a different political arena from provincial associations in India. The East India Association, founded in 1866 through Naoroji's efforts, convened meetings and circulated arguments for an audience that included British officials, parliamentarians, and people sympathetic to Indian reform. Its influence depended on the willingness of powerful listeners to take the meetings seriously. Later, the British Committee of the Indian National Congress, established in 1889, sought to maintain a more sustained campaign through publicity and parliamentary contacts. Those bodies were not substitutes for the annual Congress; they were efforts to translate its resolutions into language that might operate within British political institutions.

The existence of a committee in Britain did not make Indian political interests identical with those of its British allies. Some reformers believed that the empire could be preserved through equality and administrative improvements, while Indian critics increasingly doubted that the necessary changes would be granted without a stronger movement at home. Organizations had to choose what material would persuade metropolitan readers, and those choices shaped their representation of India. A document emphasizing 'responsible' educated petitioners might appeal to a Liberal politician while obscuring the grievances of poorer groups. Metropolitan strategy therefore created opportunities for access and dangers of selective representation at the same time.

Naoroji's parliamentary career made the practical difficulties visible. The member for Finsbury Central could ask questions and take part in debates about recruitment, finance, and Indian government, but an individual member did not control Cabinet policy or the India Office. The June 1893 debate attracted supporters and opponents who understood Indianization in terms ranging from impartial merit to the maintenance of imperial dominance. The same arguments appeared in subsequent parliamentary exchanges. Studying this sequence helps separate the publicity value of a reform campaign from its concrete administrative result. A motion's success in the Commons could be a symbolic victory without becoming the policy of the Secretary of State.

These networks also belonged to a wider nineteenth-century world of migration and public exchange. Indian students, professionals, merchants, and visitors in Britain brought personal experience into political debate, while metropolitan newspapers could circulate arguments back to India. But a London-centered approach was expensive and could reinforce dependence on elites able to travel internationally. As nationalists increasingly sought pressure from within India, arguments about the usefulness of British lobbying became part of the larger debate over strategy that would intensify after 1905.

Division V

Limits and Historical Significance of Early Nationalism

Division V · Section 76.17

Social Composition of Congress: Class, Caste, Region, and Gender

The early Congress drew most of its members from lawyers, journalists, teachers, and other professionals, together with merchants and some landholders. Many came from communities with a tradition of literacy and an early encounter with English education. In some provinces, particular castes and communities were strongly represented, while participation from others was slight. The presidencies of Bombay, Bengal, and Madras were heavily represented, northern India and Punjab somewhat less in the first years, and the princely states were largely outside the organization.

The Congress did not claim to be elected by the population, but it did claim to speak for Indian interests. This claim was criticized from several directions. Officials said it represented a small class; some Muslim leaders feared it would represent a majority religious community; some landholders and conservative groups worried that it would favor professionals. The social composition shaped its priorities. Questions of the civil service, councils, and law were central to educated professionals, whereas the concerns of cultivators and laborers, such as rent, debt, and conditions of labor, received less systematic attention in the first years, though Congress resolutions and individual leaders such as Dutt and Ranade did address agrarian conditions.

Women were few. At the 1889 Bombay session, women attended as delegates, and Kadambini Ganguly, a physician from Calcutta, was among them. She addressed the Congress at its 1890 Calcutta session. Her roles as an early delegate and a later speaker should be distinguished. Their presence was significant, but it was exceptional: women's political participation was constrained by limited education, social expectations, and legal restrictions, and reform movements of the period were still debating women's education and status.

A claim to national representation must be tested against how delegates were chosen. Early Congress participants usually came through public meetings and associations, not through an electorate encompassing India. They were disproportionately drawn from the educated professions and urban property-owning circles, with differences between provinces. The fact that a participant was Indian did not mean that every Indian understood the participant to represent them. Nor did social selectivity negate the public value of constitutional criticism. The historian's task is to hold these propositions together: Congress developed institutions for national deliberation, while its organizational base gave it only limited knowledge of the needs and wishes of most subjects of the Raj.

Occupational evidence is especially revealing. Lawyers, teachers, newspaper editors, merchants, and some landholders possessed skills useful for memorials and meetings. Agricultural laborers, small cultivators, industrial millhands, and many women had fewer opportunities to travel to annual sessions or afford subscriptions. Leaders often spoke about famine, taxation, and rural distress on the basis of reports rather than direct accountability to the affected populations. At times this produced serious investigations; at other times it made it easier to prioritize the interests of educated candidates seeking government employment. Social composition thus shaped the agenda without mechanically determining every argument.

Regional distinctions complicated the claim of unity. Bengal, western India, the Tamil-speaking south, and the Punjab possessed different educational institutions, languages, and relationships with colonial government. Some areas were highly visible in annual sessions because their professional classes already maintained associations. Others had weaker links or different political priorities. Numerical attendance at a Congress session should not be mistaken for an index of sentiment across all villages and towns. More rigorous evidence would include local subscriptions, affiliations, circulation of political writing, and the recorded response to particular campaigns.

Women entered the public record of Congress cautiously. The Bombay meeting of 1889 included women delegates, with Kadambini Ganguly among the early figures associated with this development; her more prominent public address to a Congress gathering belongs to 1890. Any attempt to enumerate 'first women' must distinguish delegates, attendees, speakers, and the date of a given contribution, since retrospective histories do not always use the categories consistently. Women's presence challenged a male-dominated institution but did not erase barriers to political participation. The chapter should acknowledge intellectual and organizational contributions outside Congress as well as the limited access to the annual sessions themselves.

Section 76.18

Competing Political Loyalties and Criticism of Congress

Not all Indians supported the Congress. Sayyid Ahmad Khan, founder of the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh and a leading figure among North Indian Muslims, criticized it in speeches in the late 1880s. He argued that Indian society was composed of different communities, that a system of election would give a numerical majority the upper hand, and that Muslims should concentrate on education and cooperation with government. He and the English principal of his college, Theodore Beck, were associated with a Patriotic Association created to oppose the Congress. His views were influential, particularly in the North-Western Provinces and Awadh, and they were part of a debate within Muslim opinion about representation.

Muslim opinion was not uniform. Badruddin Tyabji presided over Congress in 1887, and Rahimatullah M. Sayani, another Bombay lawyer, presided in 1896. Muslim delegates participated in the early sessions in significant numbers, especially from Bombay and Madras, though many were conscious of the question of community interests. Conversely, many Hindu leaders were not keen supporters of the Congress, and some princely and landholding elites remained aloof because they were uneasy about a political movement among the educated. Twentieth-century communal divisions were not inevitable, and the evidence of the 1880s shows a range of positions rather than a settled division.

Other critics objected from the opposite side. Some thought Congress too cautious, and some regional interests thought it neglected them. The establishment of the All-India Muslim League in 1906 belongs to Chapter 77.

Criticism of Congress came from several directions and for different reasons. Some Indian princes and landholding interests feared that representative institutions could weaken arrangements through which they negotiated with the colonial state. Conservative critics sometimes regarded public agitation as disruptive or questioned whether urban professionals understood religious and local obligations. Other people remained indifferent because Congress resolutions did not address their immediate concerns. It would be misleading to place all such reservations in one 'loyalist' camp. Loyalty to a dynasty, cautious cooperation with British authorities, fear of majoritarian politics, and disapproval of a particular economic policy were distinct positions.

Sayyid Ahmad Khan's critique should be set in the context of his educational and political commitments. He feared that representative arrangements advocated by Congress could disadvantage Muslims in settings where they were locally less numerous or lacked comparable access to the new professions. His concern was not simply hostility to Indian political participation, since his Aligarh work promoted advancement through education and institutional organization. Critics disagreed with his reading of the risks, including Muslims who participated actively in Congress. Badruddin Tyabji's leadership, Rahimatullah M. Sayani's later presidency, and other examples show why Muslim opinion cannot be narrated as one unified response to Congress.

The issue was connected to the nature of representation itself. If a council contained members selected from municipal bodies or associations, who decided which associations counted? If elections were broadened, what safeguards would protect groups with fewer resources or smaller populations in a particular district? Congress's public claim to speak on 'common' matters offered one solution: avoid disputes considered sectarian and emphasize fiscal or administrative changes that might benefit many communities. Yet deciding which questions were 'common' could itself exclude conflicts over education, religious institutions, or customary law. Avoiding disagreement did not eliminate differences in power.

The chapter must also resist projecting the politics of 1906 or the partition of the subcontinent back onto the 1880s. Separate political organizations and later constitutional arrangements developed under changing conditions, and their emergence cannot be read as the inevitable outcome of Sayyid Ahmad Khan's criticism. This earlier period was open-ended. Political leaders made choices in response to new institutions, administrative reforms, social competition, and their expectations about the empire. That complexity is essential to understanding the transition to the next chapter without reducing the past to a prehistory of later conflict.

Multiple Perspectives

The Early Congress as Seen by Different Groups

Moderate leaders: A forum for constitutional demands and a school of national politics, built through patience and lawful pressure.
Colonial officials: Views varied, but many regarded it as a body of a small educated class that did not represent the peasantry, and some as a cause for concern. Others, including a few sympathizers, thought it should be heard.

Section 76.19

Achievements, Contradictions, and the Growing Critique of Moderate Politics

The early Congress achieved several things of lasting importance. It created an interregional organization that endured, held annual public debate, and educated a generation in political argument. It formulated constitutional demands that set a reference point for later politics, and, through Naoroji and Dutt, it produced an economic critique of empire that reached beyond Congress. It established networks and institutions in India and Britain that later movements used.

It also had limits. Its social base was narrow, and it had no means of compelling the government. Its principal tactic, petitioning, depended on a responsiveness that colonial authorities rarely showed. The Councils Act gave limited concessions, simultaneous examinations were not carried out, and expenditure and fiscal control remained in London and Simla. Under Lord Curzon, viceroy from 1899, the government took a skeptical view of Congress and acted in ways that nationalists regarded as authoritarian, including the Indian Universities Act of 1904 and the Official Secrets Act amendments.

Repeated disappointments fostered criticism. Bal Gangadhar Tilak, editor of Kesari and The Mahratta in Poona, argued for a more assertive politics that would draw on popular religious and historical symbols, as in the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals he promoted from the 1890s; he had earlier disagreed with Ranade and Gokhale on social reform, notably in the controversy over the Age of Consent Act of 1891. He was convicted of sedition in 1897. Gokhale continued the constitutional approach and founded the Servants of India Society in 1905, and he presided over the Congress at Benares that year. Among Muslim and Bengali politicians, among younger activists, and among supporters of industrial and educational self-help, criticism of the Moderates grew.

By 1905 the movement stood at a threshold. Lord Curzon's decision to partition Bengal, announced in July 1905 and put into effect in October, provoked protest that went beyond petitions and moved into boycott and mass mobilization. That confrontation, and the divisions it exposed, is the subject of Chapter 77. The Moderates did not fail in a simple sense: their institutions and arguments made the subsequent mobilization possible.

An assessment of achievements should begin with what the organization actually controlled. The Congress could publish resolutions, establish relationships, and convene meetings; it could not appoint the viceroy, command the army, collect taxes, or impose a budget. Its legislative gains depended on authorities willing to accept limited modifications. Judging it solely by reforms enacted therefore makes its record appear narrower than a history of institution-building would suggest. By the turn of the century, nationalists had created a recognizable annual assembly, traditions of presidential debate, an archive of public demands, and networks capable of circulating political argument beyond a single presidency. Those developments made it possible for later critics to claim that Congress should act differently, rather than begin from nothing.

Its failures were nevertheless not incidental. The Indian Councils Act of 1892 left executive supremacy intact, and the 1893 Commons resolution on civil-service examinations did not overcome administrative opposition. Indian economic critics increasingly argued that the structure of imperial finance, rather than individual abuses, produced disadvantages. These were problems difficult to solve by isolated petitions. Frustration sharpened when governments treated organized public opinion as a claim to be managed rather than a source of binding political authority. The resulting critique challenged the assumption that consistent constitutional argument would necessarily produce substantial change.

Bal Gangadhar Tilak and other emerging critics called for different relationships among politics, public action, and national self-assertion, but their ideas were not identical and should not be treated as a finished political alternative in 1885. Local disputes over education, public festivals, social reform, and administrative power also influenced the appeal of more confrontational language. Gokhale, by contrast, continued to argue that disciplined constitutional criticism could expose governmental contradictions and enlarge political capacity. Their disagreement demonstrates that the split between later Moderate and more assertive currents developed through specific choices, not through an inevitable replacement of failed leaders by successful ones.

The 1905 partition of Bengal made the question of strategy more urgent, but the detailed politics of boycott, Swadeshi, revolutionary activism, and new Muslim organizations belong to Chapter 77. The important conclusion here is chronological and institutional: by the time new methods emerged, earlier Congress politics had already provided a national platform and a shared language of fiscal and constitutional criticism. The same legacy could be valued as foundational and criticized as insufficient. Serious historical evaluation requires both judgments, grounded in evidence rather than retrospective slogans.

Historical Debate

Was the Early Congress Politically Effective Despite Its Limited Social Reach?

The question: Should historians judge the early Congress by its narrow reach and modest concessions, or by the institutions, arguments, and networks it created?

Interpretation A — Effective as an institution builder: Historians in the nationalist tradition, such as Bipan Chandra and associates, stress that Moderate leaders created a national platform, an economic critique of empire, and political education, laying the foundation for later mass movements and exposing the nature of colonial rule.
Interpretation B — Limited and elite-driven: Historians in the Cambridge School tradition, such as Anil Seal, emphasized that early nationalism represented competition among educated elites for positions and influence. Sumit Sarkar and others have noted the social narrowness of Congress and its limited impact on policy.

Evidence: Both rely on Congress records, colonial correspondence, and press. Evidence of attendance and social background supports the claim of narrowness; evidence of the continuity of institutions and arguments supports the claim of influence. The debate over the origins of Congress, including the safety-valve account, forms part of this larger question. John R. McLane's work on the political context of the founding is among the studies that bridge the approaches, and D. A. Washbrook's analysis of South Indian politics shows how local interests shaped national organization.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how one regards the whole nationalist movement: as the outgrowth of principles and institutions, as elite competition, or as both. Many historians now combine elements of each.

Section 76.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the movement from regional associations to a national organization. After 1858, education, communications, and professional employment created an educated public with shared grievances. Associations in Poona, Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay organized that public, and the campaigns of the early 1880s showed that interregional coordination was feasible. The Congress, founded in December 1885, institutionalized that coordination as an annual forum, and the founding is best seen as the consolidation of earlier developments, in which Hume's role was real but not sole.

The Moderate leaders formulated constitutional demands and a political method of petition, persuasion, and parliamentary lobbying, which was a deliberate strategy shaped by law and circumstance. Naoroji and Dutt developed an economic critique that made fiscal arrangements a central political issue. The results, the Councils Act of 1892 and the Commons resolution of 1893, were partial: the first expanded participation without responsible government, and the second was never implemented.

The Congress claimed to speak for India, but its social base was narrow, and it faced criticism from Muslim leaders such as Sayyid Ahmad Khan, from conservative interests, and from younger critics. The relationship among political moderation, institutional development, economic nationalism, and growing expectations explains how the movement could be at once limited and transformative. Chapter 77 takes up the Swadeshi movement, the diversification of nationalist politics, and the challenge to Moderate leadership.

The narrative also demonstrates why no single explanation of national politics is enough. Constitutional ideas mattered because leaders used them to justify equal access to government, but ideas alone did not organize meetings. Economic grievances mattered because they offered common problems, but hardship did not automatically produce a national political association. Provincial leaders mattered because they possessed local networks, yet those networks could also restrict whose interests were represented. The formation of Congress therefore arose from the intersection of organization, communication, grievance, and a deliberate effort to create a political public whose geographical reach exceeded any one province.

Congress's early claims should be placed beside its social boundaries. The organization spoke about the welfare of India but was organized mainly by educated and professional minorities. Its records contain important arguments concerning peasants' burdens and imperial expenditure, yet direct evidence of rural political opinion is much thinner. The gap is not grounds for dismissing the movement as wholly artificial. It is a reason to ask how an organization makes a representative claim and what institutions would be needed to test that claim. A national political identity could develop before universal political participation, although those excluded from the process might contest the terms of that identity.

The chapter's documentary studies offer a method for evaluating this history. Congress proceedings reveal declared aims and public procedure; presidential addresses reveal leaders' priorities; Naoroji's economic writing reveals contested interpretations of fiscal evidence; the 1892 legislation reveals the legal scope of reform; and parliamentary debate in 1893 reveals the division between principle and implementation. None of these sources alone supplies a complete picture. Read together with regional newspapers, correspondence, and social history, they allow historians to reconstruct both the growth of political institutions and the constraints under which they operated.

The transition to Chapter 77 should therefore be framed as a question rather than a foregone conclusion. How could constitutional demands be made more effective? Who should participate in campaigns claiming to be national? What should happen when political leaders disagreed about economic policy, religion, methods, or the meaning of self-government? The Swadeshi period made these questions more visible and contentious, but it did not erase the institutions and arguments established during the first two decades of Congress politics.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Regional Politics before 1885 and Organized Nationalism after 1885

What changed? Regional associations became coordinated through annual national sessions; programs were standardized; London became an operational center; economic critique became systematic.

What remained continuous? The leadership, methods, and social base of the earlier associations; reliance on petition and the press; the dominance of English-educated professionals.

Who experienced change most directly? Delegates and leaders in the presidency cities, who gained a national forum.

Who experienced greater continuity? Cultivators, laborers, and most women, for whom daily life and relations with government changed little as a result.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that Congress consolidated earlier developments instead of creating nationalism, and that the movement's reach remained limited.

Chapter Summary

Organized Indian nationalism developed from the associations, institutions, and grievances of colonial society after 1858. Education, railways, telegraphs, newspapers, and professional employment created an educated public conscious of the gap between British principles and colonial practice.

Regional associations in Poona, Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay organized this public. They wrote memorials, cultivated the press, and learned to coordinate through campaigns against measures such as the Vernacular Press Act and the Ilbert Bill's opponents. The National Conferences of 1883 and 1885 showed that interregional gatherings were feasible.

The Indian National Congress was founded at Bombay on December 28, 1885, with 72 delegates and W. C. Bonnerjee as president. Hume's role was important, but Indian leaders brought organization and legitimacy. The "safety valve" explanation is an interpretation, not an established fact.

Annual sessions in different cities, presidential addresses, and resolutions turned the Congress into a recurring institution. Moderate leaders such as Bonnerjee, Mehta, Tyabji, Ranade, Banerjea, and Gokhale shared constitutional commitments while differing over social reform, economics, and tactics.

The Moderates' method was petition, persuasion, public meetings, and parliamentary lobbying, shaped by the legal environment and by faith in British opinion. It was a strategy, not passivity, though it depended on responsiveness that was rarely forthcoming.

Dadabhai Naoroji worked in Bombay and London, helped found the East India Association, and became Member of Parliament for Finsbury Central in 1892. His drain theory, consolidated in Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901), and R. C. Dutt's economic histories of 1902 and 1904 gave nationalism a systematic economic critique, while the specific estimates remain contested.

Constitutional demands produced the Indian Councils Act of 1892, which enlarged councils and allowed budget discussion but not responsible government, and the Commons resolution of June 2, 1893, on simultaneous civil-service examinations, which was not implemented. The British Committee of Congress extended the movement's activities to London.

Congress's social base consisted mainly of professionals, with few women, such as Kadambini Ganguly in 1889, and little direct participation by the rural majority. It was criticized by Sayyid Ahmad Khan, by officials, and by conservative interests, but Muslim leaders such as Tyabji and Sayani also held its presidency.

By 1905, repeated disappointment, the policies of Lord Curzon, and arguments from critics such as Tilak led to doubts about Moderate methods. Gokhale continued the constitutional approach. The Moderates left institutions, arguments, and networks that made later mobilization possible, and Chapter 77 turns to that transition.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political movement is built. Before the mass campaigns of the twentieth century, a few thousand educated people created associations, organized meetings, wrote memorials, and compiled statistics, and in doing so established the forms in which Indians could speak collectively to power.

It also shows the cost and constraints of that effort. The early Congress was socially narrow, dependent on a government that rarely listened, and divided over how far to go. Judging it only by what it failed to win, or only by what it later became, distorts both its achievements and its limits.

The chapter links to later developments. Economic critique reappears in Swadeshi and in later debates on planning, constitutional demands in the history of reform acts, and arguments about representation in the politics of separate electorates. It also connects regions: leaders from Bombay, Bengal, Madras, and the north learned to act together.

In the six-volume sequence, Chapter 76 marks the passage from the structures of colonial rule examined in Volume IV to the politics of resistance and negotiation that dominate Volume V. Chapter 77 begins with the threshold reached in 1905.
