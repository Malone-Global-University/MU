Part XIII — The Rebellion of 1857 and the British Raj

The Rebellion of 1857

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the evening of Sunday, 10 May 1857, in the cantonment of Meerut, some forty miles northeast of Delhi, soldiers of the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry and two Native Infantry regiments turned on their officers, opened the jail, and freed eighty-five troopers who had been sentenced the previous day to long terms of imprisonment for refusing to handle new cartridges. By the next morning, mounted rebels had crossed the Yamuna into Delhi and appealed to an aged Mughal emperor who had not commanded an army in his life. Within weeks, the authority of the East India Company had collapsed across much of the Gangetic plain and central India. Within two years, it had been restored by force, and the Company itself had ceased to govern India.

This chapter reconstructs that crisis. The event it studies has carried many names, and the choice of a name is itself an argument. British contemporaries and many later writers called it the Indian Mutiny or the Sepoy Mutiny, emphasizing the revolt of soldiers against lawful authority. Indian nationalists, beginning with V. D. Savarkar in 1909, called it the First War of Independence, emphasizing anticolonial purpose and national unity. Many modern scholars use the more neutral terms rebellion, revolt, or uprising, while still debating how military, agrarian, political, and religious dimensions fit together. This textbook uses "the Rebellion of 1857" as its principal term, and the final sections return to the debate over naming (Section 69.19).

Chapter 68 showed that resistance to Company expansion was neither new nor confined to soldiers. Sannyasi and fakir raiders, Polygar chiefs, Paik militias, Kol and Santhal cultivators, and mutinous sepoys had all challenged Company authority before 1857. Chapter 69 takes up the moment when several of those streams of grievance converged, unevenly and for different reasons, in the largest armed challenge the Company ever faced. It concentrates on 1857–1859, with essential antecedents in 1856, and it ends with the transfer of government to the Crown in 1858 and the the decline of the principal surviving armed campaigns during 1859.

The argument of the chapter is that the Rebellion of 1857 was neither merely a sepoy mutiny nor a fully unified national independence movement. It began as a mutiny in the Bengal Army, a force whose high-caste, northern recruits had developed specific grievances about pay, status, discipline, and religious observance, and who were quickly alarmed by rumors that the new Enfield cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat. But the mutiny became a rebellion because other groups had their own reasons to reject Company authority. Dispossessed rulers and their dependents, taluqdars threatened by revenue settlements, cultivators burdened by assessments and debt, townspeople and artisans, and religious leaders alarmed by missionaries and by social legislation all participated, often with objectives that differed sharply from one another and from those of the soldiers.

The geography of rebellion was strikingly uneven. Delhi, Awadh, Rohilkhand, parts of the Doab, Bundelkhand, parts of Bihar, and portions of central India saw sustained rebellion. Punjab, Sindh, Bengal's countryside, and the Madras and Bombay presidencies largely did not. Major princely states, including Hyderabad, Gwalior, Indore, Bhopal, Patiala, and Kashmir, kept faith with the Company, though their armies and subjects sometimes did not. Sikh, Punjabi Muslim, Pashtun, and Gurkha forces fought for the British, as did many soldiers recruited in the Bengal Presidency itself. Understanding 1857 therefore means explaining not only why people rebelled but why many did not, and why a movement that took Delhi in a day could not govern it.

The chapter also takes violence seriously. Rebels killed British officers, civilians, women, and children at Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Jhansi, and elsewhere. British forces and their allies responded with summary executions, collective punishment, village burnings, and the sack of cities, and Indian civilians suffered extensive killing, dispossession, and displacement whose precise scale remains impossible to establish securely. Both kinds of violence shaped memory, propaganda, and policy for generations. A responsible account must describe both without reducing one to the other and without pretending that the combatants held equal power or faced equal consequences.

The evidence is unusually abundant but sharply unbalanced. British official reports, despatches, courts of inquiry, memoirs, and letters survive in great volume. Rebel proclamations, petitions, and orders survive in smaller numbers, many of them captured and translated by British officials, and much rebel testimony is filtered through trials and interrogations. Regional archives, oral traditions, Urdu and Persian chronicles, and later commemorative literature add other voices, but with their own problems of dating and reliability. This chapter's five Primary Source Windows ask students to read this evidence critically, asking in each case who produced it, for whom, and what perspectives are missing.

Finally, the chapter explains a central paradox. The rebels failed to overthrow British power, yet their rebellion helped to end the East India Company's system of government. Parliament abolished the Company's political authority in 1858, and the Queen's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 promised a new relationship between the Crown and its Indian subjects and princes. How that promise was kept, and how the institutions of the Raj were built on the ruins of 1857, belongs to Chapter 70 onward. This chapter prepares that account, showing how a revolt that failed became one of the hinges of modern South Asian history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political, military, and landholding grievances that existed in northern and central India on the eve of 1857, distinguishing structural causes from immediate triggers.
  2. Analyze the organization, recruitment, and service conditions of the Bengal Army and explain why it, rather than the Bombay or Madras armies, became the center of mutiny.
  3. Distinguish among documented cartridge practices, official remedies, rumors, and the beliefs of sepoys in the Enfield cartridge controversy.
  4. Reconstruct the sequence from Barrackpore and Meerut to the seizure of Delhi, keeping the events of 9, 10, and 11 May 1857 distinct.
  5. Trace the spread of rebellion through Awadh, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, and Bihar, and explain how each region's grievances and leaders differed.
  6. Evaluate the authority of Bahadur Shah Zafar and the attempts of Bakht Khan and others to create rebel government, and explain why no unified command emerged.
  7. Assess the participation of peasants, taluqdars, religious leaders, women, and urban communities, using contemporary evidence where possible and distinguishing it from later commemoration.
  8. Explain why rebellion was uneven across South Asia, including the roles of Punjab, the princely states, and Indian soldiers who fought for the Company.
  9. Describe the military campaigns that recaptured Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Kalpi, and Gwalior, and explain why resistance persisted after major cities fell.
  10. Analyze violence by rebels and by British forces and their allies without false equivalence or selective omission, and identify the limits of casualty evidence.
  11. Interpret the Government of India Act of 1858 and the Queen's Proclamation as evidence for the political consequences of the rebellion, distinguishing promise from implementation.
  12. Compare the main historiographical interpretations of 1857 and evaluate their use of colonial, rebel, regional, and nationalist evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a mutiny among soldiers become a rebellion involving rulers, landholders, cultivators, and townspeople in some regions but not in others?
  2. How did the annexation policies of the 1840s and 1850s, especially the annexation of Awadh in 1856, create conditions for revolt?
  3. What role did religion and caste observance play in the military crisis, and how can historians separate real military practice from rumor without dismissing the sepoys' fears?
  4. Why did Delhi, with a weak and elderly emperor, become the symbolic center of the rebellion?
  5. How did the aims of Nana Sahib, Begum Hazrat Mahal, Rani Lakshmibai, Kunwar Singh, and Bahadur Shah Zafar differ, and what did they have in common?
  6. Why did Punjab, many princely states, and large numbers of Indian soldiers support the Company?
  7. How do historians reconstruct rebel objectives when much rebel evidence survives through British translation, trial, and interrogation?
  8. How should historians describe and compare violence by rebels and violence by British forces without erasing differences of power and scale?
  9. Why did the rebellion fail militarily, and was that failure inevitable?
  10. Why did the defeat of the rebellion lead to the end of Company government and to the Queen's Proclamation?

Historical Context

By 1856 the East India Company ruled, directly or through subordinate alliances, most of the subcontinent. As Chapters 62 through 65 showed, this dominion was the result of a century of conquest, from Plassey and Buxar in Bengal to the defeat of Mysore, the Marathas, Sindh, and the Sikh state of Punjab in 1849. A governing structure of Company-appointed collectors, judges, and revenue officials sat above village and regional society, and a very large army, mostly Indian in composition and officered by Europeans, held it together. Chapter 66 showed how revenue systems such as the Permanent Settlement, ryotwari, and mahalwari arrangements reorganized rural landholding, and Chapter 67 showed how law, education, and missionary activity reshaped the institutions of public life.

The Company state was also, by 1856, a political order in tension with the older political forms it had absorbed. The Company's sovereignty over Indian rulers rested on treaties and subsidiary alliances, but under Governor-General Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856) it increasingly rested on a claim that the Company could decline to recognize adopted heirs of rulers whose states it regarded as dependent, annexing them as "lapsed." Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, and several smaller states were taken in this way, and Awadh was annexed in February 1856 on the different ground of alleged misgovernment. Each annexation displaced courts, dependents, soldiers, and landholders who had a stake in the old order. Even states that were not annexed felt the threat.

The Bengal Army, the largest of the Company's three presidency armies, drew many of its sepoys from the same northern region, notably Awadh and the Ganga valley, and a high proportion came from landholding and high-ritual-status communities. This produced a force with strong shared identities and with sensitivity to the rhythms of rural life, caste observance, and religious practice. It also produced an army whose discontents could spread quickly across regiments linked by kinship, village ties, and the long marches and cantonment networks of Company service. The Bombay and Madras armies recruited differently and were affected differently, a contrast examined in Section 69.2.

The political culture of north India also contained powerful memories of earlier authority. Although the Mughal emperor in Delhi had been reduced to a pensioner under Company protection since 1803, the court retained ceremonial prestige, a network of dependents, and a claim on the loyalty of Muslim and many Hindu elites. Rumors of British plans to end the dynasty, and a decision in 1856 to arrange the succession on terms that would remove the royal family from the Red Fort, increased anxiety. The Company's coins and courts had replaced Mughal ones, but its sovereignty still rested in law on an earlier Mughal grant, and that ambiguity would matter in May 1857.

Students should also remember the limits of communication and transport. The electric telegraph, extended rapidly in the 1850s, allowed British officials to coordinate responses, and its use on 11 May 1857 helped alert Punjab to the fall of Delhi. Railways, by contrast, were still sparse: lines ran in parts of Bengal and the Ganga valley but did not reach Delhi or Lucknow. Armies moved on foot, horse, bullock cart, and river steamer, and the monsoon of 1857 imposed its own limits on campaigning. These conditions shaped both the pace of the rebellion and the pace of its suppression.

The evidence base requires one further warning. Much of what is known about rebel intentions comes from documents captured, translated, and interpreted by British officials, from court-martial and trial records, and from British memoirs written under the pressure of anger and grief. Indian-language chronicles, regional records, and oral histories offer different perspectives but were often recorded decades later. Historians have therefore had to read British sources against the grain and combine them with proclamations, petitions, and local records. The chapter that follows models that method.

Section 69.1

Company Rule on the Eve of 1857: Political Expansion and Accumulating Grievances

The most notorious instrument was what later writers called the Doctrine of Lapse. Governor-General Lord Dalhousie held that when a ruler of a dependent state died without a natural heir, the Company, as paramount power, could decline to recognize an adopted successor and absorb the state. Satara in 1848, Sambalpur in 1849, Jhansi in 1853–1854, and Nagpur in 1854 were annexed on this basis, together with several smaller territories. Dalhousie also refused to continue the Peshwa's pension to the adopted son of Baji Rao II, who had died in 1851, and he made known his view that the Mughal title should end with Bahadur Shah Zafar. Whether or not these policies reflected a coherent doctrine, contemporaries experienced them as a pattern: succession was no longer secure for any ruler whose state the Company coveted.

Annexation did not only remove rulers. It dissolved courts that supported armies of retainers, scholars, poets, physicians, and artisans, and it cut off pensions and revenue assignments on which many households depended. Estates held on terms of service lost their basis. In some annexed territories, the Company introduced revenue settlements and courts on Bengal models that reassessed landholding rights and displaced established holders. The new system favored those who could pay fixed revenue in cash on fixed dates, and it exposed cultivators and middle-level proprietors to auction sales and indebtedness. These changes occurred over decades and in varying forms, so they do not explain a rebellion that broke out in a particular week. They do explain why the rebellion, once begun, found sympathizers among many who had lost status, income, or security.

Awadh illustrates the combination. The Nawabs of Awadh had been Company allies since the late eighteenth century, and their kingdom, with its capital at Lucknow, was one of the richest and most culturally prominent states in north India. It was also a principal recruiting ground for the Bengal Army. Lord Dalhousie, relying on a report by the Resident, William Sleeman, and on a broader view that the kingdom was misgoverned, ordered its annexation, and the deposition of King Wajid Ali Shah, a patron of poetry, music, and theater, was carried out in February 1856. The king was removed to Calcutta. His court was dissolved, and the new administration, under successive chief commissioners James Outram, Coverley Jackson, and, from March 1857, Henry Lawrence, began a summary revenue settlement that, as Section 69.5 shows, deprived many taluqdars of villages they had held. The annexation had been strongly opposed by some British officials, and others warned that Awadh's soldiers in the Bengal Army would regard it as a breach of the Company's own commitments.

Historical Vocabulary
Doctrine of Lapse

The phrase describes the practice, associated above all with Governor-General Dalhousie, of refusing to recognize adopted heirs in dependent princely states and annexing them for want of a natural heir. The Company did not create a single formal statute by that name, and earlier governors-general had occasionally taken similar decisions. Dalhousie, however, applied the idea more systematically than his predecessors, and his minute defending annexations gave it a theoretical form.

The policy mattered because Hindu law and custom recognized adoption as a legitimate means of continuing a line, and because rulers had been told that treaties guaranteed their rights. It undermined confidence in Company promises. Historians now emphasize that it was one grievance among many, but it explains the anger of rulers such as Nana Sahib and Rani Lakshmibai, and it contributed to a general feeling, even among rulers who stayed loyal, that Company protection was conditional and revocable.

Taxation, law, and social legislation added to the unease. The Company's courts were costly, distant, and slow, and litigants often found that those who understood English legal procedure and could pay fees prevailed over holders of older customary claims. Missionary activity, which the Company officially did not sponsor but which enjoyed the quiet support of many officials, alarmed Hindus and Muslims. The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856, the abolition of sati in 1829, and an act of 1850 on inheritance by converts, which changed the property position of those who left their communities, were seen by some as part of a plan to alter society. Historians continue to debate how widely such fears were held, but proclamations issued during the rebellion repeatedly invoked religion as under threat, and the next two sections show how this anxiety acquired a military form.

Section 69.2

The Bengal Army: Recruitment, Service Conditions, Caste, Religion, and Military Discipline

The Company maintained three presidency armies, the Bengal, Madras, and Bombay armies, each with its own officers, recruitment, and customs. By 1857, the Bengal Army was by far the largest, comprising well over a hundred thousand Indian soldiers in regular and irregular units, led by British officers and supported by comparatively few European troops. It had expanded as the Company conquered the north and had been shaped by decades of recruitment from the Ganga valley, especially from Awadh, Bihar, and the Doab, among Brahmin, Rajput, and other high-ritual-status communities who also, in many cases, held land. Such recruits regarded military service as honorable and hereditary, and many regiments drew on the same villages, linked by kinship, marriage, and shared experience.

Historical Vocabulary
Sepoy (sipahi)

The word derives from Persian sipahi, meaning soldier, and in Company usage it designated an Indian infantry soldier in a European-officered regiment. Cavalry troopers were called sowars. Sepoys were professional soldiers paid by the Company, not feudal levies, though many belonged to landholding families who also valued status and land at home.

The term is important because the word "sepoy mutiny" can encourage the assumption that the rebellion was the revolt of ordinary hired soldiers, when Bengal Army sepoys were in many cases men of substance whose families depended on landholding and rural reputations. That social location helps explain both the speed with which the army's discontent connected to rural grievances and the sepoys' sensitivity about ritual status.

Within the regiments, the hierarchy was strongly racial. Indian officers, the subedars and jemadars, and the non-commissioned havildars and naiks, could rise only to limited ranks, and an Indian officer of long service might be junior in authority to a newly arrived British ensign. Pay for sepoys was fixed at low rates that had hardly changed for decades, and cost-of-living pressures were felt unevenly. Promotion in the Bengal Army had come to rely on seniority, which produced aging Indian officers and reduced incentives for talent, while British officers increasingly left regimental duty for staff and civil employments, weakening personal ties with their men. Historians have argued that the personal relationship between British officers and sepoys, once close, had eroded by the 1850s, and that British officers showed less understanding of religious sensitivity and more readiness to see Indian soldiers as childlike or untrustworthy.

A series of earlier changes had also created friction. The Company had expected the Bengal Army to serve in newly annexed territories, including Sindh and Punjab, and in these campaigns it had cut or withdrawn extra allowances (batta) that had once compensated for service outside the Company's older territories. Regiments mutinied in 1849–1850 on this question. The Post Office Act of 1854 withdrew the free postage that sepoys had enjoyed. Overseas service was a particular concern. Many high-caste Hindus believed that crossing the sea ("black water") led to loss of caste, and the Bengal Army had long had a custom, not a legal right, of not being ordered overseas. A mutiny of the 47th Native Infantry at Barrackpore in 1824 had been triggered by the order to go to Burma. In 1852 the Company again needed troops for Burma and struggled to find willing Bengal units.

The general-service enlistment order of 25 July 1856 was meant to resolve this. It made all new recruits liable to serve wherever the government ordered, including overseas. Serving sepoys retained the terms under which they had enlisted, so the act did not apply retroactively to the existing army. Historians nonetheless note the effect of the measure on morale. Sepoys feared that the existing exemption would be eroded in practice, that recruits would be drawn from lower castes or from those willing to cross the sea, and that regiments would be altered in ways that threatened the caste purity of the ranks. The perception, whatever the legal detail, was that the Company was changing the terms of service without consultation. This distinction between what the act did and what sepoys feared it implied is essential for understanding the military crisis.

Religion and caste were embedded in military life in complex ways. The Company, for generations, had accommodated sepoys' ritual requirements, allowing separate cooking, respecting festivals, and avoiding actions regarded as polluting. Brahmin sepoys, in particular, were known to have ritual purity concerns about food, water, and touch. This accommodation depended on tact. In the 1850s, evangelical Christianity gained ground among some British officers, and a few openly urged sepoys to convert. Sepoys, who had often been told that the Company respected their religion, were quick to notice. Whether or not anyone in authority planned conversion, the perception that the Company's power and Christian proselytizing were linked became a powerful element in the mutinous mood.

The other presidency armies recruited from different regional and social networks and had different service expectations. Their lower incidence of collective mutiny was not proof of universal loyalty: localized protests occurred, and many Indian soldiers served in loyal formations because of particular regimental histories, political commitments, and material circumstances. Explaining these contrasts requires attention to recruitment and command rather than essentializing any community.

Section 69.3

The Enfield Cartridge Controversy, Barrackpore, and the Escalation of Military Discontent

In the early 1850s, the British army began replacing smoothbore muskets with the rifled Pattern 1853 Enfield, a weapon with greater range and accuracy. The Company decided to introduce it for the Bengal Army too, and rifle instruction began in 1856 at musketry depots, including Dum Dum near Calcutta, Ambala, and Sialkot. The Enfield used a paper cartridge containing powder and a lubricated bullet. In loading, the soldier tore off the end of the cartridge, traditionally with his teeth, poured out the powder, and rammed the bullet down the barrel. The lubricant, typically tallow or a mixture of fats, was needed to ease loading and to prevent fouling.

By January 1857, a rumor circulated at Dum Dum and beyond that the cartridges were greased with the fat of cows and pigs. For Hindu sepoys, the cow was sacred, and biting a cartridge greased with beef fat would be a ritual offense. For Muslim sepoys, pork was forbidden. The rumor therefore appeared to threaten both communities at once and suggested a deliberate plan to destroy their religions and compel them to become Christians. One popular story, probably apocryphal in its details, held that a low-caste laborer at Dum Dum had asked a Brahmin sepoy for water from his vessel and, when the latter objected on grounds of ritual purity, had told him that the cartridges would soon destroy his caste anyway. Whatever the truth of such anecdotes, they spread quickly through cantonments, helped by letters, travel between regiments, and ordinary conversation.

Historians must separate several issues. First, there was a real practice: the Company's cartridges were greased, and the grease at various times included tallow, which in the English context could include beef fat. Whether particular batches at Woolwich or Dum Dum contained both beef and pork fat is difficult to determine, and the debate has continued since 1857. Second, there was an official response. By late January and February 1857, military authorities, alerted to the sepoys' concerns, ordered that cartridges be issued ungreased and that sepoys apply their own grease, such as beeswax or ghee, and permitted tearing the cartridge by hand rather than with the teeth. Third, there was the sepoys' perception. By the time the concession was made, many men no longer trusted the assurance, interpreted the change as an admission of guilt, and suspected that other cartridges or even flour and other supplies had been polluted. Rumor was not an irrational invention detached from reality, but it spread in a context of mistrust where official reassurance came late and unconvincingly.

The first overt resistance occurred at Berhampore, in Bengal, where the 19th Native Infantry refused percussion caps on 26 February 1857, believing that the cartridge issue was being pressed on them. The regiment was marched to Barrackpore and disbanded on 31 March, its soldiers stripped of uniforms and discharged. Disbandment, a common Company response to mutiny, carried heavy consequences, since it ended careers and removed men from the pensions and standing that service provided. It also created a body of resentful men who traveled home and carried their story with them.

Before the 19th was disbanded, on 29 March 1857, at Barrackpore, Mangal Pandey, a sepoy of the 34th Native Infantry, appeared on the parade ground armed, called on comrades to rise, and attacked the adjutant, Lieutenant Baugh, and the sergeant-major. A jemadar, Ishwari Prasad, who was ordered to arrest him, did not do so, and other sepoys held back. Pandey tried to shoot himself, was wounded, and was tried and hanged on 8 April. Ishwari Prasad was hanged on 21 April, and the 34th Native Infantry was later disbanded. Evidence for Pandey's motives is thin. British accounts described him as under the influence of bhang or opium, and his own testimony, such as it was, shows little of the political ideas later attributed to him. His enduring fame in nationalist memory reflects how he came to symbolize a moment, not what we can establish about his intentions.

Section 69.4

Meerut, May 10, 1857, and the Seizure of Delhi

Meerut was one of the largest military stations in north India. Its garrison included not only Indian infantry and cavalry but also a substantial European contingent, a battalion of rifles, a regiment of dragoons (the Carabineers), and artillery, which made it among the best-protected stations. Its commanding officer, Major-General William Hewitt, was elderly and had a reputation for inertia, and the brigade commander, Brigadier Archdale Wilson, was no more decisive. On 24 April 1857, Colonel George Carmichael-Smyth, commanding the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry, ordered ninety men of his regiment to parade for a firing exercise with the cartridges. All but five refused. Evidence suggests that the cartridges in question were not the greased Enfield cartridges but a version the men were to tear by hand; the refusal arose from the mistrust the earlier rumors had caused.

A court of inquiry and then a court-martial were convened. Eighty-five of the troopers were convicted and, on 8 May, sentenced to ten years' imprisonment with hard labor; five younger men received shorter sentences. On the morning of 9 May, the whole garrison was paraded, the eighty-five were stripped of their uniforms, shackled by blacksmiths in front of their comrades, and marched to jail. For troopers of long service, many of whom had served in the Company's wars, the ceremony was a public humiliation, and the fact that it occurred before the assembled European and Indian troops made the message plain. Several troopers are recorded as appealing to their officers and to their comrades as they were led away. The sepoys who watched were left in no doubt that the punishment for refusing the cartridges could be severe, and that they could face the same.

Delhi's garrison, three Native Infantry regiments and some artillery, joined the rebels within hours, and violence against Europeans, including the commissioner Simon Fraser, the captain of the palace guard, and missionaries, spread across the city.

British officers in Delhi attempted to resist. A group of ordnance officers led by Lieutenant George Willoughby blew up part of the magazine to keep its stores from the rebels, killing many attackers and some of their own; Willoughby survived the blast and was killed later. Telegraph signalers sent a warning to Lahore and Ambala before the line was cut, and that message allowed Punjab's officials to begin disarming doubtful regiments. The telegram remains one of the most famous in the history of British India. Its importance is that it gave Punjab, whose forces would be crucial, advance notice that Delhi had fallen.

Bahadur Shah Zafar, then in his eighties, had no army and little revenue. The rebels' appeal placed him in a dilemma. If he refused, he might be killed by mutineers or regarded as a collaborator; if he accepted, he would stand against the Company that paid his pension and would risk everything. He accepted, on 12 May, as a sovereign in whose name orders would be issued, and the rebels proclaimed him emperor of Hindustan. Historians have debated his motives, and the best evidence is that he was neither a conspirator nor a mere puppet but an old man with limited options, committed to the dignity of his house. On 16 May, a number of European prisoners held in the palace, including women and children, were killed in a courtyard, an event whose responsibility has been traced to the palace guards and princes rather than to a decision by the emperor alone, and which strengthened British resolve.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Rebellion Break Out in May 1857?

Long-term causes

Annexation of states and displacement of courts; revenue settlements that unsettled landholders; erosion of the personal bond between officers and sepoys; religious and social anxiety about missionary activity and legislation; stagnation in sepoy pay and promotion.

Short-term causes

The annexation of Awadh (1856); the General Service Enlistment Order (1856); the Enfield cartridge rumors; the disbandments at Berhampore and Barrackpore; the sentencing and shackling of the 3rd Light Cavalry troopers at Meerut.

Event

The rising at Meerut on 10 May and the seizure of Delhi on 11 May.

Immediate effects

Mutinies at cantonments across the Bengal Army; collapse of civil authority in parts of the north; a political center in the Mughal capital; British mobilization from Punjab and Calcutta.

Long-term consequences

The end of Company government; army reorganization; the Queen's Proclamation; transformed relations between the Crown, princes, and landholders.

Historical complexity

No single cause suffices. The cartridge issue mattered because it fell on soil prepared by other grievances, and the Meerut rising mattered because it occurred in a station where authority was weak and where the Delhi route was open.

Section 69.5

Awadh after Annexation: Taluqdars, Soldiers, Cultivators, and Popular Rebellion

After Delhi fell, the rebellion spread through the Bengal Army's stations in a pattern that reflected both the speed of communication and the readiness of each garrison. Within weeks, regiments rose at Aligarh, Mathura, Bareilly, Shahjahanpur, Moradabad, Benares, Allahabad, Kanpur, Lucknow, and numerous smaller posts. In many places, rebel soldiers freed prisoners, looted treasuries, destroyed official records, and left to join larger concentrations. But nowhere did rebellion take deeper root than in Awadh, a kingdom that had been annexed barely a year before and whose countryside, once the soldiers had removed the Company's administrators, largely passed out of Company control for most of a year.

The annexation had separated the kingdom's political order from its social order. The Nawab's court, with its offices, patronage, and entourage, had disappeared. Much of the population that had depended on it, including poets, musicians, pensioners, clerks, and retainers, was unemployed. At the same time, the Company's officials began a revenue settlement that assessed the land and recorded who would pay revenue directly to the state. Awadh's rural elite, the taluqdars, held large estates and exercised local authority, collecting revenue from villages, maintaining armed retainers, and acting as intermediaries between the state and cultivators. The Company viewed this as an unjustified layer standing between the state and the peasant, and the summary settlement of 1856 aimed to strip taluqdars of villages that, in the Company's view, they had acquired illegitimately. Many lost a large proportion of their holdings, and in several districts, the new revenue-holders were village proprietors, auction purchasers, or others favored by the new order.

Historical Vocabulary
Taluqdar

In Awadh the term designated a landholder who held a taluqa, a block of villages, from which he collected revenue, usually retaining a share and paying the remainder to the state. Taluqdars ranged from lineage chiefs, often Rajputs with claims to ancient family authority, to officials and revenue farmers whose estates had been acquired more recently. They maintained fortified residences and bands of armed followers.

The term is important because Company officials viewed taluqdars as obstacles to direct settlement with cultivators, but many cultivators in Awadh relied on them for protection, credit, and mediation. Thus the dispossession of taluqdars in 1856 did not simply liberate peasants from intermediaries. It removed a layer of local authority that peasants and landlords both recognized, and when rebellion came, many peasants followed their former lords rather than the Company's new revenue officers.

The people affected were not a single group. Taluqdars who lost villages feared permanent decline, and some had ancestral grievances against the Company's courts. Peasants confronted higher assessments in a period of poor harvests, and the new revenue officials were strangers who enforced collection with less flexibility than the Nawab's agents. Sepoys, many of whom came from Awadh villages, had both economic and political connections to the rural order: their families often held plots under taluqdars, and soldiers who went on leave brought home news of cantonment grievances and learned of the family's losses. Townspeople in Lucknow, including artisans who made luxury goods for the court, lost their patrons. Religious scholars and shrine managers who had received royal grants worried about the continuation of endowments.

The rebellion therefore spread with the support of this overlapping set of grievances, and historians such as Rudrangshu Mukherjee have argued that in Awadh the revolt had a markedly popular character, in which soldiers, taluqdars, and peasants acted together against a state they regarded as alien. This should not be taken to mean that all participants shared objectives. Taluqdars sought restoration of their estates and their position, and many wanted a restored Awadh court in which they would hold influence. Peasants were concerned with revenue demands, with protection against creditors and new owners, and with the lord they trusted. Soldiers wanted to avoid punishment, protect their religion, and regain the honor and standing they believed the Company had denied. Urban groups were concerned with the loss of court patronage and with local order. These aims could be reconciled in a general opposition to the Company, but they did not form a single program.

By the summer of 1857, most of rural Awadh had gone out of Company control. Villagers refused revenue, attacked police posts and records, and in many cases sheltered fugitives from British columns. The rebellion's persistence after the loss of Lucknow in March 1858 stemmed from this local base. Regional leaders such as Raja Man Singh of Shahganj, who at one point protected British refugees and at another aided the rebels, and Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah, whose role is discussed in Section 69.11, mobilized local followings that did not disappear when the army was defeated in the field. This helps explain why the British, even after retaking the capital, needed to reconquer Awadh village by village.

Section 69.6

Kanpur: Nana Sahib, the Siege, and the Violence of June–July 1857

Kanpur (then usually spelled Cawnpore) was a major military station on the Ganga, with a garrison of Indian infantry and cavalry and only a small European component. It lay on the road between Calcutta and the Punjab, and it was close to Bithur, the residence of Dhondu Pant, known as Nana Sahib, adopted son of the last Peshwa, Baji Rao II. After the defeat of the Marathas in 1818, the Company had removed Baji Rao to Bithur and granted him a large pension. When he died in 1851, the Company recognized neither Nana Sahib's claim to succeed him as Peshwa nor his entitlement to the pension, and a petition conveyed to London through an agent, Azimullah Khan, had no effect. Nana Sahib thus had a personal and political grievance, but he had also maintained cordial relations with British officers and entertained them at Bithur, and the British commander, General Sir Hugh Wheeler, trusted him enough to accept his offer of a guard for the treasury.

On the night of 4–5 June 1857, the sepoys at Kanpur rose, plundered the treasury, and, at first, prepared to march on Delhi. Nana Sahib joined them, and some accounts suggest that he and Azimullah Khan persuaded the rebels to remain and attack the British. Wheeler, an aged officer with long experience of the Indian army, had withdrawn his European families and a few hundred soldiers into a poorly designed entrenchment around two barrack buildings on an open plain, with limited supplies and a well exposed to fire. The siege began on 6 June, during the hot season, with intense bombardment from rebel artillery and numerous assaults. Within three weeks, the defenders had suffered heavy losses from shelling, heat, thirst, and disease.

On 25–26 June, negotiations began. Wheeler's garrison agreed to surrender in exchange for safe passage by boat to Allahabad. The terms are documented in British accounts, which also say that Nana Sahib approved them, and subsequent accounts agree that the occupants were taken to boats at the Satichaura Ghat on the river on 27 June. What happened then is disputed in detail. Firing broke out as the Europeans boarded the boats; the boats caught fire or were stranded; and many men were killed in the water or on shore. It is agreed that the rebel forces, including cavalry and artillery, killed many of the departing men. Historians disagree about whether the firing had been planned or began as a result of panic or an accident; some trust British survivors' testimony that treachery was intended, others point out that the evidence is incomplete and that soldiers on the riverbank, not Nana Sahib personally, may have started the attack. Women and children survivors, together with a few men, were taken prisoner.

The prisoners, around two hundred women and children by most accounts, were confined at the Savada House and then at the Bibighar, a small building in Kanpur. In the second week of July, news arrived that a British force under General Henry Havelock was advancing from Allahabad, having defeated rebel forces in battles on the way. On or about 15 July, the prisoners were killed, and their bodies were thrown into a nearby well. The decision is generally attributed to rebel leaders in Kanpur, but individual responsibility, including that of Nana Sahib, is not established by reliable evidence, and sources contradict one another about who gave the order. The killing was carried out by men from the rebel force. Havelock entered Kanpur on 17 July and found the Bibighar's ground and the well.

The role of Tatya Tope and other associates also requires care. Tatya Tope, an official of Nana Sahib's household, later emerged as the most important rebel general in central India, but his part in the events of June–July 1857 at Kanpur is described mainly through British accounts compiled after his capture or after the war. These accounts range from portraits of him as a bold commander to claims that he planned the killings. Historians generally conclude that his precise role cannot be settled from the evidence available. Nana Sahib himself fled after Havelock's arrival. His fate remains unknown; he is thought to have escaped into Nepal around 1858–1859, and rumors of his survival persisted for decades.

The consequences of Kanpur far exceeded its military importance. News of the killings of women and children, reported in Britain in sensational form, shaped public opinion and political discourse. Newspapers and pamphlets portrayed rebels as savage and inhuman, and some political voices demanded vengeance. British soldiers moving toward Kanpur and in the city itself carried out reprisals. General James Neill, who commanded at Kanpur after Havelock's arrival, ordered that captured rebels be compelled to clean the blood-stained floor of the Bibighar before being hanged, a measure designed to break caste observance as an additional humiliation. Villages near the route were burned, and many suspected rebels or bystanders were executed. These reprisals, discussed further in Section 69.16, were not the only violence of the period, but they demonstrate how Kanpur became a rationale for retaliation, and how the memory of the event was used to justify later severity.

Section 69.7

Lucknow: Begum Hazrat Mahal, Birjis Qadr, and the Struggle for Awadh

Lucknow, capital of the recently annexed kingdom, was held at the start of 1857 by a relatively small European garrison and a larger Indian one. Sir Henry Lawrence, appointed Chief Commissioner in March, was an experienced administrator who understood the dangers. He had sought to calm sepoy suspicion, and he prepared the Residency, a walled compound of buildings on high ground, as a refuge, stockpiling supplies and strengthening defenses. When the Indian troops at Lucknow rose on 30 May, Lawrence suppressed the first outbreak, but over the following weeks, the countryside around Lucknow turned against the Company. On 30 June, Lawrence, with a sortie force of some hundreds of Europeans and loyal Indian troops, was defeated at Chinhat, a few miles from the city, by a rebel force that included Awadh soldiers and local levies, and the British withdrew to the Residency. Lawrence was mortally wounded by a shell on 2 July and died on 4 July.

The Residency siege, therefore, began before the rebels had established civil authority in Lucknow, and the political side of the rebellion in Awadh developed alongside the military struggle. On 5 July, the rebels proclaimed Birjis Qadr, the eleven- or twelve-year-old son of the deposed king, Wajid Ali Shah, as ruler of Awadh (wali), with the Mughal emperor in Delhi as nominal suzerain. The young prince's mother, Begum Hazrat Mahal, assumed the work of regency. She had been a member of Wajid Ali Shah's court, and her political ability, evident in her management of competing taluqdars, soldiers, and officials, made her the effective head of the Awadh government. She appointed officials, organized revenue collection and military supply, and sought alliances with landholders and religious leaders.

The Residency's siege lasted until November. It was one of the most closely recorded episodes of the war, thanks to British memoirs and diaries, and has an extensive, partly legendary literature. The garrison and refugees, about a thousand Europeans and a comparable number of loyal Indians, including sepoys who stayed with their officers, endured bombardment, mining, shortages, disease, and sniping. Brigadier John Inglis took command after Lawrence's death. Rebel besiegers held a much larger force but failed to take the Residency by storm, in part because of poor coordination and in part because the compound's defenses held. The role of loyal Indian soldiers, sometimes overlooked in the traditional narrative, was substantial: they formed a large part of the defending force.

On 25 September, a relief column under Generals Henry Havelock and James Outram fought its way into the Residency through the streets of Lucknow at heavy cost. It did not, however, end the siege. The relievers, too few to break the encirclement and to evacuate the civilians, became part of the besieged garrison. A second relief, under Commander-in-Chief Sir Colin Campbell, arrived in November, after Campbell's force had fought its way across the Ganga and into the city, capturing the Sikandar Bagh on 16 November in a bloody assault in which many rebel defenders were killed. The garrison and civilians were evacuated between 19 and 22 November, and Campbell withdrew to the Alambagh outside the city, leaving Lucknow in rebel hands. Havelock, exhausted by campaigning and disease, died on 24 November. It is essential to distinguish the relief of September, the relief and evacuation of November, and the recapture of the city in March 1858 (Section 69.14): the first two rescued a garrison but did not reconquer Lucknow.

Section 69.8

Jhansi and Central India: Rani Lakshmibai, Dynastic Claims, and the Spread of Rebellion

Jhansi, a small Maratha-ruled state in Bundelkhand, had been a British ally since the early nineteenth century. Its ruler, Raja Gangadhar Rao, died in November 1853 without a natural heir, having adopted a boy, Damodar Rao, shortly before. Dalhousie refused to recognize the adoption and, in March 1854, annexed the state. The Rani, Lakshmibai, who was in her twenties, was granted a pension and a residence but lost the throne and, according to British sources, protested the decision through petitions, which were rejected. She therefore belonged to the circle of rulers and dependents who had been deprived by lapse, though in 1857 she lived quietly in Jhansi and was regarded by many British officers as loyal to the Company.

In early June 1857, the Company's Indian soldiers at Jhansi, men of the 12th Native Infantry and other units, rose. The rising followed the pattern of other stations: they seized the arsenal and the Star Fort and, on 7–8 June, a group of British officers, civilians, women, and children who had sought refuge in the fort surrendered on a promise of safety and were killed outside the town. The number killed is generally given as several dozen. The rebels' action was the work of soldiers and others in the town. The Rani's role is disputed. British accounts, written under the influence of Kanpur and Delhi, accused her of complicity, and some alleged that she gave the order. Evidence of her correspondence at the time suggests that she wrote to British officials after the event to say that the rebels had forced her to supply them and had threatened her, and some historians accept this explanation while others regard it as an effort to protect herself. The fair conclusion is that her knowledge and responsibility remain contested, and students should not treat either accusation or exoneration as settled fact.

After the killings, the Rani took charge of the city. She governed in the name of her adopted son, struck up relations with neighboring rulers, and attempted to organize defense. Her efforts brought her into conflict with other claimants, and in 1857 she faced attacks from the rulers of Orchha and Datia, which were allied to the British, and defeated them. Over several months she raised troops, repaired the fort, and cast cannon. Her position was not a simple continuation of her husband's rule. It was an interim authority created by the collapse of Company power in the region, and it depended on soldiers, local landholders, and her own determination. Her reasons for choosing to defend Jhansi against the British are unclear, but she was apparently convinced by early 1858 that reconciliation was impossible.

Central India more broadly was a patchwork of Maratha, Bundela, Rajput, and other states and British districts. The Gwalior Contingent, an army maintained at the expense of the state but officered by Britons, rose in June 1857 in spite of the loyalty of its ruler, Maharaja Jayajirao Scindia, and of his minister, Dinkar Rao. Troops at Indore, Mhow, Neemuch, Nasirabad, and elsewhere rose in June and July. These risings created a belt of rebellion across central India, and the rebels' ability to move between regions made it possible for commanders such as Tatya Tope to draw support from different sources. Here, too, the pattern was complex. Rulers such as Scindia and Holkar, though under pressure, refrained from joining the rebellion and in some cases helped British officers; but their armies and some of their subjects did not obey.

Tatya Tope appears in central India after leaving Kanpur. He took command of the Gwalior Contingent and with it attacked Kanpur in November 1857, nearly retaking it before being defeated by Campbell on 6 December. He then retreated toward Kalpi and Jhansi, and was a central figure in the campaigns discussed in Section 69.15. Others involved in the region included Rao Sahib, a nephew of Nana Sahib, and the Nawab of Banda, and the Rani's alliance with them reflects the web of connections among dispossessed rulers and soldiers that underlay the rebellion in central India. These were military and political networks more than ideological ones, and they allowed the rebellion to continue in the region even after Delhi's fall.

Section 69.9

Bihar and Jagdishpur: Kunwar Singh and the Eastern Campaigns

The rebellion in Bihar developed later and followed a different course from the risings at Delhi, Kanpur, and Lucknow. Bihar, part of the Bengal Presidency, was a region of dense rural population, important for opium and indigo production, and part of the Company's old territories. Its cantonment at Danapur, near Patna, held three Native Infantry regiments, and Patna itself was a city of Muslim scholars and merchants, some of whom were associated with religious reform movements. In June 1857 the Patna commissioner, William Tayler, acting on suspicion, arrested prominent local figures, including booksellers and Muslim religious leaders, and used force to suppress a disturbance. Historians disagree about whether these measures prevented a wider rising or created grievances, but the Patna episode shows how quickly suspicion could harden into repression.

The Bihar rebellion has been identified mainly with Kunwar Singh, a Rajput landholder of Jagdishpur in the Shahabad district who was, by 1857, around eighty years old. His family had once been of high status, with a large estate, but by the 1850s it was heavily in debt and, as in other regions, vulnerable to the Company's revenue system and the courts. The Company's officials regarded the estate with suspicion, and Kunwar Singh's debts led to the possibility of its management or sale. Such pressures, combined with local ties to the sepoy community and a reputation for independence, made him a natural focus for discontent when the mutiny arrived, though sources do not establish that he had planned rebellion beforehand.

On 25 July 1857, the three regiments at Danapur rose, crossed the Son, and marched toward Arrah, where Kunwar Singh joined them. Arrah's small European community, with some Sikh soldiers and loyal Indians, took refuge in a fortified house belonging to a railway engineer, and held out against a larger force from 27 July until a relief column under Major Vincent Eyre defeated the rebels near Arrah on 2–3 August. A first relief attempt from Danapur had been defeated with heavy losses. The Arrah siege became one of the best-known British episodes of the rebellion, and the evidence for it comes largely from the survivors, who naturally emphasized the heroism of the defense. For the rebel side, it showed both the ability of Kunwar Singh's following to mobilize in a region without a traditional rebel court and the limits of their organization.

After Eyre's victory, Kunwar Singh withdrew from Jagdishpur and began a long campaign across northern India. With a mobile force of soldiers, retainers, and followers, he moved west into Awadh and the area around Azamgarh, took part in the fighting in the region, and defeated British detachments, taking advantage of knowledge of the terrain and local support. In March 1858, he took Azamgarh, but had to retreat when British forces approached. He then returned toward Bihar, evading pursuit, and on 23 April 1858 defeated a British detachment under Captain Le Grand near Jagdishpur. Seriously wounded in an earlier engagement while crossing the Ganga, he died on 26 April 1858, soon after reaching his home. His brother Amar Singh continued the struggle in the region for some months, but without Kunwar Singh's authority the rebellion in Shahabad gradually faded.

The composition of Kunwar Singh's following illustrates the variety of the rebellion. Danapur sepoys formed its military core. Landholders and retainers in Shahabad, bound to him by caste, kinship, and patronage, provided men and supplies. Cultivators supported him in some localities and not in others. A number of Muslim and Hindu participants appear in the records, and the evidence suggests that his authority depended on personal loyalties more than on a defined ideology. The Company's officials responded by confiscating estates and rewarding loyal Indians, reinforcing the sense that the conflict involved competing claims to local power.

Section 69.10

Rebel Leadership and Political Authority: Bahadur Shah Zafar, Bakht Khan, and Competing Visions

The rebellion's central political problem was authority. Soldiers who mutinied had overthrown their officers, but they needed a legitimate source of command and a means of paying and feeding themselves. In Delhi, they turned to the Mughal emperor, an act that gave the rebellion a recognized sovereign and tied it to older conceptions of political order. Bahadur Shah Zafar, however, was elderly, lacked military experience, and had spent decades as a pensioner with a small court. His family, large and politically ambitious, contained princes who sought command. Mirza Mughal, a son of the emperor, was appointed commander-in-chief of the rebel forces, but he had no military experience, and his authority was ignored by the soldiers, whose regiments retained their own officers and loyalties.

The emperor issued orders, appointed officials, and attempted to restore normal administration. Revenue collection was assigned to officials in surrounding districts, and measures were taken to deal with prices and with soldiers who looted. Letters were sent to rulers in Rajputana and elsewhere asking them to support the cause, and few responded favorably. The emperor, according to the documents, also expressed concern about the discipline of the soldiers and the sufferings of Delhi's residents, which, as the city filled with mutineers, grew steadily worse. These facts show an effort at government, but they also show a limited capacity: the emperor's writ did not extend far outside Delhi, and the regiments often acted on their own.

Elsewhere, political authority took different forms. Nana Sahib asserted the position of Peshwa, drawing on Maratha tradition, but his authority was local to Kanpur and Bithur. In Awadh, Begum Hazrat Mahal and Birjis Qadr governed in the name of the restored kingdom, and they acknowledged the Mughal emperor but acted autonomously. Rani Lakshmibai ruled Jhansi on behalf of her adopted son. Kunwar Singh was a regional leader. Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah combined religious and military leadership. Some rebels invoked jihad, others drew on Hindu and Maratha symbols, and many used the language of restoring justice and protecting religion. These authorities recognized one another unevenly and had no common treasury, high command, or political program.

Section 69.11

Peasants, Taluqdars, Religious Leaders, Women, and Urban Communities

In the countryside, rebellion often took the form of attacks on the instruments of the new order: police posts, revenue offices, courts, and the records and account books that documented debts and land claims. The destruction of records, a common feature across several regions, signals a political motive. Villagers refused to pay revenue, and some collected it themselves or allowed their landlords to do so. Disputes over land sometimes drove local rebellion: communities that had lost land through auction sales or settlement used the moment to reclaim it. In other places, long-standing rivalries between villages or castes were expressed through the rebellion, or against it, so that the rebellion should be seen as a multiplicity of local conflicts as well as a general war.

A well-documented example is Shah Mal, a Jat village headman in the Baraut area, west of the Yamuna in the Meerut region. After the rising at Meerut, he mobilized villagers in a group of villages and attacked British communications and officials, taking control of local resources and supporting the rebel cause. He was killed in July 1857 in a clash with a British force, and his head was reportedly displayed. The record of his career comes from British official sources and local tradition, and the details of his objectives are unclear, but his case shows how rural leaders emerged outside the structures of rulers and soldiers. Gujar communities in the Meerut and Delhi region, who had grievances about revenue and policing, also joined the rising and attacked British lines of communication.

Religious leaders and institutions contributed to the rebellion in varying ways. Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah, known as the Maulvi of Faizabad, had been preaching against the British for some time before 1857 and had been imprisoned by the Company in early 1857. Released when the rebellion broke out, he became one of the most effective commanders in Awadh, fighting at Chinhat and later in Rohilkhand, and was respected by many Hindus and Muslims. He was killed at Powayan in June 1858 by the raja of that place, who received a reward. Other religious figures, Hindu and Muslim, preached resistance or provided spiritual sanction. Their appeals drew on religious obligation and fear of conversion, but also on political grievance. Not all religious leaders supported the rebellion: some Muslim scholars and Sufi leaders opposed it, and Syed Ahmad Khan, a Muslim official, assisted British families at Bijnor and later wrote a critical analysis of the causes of the revolt.

Women's participation is documented but difficult to reconstruct. Elite women such as Begum Hazrat Mahal and Rani Lakshmibai exercised political and military authority. The Begum of Awadh directed government, and other women, including the wife of Raja Man Singh and the women of royal households, played roles in organizing resources. Among less prominent figures, Uda Devi is remembered in Dalit commemorative tradition in Lucknow as a woman of the Pasi community who fought on the rebel side at the Sikandar Bagh, firing on British troops from a tree. British accounts of the Sikandar Bagh assault refer to a sharpshooter in a tree, and some later accounts identify the person as a woman, but the identification with Uda Devi rests mainly on later oral and commemorative sources, and historians treat the details with caution. Women also appear as victims, as refugees, and as household managers whose labor supported soldiers and civilians in besieged cities.

Urban communities experienced the rebellion differently. In Delhi, merchants and artisans faced demands for contributions, loss of trade, and the presence of unpaid soldiers; some supported the rebel cause, while others sought order and complained of plunder. In Lucknow, former court dependents, craftsmen, and townspeople supported the Begum's government and the defense of the city. In Kanpur, Allahabad, Benares, and other centers, popular crowds participated in attacks on British property and persons, and often on moneylenders as well. The rebellion therefore had a social dimension in urban areas: the revolt of the poor against the rich, or of debtors against creditors, sometimes coincided with political rebellion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through the Rebellion

For most inhabitants of northern India, 1857 was experienced less as a campaign than as a disruption of ordinary life. In rebel-held cities, shopkeepers closed or reopened according to the presence of soldiers, grain prices rose with the arrival of armies, and households hid valuables and sent women and children to the countryside. In the countryside, villages sheltered fugitives, supplied or refused grain, and watched armies pass. The monsoon of 1857 brought disease, and the heat and shortages in besieged garrisons, whether the Residency at Lucknow or the entrenchment at Kanpur, killed more people than combat in some cases.

Servants, cooks, grooms, and washermen in British households faced difficult choices. Some protected their employers at great risk; others left or joined the rebels; others were killed by either side on suspicion. Indian soldiers who stayed loyal, including those in the Lucknow garrison, faced suspicion from both camps. When the British retook cities, residents faced looting and expulsion, as in Delhi, where much of the population fled or was driven out. These everyday experiences, though less well documented than battles, are central to understanding the rebellion's social impact.

Hindu-Muslim relations during the rebellion were complex. Many proclamations emphasized unity, and soldiers of both communities served in the same units and fought together; Muslim and Hindu leaders cooperated in Delhi, Awadh, and elsewhere. The rebels' desire to protect cows in Delhi, issued in the emperor's name, reflected an effort to accommodate Hindu sensibilities. At the same time, divisions existed: some Hindu and Muslim communities sided with the British, tensions surfaced over religious practice, and in some localities, rebellion intensified long-standing rivalries. Historians caution against reading either an idealized unity or a deep communal split into the evidence. Contemporary proclamations, petitions, and local records, rather than later nationalist or communal narratives, provide the best means to reconstruct the political aims of the participants, and they point to diverse and sometimes conflicting motives.

Section 69.12

Regional Variation: Punjab, Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Princely States, and Nonparticipation

Punjab, annexed in 1849 after the Anglo-Sikh wars, had been governed by John Lawrence and a group of officers who emphasized administrative efficiency and, above all, firm control. When news arrived from Delhi, Punjab's officials moved quickly. They disarmed Bengal Army regiments at Lahore (Mian Mir), Peshawar, and other stations, and raised new regiments from Punjab's population. Sikhs, whose army the Company had defeated a few years earlier, were available for recruitment, and many were willing to serve against the Bengal sepoys, whom they distrusted and whose role in the Company's conquest of Punjab they remembered. Punjabi Muslims, Pashtuns, and others also joined. Some historians have argued that Punjabi support was driven by a desire for plunder and for revenge on Delhi, which had been the center of Mughal authority; others emphasize the pay and status offered by the Company and the lack of a comparable grievance. Not all Punjabis supported the British: some units mutinied, as at Sialkot, and some local groups rose. Punjab's contribution was nonetheless decisive, since it supplied the Movable Column under John Nicholson and a steady supply of troops, money, and supplies for the siege of Delhi.

Gurkha troops, recruited from Nepal and hill regions, formed another important component. The Sirmoor Battalion held a key position on the Delhi Ridge, and the Nepalese state under Prime Minister Jung Bahadur Rana, who had been courted by the Company, sent troops that were employed in Awadh and the Gorakhpur region and later, in larger numbers, at Lucknow. Nepal's choice reflected its own interests: it hoped to retain British goodwill and to gain territory, and after the war the Company returned a portion of the Tarai land it had earlier taken. Kashmir under Maharaja Ranbir Singh sent troops to Delhi. These cases show that groups that supported the Company acted from calculation, not because they lacked political purpose.

Princely states, which covered large areas outside Company administration, mostly remained loyal. The Nizam of Hyderabad, Afzal ud-Dawla, and his minister, Salar Jung, stayed with the Company despite pressure within the state, and a rising in Hyderabad city on 17 July 1857, an attack on the British Residency by a group of Rohilla and Arab soldiers and others, was repulsed. Scindia of Gwalior and Holkar of Indore, though their armies rebelled, kept faith, as did the Begum of Bhopal, the Sikh rulers of Patiala, Nabha, and Jind, and others. For many rulers, loyalty reflected interest: the Company's power had constrained but also protected them, and the rebellion's vision of restored Mughal or Maratha authority offered no clear advantage. Some rulers feared their own soldiers and subjects. These decisions were significant, since had Hyderabad or Gwalior joined the rebellion, British control of central and southern India might have been in doubt. It is essential, however, to distinguish rulers' choices from those of their armies and populations. At Gwalior, Indore, and Kotah, soldiers or subjects rebelled against a ruler who supported the British, and at Hyderabad some of the nobility were sympathetic to the rebels.

Bengal's countryside saw little rebellion, though there were mutinies at Barrackpore and Dhaka and Chittagong in late 1857. Bengal's landed elite, whose estates rested on the Permanent Settlement, had a stake in the Company order, and the commercial classes of Calcutta mostly supported the government. The Bombay Army was affected by mutinies at Kolhapur and elsewhere, and a few local disturbances occurred in the Karnataka and Deccan regions, but the army did not rise as a body. Madras was largely quiet. The reasons included different recruitment, the absence of a recent annexation with the Awadh pattern, differences in landholding and social structure, and the effective use of intelligence and force to suppress early signs of trouble. Absence of rebellion, however, did not necessarily indicate satisfaction. Regional disaffection and later movements, discussed in later chapters, suggest that many of the underlying tensions existed elsewhere, but circumstances did not produce a general rising.

Among the rebellion's opponents were many Indians who fought and administered for the Company: sepoys who stayed loyal, such as those in the Lucknow garrison and in other regiments, Indian officials who continued their duties, bankers who financed British operations, and landholders who provided intelligence and supplies. Their reasons varied. Some acted from conviction, some from fear of rebel violence, some from the need for pay, and some from calculation about which side would win. Historians now insist that their choices be explained, not dismissed as collaboration or betrayal, in order to understand why the Company could defeat the rebels with largely Indian forces. The chapter's later sections show that the war was in this sense a conflict among South Asians as well as between Indians and the British.

Section 69.13

The Siege and Recapture of Delhi, June–September 1857

British command in the first weeks of the crisis was slow and hesitant. The Commander-in-Chief, General George Anson, was in Shimla when the news came. He died of cholera on 27 May; successors Henry Barnard and Thomas Reed lacked siege artillery and struggled with the heat and Delhi's defenses. The Delhi Field Force defeated a rebel force at Badli-ki-Serai on 8 June and occupied the Ridge, a rocky escarpment northwest of the city that overlooked its walls. Barnard died in July and Archdale Wilson succeeded him.

For the next three months, the Ridge was a besieged position almost as much as Delhi. The British force, never more than a few thousand effective soldiers in the early weeks, was exposed to repeated attacks from the city, to cholera and heat, and to the uncertainty of reinforcement. Positions on the Ridge, such as Hindu Rao's House, where Gurkhas of the Sirmoor Battalion and soldiers of the Guides held out against repeated assaults, became symbolic. Meanwhile the rebels, who greatly outnumbered the British, faced their own problems. They had artillery and supplies from the Delhi arsenal, but pay and food were irregular, leadership was divided between princes, regimental commanders, and Bakht Khan, and coordination among units was poor. The arrival of new forces, including the Bareilly brigade in July, the Nimach and Nasirabad regiments, and others, added strength but also added competing commanders and mouths to feed.

Rebel assaults on the British positions, including major attacks in July and August, were repulsed, sometimes with heavy losses. Some historians have argued that the rebels should have turned the British flank or cut their communications, since the British lines of supply from Punjab were vulnerable; the rebels, however, never mounted a sustained campaign in the countryside to isolate the Ridge, partly because their forces were dispersed and focused on defending the city. An attempt by a rebel force to intercept the British siege train in August failed at Najafgarh, where Nicholson defeated them on 25 August. In the city, shortages and disputes increased. Merchants resisted demands, the emperor's authority was weakened, and some soldiers drifted away.

The decisive reinforcement was the Punjab Movable Column under Brigadier-General John Nicholson, which reached the Ridge on 14 August, followed in early September by the heavy siege artillery from Ferozepur. The assault was planned for 14 September. After several days of bombardment that breached the walls near the Kashmiri Gate and Water Bastion, four columns attacked. A party of engineers blew open the Kashmiri Gate in a famous episode in which several men were killed, and the troops then entered the city. Fighting inside Delhi was bitter, as rebels defended streets and houses and the British and their allies advanced with heavy casualties. Nicholson was mortally wounded on 14 September at the Lahore Gate and died on 23 September. The British took the Red Fort and the rest of the city by 20 September; fighting and clearing continued for several days. The National Army Museum dates the decisive assault and recapture to 14–21 September 1857.

The fall of Delhi was followed by widespread violence against civilians. British and allied soldiers looted, killed, and drove out much of the population, with Muslims especially suspected. The inhabitants who could fled to the countryside, where many suffered hunger and exposure; the city was largely emptied for a period, and property was confiscated, with many mosques and houses seized or damaged. Executions of suspected rebels and the hanging of those taken in the city continued for weeks. The poet Mirza Ghalib, who lived through the siege, left letters describing the terror and ruin, a valuable, though personal, source.

Bahadur Shah Zafar left the Red Fort on 17 September with members of his family and took refuge at Humayun's Tomb. On 21 September, Captain William Hodson, head of an irregular cavalry unit and intelligence service, received the emperor’s surrender at Humayun’s Tomb and brought him under British custody. The question of guarantees for the emperor’s life belongs to the contested surrender negotiations. On 22 September, Hodson captured three princes, including Mirza Mughal, Mirza Khizr Sultan, and Mirza Abu Bakr, at the tomb, and shot them at a spot outside the Delhi Gate, a killing the National Army Museum characterizes as deliberate and unjustified after the prisoners had surrendered their arms. Their bodies were displayed, and other members of the royal family were killed or executed. The capture of the city and the capture of the emperor are distinct events from his later prosecution: Bahadur Shah was tried by a military commission in the Diwan-i-Khas, the hall of private audience of the Red Fort, from January to March 1858, found guilty, and exiled to Rangoon, where he died in November 1862. The trial was a political act that cast the emperor as a rebel and murderer, even though the Mughal sovereignty on which the Company's own authority had once rested was, in law, ambiguous.

Section 69.14

British Counteroffensives: Kanpur, Lucknow, and the Reconquest of Awadh

While the siege of Delhi proceeded, British forces from Calcutta and from the sea moved up the Ganga valley. Troops had been diverted to India from the China expedition and from Mauritius, and from Britain after the outbreak became known; these reinforcements arrived in numbers only from late summer. The first column, led by Major Renaud and then by Brigadier-General Havelock, relieved Allahabad, where the fort had been held by the Company despite a rising of the native garrison on 6 June, and where Colonel Neill had meanwhile conducted harsh reprisals in the city and surrounding villages. Havelock then advanced toward Kanpur, defeating rebel forces at Fatehpur on 12 July, Aong, and Pandu Nadi, and reaching the city on 17 July, after Nana Sahib had left.

The advance to Lucknow was harder. Havelock twice crossed the Ganga into Awadh in the summer of 1857 and twice was forced back by heavy losses from disease, exhaustion, and attacks, and by the need to hold Kanpur. Only in September, with reinforcement under Outram, who generously allowed Havelock to command the relief, did the column begin its third attempt. After crossing the Ganga, it fought its way to the Residency on 25 September in heavy street fighting. As noted in Section 69.7, this relief did not lift the siege.

The relief that did was Sir Colin Campbell's. Campbell, an experienced Scottish soldier who had commanded the Highland Brigade in the Crimea, reached Calcutta in August and assembled a force of several thousand, including Highlanders and Punjab troops. He moved to Kanpur in early November and from there to Lucknow. On 16 November he stormed the Sikandar Bagh, a walled garden held by rebel soldiers, where a large number of defenders were killed in fighting that observers described as massacre, and took the Shah Najaf, the Mess House, and other positions, forming a corridor to the Residency. The evacuation of the besieged, the women, children, and the sick, was carried out between 19 and 22 November, under cover of a deception that persuaded the rebels the British were not leaving. Campbell left Outram with a force at the Alambagh, a fortified garden outside Lucknow, and returned to Kanpur, which was threatened by Tatya Tope and the Gwalior Contingent. He defeated them on 6 December.

Campbell resumed operations in early 1858, with a larger force that included reinforcements from Britain, units from Punjab, and an army of Gurkhas under Jung Bahadur, which arrived from Nepal. By early March, he commanded some thirty thousand men with extensive artillery, and he advanced on Lucknow methodically, using heavy guns to demolish rebel positions and engineers to open routes through the buildings. The rebel defenders, under the leadership of the Begum's government, military commanders, and Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah, held strong positions in palaces and gardens, but their forces were divided and lacked coordination. Fighting lasted from 14 to 21 March 1858, and the British took the city after a series of assaults. Campbell has been criticized for failing to cut off the rebels' retreat, and many of the defenders escaped into the countryside. The recapture of Lucknow in March 1858 is thus distinct from the relief operations of September and November 1857.

Control of the city did not mean control of the province. Rebel forces dispersed into Awadh's districts and Rohilkhand, where they continued to fight. The Begum, Birjis Qadr, and many leaders moved north and west. In Rohilkhand, Khan Bahadur Khan, a grandson of the last Rohilla ruler, headed a government at Bareilly that fell to Campbell in May 1858. In Awadh, taluqdars held their forts and villages, and British columns had to attack them one by one, a slow process hampered by monsoon rains and heat. The Begum and Birjis Qadr fled to Nepal at the end of 1858, where Hazrat Mahal remained in exile until her death in 1879. Recognizing that conquest alone could not secure the province, the British moved toward negotiation and amnesty offers, as discussed in Section 69.18.

Section 69.15

Jhansi, Kalpi, Gwalior, and the Final Major Campaigns of 1858–1859

In central India, the British campaign was led by Major-General Sir Hugh Rose, who commanded the Central India Field Force, assembled at Mhow and Sehore in late 1857 and early 1858. Rose moved through Saugor and Rahatgarh, relieving British garrisons and taking forts, and reached Jhansi in March 1858. The fortress of Jhansi stood on a rock, protected by thick walls, and was defended by some ten thousand fighters, including Bundela soldiers, mercenaries, and Afghan troops, with guns under the Rani's direction. Rose invested the city on 21–22 March and began a bombardment. Tatya Tope, with a relief force from Kalpi of about twenty thousand, advanced to relieve Jhansi, and on 1 April Rose, dividing his force, defeated Tatya Tope's army at the Betwa river while continuing the siege.

On 3 April the British stormed Jhansi through breaches and by escalade. The fighting continued through the city for a day and into the next, and the British and their allies killed large numbers of defenders and inhabitants, with looting and killing of civilians widely reported. Lakshmibai escaped on the night of 3–4 April, leaving the fort with a small escort, reportedly with her young adopted son, and rode some seventy miles to Kalpi, where Tatya Tope, Rao Sahib, and the Nawab of Banda had assembled. The details of her escape, including the famous leap from the fort, derive from later accounts and tradition, and historians treat them cautiously. What is secure is that she survived the fall of Jhansi and rejoined the rebel forces.

Kalpi, a river town on the Yamuna with an arsenal and foundry, was the rebels' base in central India. Rose attacked it in May after taking the intermediate positions, and fighting on 22–23 May ended in a rebel defeat, partly attributed to the heat, which exhausted both sides, and to the arrival of a camel corps. The rebels, defeated, did not retreat to Awadh or the south. Instead, in a bold move, Tatya Tope, Rao Sahib, and Lakshmibai persuaded elements of the army of Scindia of Gwalior to join them. On 1 June 1858, Scindia, who had attempted to oppose them with his own troops, found most of his soldiers going over to the rebels, and fled to Agra. The rebels occupied Gwalior, a major fortress city, proclaimed Nana Sahib as Peshwa, and distributed the treasury to the troops. This was the most significant success of the rebels in central India, but it also showed their weakness: they had no secure territorial base and could hold the city only briefly.

Rose, resuming the campaign despite the heat, advanced on Gwalior in mid-June. On 17 June, according to the National Army Museum’s campaign chronology, a fight occurred at Kotah-ki-Serai, a few miles from the city, in which Lakshmibai led cavalry against a British force and was killed; accounts differ about whether she was hit by a shot or a sabre blow, and about the dress and circumstances of her death. British commanders later wrote of her with respect as a determined opponent, and her death quickly became part of popular and nationalist tradition. Gwalior was retaken on 19–20 June. The campaign had decisively defeated the rebel army in the center of the country.

The rebellion did not end there. Tatya Tope, with a small following, escaped and, over the following ten months, conducted a mobile campaign across central India, crossing rivers, evading British columns, and raising support in Rajputana, Malwa, and the Deccan fringes. He relied on speed, local knowledge, and the sympathy of villagers and some chiefs. British forces pursued him persistently across several hundred miles. Finally, in April 1859, betrayed by Man Singh, a local chief who had been his ally and who surrendered him to the British, he was captured at Paron, tried by court-martial at Sipri, and hanged on 18 April. Nana Sahib and his associates went into Nepal, and many surviving leaders were killed, captured, or exiled by the end of 1859. On 8 July 1859, Governor-General Canning proclaimed that peace had been restored, but armed resistance and local disturbances continued in some places beyond that date. The National Army Museum's campaign history confirms this sequence from Jhansi to Gwalior and the continuation of Tatya Tope's resistance into 1859.

Section 69.16

Violence, Retaliation, Civilian Suffering, and the Suppression of Rebellion

Violence was central to the rebellion and its suppression, and any account that treats it as a side issue distorts the history. Rebel forces killed British officers, soldiers, civilians, women, and children, at Meerut and Delhi in May, at Jhansi in June, at Kanpur in June and July, and in other places. At Kanpur, the killings at Satichaura Ghat and the Bibighar were the most notorious. Rebels also killed Indian Christians, Indians who served the Company, and, in some cases, other Indians in disputes that arose in the course of the rebellion. These acts are documented in British sources, in some Indian accounts, and in later investigation, and they are historical facts that cannot be minimized or excused by pointing to the Company's own conduct.

British violence was, however, on a much larger scale and more systematic. The government quickly passed legislation allowing summary trials and martial law, including Acts of the Legislative Council in the spring and summer of 1857, and officials and officers used them. Suspected rebels were hanged in large numbers from trees, at crossroads, and in public. Some were blown from the mouths of cannon, a punishment used earlier in Indian military practice and now adopted by the British to terrify and to deny the condemned a religious burial. Villages suspected of aiding rebels were burned, their inhabitants killed or driven out, and property confiscated. In the Allahabad and Kanpur regions, Colonel Neill and others carried out hangings of villagers on slight evidence, and in Delhi and Lucknow, troops sacked the cities after capture. The killing of the Mughal princes by Hodson, mentioned above, was one case among many in which authorities treated enemies without law.

Several factors shaped British conduct. Racial attitudes, already widespread in the Company's service, hardened under the shock of the rebellion; many officers regarded Indians as treacherous and incapable of political action, and the stories of Kanpur and Delhi reinforced the view that rebels were barbarians who deserved extermination. Religious sentiment played a role, since the rebellion was described by many Britons as a clash of Christianity with heathenism or Islam. The press in India and Britain printed accounts, some exaggerated and some fabricated, of atrocities, including accusations of mutilation and rape that investigators later found unsupported. Military discipline and fear were also factors: the Company's small European force faced overwhelming numbers, and officers justified terror as necessary to restore authority. Revenge was an explicit motive. Cries of vengeance for Kanpur circulated in the army and the press and were used to justify the killing of prisoners and civilians.

Not all British officials agreed. Governor-General Canning, in a resolution of 31 July 1857, urged moderation, distinguishing between rebel soldiers who had committed murder and those who had only deserted or been swept up, and warned against indiscriminate vengeance. He was mocked in Calcutta and in the British press as "Clemency Canning," a nickname that was intended as an insult, and some Europeans petitioned for his removal. Others, including some missionaries and officials, criticized the indiscriminate killing. But the dominant mood in the field was harsh, and Canning's policy had limited effect on the conduct of troops and local commissioners. After the major campaigns, proclamations offering amnesty to rebels who laid down their arms, except those guilty of murder, gradually reduced resistance, but the offers were distrusted by many who had seen how promises were broken.

Estimating casualties is extremely difficult. British losses included soldiers killed in battle and by disease, officials and civilians killed by rebels, and women and children who died in Kanpur and elsewhere. Even for the British dead, the numbers are uncertain, since records of disease deaths and of civilian casualties were incomplete. For Indians, there is no reliable count. The deaths included rebels killed in battle, soldiers executed, civilians killed in sacked cities and villages, people who died of famine and disease resulting from displacement, crop destruction, and the disruption of trade, and Indian soldiers and civilians who died fighting for the Company. Most historians believe that Indian deaths were many times greater than British deaths, but estimates range widely, and any precise figure is unsupported. Students should be cautious about claims of exact totals from any source, and should note that the evidence is stronger for British losses, which were recorded, than for Indian ones, which generally were not.

Acknowledging violence by different participants does not imply equivalence in political power, scale, or responsibility. The rebels, who lacked a unified command, committed acts of killing that were deliberate in some cases and the result of panic and disorder in others. The Company's forces, as the agents of an established state, with command structures, laws, and the capacity to restrain or discipline troops, bore a different kind of responsibility for the systematic nature of reprisals and for the failure to prevent them. The fact that rebel violence against women and children provoked outrage in Britain, while British violence against Indian civilians was often ignored or celebrated, itself reveals the racial hierarchy of the empire and the unequal ways in which suffering was recorded and remembered. A responsible historian must describe both, resist the temptation to rank victims by nationality, and note the differences in the evidence and in the power relations behind them. The command decisions, provincial reprisals, and the politics of retribution receive fuller separate treatment in Chapter 70.

Section 69.17

The Government of India Act of 1858 and the End of Company Government

Debate in Britain about the Company's future predated the rebellion. The Company's charter had been renewed in 1833 and 1853 on terms that progressively removed its commercial functions and increased Parliament's supervision, and critics had long argued that a trading corporation should not govern an empire. The rebellion, and the shock of Kanpur and Delhi, transformed that debate. Many in Britain blamed the Company's policies and its army for the disaster, and the government of Lord Palmerston introduced a bill in early 1858 to transfer authority to the Crown. Palmerston's government fell before the bill could pass, and its successor under Lord Derby, with Lord Stanley as President of the Board of Control, brought forward a revised measure that Parliament passed in the summer.

The Act for the Better Government of India received royal assent on 2 August 1858. It abolished the Court of Directors and the Board of Control and vested the government and territories of the Company in the Crown. A Secretary of State for India, a member of the British Cabinet, became responsible to Parliament for Indian affairs, and a Council of India of fifteen members, advising and in some matters constraining him, was established in London. The governor-general in India, who continued to have executive authority, acquired the additional title of Viceroy when acting on behalf of the Crown, and Lord Canning became the first holder. The Company's armies and assets passed to the Crown, with provisions for compensation to shareholders. The Company was not formally dissolved until 1874, but its political role ended in 1858.

The reasons for the transfer were more than punitive. For British politicians, the rebellion revealed the dangers of leaving a vast empire under a company whose policies, such as annexation and interference with religion, were seen to have provoked revolt. Placing India under the Crown brought it under direct parliamentary oversight and, it was hoped, made the government more responsive to public opinion. It also suited the political calculation that a transfer of authority to the Queen, whose name carried prestige, might reconcile Indian elites, especially the princes. The change should not be exaggerated. In India, the officials who governed districts, collected revenue, and commanded troops remained largely the same. The new constitution altered the apex, not the base, of the Company state. Section 69.18

The Political and Military Settlement after 1857: Army Reorganization, Princes, and Landed Elites

On 1 November 1858, at a durbar in Allahabad, Lord Canning read a proclamation in the name of Queen Victoria announcing the assumption of government by the Crown. The proclamation had been drafted by the Derby ministry and modified at the Queen's insistence to emphasize benevolence and religious toleration, and it was translated and distributed across India. Its text is the single most important statement of the political settlement that followed the rebellion.

The proclamation's promises had immediate and long-term consequences, which should be distinguished from the promises themselves. The pledge of religious noninterference was largely kept in the sense that the government avoided overt patronage of missionary work and legislation on religious customs for several decades, though the state continued to shape religious institutions through law. The promise to respect princes was implemented through a change of policy: the Doctrine of Lapse was abandoned, and the Crown instead issued sanads (guarantees) recognizing adoption by princes. Princes who had supported the British were rewarded with honors, titles, and sometimes territory, and the states became, in the later language of the Raj, "breakwaters" against disorder. The promise of equal admission to public office was slower to be realized, as later chapters show: the covenanted civil service remained dominated by Britons, and Indian entry through competitive examination was limited by design and circumstance.

The military settlement was equally important. The rebellion had shown the danger of an army composed largely of men from one region and dominated by high-caste soldiers. A Royal Commission, the Peel Commission, reported in 1859 and recommended increased European strength: an upper proportion of approximately two Indian soldiers to one European in Bengal and three to one in Madras and Bombay for cavalry and infantry; these were commission recommendations, not a universal ratio for every army branch. Most artillery was to remain under European control, subject to limited exceptions. The Company's European regiments were transferred to the Crown, and the army was reorganized to prevent a similar mutiny. The Bengal Army's regular regiments that had mutinied were disbanded, and recruitment shifted increasingly toward Punjab, the Himalayan hills, and other regions regarded as loyal. Policies increasingly sought to balance recruitment among regions and communities, though practical regimental organization changed over time rather than instantly adopting one uniform formula. Historians connect these changes to the later development of colonial recruitment ideologies, often described as the doctrine of "martial races." That doctrine, however, developed gradually, reaching its mature form in the late nineteenth century, and should not be projected back onto the settlement of 1858–1859.

The treatment of landed elites in rebel regions illustrates British pragmatism. In March 1858, after the capture of Lucknow, Canning issued a proclamation confiscating the proprietary rights in the soil of Awadh, with exceptions for a handful of loyal landholders. Outram, then the Chief Commissioner, criticized the measure as dangerously harsh, and the proclamation was modified to offer restoration to those who submitted. The government then moved toward a different approach, seeking to win over the taluqdars. In 1859–1862, taluqdars who submitted received sanads confirming their estates, and the Company's earlier effort to dismantle their position was abandoned. The result was a rural settlement in which the taluqdars were recognized as landlords with enhanced legal status, an alliance between the colonial state and a powerful rural class that outlived the rebellion. Cultivators, who had hoped for relief from revenue pressures, found that the settlement favored landlords.

The consequences for the Mughal dynasty and the former rebel leadership were severe. Bahadur Shah and some of his family were exiled to Rangoon, others were killed or died in poverty, and the Mughal court as an institution ended. Rebel leaders were killed, captured, or fled; Nana Sahib, the Begum, and others went to Nepal; Tatya Tope was executed. Many lesser participants, including soldiers, retainers, and villagers, lost property, were imprisoned or transported, or died. Households displaced by war, including those of Delhi and Lucknow, faced destitution and loss of property. Others were pardoned under the Queen's Proclamation. The settlement thus combined reward for loyal Indians, punishment for rebels, and conciliation of those whom the government needed. Section 69.19

Interpreting 1857: Sepoy Mutiny, Popular Rebellion, and the First War of Independence

The names given to the events of 1857 reflect competing interpretations, and the debate began almost as soon as the fighting did. Early British accounts, such as John Kaye's and George Malleson's multivolume histories, treated the rising primarily as a mutiny, the result of the cartridge crisis and the disaffection of the Bengal Army, and often blamed bad policy or the sepoys' credulity. Such accounts stressed the heroism of British defenders, drew on officers' memoirs and despatches, and tended to portray rebels as either misled or malevolent. They served political purposes: a mutiny could be explained and corrected by army reform, whereas a general rebellion would have called the legitimacy of British rule into question.

Indian contemporaries offered different explanations. Syed Ahmad Khan, writing in 1858, produced a pamphlet on the causes of the revolt (Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind, later translated into English) that argued that the British government's failure to understand Indian opinion, its isolation from Indians, and its policies on religion and land had produced the crisis. He wrote as a loyal subject and a Muslim official, and his analysis aimed to persuade the government to reform. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, writing in the New York Tribune in 1857, saw in the events the beginnings of national resistance, an interpretation shaped by their own concerns.

The idea of a national war of independence was elaborated by Vinayak Damodar Savarkar in his 1909 book The Indian War of Independence, written in London, which presented 1857 as a coordinated national uprising uniting Hindus and Muslims against foreign rule. The book was banned by the British, circulated among revolutionaries, and shaped later nationalist memory; it was important as political argument and as a text of anticolonial mobilization. After independence, the Indian government commemorated the centenary in 1957 and published an official history by S. N. Sen, Eighteen Fifty-Seven, which acknowledged the elements of mutiny and local rebellion while presenting the event as a precursor of the national movement. In the same year, R. C. Majumdar published The Sepoy Mutiny and the Revolt of 1857, arguing that the evidence did not support the claim of a planned national war and that the leaders acted from limited and self-interested motives. The disagreement between Sen and Majumdar, both distinguished historians, became a landmark in Indian historiography.

Later scholarship moved away from a choice between mutiny and national war toward the study of social and regional dimensions. Eric Stokes, in The Peasant and the Raj (1978), emphasized the role of landholding and rural society in the revolt, showing that the pattern of rebellion differed across regions according to local social structures and revenue histories. Rudrangshu Mukherjee, in Awadh in Revolt, 1857–1858 (1984), argued that in Awadh the rebellion was a popular uprising in which peasants, taluqdars, and soldiers acted together against a government they perceived as alien, and that rebel action showed political intelligence and organization, not mere disorder. Tapti Roy, in The Politics of a Popular Uprising: Bundelkhand in 1857 (1994), studied the revolt in Bundelkhand and showed how local politics, the role of local chiefs, and village society shaped its course. Other historians, including Christopher Bayly, emphasized the importance of information networks, the legitimacy of local authorities, and the connections between the rebellion and earlier forms of resistance. Recent work by writers such as William Dalrymple, in The Last Mughal, has drawn on Persian and Urdu documents from Delhi to reconstruct the rebel city from within, and a growing body of regional and subaltern studies has examined the participation of lower castes, women, and marginal groups.

Historical Debate 1

Was 1857 a Sepoy Mutiny, a Popular Rebellion, or a War of Independence?

The question: How should historians characterize the uprising, and what do the names imply about the nature, objectives, and unity of the participants?

Interpretation A — A military mutiny that spread: Emphasizes that the rebellion began in the Bengal Army, was triggered by the cartridge controversy, and was most coherent where soldiers were involved. Regional variation, the weakness of coordination, and the lack of a common program suggest that it was not a national movement. Majumdar's skepticism about a planned national war is the best-known statement of this view, and it highlights the limited and often local motives of leaders.
Interpretation B — A popular rebellion and anticolonial war: Emphasizes the participation of rulers, landholders, peasants, and townspeople, particularly in Awadh and parts of central India, and the evidence from proclamations that rebels framed their struggle against foreign rule. Savarkar's account and later nationalist writing saw a national war, while Mukherjee and Roy argue for popular rebellion with its own political logic. These interpretations stress that rebel action showed purpose and that the rebellion cannot be reduced to a military protest.

Evidence: Both sides draw on British official records, rebel proclamations, trial records, regional archives, and oral traditions. The evidence shows that grievances differed by region and group and that the rebellion was geographically uneven, but also that participants expressed opposition to Company rule and invoked religion, sovereignty, and justice.

Why it matters: The characterization affects how the rebellion is remembered: as a failed mutiny, as the first stage of the independence movement, or as a complex uprising with multiple causes. Most scholars now argue that these labels capture different aspects, and that "national" in the modern sense is anachronistic for the participants, though "anticolonial" is justified for many.

The problems with both colonial and nationalist narratives are now widely recognized. Colonial narratives that depicted the rebellion as irrational disorder ignored the political purposes expressed in proclamations and the social grievances behind rural participation, and they minimized British violence. Retrospective nationalist narratives that depicted a unified modern national movement ignored the divisions among rebels, the role of Indians who fought for the Company, and the localized aims of many participants. The evidence itself is shaped by its production: British official reports and memoirs provide detail on military operations and British experience; rebel proclamations survive largely through British translation; regional archives and oral traditions preserve local memory but often in later forms; and nationalist histories reflect the concerns of their own time. A historian must read these sources critically and in combination, and must be prepared to accept that some questions, such as the motives of ordinary participants, may remain only partly answerable.

Section 69.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The Rebellion of 1857 grew from the interaction of military discontent with annexation, landholding disputes, religious anxiety, and the erosion of the Company's political legitimacy. None of these factors, taken alone, explains the rising. The grievances of the Bengal Army, which stemmed from recruitment patterns, service conditions, and the decline of the bond between officers and men, provided the immediate form of rebellion, and the cartridge controversy gave it a focus. But the speed with which mutiny became rebellion in Awadh, Delhi, Bundelkhand, and parts of Bihar reflects the presence of other aggrieved groups: dispossessed rulers such as Nana Sahib and Rani Lakshmibai, taluqdars facing the loss of estates, cultivators under revenue pressure, townspeople who had lost patrons, and religious leaders who feared for their faiths. In regions where such groups had no comparable grievance, as in Punjab, Bengal, and most of the south, the rebellion did not take root.

The geography of the rebellion shows its uneven character. It spread from Meerut to Delhi and then through the Bengal Army's stations across the Ganga valley, Awadh, Rohilkhand, Bundelkhand, and central India, but it did not become a mass movement across the subcontinent. Rebel areas shared opposition to Company rule but lacked a unified command, treasury, or program. Bahadur Shah Zafar gave the rebellion a symbolic center, Bakht Khan tried to give Delhi a functioning government, Begum Hazrat Mahal governed Awadh, and others led regional resistance, but these authorities did not form a single state. The rebel leaders were drawn from different classes and ideas of legitimacy, and the movement began without a plan. Its failure to develop a coordinated political and military structure was a major factor in its defeat, though not the only one.

Historical Debate 2

Why Did the Rebellion Fail?

The question: Why did a rebellion that controlled major cities and large areas in 1857 fail to overthrow British rule?

Interpretation A — British resources and communications: The British controlled the sea, the Ganga line, and the telegraph, and could bring reinforcements from Britain, the China expedition, and Punjab. They possessed superior artillery and supply, and the rebels lacked comparable resources. On this view, the fall of Delhi and the later reconquest followed from material advantages that the rebels could not overcome.
Interpretation B — Indian allies and princely decisions: Punjab, Nepal, the major princely states, and Indian soldiers who stayed loyal supplied manpower, money, and political legitimacy. Without them the British could not have retaken Delhi or Lucknow. Rebel failure was therefore partly a result of choices by other Indians.
Interpretation C — Rebel disunity and coordination problems: Divided leadership, shortages of pay and supplies, quarrels among commanders and between soldiers and civilians, and the lack of a unified political program prevented the rebels from exploiting their early success. Rebel areas did not act together, and the rebels did not mount a strategic offensive when the British were weakest.

Evidence: Military records, rebel and British correspondence, accounts of the Delhi and Lucknow campaigns, and studies of revenue and supply show the importance of each factor. Most historians combine them rather than choose one.

Why it matters: The question guards against treating British victory as inevitable and against reading rebel defeat as evidence that participants lacked political aims. Defeat resulted from contingent factors, including decisions by Punjab and the princes, the timing of reinforcements, and the failure to coordinate.

The rebellion's defeat nevertheless produced a fundamental constitutional change in imperial government. The Government of India Act of 1858 ended the Company's rule, and the Queen's Proclamation announced a new relationship between the Crown and its subjects and princes. The settlement preserved much of the Company's administrative machinery while reorganizing the army, abandoning annexation, reconciling princes and landed elites, and promising religious noninterference. This is the central paradox of 1857: the rebels failed to overthrow British authority, yet their rebellion helped to bring the East India Company's system of government to an end, and in doing so reshaped the form of colonial rule. The Raj that emerged was more cautious about religion and princely sovereignty, more reliant on alliances with conservative elites, and more suspicious of Indian political action, characteristics that later chapters will trace.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Rebellion of 1857 as a geographically uneven, politically diverse uprising against East India Company rule, in which a mutiny in the Bengal Army interacted with annexation, landholding disputes, religious anxiety, and local political ambitions. It argued that the event was neither merely a sepoy mutiny nor a unified national independence movement, and it traced how and why the revolt developed differently across regions.

The chapter began with the political geography of Company India in 1856. Dalhousie's annexations, including Satara, Jhansi, and Nagpur on the basis of lapse, and the annexation of Awadh in February 1856, displaced courts, rulers, and dependents. Revenue settlements unsettled landholders, and missionary activity and social legislation created religious anxiety. These structural grievances formed the background to the crisis but did not by themselves cause it.

The Bengal Army, recruited largely from high-caste communities in Awadh, Bihar, and the Ganga valley, had specific grievances: stagnant pay, racial limits on promotion, the withdrawal of allowances and postal concessions, the weakening of ties with British officers, and fears about overseas service after the General Service Enlistment Order of 1856, which applied to new recruits. The Madras and Bombay armies recruited differently and did not rise as a body.

The Enfield cartridge controversy supplied the immediate focus. Rumors that cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat spread through cantonments, official concessions came late, and the disbandment of the 19th Native Infantry and the incident involving Mangal Pandey at Barrackpore showed rising tension. The cartridge issue mattered because it fell on soil prepared by other grievances.

At Meerut, the punishment of eighty-five troopers on 9 May was followed by the rising of 10 May, the release of prisoners, and the march to Delhi. On 11 May, rebels seized Delhi and appealed to Bahadur Shah Zafar, giving the rebellion a political and symbolic center. The weakness of the emperor's authority and the difficulties of feeding and organizing the rebel forces limited what the rebels could do with their success.

The rebellion spread through Awadh, where annexation, the dissolution of the court, and the settlement of 1856 had turned soldiers, taluqdars, and cultivators against the Company, and where Begum Hazrat Mahal and Birjis Qadr led a government at Lucknow. At Kanpur, Nana Sahib led the rebellion, and the events at Satichaura Ghat and the Bibighar became central to British memory and to retaliation. At Jhansi, Rani Lakshmibai, whose adopted heir had been refused recognition, became a leader, though her responsibility for the killings of June 1857 remains contested. In Bihar, Kunwar Singh led a mobile campaign until his death in April 1858.

Beyond famous leaders, peasants, village headmen, religious figures, women, and urban communities took part in the rebellion in diverse ways and for diverse reasons. Proclamations such as that issued in Bahadur Shah's name in August 1857 show rebel leaders appealing to landholders, merchants, artisans, scholars, and soldiers, but they cannot be taken as evidence of a single ideology. Hindu-Muslim cooperation was real, but so were divisions.

The rebellion was limited by Punjab's support for the British, the decisions of major princely states, the participation of Sikh, Punjabi Muslim, and Gurkha troops, and the relative quiet of Bengal, Bombay, and Madras. Some Indians fought for the Company, others stayed neutral, and rulers' choices were not always those of their soldiers or subjects. The conflict was in part a conflict among South Asians.

British reconquest followed the recapture of Delhi in September 1857, the relief and evacuation of Lucknow in September and November 1857, the recapture of Lucknow in March 1858, and Rose's campaign from Jhansi to Gwalior in 1858. Resistance continued in dispersed form until Tatya Tope's execution in April 1859. Violence by both sides marked the war. Rebel killings of civilians were met by British reprisals on a much larger scale, and casualty totals cannot be established reliably.

The Government of India Act of 1858 transferred government from the Company to the Crown, creating a Secretary of State for India, a Council of India, and the office of Viceroy, and the Queen's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 announced promises concerning religion, princes, employment, and clemency. The army was reorganized, annexation was abandoned, and princes and taluqdars were conciliated. Historians have interpreted the rebellion as mutiny, popular rebellion, and war of independence, and the best scholarship combines these perspectives. The central paradox is that the rebellion failed but helped end the Company's rule.

Chapter Reflection

The Rebellion of 1857 matters because it was the largest challenge the East India Company faced and because its suppression reshaped colonial government. It revealed how fragile a state could be when its army, its landholders, and its legitimacy were in tension, and it showed how local grievances could be joined to a general crisis. It also showed the limits of such a joining: the rebellion's regional variety, the choices of those who stayed loyal, and the absence of a unified command help explain why it failed.

Its legacies were large. The Raj that followed was more cautious about religion, more dependent on princes and landed elites, more anxious about the army, and more suspicious of Indian political action. Memory of 1857 also became a contested resource: for British writers a story of heroism and betrayal, for nationalists a first war of independence, for historians a complex uprising requiring regional study. The violence of the period, on both sides, left a lasting mark.

Within the six-volume series, the chapter marks a hinge. It closes the period of Company conquest and consolidation traced in Chapters 62 through 68 and opens the era of Crown rule. It also connects regions: Delhi and Awadh, Bihar and central India, Punjab and Nepal, princely states and British districts. Chapter 70 follows the rebellion's suppression and the political decisions of 1858, and Chapter 71 turns to the institutions of the British Raj that were built on its legacy.
