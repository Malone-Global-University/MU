Part XII — Company Rule and Colonial Conquest

Mysore, the Marathas, and British Expansion

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 10 September 1780, a detachment of the East India Company's Madras army marching toward Conjeeveram, in the Carnatic, was surrounded by the army of Haidar Ali of Mysore near the village of Pollilur. By the end of the day the detachment had been destroyed as a fighting force. The Company's position around Madras was suddenly precarious, and nothing about the outcome suggested that a trading corporation was destined to dominate southern India. Thirty-eight years later, in 1818, the Peshwa Baji Rao II, nominal head of the Maratha Confederacy, surrendered to a Company officer in central India, his office abolished, his capital Pune in Company hands. Between these two moments lies the story of this chapter.

Chapters 62 and 63 traced how the East India Company acquired fiscal and political control over Bengal after Plassey and Buxar and then converted that control into a bureaucratic, military-fiscal territorial government under Warren Hastings, Cornwallis, and their successors. This chapter asks what the Company did with those resources. Between 1767 and 1818 it fought four wars against Mysore and three against the Maratha states, together with a sequence of subordinate campaigns, and by 1818 it had become the predominant political and military power across most of the subcontinent south of the Sutlej and east of the Indus. The chapter follows that sequence of wars, but its purpose is explanation rather than a catalogue of battles.

The central claim of the chapter is that Company predominance was not automatic. Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan was a centralized, highly mobilized regional state with an effective cavalry, a modernizing infantry, innovative rocket and artillery arms, a developed revenue administration, and an active foreign policy that reached the French, the Ottomans, and others. The Maratha states, whether considered together as a Confederacy or separately as the houses of the Peshwa, Shinde, Holkar, Bhonsle, and Gaekwad, commanded some of the largest armies in South Asia, controlled enormous revenues, and repeatedly defeated or checked Company forces. These states won battles, forced the Company to negotiate, and preserved their autonomy for decades. Their eventual subordination requires explanation, and the explanation lies less in any inherent military or cultural inferiority than in a specific interaction of finance, logistics, diplomacy, and political fragmentation.

That interaction is the chapter's analytical framework. The Company could draw on the revenues of Bengal and other territories to pay standing armies of sepoys, to maintain credit with Indian bankers, and to sustain campaigns far from their bases. It could coordinate three presidencies, though not always smoothly, and it could rely on diplomatic alliances with other Indian states, notably the Nizam of Hyderabad and, at critical moments, Maratha leaders themselves. Its opponents, by contrast, frequently faced rivals within their own political worlds who preferred a Company alliance to the dominance of a neighboring Indian power. The wars were therefore not simple contests between "Indian" and "British" blocs; they were episodes in a larger competition for power in which Indian states made their own strategic choices, often with limited information and under severe constraints.

The chapter also gives attention to an institution that shaped these outcomes more than any single battle: the subsidiary alliance. Developed unevenly in the 1790s and systematized under Richard Wellesley after 1798, this system offered Indian rulers military protection in exchange for the stationing of a Company-controlled force at their expense, the admission of a British Resident, the surrender of independent diplomacy, and often the cession of territory. Treaties such as that of Bassein in 1802 allowed the Company to extend its authority over states that were never formally annexed. Understanding the difference between annexation, subsidiary alliance, and the broader claim to paramountcy is essential for reading the political map of 1818.

The evidence for these events is abundant but unevenly distributed. The British Library's India Office Records, published collections of treaties, and Company correspondence preserve detailed accounts of campaigns and negotiations, but they represent the perspectives of Company officials. Persian chronicles, Marathi bakhars and correspondence, Kannada and other regional traditions, French records, and the surviving material culture of Mysore and the Maratha states offer other viewpoints, though fewer of them survive in forms easily available to students. Throughout the chapter, source windows ask who produced each document, for whom, and what perspectives are missing. Casualty figures and battle narratives are used with caution, and where a document has not been independently collated this is stated explicitly.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I examines Mysore under Haidar Ali and the first two Anglo-Mysore Wars. Division II treats Tipu Sultan's state, the third and fourth wars, the fall of Seringapatam, and the subsidiary alliance system. Division III turns to the Maratha Confederacy, the First Anglo-Maratha War, the Treaty of Bassein, and the Second Anglo-Maratha War. Division IV follows the Maratha states after 1805 through the Pindari campaign, the Third Anglo-Maratha War, and the settlement of 1818. Division V steps back to assess the new political geography, the military-fiscal foundations of expansion, and the historiography. The earlier histories of Shivaji, the Peshwas, and Mysore's rise appear in Chapters 58 and 59 and are only summarized here.

The chapter ends in 1818, with the Company dominant across much of southern and western India but not yet supreme across the whole subcontinent. The Sikh kingdom of Ranjit Singh, Sindh, and other territories remained outside Company control; their subordination belongs to Chapter 65. Students should carry forward the lesson that predominance was a historical achievement, built from contingent circumstances and contested at every stage, rather than a foregone conclusion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Haidar Ali built Mysore into a centralized regional military state and why its institutions made it a formidable rival to the Company.
  2. Trace the causes, campaigns, and treaties of the four Anglo-Mysore Wars, distinguishing the documented dates and provisions of each settlement.
  3. Analyze Tipu Sultan's military, fiscal, commercial, and diplomatic policies as those of a state-builder, using carefully attributed evidence rather than inherited stereotypes.
  4. Distinguish the Maratha Confederacy from the individual Maratha states of the Peshwa, Shinde, Holkar, Bhonsle, and Gaekwad, and explain why collective action was difficult.
  5. Reconstruct the causes and outcomes of the three Anglo-Maratha Wars, including the Treaties of Salbai, Bassein, Deogaon, Surji-Anjangaon, and Pune.
  6. Define and compare annexation, subsidiary alliance, Residency, and paramountcy as distinct instruments of Company power.
  7. Assess the contribution of Bengal revenue, credit, logistics, and the sepoy army to Company expansion without reducing the wars to European technological superiority.
  8. Evaluate the role of Indian allies, financiers, suppliers, and soldiers in the Company's victories.
  9. Explain the role of regional rivalry, succession disputes, and political fragmentation in the defeat of Mysore and the Maratha states.
  10. Interpret treaty texts and archival correspondence as evidence, identifying the authorship, purpose, and limitations of each source.
  11. Describe the political map of South Asia in 1818, including the territories that remained outside Company control.
  12. Compare major historiographical interpretations of Company expansion and assess the question of inevitability.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why was a commercial corporation able to defeat or subordinate states with substantial armies, revenues, and administrative traditions?
  2. What distinguished the military and fiscal institutions of Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, and what were their constraints?
  3. How did diplomacy and alliance-building shape the outcome of the Anglo-Mysore Wars as much as battlefield events did?
  4. In what sense was the Maratha Confederacy a political system rather than a single state, and how did this affect its response to the Company?
  5. How did succession disputes and factional rivalry within Indian states create openings for Company intervention?
  6. What was a subsidiary alliance, and how did its operation differ from its stated purpose?
  7. How should historians weigh treaty texts against the political realities in which they were negotiated and enforced?
  8. What roles did Indian soldiers, bankers, suppliers, and local populations play in the wars and their aftermath?
  9. How have British imperial, Indian nationalist, and modern scholarly accounts interpreted Haidar Ali, Tipu Sultan, Baji Rao II, and the Wellesleys, and why do they differ?
  10. What parts of the subcontinent remained outside Company control in 1818, and why does that matter for understanding the pace of conquest?

Historical Context

The wars of this chapter unfolded in the political landscape produced by the eighteenth-century transformation examined in Part XI. The Mughal empire survived as a source of legitimacy but no longer as a single administrative machine, and its successor regimes, including Hyderabad, Awadh, Bengal, and the Maratha and Sikh polities, competed with one another for revenue, territory, and military manpower. Chapter 58 traced the Maratha expansion from Shivaji's forts to the Peshwa-led confederacy that reached from Gujarat to Orissa, and Chapter 59 introduced Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan within a wider account of regional military innovation. Chapter 61 explained how European trading companies became participants in this competition, and Chapters 62 and 63 showed how the English Company converted its position in Bengal into a territorial state with a bureaucracy, a revenue system, and a large army.

Geography frames the chapter. Mysore occupied the southern Deccan plateau, with access through hill passes to the Carnatic plain on the east and to the Malabar Coast on the west, and with Seringapatam on an island in the Kaveri river as its fortified capital. The Maratha heartland lay on the western Deccan, around Pune and the Western Ghats, but Maratha influence extended through Malwa, Gujarat, Nagpur, and Hindustan. The Company's three presidencies, Bengal, Madras, and Bombay, were linked by sea but divided by distance, and the monsoon shaped when armies could move and when supplies could be shipped. Rivers, forts, passes, and ports determined campaign possibilities. Chapter 1's emphasis on the interaction of environment and political choice applies here as much as to the ancient world.

The wars also took place within a globally connected conflict. The Company's wars with Mysore overlapped with the Seven Years' War's aftermath, the American War of Independence, and the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. French naval and military assistance to Mysore, French officers in Maratha service, and British fears of French influence all shaped decisions in Calcutta and London. Richard Wellesley's actions after 1798 cannot be understood without the strategic anxieties generated by Napoleon's expedition to Egypt that year. At the same time, historians increasingly emphasize that the principal drivers of events were local: succession disputes, revenue rights, fortress control, and competition among Indian states for dominance.

Students should also note the evidentiary imbalance. The Company produced vast quantities of correspondence, minutes, treaties, and military reports, many now held in the India Office Records in the British Library. Indian courts produced Persian, Marathi, and other records, but many were lost, destroyed, or dispersed, particularly after the fall of Seringapatam in 1799, when Tipu's library and archive were scattered, and after the dissolution of the Peshwa's government in 1818. A narrative based only on Company sources risks reproducing the Company's own account of its victories. This chapter uses British documentary sources where they are strongest, on treaties and campaign detail, while seeking to represent Indian political objectives and constraints through attributed modern scholarship.

Section 64.1

Mysore as a Regional Military State: The Wodeyars, Haidar Ali, and the Deccan

Mysore in the mid-eighteenth century was a relatively modest kingdom on the southern plateau, governed in the name of the Wodeyar dynasty but controlled by powerful ministers and commanders. The transformation of that kingdom into the most formidable military state in southern India was the work of Haidar Ali, an officer of Muslim background who rose through the Mysore army and, from about 1761, became its effective ruler. The Wodeyar king continued to exist as a ceremonial sovereign, and Haidar governed through the offices and revenue structures of the old kingdom, supplementing them with new ones. Chapter 59 introduced his rise; the task here is to understand what kind of state he built and why it mattered to the Company.

Haidar's power rested on a combination of military recruitment, revenue extraction, and territorial expansion. Mysore's position on the Deccan plateau gave it control of routes between the interior and the Malabar Coast, as well as access to agricultural districts in the Kaveri basin and the Tungabhadra region. Haidar expanded northward and westward, absorbing territories such as Bednur and Sira in the 1760s and extending influence over Malabar. These acquisitions provided revenue, ports, timber, pepper, sandalwood, and manpower. They also placed Mysore in direct competition with the Marathas to the north, the Nizam of Hyderabad to the northeast, and the Carnatic and the Company to the east and south.

The administrative structure beneath Haidar combined continuity with centralization. Revenue officers, military commanders, and the landholding and local elites who controlled villages and forts had all to be managed. Haidar tended to replace hereditary intermediaries with salaried or directly supervised officials where possible, and he reorganized the collection of revenue to support a standing army. Historians such as Mohibbul Hasan, in his history of Tipu Sultan, and later scholars of Mysore's political economy have emphasized this state-centered character. The relationship between ruler, commanders, revenue officers, and regional elites was nonetheless a negotiated one, and local polygars and chiefs resisted, rebelled, or bargained when they could.

Military organization was the heart of Haidar's state. Mysore maintained a large cavalry, drawing on both regional horse-breeding resources and imports, and cavalry gave Haidar's armies the mobility that so disconcerted Company commanders. Infantry units were trained in more disciplined fashion than traditional levies, partly through contact with French officers and with sepoy practice on the Coromandel Coast. Artillery, fortifications, and the supply of munitions were maintained by state arsenals, and Haidar employed European and Indian specialists. Forts across the plateau, such as Bangalore and Seringapatam, anchored the territorial structure. The army was financed by land revenue, tribute from subordinate chiefs, and the proceeds of campaigns, and the whole system depended on continuous warfare or the threat of it to sustain itself.

Mysore's strategic environment explains much of its foreign policy. Haidar could not afford to be isolated. He alternated between hostility and accommodation toward the Marathas, who periodically invaded Mysore and extracted payments; he competed with the Nizam, who claimed overlordship over parts of the southern Deccan under old Mughal arrangements; and he regarded the Company's growing influence in the Carnatic, through its alliance with the Nawab of Arcot, as a threat to his eastern frontier. The Company, for its part, viewed Mysore as a rival whose ports in Malabar and relations with the French threatened its commercial and strategic position. These mutual perceptions produced the wars described in the sections that follow.

The evidence for Haidar's state comes from several quarters. Persian chronicles written at the Mysore court and by later authors preserve a Mysorean point of view; Company records describe Mysore as an adversary; and French accounts offer another perspective. Each must be read critically. Company sources tend to emphasize Haidar's "usurpation" of the Wodeyar throne and his military threat, while later nationalist and regional histories sometimes treat him as a heroic anticolonial figure. A more useful approach, followed here, is to treat Haidar as a ruler pursuing the interests of a rising military state in a highly competitive environment, using the instruments available to him.

One further distinction is necessary between territorial expansion and administrative capacity. Captured districts did not automatically generate spendable cash for Haidar's soldiers. Officials had to collect assessments, transport bullion or accounting claims, buy food, and negotiate with local householders and chiefs. In peacetime some of these relationships could be maintained through accommodation; during campaigning soldiers and officers might impose requisitions that weakened the revenues they expected to collect. Mysore's military strength therefore rested not on a single technical innovation but on the ongoing ability of administrators to convert agricultural production and market activity into supplies for a moving army.

Section 64.2

The First Anglo-Mysore War, 1767–1769: Alliances, Campaigns, and the Treaty of Madras

The First Anglo-Mysore War began in 1767, and its alignments show how unstable the political landscape was. The Company's Madras government concluded an alliance with the Nizam of Hyderabad, Nizam Ali Khan, to counter Haidar's expansion, and the Maratha Peshwa's forces also operated against Mysore that year. Haidar, however, proved a skilled diplomat as well as a commander. He bought off Maratha pressure through payments and persuaded the Nizam to abandon the Company and join him. The Company thus found itself, within months, facing a combined Mysore-Hyderabad force rather than leading a coalition against Haidar alone. The episode illustrates that the wars of this period were not binary struggles but shifting multi-party contests in which alliances could dissolve quickly.

The campaigns that followed exposed weaknesses on both sides. Company forces under officers such as Joseph Smith defeated the allied army at Changama and Tiruvannamalai in 1767, but Haidar withdrew rather than being crushed, and his cavalry then devastated the Carnatic countryside and attacked Company supply lines. The Company's army was constrained by dependence on slow-moving baggage, on the reliability of its Indian allies, and on the Nawab of Arcot's finances. Haidar's mobility allowed him to avoid decisive battles on unfavorable terms and to strike elsewhere. In 1768 he also faced a Company expedition against his Malabar possessions, and the Bombay presidency's naval operations threatened his coastal holdings. Mysorean forces responded by recovering ground and moving against the Company's heartland.

The decisive moment came in early 1769, when Haidar led a rapid cavalry march that brought his forces to the vicinity of Madras itself. The Company's government, whose officers had been engaged elsewhere, was alarmed by the sudden appearance of a hostile army near its principal settlement in the south. The Madras Council's anxiety, together with the difficulty of sustaining a distant war and the expense of campaigning in the Carnatic, disposed it to accept terms. The negotiated treaty is conventionally associated with the Treaty of Madras, concluded in early April 1769; the documentary date is usually given as 3 or 4 April, and students will encounter both in the literature.

The treaty provided for the mutual restoration of conquered territory and for the exchange of prisoners, and it contained provisions for mutual assistance in defensive war. Published collections of Indian treaties preserve its text. The defensive clause was to have lasting significance. Haidar understood it as committing the Company to assist him if the Marathas attacked Mysore, while the Company's officials, for their part, regarded themselves as obliged only in certain circumstances and were reluctant to risk a conflict with the Marathas. When the Marathas invaded Mysore in the early 1770s and the Company did not assist, Haidar concluded that the Company's engagements could not be relied upon. Many historians regard this perceived breach as an important background to the Second Anglo-Mysore War.

The First Anglo-Mysore War thus produced a settlement that satisfied neither party and left unresolved the question of how far the Company would commit itself to Mysore's defense. It also revealed patterns that would recur. Indian states could defeat or outmaneuver Company forces in the field; the Company's strengths lay in the ability to sustain its army through the resources of its presidencies and to return to the war after setbacks; and the decisive factor in the outcome was often diplomatic, determined by which parties held to or abandoned their alliances.

The war also established a lasting distinction between the diplomatic language of mutual defense and the more conditional calculations of the parties. The Company's officers could promise assistance in a treaty while their superiors weighed whether a later attack counted as a defensive emergency, whether another alliance constrained action, and whether revenue was available to mobilize troops. Haidar read the agreement through the immediate security needs of Mysore. Those differing expectations mattered more than a simple claim that one side was intrinsically perfidious, and they help explain why the 1769 peace failed to create a stable system of regional security.

Section 64.3

The Second Anglo-Mysore War, 1780–1784: Pollilur, Porto Novo, and the Wider Conflict

The settlement of 1769 did not hold. During the 1770s Haidar's relations with the Company deteriorated for several reasons: the perceived failure of the Company to honor the defensive clause, its involvement in Maratha affairs during the First Anglo-Maratha War, its relations with Hyderabad, and the effects of the European war. When France entered the American war against Britain, the Company moved against French possessions in India, and in 1779 it captured Mahé, a French settlement on the Malabar Coast. Haidar regarded Mahé as under his protection and as a crucial port through which he received French supplies and military stores. The Company's seizure of it was a direct challenge to Mysore's interests and a principal immediate cause of renewed war.

Haidar's strategy in 1780 was part of a larger diplomatic design. He sought to coordinate action with the Marathas and the Nizam against the Company, an alignment encouraged by the Maratha minister Nana Fadnavis, who was himself engaged against the Company in the First Anglo-Maratha War. The alliance never became an effective unified command, and the Nizam's and Marathas' participation soon diminished, but Haidar's invasion of the Carnatic in July 1780 caught the Madras government unprepared. His army overran much of the countryside and besieged important places such as Arcot. At Pollilur, near Conjeeveram, on 10 September 1780, a Company detachment under Colonel William Baillie, which had been ordered to join a larger force under Sir Hector Munro, was surrounded and destroyed after a hard-fought engagement. The defeat was among the most serious the Company had suffered in India, and it was widely discussed in Britain.

The response required resources from beyond Madras. The Bengal government under Warren Hastings sent Sir Eyre Coote with reinforcements and funds, an example of the cross-presidency coordination described in Chapter 63. Coote defeated Haidar at Porto Novo on 1 July 1781 and fought further engagements, including Pollilur again and Sholinghur, that checked but did not destroy Mysore's army. Haidar continued to operate in the Carnatic and to hold the strategic initiative in many areas, while the Company faced severe problems of supply, disease, and the loyalty of its Carnatic allies. Hastings also addressed the wider Indian situation through diplomacy, and the Treaty of Salbai of 1782 with the Marathas (§64.10) removed one of Haidar's potential partners from the war.

Haidar Ali died on 7 December 1782 while campaigning near Chittoor. His son Tipu Sultan, who was operating on the western side, returned to secure the succession and continued the war. The transition went smoothly, a testimony to the institutional depth of the Mysore state. The war's remaining theaters were Malabar and Kanara, where Mysorean forces defeated a Company expedition under Brigadier Richard Matthews at Bednur in 1783, capturing officers and soldiers, and the siege of Mangalore, which Tipu undertook in 1783–1784. Naval factors also mattered: French naval operations under Suffren in the Bay of Bengal influenced the balance on the coast, and French military participation on land, limited and uneven, gave Mysore some additional capacity. The end of the European war in 1783 reduced the prospect of further French support.

The Second Anglo-Mysore War is important for demonstrating the limits of Company superiority. Mysore remained a first-rank military power throughout the conflict despite the Company's access to Bengal funds. The Company prevented decisive defeat, but it did so with difficulty, and its leaders emerged from the war with respect for Mysore's capabilities and concerned about the vulnerability of the Carnatic and the Malabar coast. The war also imposed heavy costs on populations who lived in the contested zones, an issue examined in the Everyday Life feature below.

The campaign shows why a victory in one engagement did not settle the balance of power. Heavy casualties and captured guns could destroy a detachment while leaving the Company's port, shipping, credit, and ability to mobilize new units intact. Conversely, a defensive victory by Company infantry rarely eliminated Mysore's mobile cavalry or its resources behind the plateau forts. Commanders therefore fought repeatedly for junctions, depots, grain routes, and access to coastal relief. The result was a prolonged war of attrition in which environmental conditions and supply sometimes mattered more than the conspicuous battlefield result recorded by either side's chroniclers.

Section 64.4

The Treaty of Mangalore, 1784, and the Balance of Power in Southern India

By late 1783 both Tipu Sultan and the Madras government had reasons to seek peace. Tipu's forces held the siege lines at Mangalore, which had been defended by Company troops and was suffering severe shortages, but the continuing war strained Mysore's resources, and the loss of French support altered his strategic position. The Company, for its part, was under pressure from financial exhaustion, military setbacks, and the instructions of the Governor-General and the Directors to end a costly war. The Madras government, led by Lord Macartney, dispatched commissioners to Tipu's camp, and negotiations produced the Treaty of Mangalore, signed on 11 March 1784.

The treaty's central principle was reciprocal restitution. Each party was to return territories captured from the other, and prisoners held by both sides were to be released. Tipu was thereby restored to authority over the Malabar and Kanara coasts as well as the Mysore plateau, and the Company recovered the places it had lost in the Carnatic and elsewhere. The treaty did not impose indemnities or cessions upon Mysore, and its reciprocal provisions treated the contracting authorities as capable of binding themselves without a territorial capitulation. That the Company was willing to accept such terms indicates how seriously its officials assessed the Mysorean threat. The settlement drew criticism from some Company officials, and Madras’s negotiation of a major peace raised disputes about authority within the Company government. The precise positions of individual officials require consultation of their dated correspondence rather than attribution from later reputations.

For Tipu, the treaty confirmed that he had inherited a state able to fight the Company to a standstill. It also gave him a basis for pursuing his own agenda of consolidation, reform, and diplomacy during the later 1780s. At the same time, the treaty left unresolved the underlying strategic problem: Mysore's control of the Malabar Coast and its relationship with France continued to alarm the Company, while Tipu regarded the Company's presence on the coast and its influence in the Carnatic as threats. Peace therefore depended on mutual restraint, which proved fragile.

The costs of the war fell on populations as well as states. The devastation of the Carnatic by Mysorean cavalry and by the Company's own forces, the disruption of agricultural cycles, the requisitioning of grain and transport animals, and the movement of refugees and prisoners all affected cultivators, weavers, merchants, and townspeople. Prisoners held by Tipu, including British soldiers and officers, as well as Indian civilians transferred to Mysore, became a subject of controversy both at the time and subsequently, and historians treat the numbers and conditions with caution because the sources are partisan. The fiscal consequences for the Carnatic's government and the Company's debts also shaped later British policy toward the Nawab of Arcot.

By the mid-1780s the balance of power in southern India was thus finely poised. Mysore under Tipu possessed a large army and a defensible, well-fortified core; Hyderabad and the Marathas were distracted by their own concerns; and the Company, strengthened by the reforms of Pitt's India Act of 1784 and the later arrival of Cornwallis, was moving toward a more systematic and centralized control of policy. The next war would be waged under very different conditions, with a Governor-General personally commanding and with Hyderabad and Maratha forces fighting alongside the Company.

Implementation of the peace mattered as much as its ceremonious signing. The commissioners' surviving journal follows correspondence, exchanges of drafts, and the problems of obtaining signatures and acknowledgment from different Company governments. Article 10 stipulated further subscription by the Madras authorities and acknowledgment by Bengal and Bombay. Those procedures demonstrate that the Company's presidency system was not a single voice, and that a treaty accepted in the field still had to be carried through administrative channels. The release of captives and evacuation of places also required practical coordination long after the principal negotiators had agreed to the language of reciprocal restoration.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through the Wars

The wars are often narrated through commanders and treaties, but their direct burdens fell on cultivators, weavers, artisans, merchants, camp followers, and soldiers' families. Armies in this period lived substantially off the country, drawing grain, fodder, bullocks, and carts from villages through purchase, requisition, or plunder. Mysorean cavalry raids in the Carnatic in 1780 drove villagers from their fields, disrupted textile production around Madras, and contributed to scarcity. Company forces and their allies likewise took supplies. Contemporary Company sources often describe the devastation as the work of the enemy, so the extent of damage caused by each side must be judged with caution.

Armies were accompanied by large numbers of non-combatants: grain merchants, bazaar traders, bullock drivers, washermen, women and children, and bankers' agents. A Company or Mysorean army on the march was in effect a mobile city, and its movement depended on a market of Indian suppliers. Sepoys, the Indian soldiers of the Company's army, were recruited from specific regions and communities, were paid in cash, and were often accompanied by families. Their pay, and the regularity with which it arrived, affected morale and loyalty. The movement of prisoners, including those transferred between Mysore and the Carnatic, uprooted communities, and the conditions of captivity remain debated because the surviving accounts largely come from the captors' opponents.

Section 64.5

Tipu Sultan's State: Military Reform, Revenue, Commerce, and Diplomacy

Tipu Sultan has been remembered in strikingly different ways: as a tyrant in many British accounts, as a heroic anticolonial martyr in many later nationalist ones, and as a controversial figure in contemporary political argument. A historian's task is to reconstruct the state he governed. Tipu inherited an army and administration from Haidar and spent the years after 1784 elaborating them. In this effort he was not merely preparing to fight the Company; he was building a monarchy, with its own symbols, legal and commercial institutions, and claims to legitimacy.

Military reform was a central part of his program. Tipu reorganized his infantry into more systematically structured units, expanded and refined his artillery, and maintained a strong cavalry, though he also encountered difficulty in sustaining horse supply. Mysore's army made distinctive use of iron-cased rockets, whose casings allowed higher pressure and longer range than earlier bamboo or paper types. Mysorean rocket troops were deployed in the wars and impressed British observers; after 1799 captured rockets were sent to Britain, where they contributed to later development by William Congreve. Historians caution, however, against exaggerating their battlefield effect: rockets were useful for harassment and morale rather than decisive on their own. Kaushik Roy and others have placed Mysorean innovations within a wider Indian military culture of adaptation, in which Indian rulers incorporated European-style drill, firearms, and engineering without simply imitating European models.

Tipu's revenue and administrative measures aimed at greater state control. He sought to reduce the power of intermediaries, to strengthen the supervision of revenue collection, and to extend the state's participation in the economy. A commercial department organized state trading in goods such as sandalwood, pepper, and other commodities, with agents or factories in coastal and overseas locations. He issued distinctive coinage, introduced a new calendar and a system of nomenclature for months and years, and reformed court institutions. These measures were consistent with the aim of constructing a distinctive Mysorean sovereignty, expressed through a ruler's titles and symbols. Kate Brittlebank's study of Tipu's search for legitimacy emphasizes how his use of the title "Sultan," his relationship with Mughal and Ottoman sources of authority, and his religious and royal iconography addressed the problem of a ruler who had not inherited a throne.

Diplomacy extended far beyond the subcontinent. Tipu sent embassies to Constantinople in the mid-1780s, seeking Ottoman recognition and assistance, and to France in 1788, where his envoys were received at the court of Louis XVI. He also maintained contact with other potential partners. These initiatives reflected his understanding that Mysore needed external allies against the Company and its Indian partners. They produced symbolic recognition and some commercial and military contacts, but no decisive alliance. In the late 1790s, his contacts with French agents became a pretext for Wellesley's war, though the extent of French assistance actually available to Tipu was limited.

Source criticism is especially needed regarding Tipu's treatment of particular religious and regional communities. Evidence exists that he patronized Hindu institutions, including the Sringeri monastery, and employed Hindu officials in his administration. Other evidence, from British accounts and from the traditions of communities in Malabar, Coorg, and the coast, describes forced conversions, the deportation of populations, and the destruction of temples and churches during his campaigns. Some of these accounts originated with adversaries or with the Company, which had an interest in portraying Tipu as a tyrant, and some of the scholarly literature has been shaped by modern political concerns. The more careful scholarship treats Tipu's policies as shaped by political and military objectives, particularly in areas of resistance, rather than as the expression of a single religious ideology, while acknowledging that coercion against particular groups is documented. Students should therefore avoid both celebratory and uniformly hostile accounts and attend to the specific sources on which each claim rests.

Finally, distinctions must be made between documented reforms and exaggerated claims. Tipu was not a modernizer who had overtaken Europe in military technology, nor a backward oriental despot; he was an energetic ruler of a state under pressure, drawing on multiple traditions. Mysore's revenue base was smaller than the combined resources of the Company's Indian territories, and its dependence on continuous mobilization created strains. These are matters of institutional capacity, not cultural inferiority, and they explain the outcome of the struggle better than claims about civilization or character.

Tipu's use of new calendars, seals, coin legends, and official vocabulary formed part of this state-making project. Such changes expressed royal authority, altered the visual and administrative language of government, and made officers acknowledge an increasingly distinctive Mysorean court. They should not be reduced either to evidence of intolerance or to symbols of secular modernization; their meanings varied across contexts. Economic measures were equally selective. State workshops and monopoly claims coexisted with older merchants and brokers, and the government relied on their expertise even when it sought closer supervision of commerce. Mysore's ambitions therefore exceeded the building of an army, but the army's maintenance created persistent fiscal pressure.

Section 64.6

The Third Anglo-Mysore War, 1790–1792: Travancore, Cornwallis, and the Allied Coalition

The immediate cause of the Third Anglo-Mysore War lay on the southwestern coast, in the relations among Mysore, the kingdom of Travancore, and the Company. Travancore, ruled by Rama Varma, had a treaty relationship with the Company, and in 1789 it acquired from the Dutch the forts of Cranganore and Jaikottai, places that Tipu regarded as lying within his claim to suzerainty over the northern Malabar region and its former Cochin tributaries. Travancore's fortified defensive line, the Nedumkotta, ran along its northern frontier. Tipu attacked the line in December 1789, and the Madras government, regarding Travancore as an ally whose security was linked to Company interests, treated the attack as a casus belli. The war formally began in 1790, with the Company declaring hostilities.

Cornwallis, who had arrived in India as Governor-General in 1786 (see Chapter 63), approached the conflict as a strategic opportunity. He assembled a coalition, concluding alliances with the Nizam of Hyderabad and with the Maratha government at Pune, both of which had grievances against Tipu and territorial interests in the northern parts of his state. The alliance was characteristic of the period: the Company's partners were not subordinates but autonomous states pursuing their own aims, and their cooperation had to be bargained for and sustained. The first campaigns in 1790 under the Madras commander, General Medows, were inconclusive, and Tipu used his cavalry and fortifications to avoid decisive defeat.

Cornwallis then took command in person in early 1791, which signaled the importance of the war to the Company. He advanced from the Carnatic through the passes onto the plateau, captured Bangalore in March 1791, and moved toward Seringapatam. Supply difficulties, the arrival of the monsoon, disease among men and draft animals, and Tipu's mobility forced a withdrawal in mid-1791, but the Company's forces returned in early 1792 with strengthened support from the allies, including Maratha cavalry under Parashurambhau Patwardhan and Hyderabad troops, as well as better-organized transport. In February 1792 they approached Seringapatam, whose fortifications and island position were formidable, and prepared a siege. Tipu recognized the danger of continued war and opened negotiations.

The definitive Treaty of Seringapatam of 18 March 1792 followed preliminary articles reached in late February. Its terms were severe: Tipu agreed to cede roughly half of his territories, distributed among the Company, the Nizam, and the Marathas in shares assessed by revenue rather than as three geographically equal blocks; to pay an indemnity of three crore and thirty lakh rupees; and to deliver two of his sons as hostages to guarantee fulfillment. The Company's share included territories on the Malabar coast, the Baramahal, and Dindigul, which improved its strategic position on both the western and southern approaches to Mysore. The Marathas and Hyderabad received territories on Mysore's northern borders. The settlement did not annex Mysore; it reduced it and imposed obligations, leaving Tipu as a sovereign ruler of a diminished state.

The war revealed both Company strengths and constraints. Its fiscal and logistical resources, together with the commitment of its Governor-General and the cooperation of allies, enabled it to sustain a campaign that earlier commanders had been unable to complete. At the same time, the war was not a walkover. It took repeated attempts, Tipu's cavalry inflicted serious harassment, and the coalition was held together by shared opportunism rather than by loyalty. Tipu, for his part, faced the loss of much of his resource base and the humiliation of the hostage arrangements, and he began reconstruction, which Company observers perceived as preparation for revenge.

The 1792 settlement must also be read as an agreement among victors who did not share identical ambitions. Hyderabad and the Maratha leadership expected compensation for their military participation and for older territorial claims; Cornwallis sought to weaken Mysore without leaving an uncontrollable vacuum or creating an unduly powerful ally. The cessions were apportioned with attention to estimated revenue and access rather than as three mathematically equal slices of a map. The stipulated three crore and thirty lakh rupees represented an indemnity distinct from the land transfers. The hostage requirement was an additional guarantee, and it transformed Tipu's family into an instrument of interstate security without transferring Mysore's remaining sovereignty.

Section 64.7

The Fourth Anglo-Mysore War and the Fall of Seringapatam, 1799

The Fourth Anglo-Mysore War resulted from the intersection of Tipu's diplomacy with the strategic outlook of a new Governor-General. Richard Wellesley, Lord Mornington, arrived in India in 1798 with the conviction that the Company needed to eliminate rival centers of power that might ally with France. Napoleon's expedition to Egypt in 1798 intensified concerns, and Tipu's contacts with French agents, including the proclamation issued at Mauritius (Île de France) in early 1798 inviting recruits for service in Mysore, gave Wellesley the grounds he wanted. Whether Tipu was a real threat or a convenient target is debated; many historians stress that Wellesley was predisposed to war and exaggerated the danger, while recognizing that Tipu had indeed sought French help.

Wellesley's preparations were thorough. In 1798 he concluded a new treaty with Hyderabad that expanded the subsidiary force stationed there and required the Nizam to dismiss the French-trained corps in his service, thereby removing a potential channel of French influence and securing Hyderabad's military cooperation. He then issued ultimatums to Tipu demanding the dismissal of French personnel and the acceptance of a Company Resident, which Tipu in effect refused. In early 1799 the Company assembled forces from Madras and Bombay, with Hyderabad's contingent, and the allied armies advanced into Mysore. The principal army under General George Harris, with Arthur Wellesley commanding Nizam's troops, defeated Tipu at Malavalli in March and moved to Seringapatam.

The siege followed the pattern of earlier sieges but proceeded rapidly. The Company's engineers and artillery established batteries against the western face of the fort, and a breach was opened. The assault on 4 May 1799 stormed the breach and overcame the defenders. Tipu Sultan was killed in the fighting at the fort; his body was identified later that evening. The fall of the capital effectively ended the war and Tipu's state. The storming was followed by plunder of the city and the palace, in which the contents of the treasury, the library, and the royal collections were dispersed, as Company officers, soldiers, and camp followers participated in an organized and unorganized looting. Items from Seringapatam entered museums and private collections in Britain, and their provenance remains a subject of contemporary debate.

It is important to distinguish the military victory from the political decisions that followed. The capture of the capital settled that Tipu's dynasty would not continue; it did not determine what would replace it. Wellesley's government debated whether to annex Mysore entirely, to partition it, or to restore the Wodeyars. Letters preserved among contemporary Company papers, including correspondence in the British Library from May and June 1799, discuss the capture, the circumstances of Tipu's death, and plans for governing the former Mysorean territories. They show a government weighing strategic security, the claims of its allies, the legitimacy it needed in the eyes of Mysore's population, and the cost of direct administration. Both the Company and its allies stood to gain from the settlement.

The operation also illustrates the composition of Company armies. Most of the troops who stormed Seringapatam were Indian sepoys from the Madras and Bombay armies, commanded by European officers, together with contingents from Hyderabad and the Company's British regiments. The participation of Indian soldiers in the destruction of Mysore, as in other campaigns, reminds students that the conquest was not a simple confrontation between Indian and European armies. The sepoys' motives included pay, regimental loyalty, local connections, and in some cases their own political or religious affiliations, and there is little direct evidence of their personal views.

After the assault, identifying Tipu's body and securing the palace archives, military stores, and treasury became matters of political importance. Victorious officers had incentives to depict the ruler's death as honorable combat while justifying the campaign as necessary; Mysorean witnesses had different reasons to preserve accounts of fidelity, loss, and the treatment of civilians. The fall also scattered members of the court and military establishment whose careers did not end with the sultan's. Some entered new employment; others remained under surveillance or sought patrons elsewhere. The postwar settlement is unintelligible unless the defeat of a ruler is distinguished from the fate of the institutions and people who served him.

Section 64.8

The Partition of Mysore, the Restoration of the Wodeyars, and Subsidiary Alliances

The settlement of Mysore in 1799 combined partition with restoration. Wellesley's commissioners, working with the allies, divided the former kingdom. The Company took territories on the coast, including Kanara, and in the south, including Coimbatore and Wynad, along with the city of Seringapatam and its immediate surroundings, which gave it control of the strategic corridors connecting its Madras and Malabar possessions. The Nizam received northern districts, including Gooty and Gurramkonda; the Peshwa, who had not joined the 1799 campaign, was offered a share but declined it, and that allocation was divided between the Company and the Nizam. The Nizam subsequently ceded his 1792 and 1799 Mysore acquisitions to the Company under the treaty of 12 October 1800 to finance an enlarged subsidiary force. The central part of the former kingdom was reconstituted as a smaller Mysore under a child ruler of the Wodeyar family, Krishnaraja III, whose government was effectively directed by his minister Purnaiah, who had served under Haidar and Tipu.

A subsidiary treaty with the restored Wodeyar government, concluded on 8 July 1799, tied Mysore closely to the Company; related partition arrangements among the victors were distinct instruments. It provided for a subsidiary force stationed in Mysore and paid for by the state, gave the Company rights over Mysore's foreign relations and defense, and authorized intervention in the event of misgovernment. The restoration thus served the Company's objectives in several ways: it supplied a form of legitimate indigenous rule, avoided the costs of direct administration of a large territory, reconciled the population by presenting continuity with the pre-Haidar kingdom, and placed the dependent state's military in Company hands. The restoration also allowed the Company to portray the war as a liberation from "usurpation," an interpretation that later historians have treated critically.

The Mysore settlement belongs to a broader system. A subsidiary alliance was a treaty arrangement by which the Company undertook to protect an Indian ruler against external enemies, and sometimes internal ones, in return for the ruler's agreement to maintain a Company-controlled force at his expense and to accept restrictions on his freedom of action. The system had antecedents in earlier arrangements with the Nawab of Arcot and Awadh and with Hyderabad in the 1790s, but under Wellesley it became a deliberate strategy. In 1798 Hyderabad accepted an enlarged subsidiary force and the dissolution of its French-trained units, and in 1800 a further treaty provided for the cession of districts, later known as the Ceded Districts, in lieu of cash payments, which brought Bellary and Cuddapah under Company administration. Awadh's cession of territory in 1801 followed the same logic.

The system's mechanisms are worth distinguishing. A British Resident at the ruler's court served as the Company's political agent, monitoring policy, influencing succession, and communicating with Calcutta. The subsidiary force, nominally for the state's defense, was commanded by Company officers and answerable to the Company. Treaties typically restricted the ruler from entering into diplomatic relations with other states without Company consent and often required the ruler to accept the Company's arbitration in disputes with other Indian states. Cession of territory to pay for the force, or a fixed subsidy, transferred fiscal resources. Individual treaties differed in provisions, and the practical operation changed over time with the balance of power and the personalities of Residents.

Subsidiary alliance must not be confused with annexation. A state under such an alliance retained its ruler, court, and internal administrative institutions, and the Company did not formally govern it. But the alliance subordinated its external and military policy, and Residents often exerted influence over internal matters. This combination of formal sovereignty and practical dependence made the system a central instrument of Company expansion because it extended Company influence at relatively low cost, preserved indigenous elites as intermediaries, and avoided the burden of direct rule. It also created resentment among rulers who found their autonomy curtailed, as the cases of the Peshwa and other Maratha leaders would show.

The restored kingdom depended on a political compromise. Purnaiah's continued administrative work preserved valuable revenue knowledge and personnel from the preceding regime, while the new Wodeyar court supplied dynastic symbolism that Company officials considered useful. The separate treaty and partition documents reveal two linked but different operations: allocation of territory among the powers that had defeated Tipu, and regulation of the surviving Mysore state's dependence. The Nizam's later transfer of his Mysorean acquisitions in 1800 demonstrates the further conversion of the fruits of one coalition war into financial support for a permanent Company-controlled force. Such arrangements were dynamic, not a fixed constitutional template shared by every Indian ruler.

Historical Vocabulary
Subsidiary Alliance

A treaty system in which an Indian ruler accepted the permanent stationing of a Company-officered force, paid for it through cash subsidies or ceded territory, admitted a British Resident, and gave up independent diplomacy, in return for protection against external enemies. The term is associated especially with Richard Wellesley, though earlier arrangements existed.

The word "alliance" can mislead modern readers: the treaties were negotiated between unequal parties, often under military pressure, and their effect was to subordinate the weaker ruler's external policy. At the same time, they were not annexations, and rulers retained internal authority. Understanding the term is essential for distinguishing the different forms of Company power examined in §§64.16–64.17.

Historical Debate 2

Did Subsidiary Alliances Provide Security or Institutionalize Political Dependency?

The question: Were subsidiary alliances protective agreements that benefited rulers by providing external security and internal stability, or were they instruments by which the Company converted independent states into dependencies?

Interpretation A — Security: Defenders of the system, including many Company officials at the time, argued that it protected states from external attack and from the disorder of the period, saved rulers the expense of maintaining large armies, and brought stability. Some rulers, such as the Nizam in the 1790s, did seek Company protection for their own reasons, including fear of Maratha or Mysorean power and the desire to secure their succession.
Interpretation B — Dependency: Critics, then and since, emphasize that the system required rulers to pay for forces they did not control, surrendered territory and diplomatic freedom, and admitted Residents who could intervene in internal affairs. On this view the treaties were negotiated under coercion and functioned as a mechanism of political subordination that left rulers with the appearance of sovereignty but not its substance.

Evidence: The treaty texts, Resident correspondence, financial records of subsidy payments and territorial cessions, and the later histories of Hyderabad, Mysore, and the Peshwa's government bear on the question. The cases differ: Hyderabad entered the system partly from perceived need; the restored Wodeyar state emerged from conquest; the Peshwa's acceptance in 1802 followed military defeat and flight (§64.11).

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the nature of Company expansion: whether it was primarily conquest, or a negotiated and gradual process in which Indian rulers had agency. It also shapes judgments about indigenous rulers' choices.

Section 64.9

The Maratha Confederacy after Panipat: Peshwas, Sardars, and Regional Power

The Maratha defeat at the Third Battle of Panipat in January 1761 (Chapter 58) was a heavy blow, but it did not end Maratha power. Under the Peshwa Madhavrao I, the Marathas recovered influence in Malwa, Hindustan, and the Deccan during the 1760s, and by the 1770s Maratha armies were again operating in the north. The Peshwa at Pune remained the chief executive of the Maratha polity, acting in the name of the Chhatrapati at Satara, but the instruments of Maratha power had become decentralized. Military commanders who had been granted territorial and revenue rights, the sardars, built their own bases, armies, and administrations, and the Peshwa's authority over them rested on a mixture of patronage, legitimacy, negotiation, and, sometimes, force.

The principal houses that emerged were the Shinde (Scindia), based in Malwa and later at Gwalior and Ujjain; the Holkar of Indore; the Bhonsle of Nagpur, who controlled Berar and the eastern Deccan; and the Gaekwad of Baroda in Gujarat. Each possessed substantial revenue resources, military establishments, and diplomatic interests of its own. Mahadji Shinde, who rose to prominence after Panipat, became the most formidable of them. He took control of Delhi in 1771–1772 and later held the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II under his protection, trained infantry and artillery on European lines with the help of foreign officers such as Benoît de Boigne, and acted as a major power in north India. Holkar rulers, including the able Ahilyabai Holkar (d. 1795) as the administrator of Indore's territories, governed with distinct approaches.

The relationships among Peshwa, sardars, and Pune's court were complex. Chauth and sardeshmukhi, the Maratha rights to levy a proportion of revenue in territories not under direct Maratha control, formed the legal basis of much Maratha revenue extraction, and the assignment of these rights supported the sardars' armies. Court patronage and offices at Pune, family connections, and rivalries over succession all affected politics. Stewart Gordon, in his study of the Marathas, describes the political system as built on military entrepreneurs who raised and equipped forces, were remunerated through revenue rights, and negotiated their positions with the central government. It was therefore a flexible and resilient system, able to field large armies and to recover from defeat, but also one in which rivalries could paralyze collective decisions.

At Pune the dominant figure after the 1770s was Nana Fadnavis, an experienced minister who managed the government during the minority of the Peshwa Sawai Madhavrao and thereafter, until his death in 1800. Nana worked to preserve the balance among the Maratha chiefs, to resist Company influence, and to protect the Peshwa's authority, but he could not control the ambitions of Mahadji Shinde or other chiefs, who sometimes cooperated with him and sometimes opposed him. The Peshwa's position was further weakened by repeated succession crises and the lack of a strong adult ruler after Sawai Madhavrao's death in 1795. His successor, Baji Rao II, assumed the office in 1796 after prolonged intrigue.

The Confederacy must therefore not be treated as a centralized kingdom whose leaders acted together against the Company. It was a loose, shifting association whose members cooperated when their interests aligned and competed when they diverged. This feature had two consequences for the wars described below. First, no single decision could mobilize the whole Maratha military system; leaders had to be persuaded, and their forces were not interchangeable. Second, the Company was able to negotiate separately with different Maratha rulers, to exploit rivalries, and to defeat or neutralize them one by one. Its success lay less in overwhelming a united opponent than in dealing with a divided political field.

The political economy of the Confederacy was inseparable from its military structure. Regional houses maintained troops through revenue assignments, tribute rights, and relationships with armed retainers and local banking networks. A successful campaign might strengthen a commander by expanding his claims while diminishing the authority of the Peshwa who nominally stood above him. Chiefs could support the common Maratha interest in one season and quarrel over routes, fortresses, or finance in the next. This did not make the Confederacy unreal; it means that its cohesion should be assessed through actual agreements and joint operations instead of measured against the institutions of a centralized European state.

Section 64.10

The First Anglo-Maratha War, 1775–1782: Succession, Wadgaon, and Salbai

The First Anglo-Maratha War arose from a succession dispute at Pune. After the death of the Peshwa Madhavrao I in 1772, his brother Narayanrao succeeded and was murdered in 1773, an event in which Narayanrao's uncle Raghunathrao (Raghoba) was implicated. Raghunathrao claimed the office, but a group of ministers, including Nana Fadnavis, supported the posthumous infant son of Narayanrao, Sawai Madhavrao, born in 1774, and governed in his name. Raghunathrao, expelled from Pune, sought external help and turned to the Company's Bombay government, which saw an opportunity to gain territory, including the island of Salsette and the port of Bassein, near Bombay.

In 1775 Bombay concluded the Treaty of Surat with Raghunathrao, promising military support in exchange for territorial cessions. The Company's Supreme Council in Calcutta, newly created under the Regulating Act of 1773 (Chapter 63), disapproved of Bombay's unauthorized initiative, and in 1776 negotiated a different agreement, the Treaty of Purandar, with the Pune ministers. The disagreement between Bombay and Calcutta, and subsequent shifts in policy, illustrate how the Company's internal divisions weakened its strategy. Bombay's officials later resumed support for Raghunathrao, and the war continued intermittently. The inconsistency of the Company's presidencies and their rivalry over policy made it hard for them to present a coherent position, a problem that the later centralization of authority under Governors-General was designed to address.

The decisive engagement came in January 1779 at Wadgaon, west of Pune. A Bombay force advancing toward Pune was outmaneuvered by Maratha forces led by Mahadji Shinde and others, who harassed its supply lines and surrounded it, and the Company officers agreed to the Convention of Wadgaon, which required a retreat and the surrender of conquests. The convention was repudiated by the Bengal government, but it was a major humiliation. Calcutta then responded with its own military initiative, dispatching a force under Colonel Thomas Goddard that marched across central India to Gujarat, where it took Ahmedabad and Bassein, and another under Captain William Popham, which in 1780 captured the fortress of Gwalior from the Marathas. These operations showed the Company's capacity to project force over great distances, but they did not break Maratha power.

The war ended with the Treaty of Salbai, concluded in May 1782 after negotiations in which Mahadji Shinde served as mediator and guarantor. The treaty provided for the restoration of most conquests, recognized Sawai Madhavrao as Peshwa, provided a pension for Raghunathrao, and allowed the Company to retain Salsette. It also contributed to peace between the Marathas and the Company for about two decades, and it freed the Company to concentrate on Mysore (§64.3). For the Marathas, it affirmed the success of Nana Fadnavis's policy of resisting Company intervention in Pune's affairs and underscored the influence of Mahadji Shinde.

The war demonstrated Maratha military resilience, the effect of Company internal divisions, and the importance of succession disputes as openings for outside intervention. British Library records catalog extensive documentation on Maratha affairs for the 1770s and on the Treaty of Salbai, and they show that the Company regarded the Marathas as the most substantial indigenous power in India. For the next two decades, the Marathas' own difficulties, including the deaths of Mahadji Shinde (1794) and Nana Fadnavis (1800) and the weakness of Baji Rao II, would present the Company with the opportunities that the war had not provided.

The settlement at Salbai reflected compromises by several authorities rather than one decisive battlefield verdict. The Company had not been able to enforce the Bombay government's initial political preferences, while Maratha leaders also had reasons to reduce the burden of fighting and to stabilize the Peshwa's position. Mahadji Shinde played an important intermediary role, showing that chiefs usually remembered as military adversaries could act as guarantors and negotiators. The episode cautions against treating the First Maratha War as merely a prelude to defeat: for many years after 1782 the Company had to consider Maratha armies and diplomacy when deciding its own course.

Section 64.11

The Treaty of Bassein, 1802: Maratha Politics and the Crisis of Peshwa Authority

The political situation at Pune after Nana Fadnavis's death in March 1800 grew increasingly unstable. Baji Rao II, who had long resented his dependence on Nana, sought to assert authority but lacked a secure base, and he came into conflict with the powerful chiefs Daulat Rao Shinde, who had succeeded Mahadji Shinde and exercised considerable influence at Pune, and Yashwantrao Holkar, the ambitious leader of the Holkar house. Rivalry between Shinde and Holkar, a feud that had its own history, shaped the crisis: each sought to control the Peshwa, and the Peshwa sought to play them against each other. The assassination of Holkar's brother Vithoji at the Peshwa's order in 1801 further embittered the situation.

On 25 October 1802, Holkar's forces defeated the combined army of the Peshwa and Shinde near Pune, at Hadapsar. Baji Rao fled the capital and sought refuge on the coast, eventually reaching Bassein, where he entered negotiations with the Company. His objective was to recover his position at Pune and protect his authority from rival chiefs, and he judged that Company assistance offered the best prospect. Wellesley, for his part, had long sought a means of drawing the Peshwa into the subsidiary system, and he regarded the crisis as an opportunity. The aims of the two men diverged: the Peshwa wished to secure his office, while Wellesley wanted to establish Company predominance in Maratha politics.

The Treaty of Bassein, signed on 31 December 1802, was the outcome. By its terms, the Peshwa accepted a permanent subsidiary force of Company troops, to be paid for by the assignment of territory yielding a specified revenue; he agreed not to enter into negotiations with other states or to wage war without consultation with the Company; he accepted the Company's mediation in his disputes with other states, notably Hyderabad; and he agreed to dismiss European (non-British) personnel in his service. In return the Company undertook to restore him to Pune and defend his government. The treaty was therefore both a restoration agreement and a statement of subordination. In May 1803 Company forces, including a column under Arthur Wellesley, escorted Baji Rao back to Pune.

Other Maratha leaders interpreted the treaty as a threat to the political balance. The Confederacy's equilibrium depended on the Peshwa's role as a nominal center that no single chief could control; a Peshwa under Company protection broke that arrangement and gave the Company an arbiter's position over Maratha affairs. Shinde and the Bhonsle of Nagpur, in particular, regarded the treaty as an infringement of Maratha sovereignty, and they prepared to resist. Holkar, though he had been the Peshwa's adversary, also had reason to oppose the Company. It is important to note, however, that these leaders did not at once form a united alliance, and that the Gaekwad of Baroda, who accepted a subsidiary arrangement in 1802, took a different course. The treaty thus split as well as provoked the Maratha states.

Treaties concluded under the pressure of flight or military intervention demand attention to the contracting parties' unequal alternatives. Baji Rao II could formally consent to the Bassein clauses, yet his capacity to refuse was limited by the immediate loss of Pune and the hostility of powerful chiefs. The Company's promise of restoration had real value to him and was not a fictional bargain. It also transferred the responsibility for his security to a power whose objectives were wider than the Peshwa's recovery of office. Historians should therefore evaluate the treaty both as an intelligible decision by a threatened ruler and as a mechanism through which Company supervision entered the core of Maratha politics.

Section 64.12

The Second Anglo-Maratha War, 1803–1805: Assaye, Laswari, Delhi, and Regional Settlements

The Second Anglo-Maratha War began in 1803 when Daulat Rao Shinde and Raghuji Bhonsle II of Nagpur, unwilling to accept the consequences of Bassein, moved their forces together. The Company, prepared by Wellesley, fought on two main fronts: in the Deccan, under Arthur Wellesley, and in the north, under General Gerard Lake, the commander-in-chief. The simultaneous operations, coordinated from Calcutta, illustrate the Company's ability to deploy large forces across theaters, and Wellesley had also laid in supplies, funds, and allied support, including Hyderabad's contingent.

In the Deccan, Arthur Wellesley captured the fortress of Ahmednagar in August 1803, then met the combined Shinde and Bhonsle armies at Assaye on 23 September 1803. Outnumbered, with a force of British and sepoy troops, he attacked the Maratha position near the Kaitna river, where the Marathas' artillery, much of it trained and directed by European-style methods, inflicted severe losses. The Company forces nonetheless held the field, and the battle was a costly victory that Wellesley later regarded as among his hardest. (Source caution: some catalogues and captions give 24 September; 23 September is the conventional date.) Further engagements at Argaon in November and the capture of the fortress of Gawilghur in December ended the Deccan campaign, with the Bhonsle forces defeated.

In the north, Lake's army took Aligarh from the forces trained by the French-born officer Pierre Cuillier-Perron in September 1803, defeated the Shinde's army at Delhi on 11 September, and entered the city, taking the blind Mughal emperor Shah Alam II under Company protection. Agra fell in October, and on 1 November Lake defeated the remaining Shinde regular forces at Laswari. These successes removed Shinde's northern army and his control of the Mughal emperor, with symbolic importance: the Company now held the person of the Mughal sovereign, and with him a claim to legitimacy that Mahadji Shinde had once held.

Peace treaties followed in December 1803. By the Treaty of Deogaon (17 December), the Bhonsle of Nagpur ceded Cuttack and Balasore on the eastern coast, connecting the Company's Bengal and Madras territories, and territories in the west of his domain, and accepted restrictions, including the admission of a Resident. By the Treaty of Surji-Anjangaon (30 December), Shinde ceded the territory between the Ganges and the Yamuna, together with Delhi and Agra, and other territories, including Ahmednagar and Broach, and recognized the arrangements of Bassein. The settlements gave the Company a continuous belt of territory and a decisive position in the north.

The war did not eliminate Maratha power. Yashwantrao Holkar, who had not joined the Shinde-Bhonsle coalition, opposed the Company in 1804. A British detachment under Colonel William Monson was forced into a disastrous retreat in the monsoon of 1804, and Holkar's forces, together with those of the Jat ruler of Bharatpur, resisted the Company's offensive. Lake's repeated assaults on the fortress of Bharatpur in early 1805 failed, a notable check. The war was costly, and the expense and setbacks contributed to criticism of Wellesley in London and to his recall in 1805. Subsequent arrangements, including the treaty with Holkar concluded in late 1805 at Rajpurghat, allowed Holkar to retain much of his territory. The Company's next Governor-General, Cornwallis, who died shortly after arriving in 1805, and his successor, George Barlow, followed a policy of retrenchment.

The war thus increased the Company's territorial and diplomatic reach substantially without eliminating every Maratha state. It also revealed Maratha military capacity, particularly the use of European-style infantry and artillery, and the limits of the Company's ability to prevail against a fortress or against irregular warfare. The Marathas lost because their forces were divided, because the Company's resources and logistics proved sustainable, and because Wellesley's diplomacy kept some Maratha leaders out of the war. The peace left the Peshwa, Shinde, Holkar, Bhonsle, and Gaekwad with greatly different relationships to the Company, the subject of the next division.

The outcomes of the 1803 campaigns were not uniform across theaters. Field victories gave the Company leverage in negotiations, but the defense of Bharatpur showed that its artillery and infantry could be exhausted by determined fortification and inadequate preparation. Maratha commanders sometimes deployed well-trained infantry and excellent guns, yet disagreements over strategy and political priorities made concentration difficult. Wellesley's armies depended on local carriers, cattle, grain merchants, and banking credit, so their apparent battlefield cohesion concealed an extensive network of South Asian contributors. The importance of those networks is one reason modern military historians have moved away from the idea that European drill alone decided the war.

Section 64.13

The Company and the Maratha States after 1805: Diplomacy, Residents, and Regional Competition

The decade after 1805 was one of retrenchment on the Company's side and consolidation, with persistent turbulence, among the Maratha states. The directors in London, alarmed by the expense of Wellesley's wars, instructed his successors to avoid new commitments, and Governors-General such as George Barlow and Lord Minto sought to limit interference in Indian states. The Company's hold on its new territories was nevertheless secure, and its Residents at the courts of the Peshwa, Shinde, Bhonsle, and others remained in place. During this period, formal relations remained intact while rulers sought to rebuild their positions.

The Maratha states followed divergent paths. The Peshwa Baji Rao II, restored to Pune by Company forces and bound by the treaty of Bassein, attempted to rebuild his authority by managing his revenue and favoring administrators of his choice, sometimes in tension with powerful jagirdars and southern chiefs. The house of Shinde under Daulat Rao, based increasingly at Gwalior, retained a large army but faced fiscal difficulties and unrest. The Holkar state at Indore declined after Yashwantrao Holkar’s death in 1811. His minor successor, Malhar Rao Holkar (usually numbered III), governed under the regency of Tulsi Bai, whose authority was contested by military leaders; she was killed by factions in the army in December 1817, shortly before Mahidpur. The Bhonsle state of Nagpur, subject to a subsidiary arrangement after 1816, was beset by intrigue. The Gaekwad of Baroda, tied to the Company since 1802, relied on its support in disputes with the Peshwa over revenue claims.

Company Residents, among them Mountstuart Elphinstone, who was Resident at Pune from 1811, played increasingly important roles. A Resident gathered information, mediated disputes between the host state and others, advised on succession, and communicated with the Governor-General. Where Company influence was strong, Residents' counsel became hard to refuse. Revenue disputes over areas in which the Peshwa and the Gaekwad had competing claims, the recruitment of soldiers who might serve either the Company or an Indian prince, and the activities of armed bands all required the Residents' attention. Company diplomacy therefore increasingly shaped decisions within states that were never formally annexed, an expression of the logic of the subsidiary system.

A change of Company policy accompanied the arrival of Francis Rawdon-Hastings, Earl of Moira and later Marquess of Hastings, as Governor-General in 1813. Hastings, a soldier, was more willing than his immediate predecessors to use force to settle the political order of central and western India. He had experience of the Nepal War of 1814–1816, which had brought Company territory to the Himalayan foothills, and he was convinced that the unsettled conditions of central India, including the activities of the Pindaris, justified a comprehensive campaign. The Court of Directors gave qualified approval. The conditions for the final Maratha war thus came from a combination of a more assertive Governor-General, a perception of instability in central India, and tensions at Pune.

The years of uneasy peace also changed the political environment for Indian courts. Residents gathered information about succession, debt, and troop movements, and their reports could become a pretext for demands that went beyond the literal terms of earlier treaties. Local leaders could appeal to these agents against rivals, but doing so risked strengthening the very authority they hoped to use instrumentally. Holkar's minority regime illustrates the problem especially clearly: the ruler's age, Tulsi Bai's disputed regency, and the power of soldiers and commanders made coherent diplomacy difficult even before Company forces entered the field. The interaction of internal conflict and external pressure was more consequential than a simple narrative of political decline.

Section 64.14

The Pindari Campaign and the Origins of the Third Anglo-Maratha War, 1817

The Pindaris were bands of mounted irregulars, many originating as camp followers and auxiliaries of Maratha armies, who operated in central India, especially Malwa and the Narmada valley, and who raided in the Deccan and in Company territories. Major Pindari band leaders included Chitu, Karim Khan, and Wasil Muhammad; Amir Khan, later the Nawab of Tonk, was instead an important Pathan mercenary commander with his own organized force who dealt with Pindari leaders and Maratha rulers but should not be classified unqualifiedly as a Pindari chief. These commanders occupied distinct positions in a shifting military labor market, and some held lands or recognized patronage from Maratha rulers. They were not an organized state, nor simply bandits: they belonged to the military labor market of the period, in which mounted men sought employment, plunder, and patronage. Their raids in the early 1810s, including attacks on Company-protected districts, were used by Company officials as a justification for intervention.

It is important to distinguish Pindari raiders from the formal armies and governments of the Maratha states. Some Maratha rulers, notably Shinde and Holkar, tolerated or used Pindari bands, and Company officials charged them with complicity; in the formal sense, though, the Pindaris were separate from the courts and often independent actors. The distinction mattered because the Company's campaign presented itself as police action against marauders while also functioning to compel Indian rulers to accept new obligations. Hastings assembled large forces in 1817, deploying more than a hundred thousand men in a double-sided operation, and negotiated agreements with Shinde, such as the Treaty of Gwalior in November 1817, that bound him to cooperate against the Pindaris and limited his autonomy.

At Pune, the relationship between Baji Rao II and the Company deteriorated sharply in 1815–1817. In July 1815 Gangadhar Shastri, a minister and envoy of the Gaekwad of Baroda who was at Pune for negotiations on disputed revenues and who had come under Company protection, was murdered at Pandharpur. The suspicion fell on Trimbakji Dengle, a favorite of the Peshwa. Elphinstone, the Resident, demanded Trimbakji's surrender. The Peshwa complied and Trimbakji was imprisoned in the fort of Thane, but he escaped in 1816, and the Company suspected the Peshwa of aiding him and of gathering troops. Baji Rao, for his part, sought to resist further erosion of his authority and may have encouraged resistance, though the exact extent of his planning is debated.

In June 1817 Elphinstone, with military pressure at hand, compelled the Peshwa to sign the Treaty of Pune. Its terms required him to renounce his headship of the Maratha Confederacy and his claims over the Gaekwad, to cede territory including fortresses and districts, to accept further restrictions on his relations with other states, and to agree to the surrender of Trimbakji's family members. The treaty represented a deep reduction of Peshwa autonomy, going beyond Bassein. British Library records on the 1815–1817 disputes and the treaty permit direct comparison of the terms of 1802 and 1817 (see Source Window 5).

The road to war thus involved three developments that converged in 1817: a Governor-General willing to take military initiative, a campaign against the Pindaris that created an enormous Company military presence in central India, and an increasingly desperate Peshwa whose autonomy had been progressively eroded. The Company's broader mobilization and the Peshwa's declining autonomy, rather than a single event, made renewed warfare likely, and when it came the Peshwa's attack on the Pune Residency in November 1817 provided the formal occasion.

The military labels of this period can obscure important distinctions. Pindari bands were usually mounted raiding formations tied to patronage networks but not synonymous with every independent cavalry company or Pathan mercenary household in central India. Amir Khan's force pursued its own territorial and diplomatic arrangements, whereas Chitu and Karim Khan headed major Pindari bodies; personnel moved between such formations, but their chains of command were not identical. Company narratives often grouped disparate armed men under the language of predation, while rulers who employed them might treat them as auxiliary troops. Making these distinctions enables students to evaluate the political purpose of an ostensibly anti-raiding campaign.

Historical Vocabulary
Pindari

A member of one of the mounted irregular bands that operated in central India in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, drawing on a long tradition of auxiliary cavalry associated with Maratha and other armies. Pindari leaders could hold land, receive patronage, and command thousands of horsemen, but they were not a state and operated for plunder as well as service.

British sources often use the word as a synonym for "bandit" or "freebooter," which reflects the Company's justification for intervention. Historians prefer to treat Pindaris as part of a wider military labor market, shaped by the contraction of employment as armies were disbanded and by the political fragmentation of central India, while recognizing that their raids caused real suffering to cultivators.

Section 64.15

The Third Anglo-Maratha War, 1817–1818: Khadki, Sitabuldi, Mahidpur, and the Defeat of the Peshwa

The war opened at Pune. On 5 November 1817, after the Company brought additional troops toward the city and the Residency was burned, Peshwa forces attacked the Company's force at Khadki (Kirkee), near Pune. The Company's brigade, commanded by Lieutenant-Colonel Charles Burr, with a force far smaller than that of the Peshwa, held its ground against attacks, partly because the Peshwa's army was not well coordinated and its commanders were divided. The Peshwa withdrew, and Pune fell to Company forces under Brigadier-General Lionel Smith later in the month. The defeat did not end the Peshwa's war, but it removed him from his capital.

Meanwhile the war spread. In Nagpur, Appa Sahib (Mudhoji II), a collateral Bhonsle claimant who became regent after Raghuji II died in March 1816, accepted the May 1816 subsidiary treaty while Parsoji, Raghuji’s son, was still nominal ruler. After Parsoji’s suspicious death in early 1817, Appa Sahib was installed as ruler; his relationship with Resident Richard Jenkins subsequently deteriorated. Contemporary accounts accused him of responsibility for Parsoji’s death, but the accusation requires attribution rather than presentation as an established judicial finding. On the night of 26–27 November 1817, Bhonsle forces attacked the Residency escort on the hills of Sitabuldi, near Nagpur. The small Company force, composed largely of Madras sepoys with limited cavalry and a few guns, held the hills against heavy odds in a closely fought action, and reinforcements arrived. Appa Sahib was subsequently arrested and deposed, and later escaped, ending his career in exile.

In central India, the principal engagement took place at Mahidpur on 21 December 1817, where the army of Holkar, formally under the minor Malhar Rao Holkar III but dominated by military leaders following the death of regent Tulsi Bai, was defeated by a Company force under Sir Thomas Hislop, with Sir John Malcolm's division also in the theater. The Holkar government, whose army had been attracted to the war party, then accepted the Treaty of Mandsaur in January 1818, which ceded territory and imposed Company control over its foreign relations. The Shinde, constrained by the Treaty of Gwalior, did not fight, though some of his commanders were suspected of sympathy for the Peshwa. The war thus did not involve a unified Maratha alliance against the Company; different rulers acted at different times, and some did not act at all.

The war continued in the Deccan, where the Peshwa moved from place to place, pursued by Company columns. On 1 January 1818, a detachment of the Bombay Native Infantry under Captain Francis Staunton, marching toward Pune, met a much larger Peshwa force at Koregaon, on the Bhima river. The Company detachment, conventionally counted at roughly 800–850 personnel including its infantry, auxiliary horse, and artillery, held the village during repeated attacks; a substantial number of its Indian infantrymen were Mahars, and the Peshwa’s attackers withdrew by night. Accounts disagree on the battle’s outcome and on the explanation for the withdrawal, and the size of the entire nearby Peshwa host must not be confused with the number of troops directly attacking the village. The engagement was militarily limited, but it later acquired great significance in Dalit memory. In the twentieth century B. R. Ambedkar and Mahar communities commemorated Koregaon as a symbol of Mahar courage and as a statement against caste oppression under Peshwa rule, and the site is today a place of annual gathering. Students should keep separate what is known about the soldiers' motives in 1818, for which direct evidence is sparse, and the meanings that later communities attached to the battle.

Further actions at Ashti in February 1818, in which the Peshwa's commander Bapu Gokhale was killed, and the fall of forts finished the military campaign. Baji Rao II, with dwindling forces and having lost the support of many chiefs, surrendered to Sir John Malcolm in early June 1818. The war involved Company sepoys, Indian and British officers, artillery, cavalry, and supply systems, and varied by circumstance: at Khadki and Sitabuldi, small Company forces survived Indian attacks because of discipline, position, and the divided command of their opponents; at Mahidpur, a larger Company force defeated an army whose political leadership was already negotiating. Avoiding a story of unified resistance, one sees a collapse of a political order whose component states had lost the ability and the will to coordinate.

Numbers attached to these engagements require particular restraint. Contemporary accounts of Khadki and Sitabuldi frequently compared small Company detachments with the whole army available to their opponents, even though only part of the larger force engaged at a given moment. At Koregaon the conventional figure of roughly eight hundred refers to the Company detachment including its various arms; it must not be compared directly with the tens of thousands associated with the Peshwa's nearby host as though all took part in the assault. Reports of killed, wounded, and missing also differ among official returns and later narratives. A careful military account should explain defensive position, command, reinforcement, and exhaustion before invoking numerical disparity as proof of superiority.

Section 64.16

The Settlement of 1818: Maratha Subordination and British Paramountcy

Hastings and his officers determined the settlement. The office of Peshwa was abolished, and most of the Peshwa's territories were annexed to the Company's dominion and placed under Mountstuart Elphinstone as Commissioner of the Deccan. Baji Rao II was pensioned and sent to live at Bithur, near Kanpur, where he spent the rest of his life. The Company restored a reduced kingdom of Satara under Pratap Singh, a descendant of Shivaji, a symbolic restoration that presented the Company as the protector of Maratha legitimacy while removing the Peshwas' power. The Bhonsle state of Nagpur was placed under a minor ruler with Company supervision, and Holkar's and Shinde's states, along with Baroda, remained as subordinate states.

The arrangement formalized what is called British paramountcy: the Company's claim to superior authority over Indian rulers, particularly in external relations, war, diplomacy, and, increasingly, succession, while the rulers retained varying degrees of internal government. It was in effect a hierarchy of political forms. Some territories, such as the former Peshwa lands, were directly administered. Others were governed by Indian rulers bound by subsidiary treaties. Still others, like Satara, were restored as dependent kingdoms. The relationships were not uniform: Hyderabad's, Mysore's, Nagpur's, Shinde's, and Holkar's treaties differed, and their practical working depended on the strength of the ruler and the Resident.

Students should distinguish among the forms. Annexation meant direct assumption of government. Subsidiary alliance meant retention of an Indian ruler with a Company force, Resident, and restricted diplomacy. A protectorate-like relationship described states whose defense and foreign policy the Company controlled without necessarily imposing a subsidiary force. Beyond these, the Company claimed rights of mediation and, in time, of supervising successions. The terminology was fluid, and the formal doctrine of paramountcy was refined later in the nineteenth century (Chapter 71), but the practices that underlay it were established in the settlements of 1799 and 1818.

The extent and the limits of Company supremacy in 1818 deserve emphasis. The Company dominated the Deccan, the Ganges plains, southern India, and much of central and western India, and remaining independent states, notably the Sikh kingdom under Ranjit Singh, retained significant military capabilities even though the Company enjoyed a predominant position in much of southern, eastern, and central India. It had not, however, established uncontested sovereignty over the whole subcontinent. The Sikh kingdom of Ranjit Singh in Punjab, Sindh, Nepal, and many smaller states remained outside direct Company control, and the Company's hold over some conquered regions was untested. Revolts, local resistance, and rural unrest, which Chapter 68 examines, continued. The settlement of 1818 was therefore a high point of Company power in the south and west, not the end of Indian political independence.

The 1818 settlement did not abolish Maratha political society. Courts, religious foundations, families of commanders, village institutions, and networks of revenue and credit survived in newly dependent states or in British-administered districts. Some chiefs negotiated terms that preserved estates and titles; others lost service and patronage. Baji Rao's pension and the reconstituted Satara court exemplify the use of money and dynastic recognition to redirect loyalty. The Company could claim supremacy because it had defeated the Peshwa's government, but its everyday influence continued to depend on agreements with those who controlled resources and social authority locally. This helps explain why direct rule and indirect dependency developed side by side.

Historical Vocabulary
Paramountcy

The Company's claim to supreme political authority over Indian states, expressed in its control of their external relations, its right to arbitrate disputes, and its influence over succession, while allowing most rulers to govern internally. The term gained doctrinal force in the later nineteenth century, but the practices it describes took shape in the subsidiary treaties of 1799–1818.

Paramountcy should not be confused with annexation. A paramount power asserted superiority without necessarily assuming government. This allowed the Company to rule much of India through indigenous rulers, a pattern that persisted until 1947 (Chapter 71).

Cause and Effect

Why the Company Achieved Predominance, 1767–1818

Long-term causes

The fiscal and military resources of Bengal and other Company territories; the sepoy army and its institutional continuity; the fragmentation of Mughal authority into competing successor states; and the Company's access to credit and Indian financial networks.

Short-term causes

Succession disputes, such as the Peshwa's in 1802; alliances of convenience with Hyderabad and Maratha leaders; Wellesley's and Hastings's aggressive policies; and strategic anxieties about France.

Immediate effects

Destruction of Tipu Sultan's state; the Treaty of Bassein; cession of territories in 1803 and 1818; abolition of the Peshwaship; installation of Residents and subsidiary forces across much of the subcontinent.

Long-term consequences

A hierarchical political order of directly ruled provinces and subordinate princely states; the weakening of the main rivals to Company rule; the preparation of the way for Company expansion into Sindh and Punjab (Chapter 65).

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outcome. Mysore and the Marathas won battles and forced concessions, and the Company suffered setbacks at Pollilur, Wadgaon, and Bharatpur. The result depended on the interaction of resources, logistics, and diplomacy with the political divisions of Indian states.

Section 64.17

Territorial Expansion, Princely States, and the New Political Geography of India

Between 1767 and 1818 the political map of South Asia changed profoundly. In 1767 the Company directly governed Bengal, Bihar, and parts of Orissa through the diwani (Chapter 62), together with scattered coastal enclaves around Madras, Bombay, and elsewhere. By 1818 it directly governed, in addition, the Carnatic, much of the Malabar and Kanara coasts, the Ceded Districts, Mysore's former southern and coastal territories, the Ganges-Yamuna Doab and Delhi region, Cuttack, and, after 1818, the greater part of the former Peshwa dominions. This was a vast, though not continuous, domain, administered by the presidencies of Bengal, Madras, and Bombay.

Alongside these areas lay states ruled by Indian princes under subsidiary or other treaty arrangements. Hyderabad, the largest, retained its Nizam and administration under a Resident and subsidiary force. Mysore, reduced to a central core under the Wodeyars, was similarly bound. Awadh, Baroda, Nagpur, Indore, Gwalior, and many smaller states in Rajputana and central India accepted varying degrees of subordination, with the Rajput states entering protective treaties in 1817–1818. The Nawab of Arcot's authority in the Carnatic had been progressively reduced, and by the early nineteenth century the Carnatic's government was largely in Company hands. These "princely states," as they later came to be known, covered a large part of the subcontinent and would persist, as Chapter 71 explains, until 1947.

Geography explains the pattern. Company territories formed strategic corridors: a coastal belt linking Bengal and Madras through Cuttack after 1803; the Ganges valley linking Calcutta to Delhi; the Madras-Malabar link through the Palghat and the plateau after 1799; and the western coast and the Deccan after 1818. Control of river systems such as the Ganges, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri, of ports such as Madras, Bombay, Surat, Bassein, and the Malabar harbors, and of passes through the Ghats facilitated military movement and trade. Forts such as Seringapatam, Gawilghur, and Ahmednagar anchored regions. The Company's strategic gains were therefore both territorial and logistical, creating lines of communication that reduced its vulnerability.

The changes in the Indian states' territories were as important. Mysore lost nearly half its lands in 1792 and the rest of its former expanse in 1799. The Maratha states ceded land in 1803 and 1818 and lost the Peshwa's institutions of central authority. The Nizam gained and lost: he acquired territory from Mysore but ceded the Ceded Districts and accepted dependence. These changes were not merely subtractions from Indian power; they reconfigured the relationships among Indian states, which now looked to Residents and Calcutta rather than to each other as arbiters.

The subcontinent in 1818 was nonetheless not completely under British rule. Ranjit Singh's Sikh state in Punjab was at its height of power; Sindh was ruled by the Talpur Amirs; Nepal, defeated in 1814–1816, retained its independence under the Treaty of Sugauli; Sri Lanka's coastal regions were British, but the Kandyan kingdom survived until 1815; and numerous smaller territories, hill states, and tribal regions lay beyond effective control. The conquests of Sindh and Punjab belong to Chapter 65.

A map of these settlements should therefore identify kinds of authority rather than paint a single undifferentiated British color. The Company's ability to station troops, control an ally's diplomacy, demand tribute, or collect land revenue did not always coincide geographically. The Company's strategic positions along roads and river valleys might be continuous without the intervening countryside being administered under identical laws. Nor did a ruler's acceptance of a Resident extinguish every local claim to legitimacy. The shape of territorial supremacy was produced through layers of unequal arrangements, not simply the movement of borders after battle.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Political Order of South Asia, 1760s–1818

What Changed

  • The Company moved from a coastal trading corporation to the paramount power across much of the subcontinent.
  • Mysore ceased to be an independent military state; the Peshwa's office was abolished.
  • Residents, subsidiary forces, and treaties replaced diplomacy among Indian courts as a framework of interstate relations.
  • Indian soldiers increasingly served under Company command.

What Stayed the Same

  • Indian rulers, courts, and elites remained in place across large regions.
  • Village society, revenue practices, and local landholding continued, though under new fiscal pressures.
  • The military labor market of mounted and infantry soldiers continued to supply armies.
  • Regional identities and religious institutions persisted.

Who experienced change most directly? Rulers, ministers, and military elites of Mysore and the Maratha states, and soldiers, gained or lost employment; cultivators in contested zones suffered the immediate effects of war. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers in regions where local revenue officers and landholders remained in place. Why the pattern matters: It shows that conquest was largely a change at the top of the political order, with continuity in many local structures that the Company drew on.

Section 64.18

The Military-Fiscal Foundations of British Expansion: Armies, Revenue, Credit, and Logistics

Chapter 63 described the Company state as a military-fiscal regime in which revenue extraction and army maintenance sustained each other. The wars of this chapter show the regime at work. Bengal's land revenue, collected after 1765 and stabilized by the Permanent Settlement of 1793, supplied funds that the Company could use to pay sepoys regularly, an advantage in a period when many Indian armies suffered from arrears. The Company could remit money from Bengal to Madras and Bombay, as when Warren Hastings sent funds and troops south in 1781, and it could borrow from Indian bankers and merchants, sometimes at substantial interest, and from British investors in London. The ability to raise credit depended on its reputation for eventual payment, itself reinforced by its victories.

The wars were expensive, and military debt accumulated. Wellesley's campaigns increased the Company's debt substantially, and London's reaction to that expense contributed to his recall. Nevertheless, the Company's financial system outlasted the strain because it commanded revenues from territories conquered in each war. Mysore's coastal provinces, the Ceded Districts, the Doab, and Cuttack provided new revenue to support armies in the next campaign. Conquest thus financed further conquest, though not without cost, and the cycle has been characterized by historians as a self-reinforcing process.

Logistics determined campaign feasibility. Armies required grain, forage, draught bullocks, carts, and porters, much of it purchased from Indian merchants and grain-carriers, the banjaras. Company forces depended on commissariat contractors and on arrangements with local merchants and bankers; the Company's regular pay helped to attract suppliers who might otherwise have preferred the opponent. Siege equipment, including heavy guns and powder, was transported with difficulty. Monsoon rains, rivers in spate, and disease could halt campaigns, as Cornwallis found in 1791. The Company's advantages came from planning and from assembling its resources before war began, as Wellesley did in 1799 and Hastings in 1817.

Mysore and the Maratha states possessed formidable capabilities. Tipu's army combined cavalry, drilled infantry, artillery, and rockets, and was supported by a revenue and arsenal system. The Marathas fielded large cavalry forces, light and mobile, that excelled at raiding and at cutting supply lines, as at Wadgaon and in the Carnatic; Shinde's armies included infantry trained by European and other officers and substantial artillery, which performed well at Assaye. Fortified places, such as Seringapatam, Bharatpur, and Maratha hill forts, were difficult to take. Many European specialists, some of them French, served in Indian armies, but their influence was limited by political suspicion and by the particular structures of Indian armies.

The Company's armies were composed largely of Indian sepoys, recruited from regions including Bengal, Madras, and the Bombay coast, and organized in regiments with European officers. Sepoys provided the bulk of the infantry and, as at Koregaon and Sitabuldi, often the decisive element. Company armies had the advantage of regular pay and institutional continuity, but they also had weaknesses: sepoy grievances over pay, rank, and religious practice, which would erupt in later years (Chapter 69), and a dependence on cavalry from allies, since the Company's own mounted units were fewer. Kaushik Roy has argued that the Company's success depended on its ability to combine European-style organization with Indian military labor and logistics, rather than on any simple technological superiority.

Explaining the wars by a narrative of European technological superiority therefore fails. Mysorean rockets, Maratha artillery, and Indian cavalry were effective; the Company was often outgunned or outnumbered, as at Assaye. What distinguished it was an institutional capacity to coordinate resources across regions, to sustain armies financially, and to take advantage of political divisions among its rivals. The ability to move money, credit, and men between Bengal, Madras, and Bombay gave it a strategic reach that no single Indian state could match.

Historical Debate 1

Were Mysore and the Maratha States Militarily Outmatched or Politically Disadvantaged?

The question: Did the Company prevail because its military and financial organization was superior, or because Indian states were divided and politically disadvantaged?

Interpretation A — Military and fiscal superiority: Emphasizes the Company's disciplined, regularly paid armies, the revenue of Bengal, maritime logistics, and its ability to concentrate resources. On this view, the Company's institutions gave it a structural advantage in prolonged conflict, even when it lost individual battles.
Interpretation B — Political disadvantage and fragmentation: Emphasizes the rivalries among Indian states, the Nizam's and Maratha leaders' decisions to ally with the Company against Mysore, succession disputes at Pune, and the failure of Maratha chiefs to coordinate. On this view, the Company exploited divisions rather than overcoming united resistance. Work associated with C. A. Bayly and with historians of Maratha politics stresses the role of Indian collaborators and political competition.

Evidence: Battle outcomes, financial records, treaties, and diplomatic correspondence. Mysore's innovations and the Marathas' military success in some engagements complicate any claim of simple superiority.

Why it matters: The question affects whether conquest is explained by European exceptionalism or by contingent politics, and thus whether Indian states appear as passive victims or as active participants.

Student task: Distinguish between treaty provisions and their operation, and decide which explanation better accounts for the defeats of Mysore in 1799 and of the Peshwa in 1818. Consider Pollilur, Wadgaon, and Bharatpur as counterexamples.

Section 64.19

Historiography of Expansion: Conquest, Alliance, Resistance, and the Question of Inevitability

Interpretations of Company expansion have changed greatly. Nineteenth-century British imperial histories, including those of James Mill and later writers, often presented the wars as the working out of a civilizing destiny or a defensive necessity, and they portrayed rulers such as Tipu as tyrants and the Maratha chiefs as predatory. Indian nationalist narratives, in contrast, honored Tipu and some Maratha leaders as early resisters, sometimes reading modern nationalism back into eighteenth-century politics. Both frameworks have been criticized for imposing later categories on a period when states acted according to their own interests.

Modern scholarship has emphasized the role of Indian agency and institutions. C. A. Bayly, in Indian Society and the Making of the British Empire (1988) and Imperial Meridian (1989), argued that Company expansion drew on Indian commercial, financial, and military networks and on the political dynamics of regional states, so that the conquest was in part a product of Indian conditions. P. J. Marshall, in Bengal: The British Bridgehead (1987), emphasized the Company's resource base in Bengal and the way its gains were achieved within an Indian political context, rejecting simple collapse-of-India narratives. Stewart Gordon's The Marathas, 1600–1818 (1993) treats the Maratha polity as a military-fiscal system with distinctive strengths and constraints, and Kate Brittlebank's Tipu Sultan's Search for Legitimacy (1997) examines Tipu's use of religious and royal symbols to establish his authority. Kaushik Roy's War, Culture and Society in Early Modern South Asia (2011) analyzes military change and the military labor market, and Randolf G. S. Cooper’s The Anglo-Maratha Campaigns and the Contest for India (2003) concentrates on the war of 1803 and its military economy, rather than providing equally detailed coverage of all campaigns through 1818. Mohibbul Hasan's History of Tipu Sultan (1951) remains an influential older biography. Irfan Habib’s edited State and Diplomacy under Tipu Sultan: Documents and Essays (Tulika, 2001; later edition 2014) collects documents and essays on Mysore’s diplomacy. (Students should consult these works directly; the summaries here are brief and the arguments more nuanced than they can convey.)

Historians also debate the role of individuals. Wellesley's reputation has been contested between admirers who see a far-sighted statesman and critics who see a reckless imperialist; Baji Rao II has been presented as treacherous or as a ruler under impossible pressure; Tipu as a modernizer or as a fanatic. More careful assessments treat each as a political actor whose choices were shaped by structures and constraints. The question of inevitability remains. Some historians point to the Company's resources and institutional continuity as making eventual predominance likely; others stress the contingency of events such as Wadgaon, Pollilur, or the death of Mahadji Shinde in 1794, which might have altered outcomes.

The Company's leaders themselves faced uncertainty. Cornwallis in 1791, Wellesley in 1803, and Lord Hastings in 1817 each risked failure, and reverses such as Monson's retreat and the failures at Bharatpur showed that victory was not assured. The distinction between eventual predominance and contemporary uncertainty is central: a historian writing with knowledge of the outcome should not assume that participants saw it coming. This chapter has therefore treated Mysore and the Maratha states as political systems with their own strengths and constraints and has sought to explain their defeat without reducing it to inevitability or to inferiority.

The bibliography itself helps demonstrate the change in historical explanation. Diplomatic editions provide treaty language, military histories reconstruct troop movements, and studies of political economy ask how governments raised and spent the resources that sustained campaigns. These sources answer different questions and should not be collapsed into one explanation. Even a reliable victory report may describe the immediate tactical result more convincingly than the motives of a ruler or the long-term effect on agriculture. Students comparing interpretations should therefore identify each author's evidence, the scale of analysis, and any unstated assumptions about the comparative capacities of Indian and Company institutions.

Section 64.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The wars of 1767–1818 transformed a regional rivalry into an unequal political order. In the first phase, the Company and Mysore fought as comparable powers, and the treaties of 1769 and 1784 reflected reciprocity. By 1792 the Company, with allies, could impose cessions, an indemnity, and hostages; in 1799 it ended Mysore's independent existence, partitioning the state and restoring a dependent dynasty. Meanwhile, the Maratha Confederacy, strong enough to force the Convention of Wadgaon in 1779 and the Treaty of Salbai in 1782, was weakened by succession struggles and rivalries, so that by 1802 the Peshwa sought Company protection at the price of subordination. The Second Anglo-Maratha War extended Company territory and the Third ended the Peshwaship.

Comparing the wars shows both common features and differences. Mysore was a centralized state whose defeat required the destruction of its ruler; the Maratha Confederacy was a polycentric system that was dismantled in stages, with different states settled differently. The Anglo-Mysore wars were fought primarily on the plateau and coast of the south, with the strategic problem of fortress capital and coastal supply; the Maratha wars were fought across the Deccan, Malwa, and Hindustan, with the problem of coordination among theaters. The settlements of 1799 and 1818 both combined annexation of key territories, retention of reduced indigenous dynasties as dependent rulers, and the installation of Residents and subsidiary forces, a template for later expansion.

The contributions of different factors were interlocking. Financial resources from Bengal and Company credit sustained armies; military administration and the sepoy system provided continuity; alliances with Hyderabad and with Maratha leaders were critical in 1792 and 1799; internal regional competition and succession disputes opened doors to intervention; diplomacy, including Wellesley's subsidiary treaties and Hastings's agreements with Shinde and others, isolated opponents; and geographic strategy, as in the acquisition of Cuttack, Seringapatam, and the Doab, secured corridors of communication.

The central question is why a commercial corporation could defeat or subordinate powers with substantial armies, resources, and administrative traditions. The answer proposed by the evidence is that the Company was no longer merely a commercial corporation. By the 1790s it was a territorial state with a bureaucracy, a large army, and a revenue system, able to coordinate across regions and to exploit political divisions. Its opponents were capable but operated within a political landscape of rival states that often preferred to ally with the Company rather than with each other. Its predominance was an achievement of institutions and politics, contested at every stage.

Students should also remember that conquest did not mean the end of Indian political independence, which the Sikh state and others preserved, or of Indian resistance, which continued in many forms. They should likewise remember the human cost: the devastation of campaigns, the plunder of Seringapatam, the displacement of rulers and soldiers, and the dependence imposed on states that kept their thrones. Chapter 65 turns to the northwest, where Ranjit Singh's Punjab, Sindh, and the final expansion of Company rule complete the geographic sequence begun here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the wars through which the English East India Company, between 1767 and 1818, became the predominant power across much of southern and western India. It argued that this outcome was not automatic: Mysore and the Maratha states were significant powers with their own institutions, objectives, and strategic choices, and the Company's victories resulted from a specific combination of finance, logistics, diplomacy, and the political divisions of its rivals.

Division I examined Mysore under Haidar Ali, who from about 1761 built a centralized military state with strong cavalry, disciplined infantry, artillery, and an effective revenue system. In the First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769), shifting alliances and Haidar's mobility brought his cavalry to Madras and produced the Treaty of Madras, whose defensive clause became a source of later dispute. In the Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784), the defeat at Pollilur showed the vulnerability of Company forces, and only Bengal's reinforcements, the death of Haidar, and the end of French support led to the Treaty of Mangalore, a reciprocal peace.

Division II treated Tipu Sultan as a state-builder who reformed his army, introduced rocket and artillery innovations, expanded state commerce, issued distinctive coinage, and conducted diplomacy with France and the Ottoman world. His treatment of particular communities is documented but requires careful source criticism. The Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792), prompted by the dispute over Travancore and fought by Cornwallis with Hyderabad and Maratha allies, ended in the Treaty of Seringapatam, with cessions, an indemnity, and hostages. The Fourth War, under Richard Wellesley, ended with the storming of Seringapatam on 4 May 1799 and Tipu's death.

The settlement of 1799 partitioned Mysore, restored a Wodeyar ruler in a reduced kingdom, and bound it by a subsidiary treaty. This belonged to a wider subsidiary alliance system, developed with Hyderabad in 1798 and 1800 and elsewhere, by which rulers accepted Company-officered forces, Residents, and restrictions on diplomacy. Such alliances differed from annexation but subordinated external and military policy, a feature debated by historians who ask whether they provided security or institutionalized dependency.

Division III turned to the Maratha Confederacy, a loose association of the Peshwa and the sardar houses of Shinde, Holkar, Bhonsle, and Gaekwad, whose decentralized system produced large armies but made collective action difficult. The First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782), arising from a Peshwa succession dispute, saw the Company's defeat at Wadgaon and ended with the Treaty of Salbai. After Nana Fadnavis's death and Holkar's victory at Pune in 1802, the Peshwa accepted the Treaty of Bassein, which brought the Maratha center into the subsidiary system and provoked the Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805).

That war, fought in the Deccan and the north, brought victories at Assaye, Delhi, and Laswari, and the treaties of Deogaon and Surji-Anjangaon transferred substantial territory. Yet Monson's retreat and the failure at Bharatpur showed the limits of Company power, and the war did not eliminate the Maratha states. Division IV followed the period after 1805, in which Residents increased their influence, and the Pindari campaign and the Peshwa's declining autonomy under the Treaty of Pune (1817) led to the Third Anglo-Maratha War. The fighting at Khadki, Sitabuldi, Mahidpur, and Koregaon did not involve a unified Maratha resistance, and the war ended with the surrender of Baji Rao II and the abolition of the Peshwaship in 1818.

The settlement of 1818 consolidated British paramountcy: a hierarchy of directly governed territories, subsidiary states, and dependent kingdoms. Division V described the new political geography, in which the Company controlled strategic corridors and large territories while Indian rulers retained internal government in many regions, and noted that Punjab, Sindh, Nepal, and other territories remained outside Company control.

The chapter analyzed the military-fiscal foundations of expansion: Bengal's revenue, credit, sepoy armies, and logistical organization, set against the real capabilities of Mysore and the Maratha states. It rejected explanations based on automatic European technological superiority and emphasized the interaction of institutions, alliances, and political fragmentation. It then surveyed the historiography, from imperial and nationalist narratives to modern scholarship by Bayly, Marshall, Gordon, Brittlebank, Roy, Cooper, and others.

Throughout, the evidence came largely from Company archives, including treaty texts and correspondence, whose perspective must be weighed against Indian sources and attributed scholarship. The chapter closes in 1818, with Company dominance in the south and west established but incomplete, preparing the way for the northwestern conquests of Chapter 65.

A chief revenue or financial minister, such as Purnaiah in Mysore after 1799.
Carnatic
The southeastern coastal plain around Madras, ruled nominally by the Nawab of Arcot, the main arena of the early Mysore wars.
Presidency
One of the three Company administrative and military divisions: Bengal, Madras, and Bombay.
Mysorean rocket
An iron-cased rocket used by Haidar's and Tipu's armies, effective for harassment and morale.
Siege train
Heavy artillery and equipment transported to reduce fortified places.
Indemnity
A payment imposed on a defeated state, as on Tipu Sultan in 1792.
Hostage
A person, often a royal son, held to guarantee fulfillment of treaty obligations.
Pindari
A member of mounted irregular bands in central India, targeted in the campaign of 1817.
Bakhar
A Marathi prose chronicle, a source for Maratha history.
Treaty of Salbai
The 1782 settlement ending the First Anglo-Maratha War.
Treaty of Bassein
The 31 December 1802 agreement by which the Peshwa accepted a subsidiary force and limits on diplomacy.
Treaty of Mangalore
The March 1784 peace between Tipu Sultan and the Company, based on reciprocal restitution.
Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)
The settlement ending the Third Anglo-Mysore War, with cessions, indemnity, and hostages.
Ceded Districts
Territories (including Bellary and Cuddapah) ceded by Hyderabad to the Company in 1800 in lieu of subsidiary payments.
Military-fiscal state
A state whose revenue system and army sustain one another, a concept used to explain the Company's capacities.
Princely state
A state ruled by an Indian prince under treaty relations with the Company or Crown.
Wodeyar
The royal house of Mysore, nominally sovereign under Haidar and Tipu and restored in 1799.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how the political landscape that would define the nineteenth century took shape. Between 1767 and 1818, a system of directly governed provinces and subordinate states emerged that structured South Asian politics until 1947, and the instruments developed in these wars, Residents, subsidiary forces, and paramountcy, outlasted the Company itself.

It also shows how conquest worked in practice. The Company's success rested less on a clash of civilizations than on the interaction of revenue, credit, logistics, diplomacy, and the choices of Indian rulers and soldiers, and in this sense Chapters 62 through 64 form a single argument about the making of a colonial state, in which Chapter 62 explains the acquisition of resources, Chapter 63 their organization, and this chapter their use.

The chapter also reminds students that defeated states were not static. Mysore's reforms, Maratha political structures, and the sepoys' own motives show societies actively shaping their circumstances, and the memories of these events, from Tipu Sultan to Koregaon, continue to be contested. Reading them with care means separating what the evidence supports from what later politics has made of it.

Chapter 65 now turns to the northwest, where Ranjit Singh's Sikh kingdom, Sindh, and the annexation of Punjab complete the geographic expansion of Company rule begun in Bengal and extended across the south and west in the wars of this chapter.
