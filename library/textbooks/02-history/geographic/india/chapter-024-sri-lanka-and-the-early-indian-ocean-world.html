Sri Lanka and the Early Indian Ocean World

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Chapter 23 followed the Tamil-speaking mainland of Tamilakam through its chiefs, its poets, and its ports. This chapter crosses the narrow, shallow channel of the Palk Strait to an island whose early history has been reconstructed through an unusually rich, and unusually complicated, combination of archaeology, royal inscriptions, and one of South Asia's most influential bodies of historical writing: the Pali chronicle tradition.

At Anuradhapura, in the dry north-central plain of Sri Lanka, centuries of excavation have uncovered a city whose scale surprises many students encountering it for the first time: monumental stupas rising well over a hundred feet, extensive monastic complexes, elaborate urban water-management systems, and a surrounding countryside laced with reservoirs, channels, and paddy fields that together sustained one of the early historic world's most sophisticated dry-zone agricultural civilizations. Long before European travelers described Ceylon as a fabled island of gems and cinnamon, Anuradhapura had already stood for the better part of a millennium as a major South Asian political and religious center, its monks studying and teaching alongside the merchants, artisans, and cultivators who built and sustained it.

Sri Lanka's early historic development did not occur in isolation. Its coasts opened onto sea lanes connecting the eastern and western halves of the Indian Ocean; its northern regions lay within easy reach of Tamil-speaking South India across a strait narrow enough to be crossed on modest craft; and its religious institutions, from an early date, formed part of a wider Buddhist world stretching from the Gangetic heartland examined in earlier chapters to the monastic networks this textbook will trace further in Chapter 25. At the same time, the island developed political institutions, a distinctive Buddhist textual and historiographical tradition, and forms of agrarian organization that were genuinely its own, shaped by an environment considerably different from that of the mainland regions examined in the preceding three chapters.

This chapter also introduces a source-critical challenge unlike any this textbook has faced since its treatment of the Buddha's and Mahavira's biographies in Chapters 11 and 12. Sri Lanka possesses, in the Pali chronicle tradition examined at length in Section 24.6, an extraordinarily detailed narrative of its own early kings, its conversion to Buddhism, and the foundation of its major religious institutions — composed, however, centuries after the events it describes, within a monastic community with its own historical and religious purposes. Historians must therefore constantly ask a question that recurs throughout this chapter: what does archaeology independently confirm, what does inscriptional evidence independently confirm, and what belongs, instead, to a later community's remembered and carefully arranged account of its own origins? None of these kinds of evidence should simply be discarded in favor of another. Learning to read them together, without collapsing their real differences, is this chapter's central historical task.

The chapter begins, appropriately, before any chronicle narrative and before Buddhism itself, with the island's archaeological record of early settlement, agriculture, and Iron Age society. It then turns to Anuradhapura's emergence as a genuine early historic city, to the political culture of kingship that developed around the control and sponsorship of irrigation, to the introduction and subsequent institutional development of Buddhism, to the remarkable historiographical tradition that Buddhism helped produce, to the island's ongoing relationships with South India, and finally to Sri Lanka's place within the wider commercial world of the Indian Ocean — a theme Chapter 25 will generalize into a fuller regional synthesis.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Sri Lanka neither as an isolated island story cut off from the historical developments examined elsewhere in this volume, nor as a simple extension of Tamil or North Indian history transplanted across water. It was, instead, a distinctive South Asian society, formed through indigenous development and sustained external connection alike — a pattern this chapter argues characterizes much of the early historic world this volume has traced since Chapter 19.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the archaeological evidence for early settlement, agriculture, and Iron Age society in Sri Lanka prior to the development of Anuradhapura.
  2. Trace the growth of Anuradhapura from an Iron Age settlement into a major early historic city and explain the evidence for its urban development.
  3. Explain the relationship between kingship, irrigation infrastructure, and agrarian surplus in early Sri Lankan political culture.
  4. Distinguish between the Pali chronicle account of Buddhism's introduction to Sri Lanka and what inscriptions and archaeology independently establish, including the specific and limited evidentiary weight of Ashoka's own inscriptions.
  5. Identify the major early monastic institutions of Anuradhapura, including the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana, and distinguish their traditional foundation narratives from archaeologically securable fact.
  6. Evaluate the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa as historical sources, assessing both their value and their limitations as retrospective, monastically composed literature.
  7. Describe Sri Lanka's political, commercial, and religious connections with South India, including the Elara-Dutthagamani tradition, without projecting later Sinhala-Tamil national identities backward onto the early historic period.
  8. Explain Sri Lanka's position within early Indian Ocean maritime exchange, using specific archaeological and epigraphic evidence such as the Godavaya port site.
  9. Synthesize the island's early historic development as a distinctive South Asian society shaped by both indigenous institutions and sustained external connection.
  10. Assess the comparative strengths and limitations of archaeology, epigraphy, and chronicle literature as evidence for early Sri Lankan history.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does archaeology reveal about Sri Lankan society before the emergence of Anuradhapura and before the arrival of Buddhism?
  2. How did Anuradhapura grow from an Iron Age settlement into one of early historic South Asia's major cities?
  3. Why did control over irrigation and agrarian surplus become so closely tied to royal legitimacy in Sri Lanka?
  4. What do Ashoka's own inscriptions actually claim about Tamraparni, and how does that differ from what the Sri Lankan chronicles later narrate about Mahinda's mission?
  5. How did Buddhist monastic institutions, including the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana, become embedded within Sri Lankan political and economic life?
  6. Why must historians read the Mahavamsa and related chronicles as both valuable evidence and a retrospective, purpose-driven literary tradition?
  7. How did Sri Lanka's relationship with South India, including episodes such as the Elara-Dutthagamani tradition, shape its early historic development?
  8. What was Sri Lanka's role within the wider Indian Ocean commercial world of this period, and what does a site such as Godavaya reveal about that role?
  9. In what sense was Sri Lanka both a distinctive island society and an active participant in wider South Asian and Indian Ocean systems?

Historical Context

Chapter 23 examined the political, literary, and commercial world of Tamilakam, the early historic Tamil-speaking region of the South Indian mainland, tracing the Chera, Chola, and Pandya chiefdoms, the Sangam poetic corpus, and the ports that connected the far south to Roman Mediterranean and Southeast Asian trade. That chapter closed by gesturing across the Palk Strait to Sri Lanka, a closely connected but historically distinct island world. This chapter takes up that handoff directly.

Sri Lanka's geography sets important terms for its early history. The island's north-central and eastern regions form a broad dry zone, receiving the bulk of their rainfall during a concentrated monsoon season and requiring water storage and irrigation to sustain intensive rice cultivation across the rest of the year, a challenge examined closely in Section 24.3. The island's southwestern regions, by contrast, receive more reliable year-round rainfall and developed different agricultural and settlement patterns whose fuller treatment belongs to later volumes as Sri Lanka's political geography shifted southward in later centuries. This chapter's narrative centers chiefly on the dry-zone civilization associated with Anuradhapura, because this is where the great majority of the early historic archaeological, inscriptional, and literary evidence is concentrated.

The island's connections to the wider world were never episodic interruptions to an otherwise self-contained history. The Palk Strait separating Sri Lanka from the Tamil mainland is narrow and shallow, historically navigable by modest craft, and it functioned throughout the island's history as a corridor of migration, trade, warfare, and religious exchange rather than as a barrier. Sri Lanka's coasts also opened directly onto the Indian Ocean's monsoon-driven maritime routes, placing the island within reach of ports on both coasts of the Indian subcontinent, and, more distantly, of the Arabian Sea and Roman Mediterranean trade examined in Chapter 23 and generalized further in Chapter 25.

Sri Lanka also occupies a distinctive place in South Asian historiography because of the survival of the Pali chronicle tradition, above all the Dipavamsa and the Mahavamsa, composed within Sri Lankan Buddhist monastic communities beginning in roughly the fourth century CE but describing events reaching back to the third century BCE and, in legendary material, considerably earlier still. This textbook has cautioned repeatedly, since its treatment of the Buddha's biography in Chapter 11, against treating retrospective religious literature as unmediated contemporary reportage. That caution applies with particular force to this chapter, because the Mahavamsa's narrative of Buddhism's introduction to the island has shaped not only later Sri Lankan religious identity but also modern political discourse, a point this chapter addresses directly and with appropriate historical discipline in Sections 24.4 and 24.6.

Section 24.1

Early Settlement and Archaeology

Sri Lanka's history did not begin with kings, chronicles, or Buddhism. Long before any of these, communities inhabited the island's varied environments — coastal lowlands, dry-zone plains, and the wetter southwestern hill country — exploiting local resources through foraging, fishing, and, increasingly across the first millennium BCE, farming and iron-based technology. Archaeological evidence for this earlier period remains considerably less extensive than the evidence available for the Mature Harappan or Vedic worlds examined in earlier chapters, but recent excavation has steadily extended the documented sequence of settlement, agriculture, and craft production well before Anuradhapura's rise to political prominence.

Iron Age communities in Sri Lanka, attested from roughly 1000–600 BCE, practiced settled agriculture, kept domesticated animals, worked iron for tools and weapons, and, in many regions, buried their dead within megalithic burial traditions involving stone-lined graves and associated grave goods. These burial traditions show meaningful connections with contemporaneous South Indian megalithic cultures examined in Chapter 8, a pattern consistent with the cross-strait movement of people, goods, and technology that this chapter traces throughout, without implying that Sri Lankan Iron Age society was simply an extension of the South Indian mainland. Distinctive local forms of pottery, settlement organization, and burial practice indicate genuine regional development alongside contact and exchange.

Archaeobotanical and archaeozoological evidence from this period documents cultivation of rice and other crops, along with animal husbandry, laying an agricultural foundation that would later support the far more intensive dry-zone irrigation systems examined in Section 24.3. Settlement surveys indicate a growing number and density of village sites across the later first millennium BCE, a pattern of gradual population growth and agricultural intensification broadly comparable to developments this textbook has traced for other regions of South Asia during the same general period, including the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9 and the South Indian Iron Age societies examined in Chapter 8.

It is essential for students to keep this archaeological sequence clearly distinct from the much later Pali chronicle narrative examined in Sections 24.4 and 24.6. The Mahavamsa opens with legendary material concerning the island's earliest settlement, including narratives of migration from North India associated with a figure named Vijaya, material that later Sri Lankan tradition has treated as foundational to Sinhala identity. This textbook does not treat such legendary origin narratives as archaeological or historical fact. They belong, instead, to a later community's understanding of its own deep past, composed centuries after the archaeological Iron Age this section has just examined, and they are best studied as evidence for later historical memory rather than as a reliable guide to the island's actual early settlement history. The genuine archaeological record — Iron Age agriculture, megalithic burial, growing settlement density, and technological development — provides the historically secure foundation from which Anuradhapura's urbanization, examined next, eventually emerged.

Section 24.2

Anuradhapura

Anuradhapura, situated in the north-central dry zone near the Malvatu Oya river, developed across many centuries from an Iron Age settlement into one of early historic South Asia's most substantial cities. Archaeological excavation at the site's citadel mound indicates a Protohistoric Iron Age occupation beginning around 900–500 BCE, considerably earlier than the site's emergence as a major political and religious center. From roughly 500 BCE onward, the settlement entered a transitional phase of Early Historic urban growth, with expanding habitation deposits, increasing craft production, and, by the third century BCE, the first monumental religious architecture examined further in Section 24.5. This long, layered sequence is consistent with this chapter's broader argument that Sri Lankan urbanization, like the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9, was the outcome of a cumulative multi-century process rather than a sudden foundation.

By the third century BCE, Anuradhapura had become a substantial urban center, with evidence including extensive habitation deposits, craft production areas, and growing monumental construction. The city's growth depended directly on the surrounding dry-zone agricultural landscape and its increasingly elaborate irrigation infrastructure, examined in Section 24.3: a large urban population required a correspondingly large and reliable agricultural surplus, and Anuradhapura's rulers invested heavily, across many generations, in the water-management systems that made such a surplus possible.

Anuradhapura functioned simultaneously as several different kinds of center. As a political capital, it housed royal administration and, in later centuries, an increasingly elaborate court. As a religious center, it became host to some of the Buddhist world's most significant monastic institutions and monuments, examined in Section 24.5. As an economic center, it supported craft production, regional exchange, and connections extending, via Sri Lanka's ports, into the wider Indian Ocean commercial world examined in Section 24.8. Students should resist reducing Anuradhapura to any single function; its historical significance lies precisely in how thoroughly these political, religious, and economic roles reinforced one another.

This textbook also cautions against a common oversimplification found in some popular accounts: that Anuradhapura was founded, essentially complete, by a single royal act. The archaeological sequence instead shows a long, cumulative development — beginning in the Iron Age, passing through several centuries of Early Historic transition, and only reaching monumental urban scale by the Mauryan-contemporary third century BCE — unfolding across many successive generations and rulers, closely paralleling the developmental patterns this textbook has traced for early historic cities elsewhere in South Asia, including the Gangetic cities examined in Chapter 9 and Tamilakam's ports examined in Chapter 23.

Section 24.3

Kingship and Irrigation

The dry zone surrounding Anuradhapura receives concentrated seasonal rainfall, chiefly during the northeast monsoon, followed by an extended dry period during which intensive rice cultivation would be impossible without stored and carefully distributed water. Meeting this challenge required substantial investment in tank irrigation — artificial reservoirs, often of considerable size, that captured monsoon runoff for release across the growing season through networks of channels — and, over time, increasingly elaborate cascade systems linking multiple reservoirs along a single drainage basin so that water released from an upstream tank could be captured and reused by tanks farther downstream.

Sri Lankan kingship became closely, though not exclusively, identified with the sponsorship, expansion, and symbolic control of this irrigation infrastructure. Royal inscriptions and later chronicle tradition alike celebrate rulers for constructing or enlarging major reservoirs, and this association between kingship and water management recurs as a central theme in Sri Lankan political culture across many subsequent centuries examined in later volumes of this textbook. This textbook must caution, however, against a simplistic formula sometimes encountered in popular treatments: the claim that kings alone, through singular acts of royal will, created dry-zone civilization from nothing. The archaeological and comparative evidence instead suggests a more layered picture. Local communities accumulated engineering knowledge and organized labor for smaller-scale water management long before any single monumental royal reservoir, and much of the dry zone's cumulative irrigation infrastructure represents the combined product of many generations of incremental investment by local communities, provincial administrators, religious institutions, and kings alike, rather than the achievement of isolated royal genius.

What royal sponsorship of large-scale irrigation works did accomplish, however, was historically significant in its own right. Major reservoirs required labor mobilization, resource coordination, and sustained administrative capacity that only a state with substantial coercive and organizational reach could typically provide, and the ability to undertake such projects was itself a demonstration and reinforcement of royal authority. The agricultural surplus generated by expanded irrigation supported taxation, population concentration at centers such as Anuradhapura, the maintenance of an increasingly elaborate court and administration, and, crucially for the following sections, sustained patronage of Buddhist monastic institutions. This chapter's Cause and Effect feature below examines this relationship between water, surplus, and royal power directly.

Section 24.4

Introduction of Buddhism

No episode in early Sri Lankan history requires more careful source-critical handling than the introduction of Buddhism to the island. The Pali chronicle tradition, examined fully in Section 24.6, preserves an elaborate and celebrated narrative: that the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, examined in Chapter 16, sent his son or close relative Mahinda as a missionary to Sri Lanka during the reign of the Sri Lankan ruler Devanampiya Tissa, that Mahinda's teaching led to the king's conversion and the establishment of Buddhism as a royally sponsored religion, and that a series of subsequent events — the arrival of a branch of the bodhi tree, the establishment of an order of Buddhist nuns under Ashoka's daughter Sanghamitta, and the foundation of major monastic institutions — followed in relatively short order.

This textbook must state precisely, rather than loosely, what Ashoka's own inscriptions actually say, because this evidence is frequently overstated in popular accounts. Ashoka's Major Rock Edict II refers to Tamraparni — the ancient name for Sri Lanka — among the territories beyond his own borders where he claims to have provided medical treatment for people and animals, and Major Rock Edict XIII claims that his dhamma, examined in Chapter 16, had reached as far as Tamraparni among other named realms. These are genuine, contemporary, third-century-BCE epigraphic references to Sri Lanka, and they establish that Ashoka's court was aware of the island and claimed some form of dhamma-oriented outreach toward it. What they do not do is independently confirm the specific Mahinda-Devanampiya Tissa encounter, its precise date, its dramatic circumstances, or the court-sponsored conversion mechanism the chronicles later narrate in such vivid detail. The edicts demonstrate Mauryan awareness and claimed outreach; they are not a Mauryan record of a completed mission or a royal conversion.

Independent evidence from Sri Lanka itself is, in some respects, more secure. Archaeological and epigraphic evidence, examined further in the Epigraphy Study feature below, documents an organized, patronized Buddhist monastic presence on the island from at least the third century BCE, in the form of Brahmi cave inscriptions recording donations to monks. This body of evidence establishes with reasonable confidence that Buddhism became an organized, supported institutional presence in Sri Lanka at approximately the period the chronicles describe. Taken together, the Ashokan inscriptions and the Sri Lankan donative inscriptions give historians grounds for confidence that a significant, roughly third-century-BCE process of Buddhist institutionalization did occur on the island, very possibly connected in some way to the wider Mauryan Buddhist world.

What neither body of contemporary evidence establishes is the chronicle narrative's specific cast of characters, its precise sequence of miraculous and dramatic events, its exact dialogue, or many of its more elaborated details, which were composed several centuries after the events they describe within a monastic tradition invested in presenting Buddhism's arrival as a decisive, providentially guided turning point in the island's history. This textbook's position, consistent with its treatment of the Buddha's own biography in Chapter 11, is that the general historical claim — meaningful third-century-BCE contact between the Mauryan dhamma world and Sri Lanka, together with the establishment of an organized Buddhist monastic presence on the island — is reasonably well supported by converging independent evidence, while the chronicle's specific narrative details, including the particular royal-court mechanism it describes, belong to later Buddhist historical memory as much as to contemporary historical record. Section 24.6 examines this distinction, and its broader implications for reading Sri Lankan chronicle literature, in considerably greater depth.

Section 24.5

Monasteries and Royal Patronage

Whatever the precise circumstances of Buddhism's introduction, the religion's subsequent institutional development in Sri Lanka is documented with considerably greater confidence through archaeology and epigraphy, and it took shape around a small number of major monastic complexes whose specific names and histories students should know. Across the centuries following the period examined in Section 24.4, Anuradhapura became host to an increasingly elaborate landscape of Buddhist monastic institutions, of which three stand out with particular clarity in both the archaeological record and later tradition.

The Mahavihara, "Great Monastery," was, according to chronicle tradition, the earliest of Anuradhapura's major monastic foundations, associated directly with the period of Mahinda and Devanampiya Tissa examined in Section 24.4. It was within Mahavihara circles that the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa, examined in Section 24.6, were composed, and the Mahavihara remained an institution of major and lasting importance. It did not, however, stand alone or unchallenged: it later existed alongside powerful competing fraternities, especially Abhayagiri and Jetavana, whose fortunes varied considerably with royal patronage across the following centuries. Abhayagiri, founded according to tradition somewhat later, developed into a major monastic complex in its own right, at times standing in productive tension, and occasionally open rivalry, with the Mahavihara over doctrine, patronage, and institutional authority — a reminder that Sri Lankan Buddhism, like the Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical traditions examined in Chapters 11 through 13, was never a single, internally unified institution. Jetavana, a third major complex, became home to one of the largest brick stupas constructed anywhere in the ancient world, a monument whose sheer scale testifies to the resources that royal and non-royal patrons alike were prepared to commit to Buddhist monumental construction.

Students should distinguish carefully between what chronicle tradition claims about each institution's founding — specific royal patrons, precise dates, and dramatic foundation narratives — and what archaeology can independently confirm, which is generally a longer and more gradual pattern of construction, expansion, and renovation across many centuries rather than a single founding moment. UNESCO's own identification of these three complexes as the major monastic centers of ancient Anuradhapura reflects modern archaeological consensus about the site's institutional landscape, even where specific foundation legends remain, as this chapter has emphasized throughout, the product of later retrospective composition.

Royal patronage played a central and repeatedly documented role in this institutional development. Kings sponsored the construction of major stupas and monasteries, granted land and villages for their upkeep, and, in the process, forged a close and mutually reinforcing relationship between royal legitimacy and Buddhist institutional support broadly comparable to the pattern this textbook examined for Ashoka's own relationship to Buddhism in Chapter 16. A ruler's standing as a patron of the sangha became an important component of royal ideology, and major monastic foundations frequently bear direct or indirect testimony, through inscriptions and later chronicle record alike, to specific royal donors.

Royal patronage, however, was not the only source of support the sangha received, and this chapter's Epigraphy Study feature below illustrates the point directly: the earliest Brahmi cave inscriptions recording donations to monks name a wide range of donors, including local officials, merchants, and other non-royal individuals, indicating that Buddhist monastic support drew on a broader base of Sri Lankan society from an early date rather than depending exclusively on royal largesse. The monastic communities that developed at the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, Jetavana, and elsewhere on the island also became significant economic actors in their own right, administering substantial landed endowments, organizing agricultural labor on monastic estates, and, as Section 24.8 will show for the port of Godavaya, drawing revenue directly from commercial activity.

Section 24.6

Pali Literary Traditions

Sri Lanka's Buddhist monastic communities produced one of premodern South Asia's most historically significant bodies of literature: the vamsa, or chronicle, tradition, most notably the Dipavamsa, generally dated to around the fourth century CE, and the considerably more polished and extensive Mahavamsa, generally dated to around the fifth century CE, both composed in Pali within Sri Lankan monastic circles associated with the Mahavihara examined in Section 24.5. These texts narrate Sri Lankan history from legendary early settlement through the introduction of Buddhism and the reigns of numerous subsequent kings, organizing that history explicitly around the fortunes of Buddhism on the island.

This textbook treats the vamsa tradition as genuinely important historical evidence while insisting, with the same discipline applied to Vedic, Buddhist, and Jain textual traditions in earlier chapters, that its evidentiary status must be carefully specified. The Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa were composed several centuries after the events their earliest sections describe, within a specific monastic community — the Mahavihara — whose historical writing served identifiable religious and political purposes: legitimating the Mahavihara's own institutional authority, at times in implicit competition with rival establishments such as Abhayagiri examined in Section 24.5; presenting Buddhism's establishment on the island as a providentially guided turning point; and organizing centuries of dynastic history around the theme of royal support for, or neglect of, the sangha. A chronicle composed under these circumstances is simultaneously evidence for the events it describes and evidence for how a later community remembered, arranged, and gave meaning to those events — and these two kinds of evidence, this textbook has argued consistently since Chapter 11, are not the same thing and must not be conflated.

This does not mean the chronicles are historically unreliable in any simple sense. Where their claims can be checked against independent archaeological or epigraphic evidence — as Section 24.4 demonstrated for the general fact, though not every specific detail, of Buddhism's third-century-BCE introduction, and as Section 24.5 demonstrated for the existence and rough sequence of the major Anuradhapura monasteries — the chronicles often prove broadly consistent with what other evidence supports, and historians of early Sri Lanka rely on them extensively for regnal chronology, institutional history, and much else that no other surviving source documents. What the chronicles cannot provide is independent, unmediated access to events several centuries before their composition, narrated in vivid and often miraculous detail by authors who had every reason to shape that narrative toward particular religious and institutional ends.

Students should also understand that the Mahavamsa's narrative has carried significant weight well beyond ancient historiography. Its account of Buddhism, kingship, and the island's early population — including the Vijaya origin narrative examined in Section 24.1 and the Elara-Dutthagamani episode examined in Section 24.7 — has been invoked at various points in modern Sri Lankan political discourse, a legacy this textbook will address directly in later volumes when it examines twentieth-century Sri Lankan nationalism and civil conflict. Reading the chronicles responsibly, as this section has attempted to demonstrate, requires holding both their genuine value and their real limitations in view at once, resisting the temptation either to dismiss them as pure invention or to treat them as an unmediated factual record.

Section 24.7

South Indian Connections

Chapter 23 examined Tamilakam's own political, literary, and commercial world in detail; this section addresses that same region from the vantage point of Sri Lankan history, treating the Palk Strait not as a boundary separating two unrelated worlds but as a narrow, navigable, and historically active corridor of connection. Sri Lanka's relationship with South India across the early historic period took many forms: merchant activity and trade, examined further in Section 24.8; the movement of people, including settlers, laborers, and religious specialists; dynastic and diplomatic contact; periodic South Indian political and military involvement in island affairs; and the exchange of religious, artistic, and literary influence in both directions.

The chronicle tradition itself preserves a particularly well-known episode illustrating this pattern: the account of Elara, remembered by the Mahavamsa as a ruler of South Indian origin who held power over part of the island for several decades during roughly the second century BCE, and his eventual defeat by the Sri Lankan prince Dutthagamani, whose subsequent reunification of the island under a single ruler became, in later Buddhist historical memory, a celebrated model of righteous Buddhist kingship. This textbook treats the Elara-Dutthagamani narrative as an especially instructive source-critical case study rather than as a straightforward historical account to be accepted or rejected wholesale. On one hand, the episode plausibly reflects a genuine historical pattern documented independently elsewhere in this chapter: periods of South Indian political involvement in island affairs, consistent with the broader cross-strait connection this section examines. On the other hand, the Mahavamsa's specific narrative — its framing of the conflict, its emphasis on Dutthagamani's Buddhist piety, and its treatment of Elara as a notably just and respected, if non-Buddhist, ruler even in defeat — reflects the concerns of a later monastic community engaged in constructing a model of ideal Buddhist kingship, examined further in Section 24.6, rather than a neutral contemporary record of second-century-BCE events.

This textbook resists reading this recorded history through the lens of later national categories. It would be a serious historical distortion to describe the Elara-Dutthagamani episode, or South Indian involvement in early Sri Lankan affairs more generally, as a "foreign invasion" of a settled Sinhala nation defending itself against a unified Tamil enemy, just as it would be a serious distortion to treat the island as simply an extension of Tamilakam. Both formulations import a modern national and ethnic framework — Sinhala versus Tamil as fixed, opposed political identities — that did not exist in anything like its later form during the period this chapter examines. Early historic South India and Sri Lanka instead present a picture of overlapping political competition, commercial partnership, and cultural exchange among rulers, merchants, and religious communities whose identities were considerably more fluid, regionally specific, and situationally defined than later nationalist historiography, on either side of the strait, has sometimes suggested. The Elara-Dutthagamani tradition's enduring popularity precisely because of its later symbolic uses is itself a lesson in how ancient historical memory can be reshaped by, and mobilized within, entirely different political contexts many centuries afterward.

At the same time, this chapter does not dissolve Sri Lanka's historical distinctiveness into an undifferentiated South Indian regional history. The island developed its own royal lineages, its own monastic institutions examined in Section 24.5, its own literary tradition examined in Section 24.6, and its own dry-zone agrarian civilization examined in Section 24.3, none of which simply replicated Tamilakam's political or cultural patterns. The accurate historical picture is one of genuine connection and genuine distinctiveness held together, a pattern this chapter's Continuity and Change feature below examines directly by comparing Sri Lanka with Tamilakam across several dimensions.

Section 24.8

Indian Ocean Trade

Sri Lanka's geographic position — astride the sea lanes linking the western Indian Ocean to the Bay of Bengal and, beyond it, to Southeast Asia — gave the island a significant role within the wider early historic maritime commercial world this textbook examined for Tamilakam's ports in Chapter 23 and will generalize further in Chapter 25. Archaeological evidence from Sri Lankan coastal and inland sites, including imported ceramics, glass and stone beads, and coins, documents the island's participation in exchange networks reaching South India, the Arabian Sea world, and, more distantly, the Roman Mediterranean and Southeast Asia.

One site illustrates this participation with unusual concreteness. Godavaya, a port on Sri Lanka's southern coast, has yielded both underwater archaeological evidence — including a nearby offshore shipwreck assemblage documenting early maritime cargo movement in the Godavaya coastal zone — and a land-based inscription, dated to roughly the second century CE, recording that toll or customs revenue collected at the port was granted to a nearby Buddhist vihara. This single piece of evidence draws together several of this chapter's central themes at once: maritime commerce, examined throughout this section; monastic institutions and their economic support, examined in Section 24.5; and the close, often formalized relationship between royal or provincial administration and Buddhist religious establishments, examined since Section 24.4. Rather than treating monastic patronage and maritime trade as separate subjects, the Godavaya evidence shows a single administrative mechanism by which the wealth generated at a working port was channeled directly into the support of a religious institution.

Sri Lanka's own exports likely included gemstones, for which the island became famous in later classical and medieval accounts, along with spices, pearls, and other goods, exchanged through coastal ports such as Godavaya and others whose full archaeological documentation remains less extensive than that available for some Tamil ports examined in Chapter 23. Sri Lanka's participation in this exchange should not be understood as a passive reception of external trade; the island's merchants, rulers, and monastic institutions engaged deliberately with these commercial networks, using the wealth generated to support the agrarian, political, and religious developments examined earlier in this chapter.

This section deliberately maintains a disciplined scope. Chapter 25 is assigned the fuller synthesis of Indian Ocean monsoon navigation, Roman Mediterranean commerce, merchant communities, Buddhist religious networks, and cultural transmission across the entire early historic South Asian and Indian Ocean system. This section's task is narrower: to establish what participation in that wider system meant specifically for Sri Lanka — a source of commercial wealth, directly documented at sites such as Godavaya; a channel for continued religious and cultural connection to the Buddhist world beyond the island, examined already in Sections 24.4 through 24.6; and a further dimension of the island's demonstrated historical pattern of engagement rather than isolation.

Section 24.9

Sri Lanka in the Early Historic World

This section draws together the threads traced separately across Sections 24.1 through 24.8 into a single synthesis. Early historic Sri Lanka developed through the convergence of several interlocking processes: an indigenous archaeological sequence of settlement, agriculture, and Iron Age society, examined in Section 24.1; the growth of Anuradhapura from a ninth-century-BCE Iron Age settlement into a major early historic city, examined in Section 24.2; a distinctive political culture in which royal legitimacy became closely tied to the sponsorship and management of dry-zone irrigation and the agrarian surplus it made possible, examined in Section 24.3; the introduction and subsequent institutional embedding of Buddhism through named establishments such as the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana, examined in Sections 24.4 and 24.5; a remarkable historiographical tradition through which the island's Buddhist monastic communities preserved, and shaped, its own historical memory, examined in Section 24.6; sustained political, commercial, and religious connection with South India across the narrow Palk Strait, including episodes such as the Elara-Dutthagamani tradition, examined in Section 24.7; and active participation in the wider commercial world of the Indian Ocean, documented concretely at sites such as Godavaya, examined in Section 24.8.

No single one of these developments explains Sri Lanka's early historic trajectory in isolation. Anuradhapura's growth depended on irrigation-supported agrarian surplus; that surplus, in turn, supported the increasingly elaborate monastic institutions examined in Section 24.5; those institutions produced the historiographical tradition examined in Section 24.6, which has shaped how both ancient and modern observers understand the island's history; and all of these developments unfolded within, rather than apart from, a wider field of South Indian and Indian Ocean connection. Sri Lanka's insularity — its status as an island, geographically distinct from the subcontinental mainland — created particular historical possibilities, including a degree of political and institutional distinctiveness not found in every mainland region this textbook has examined, but that insularity did not produce isolation. The island's early historic civilization was built through selective, active, and sustained engagement with wider South Asian and Indian Ocean worlds, not despite the sea that surrounded it, but very often because of it.

This synthesis positions Sri Lanka as a genuine case study in a broader argument this volume has developed since Chapter 19: that early historic South Asia consisted of multiple, regionally distinctive political and cultural worlds — the Gangetic heartland, the post-Mauryan northwest examined in Chapter 20, the Kushan-connected north examined in Chapter 21, the Deccan examined in Chapter 22, Tamilakam examined in Chapter 23, and now island Sri Lanka — each shaped by its own environment, institutions, and historical development, while remaining linked through overlapping networks of trade, religion, and political contact that Chapter 25 will now draw together into a single regional synthesis.

Section 24.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced early Sri Lankan history from its archaeological foundations through its emergence as a distinctive, richly documented, and internationally connected early historic civilization. A single chain of development runs through the preceding nine sections: settlement and archaeology gave rise to Anuradhapura's urbanization; Anuradhapura's urbanization depended on, and reinforced, a political culture organized around irrigation and agrarian surplus; that surplus supported the introduction and institutional embedding of Buddhism through specific monastic establishments; those institutions produced an extraordinary historiographical tradition; and the island's entire trajectory unfolded within a field of sustained connection to South India and the wider Indian Ocean world rather than in isolation from it.

Section 24.1 established the necessary starting point: an indigenous Iron Age archaeological sequence, dated to roughly 1000–600 BCE and connected to but distinct from South Indian megalithic traditions, that this textbook has carefully distinguished from the much later legendary origin narratives preserved in Pali chronicle literature. Section 24.2 traced Anuradhapura's long, cumulative development — from a Protohistoric Iron Age settlement around 900–500 BCE, through Early Historic transition beginning around 500 BCE, to a substantial city by the third century BCE — resisting any account that would reduce this development to a single royal founding act. Section 24.3 examined the historically significant, but not simplistically deterministic, relationship between kingship and dry-zone irrigation, emphasizing that royal sponsorship of major reservoirs built upon, rather than single-handedly created, generations of accumulated local water-management knowledge.

Sections 24.4 through 24.6 formed this chapter's methodological core. Section 24.4 modeled, for the introduction of Buddhism specifically, the same disciplined separation this textbook has applied since Chapter 11 between what independent contemporary evidence can establish and what belongs to later, retrospective religious narrative — showing precisely how Ashoka's Major Rock Edicts II and XIII attest Mauryan awareness of and outreach toward Tamraparni without independently confirming the specific Mahinda-Devanampiya Tissa narrative the chronicles later supply. Section 24.5 traced Buddhism's subsequent institutional embedding through the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana, and through both royal and non-royal patronage. Section 24.6 examined the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa directly, arguing that these chronicles are simultaneously valuable historical evidence and purposeful, retrospective monastic literature, and that responsible historical use of them requires holding both characteristics in view without collapsing one into the other.

Sections 24.7 and 24.8 positioned Sri Lanka within its wider regional and maritime context. Section 24.7 examined the island's relationship with South India as a pattern of sustained connection across the Palk Strait, using the Elara-Dutthagamani tradition as a concrete case study in why later Sinhala-Tamil national categories should not be projected onto the second century BCE, while explicitly rejecting both the reduction of Sri Lanka to a Tamilakam extension and the treatment of every instance of South Indian involvement as ethnic conflict. Section 24.8 examined Sri Lanka's participation in Indian Ocean commerce, using the Godavaya port site's shipwreck evidence and monastic revenue inscription as concrete testimony, while deliberately preserving the fuller regional synthesis for Chapter 25.

Section 24.9 drew these threads together into this chapter's central interpretive claim: Sri Lanka's early historic civilization was distinctive without being isolated, connected without losing its own institutional and historiographical character. Taken as a whole, this chapter's recurring methodological lesson is that no single kind of evidence — archaeology, contemporary inscription, or retrospective chronicle — can carry the full weight of historical reconstruction alone. Ashoka's edicts confirm awareness but not narrative detail; Brahmi cave inscriptions confirm an early organized sangha but not the specific missionary story; the Godavaya inscription confirms monastic revenue from trade but says nothing of Buddhism's introduction two centuries earlier; and the Mahavamsa supplies a rich, coherent narrative whose specific claims require constant testing against these other, more contemporary forms of evidence. Chapter 25 now turns to the broader synthesis this chapter has repeatedly deferred, examining the full system of overland and maritime trade, Roman Mediterranean commerce, merchant communities, Buddhist networks, Brahmanical expansion, and cultural transmission that linked Sri Lanka, Tamilakam, the Deccan, the Kushan north, and the wider South Asian world into a single connected region by the close of the early historic period.

Primary Source Window

The Mahavamsa on Mahinda's Arrival

Text: Mahavamsa, chapters describing the mission of Mahinda and the conversion of Devanampiya Tissa · Tradition: Pali chronicle literature composed within the Mahavihara monastic community at Anuradhapura · Approximate date of composition: c. 5th century CE, several centuries after the events described · Type: Retrospective monastic historical narrative

Context: As Section 24.6 explained, the Mahavamsa was composed within a specific monastery with a clear interest in presenting Buddhism's establishment on the island as a decisive, providentially significant event. It should not be read as a contemporary transcript.

Paraphrase: The chronicle narrates that Mahinda, having traveled to Sri Lanka, encountered King Devanampiya Tissa while the king was hunting, engaged him in instructive dialogue demonstrating the king's readiness to receive the Buddhist teaching, and subsequently taught the king and his court, leading to the king's conversion and his active sponsorship of Buddhism's establishment on the island, including land grants and the beginning of major monastic construction that later tradition associates with the founding of the Mahavihara.

Analysis: This narrative brings together several of this chapter's central concerns: the close association between kingship and religious patronage examined in Section 24.5, and the methodological challenge of distinguishing a retrospective, literarily shaped account from independently verifiable historical fact, examined in Sections 24.4 and 24.6. The general claim of meaningful third-century-BCE contact between the Mauryan dhamma world and Sri Lanka, together with the establishment of an organized Buddhist monastic presence on the island, is reasonably well supported by independent evidence, including Ashoka's own inscriptional references to Tamraparni; the specific narrative details of this encounter, including the particular royal-court mechanism it describes, are not independently verifiable and reflect the concerns of the later monastic community that composed and preserved this account.

Questions for students:

  1. What elements of this narrative might be considered plausible in general outline, and which specific details cannot be independently verified?
  2. Why might a monastic community have particular reasons to narrate its founding in this dramatic, providentially framed way?
  3. How does this source illustrate the broader methodological caution this textbook has applied to retrospective religious literature since Chapter 11?
  4. How does this narrative differ in evidentiary weight from Ashoka's own inscriptional reference to Tamraparni discussed in Section 24.4?
Epigraphy Study

Early Brahmi Cave Inscriptions

Evidence type: Short donative inscriptions carved above cave shelters used by Buddhist monks · Script: Brahmi, closely related to the script used in Ashokan inscriptions examined in Chapter 16 · Date: generally attributed to the 3rd century BCE onward, considerably predating the composition of the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa

Context: Hundreds of cave shelters across Sri Lanka bear short inscriptions recording that a named individual donated the cave to the Buddhist monastic community. These inscriptions are contemporary with the events they record, in contrast to the much later chronicle literature examined in Section 24.6.

Description of the evidence: A typical inscription records a donor's name, sometimes an official title or occupation, and a brief formula dedicating the cave to the sangha. Donors named across the corpus of these inscriptions include officials, householders, and others, not exclusively royalty.

Analysis: These inscriptions provide historians with secure, contemporary evidence for an organized, actively supported Buddhist monastic presence in Sri Lanka from an early date, independent of the later chronicle narrative. Their broad, non-exclusively royal donor base also supports this chapter's argument in Section 24.5 that Buddhist patronage drew on a wider base of Sri Lankan society than royal sponsorship alone.

Questions for students:

  1. Why do historians treat these inscriptions as stronger evidence for an early date than the chronicle narrative alone?
  2. What does the range of donors recorded in these inscriptions suggest about the social base of early Buddhist patronage?
  3. How do brief inscriptions like these complement, rather than replace, the fuller narrative preserved in the Mahavamsa?
Historical Debate

Reconstructing the Introduction of Buddhism to Sri Lanka

The question: How much of the Mahavamsa's detailed account of Buddhism's introduction to Sri Lanka should historians treat as historically reliable, given that the chronicle was composed centuries after the events it describes?

Interpretation A — The chronicle's core narrative is broadly reliable: On this view, the convergence between Ashoka's own inscriptions naming Tamraparni as a destination of his dhamma-oriented outreach, the early Brahmi donative inscriptions, and the chronicle's broad narrative outline provides reasonable confidence that the chronicle preserves an authentic historical memory, transmitted and eventually written down by a community with strong institutional reasons to preserve accurate information about its own founding.
Interpretation B — The narrative's specific details reflect later monastic composition: On this view, while the general fact of a significant introduction of Buddhism during roughly this period is well supported, Ashoka's edicts fall well short of confirming a specific mission led by a named individual to a named king, and the chronicle's specific dialogue, dramatic incidents, and providential framing were shaped, and in some cases likely invented, by later compilers writing for religious and institutional purposes.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of independent evidence — Ashoka's Major Rock Edicts II and XIII, Sri Lankan Brahmi cave inscriptions, and archaeological evidence for early monastic institutions such as the Mahavihara — but weigh the chronicle's specific narrative content differently.

Why it matters: This textbook adopts a position between these two poles, consistent with its treatment of the Buddha's own biography in Chapter 11: the general historical claim is well supported, while specific narrative details remain the product of later composition and should be read as such.

Student task: Using the evidence described in Sections 24.4 through 24.6 and the Epigraphy Study above, explain which specific claims about Buddhism's introduction to Sri Lanka can be considered well supported, and which cannot.

Historical Vocabulary
Vihara, Stupa, and Dagoba

A vihara is a Buddhist monastic residence or complex, housing resident monks and often associated with teaching, study, and administration of monastic property. A stupa is a domed or mound-shaped monument, typically enshrining relics associated with the Buddha or other revered figures, and serving as a focus of devotional practice. In Sri Lanka, such monuments are commonly called dagobas, a term derived from the Pali/Sanskrit dhatugarbha, "relic chamber." The Jetavana dagoba, examined in Section 24.5, reached monumental scale, among the largest brick structures anywhere in the ancient world.

Historical Vocabulary
Vamsa Literature

Vamsa, literally "lineage" or "genealogy," names a genre of Pali chronicle literature, produced within Sri Lankan Buddhist monastic communities, that narrates the island's political and religious history organized around royal succession and the fortunes of Buddhism. The Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa, examined in Section 24.6, are the genre's most significant early examples, both associated with the Mahavihara examined in Section 24.5. Students should treat "vamsa literature" as a genre with its own conventions and purposes, comparable in some respects to the Puranic literature this textbook will examine in later volumes, rather than as a neutral factual record.

Infrastructure Study

The Irrigation Tank

A Sri Lankan irrigation tank is an artificial reservoir formed by constructing an earthen embankment, or bund, across a natural drainage line to capture and store monsoon runoff. Sluices allowed controlled release of water into channels feeding surrounding paddy fields, and in a cascade system, water draining from one tank's fields could be captured by a tank farther downstream, allowing repeated reuse of the same water across a river basin.

Royal inscriptions and chronicle tradition alike credit specific kings with constructing or enlarging major reservoirs, and such projects certainly required royal-level resource mobilization. But archaeological survey across the dry zone documents thousands of smaller tanks, many likely built and maintained by local communities without direct royal involvement, accumulated gradually across many generations. The historically accurate picture, as Section 24.3 argued, combines both: royal sponsorship of major works layered atop, and reinforcing, a much broader base of local water-management practice.

Multiple Perspectives

Life around Anuradhapura: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical illustrations grounded in archaeological and inscriptional evidence, not quotations from named historical individuals.

A king sponsoring a major reservoir and a monastery: Royal inscriptions and chronicle tradition suggest a ruler concerned with demonstrating authority and piety simultaneously, mobilizing labor and resources for irrigation projects and monastic land grants that supported both agrarian revenue and religious merit.
A monk resident at the Mahavihara or Abhayagiri: Evidence for monastic landholding and textual production suggests a life organized around study, teaching, ritual observance, and, for some, participation in the scholarly work that eventually produced the vamsa chronicles — work that could also involve institutional rivalry between establishments.
A cultivator working paddy fields fed by a tank system: Dependent on the reliable functioning of irrigation infrastructure, a cultivating household's livelihood was directly tied to the political and religious institutions that sponsored and maintained that infrastructure.
A merchant collecting or paying toll revenue at a port such as Godavaya: Evidence for monastic revenue drawn from port tolls suggests a commercial world directly linked, through formal administrative arrangement, to nearby religious institutions.
A traveler or settler from Tamil-speaking South India: Regular movement across the Palk Strait for trade, religious pilgrimage, or service at the Sri Lankan court would have made the island a familiar, closely connected destination rather than a distant or unfamiliar land.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Tamilakam and Sri Lanka Compared

What They Shared

  • Close geographic proximity across the narrow, navigable Palk Strait, examined in Section 24.7
  • Participation in overlapping Indian Ocean commercial networks, examined in Section 24.8 and Chapter 23
  • Sustained political, commercial, and religious contact and exchange throughout the early historic period, including episodes such as Elara's rule examined in Section 24.7

What Differed

  • Tamilakam's history survives chiefly through the Sangam poetic corpus, examined in Chapter 23, while Sri Lanka's survives chiefly through the vamsa chronicle tradition associated with the Mahavihara, examined in Section 24.6 — genuinely different genres of literature with different purposes
  • Sri Lanka developed a dry-zone irrigation civilization centered on Anuradhapura, while Tamilakam's political geography centered on competing Chera, Chola, and Pandya chiefdoms without a single comparable capital
  • Buddhism became closely and durably identified with Sri Lankan kingship through institutions such as the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana, while Tamilakam's early historic religious life, examined in Chapter 23, remained considerably more religiously plural

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both shared connection and genuine distinctiveness prevents students from treating Sri Lanka as either an isolated island story or a simple extension of the Tamil mainland.

Cause and Effect

Water, Surplus, and Royal Power

Long-term causes

The dry zone's seasonal rainfall pattern made intensive rice cultivation dependent on stored and carefully managed water across the dry season.

Short-term causes

Individual rulers invested in constructing or enlarging specific reservoirs, mobilizing labor and resources through royal administrative capacity.

Effect

Expanded irrigation produced greater agricultural surplus, supporting population concentration at Anuradhapura, taxation, and sustained patronage of Buddhist monastic institutions such as the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana.

This textbook's response

This chapter resists reducing dry-zone civilization to royal achievement alone, insisting that local, cumulative water-management knowledge underlay even the most celebrated royal projects, examined in Section 24.3.

Historical complexity: Not every reservoir or channel in the dry zone can be attributed to a named king; much of the infrastructure represents accumulated, largely anonymous local investment across many generations.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village, Monastery, and Field

For most inhabitants of the dry zone, daily life centered on the agricultural cycle governed by irrigation and monsoon timing: preparing and planting paddy fields, managing water allocation from tank and channel systems, and participating in the broader economy that sustained both royal administration and monastic institutions. Villages near major monastic centers such as the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana were often directly tied to those institutions through land grants, labor obligations, and religious observance, a pattern that connected ordinary agricultural life closely to the religious and political developments examined throughout this chapter.

Evidence for the social organization of these communities remains more limited than for the political and religious history this chapter has emphasized, but the broad base of donors recorded in the Brahmi cave inscriptions, examined in the Epigraphy Study above, and the direct dedication of port toll revenue to monastic support documented at Godavaya, examined in Section 24.8, together suggest a society in which participation in religious patronage extended well beyond the royal court, involving officials, merchants, cultivators, and other householders whose specific lives are otherwise largely undocumented.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has integrated early Sri Lankan history into this textbook's wider South Asian narrative while treating the island's own institutions, evidence, and historiography with the depth and independence they deserve. It began with the island's archaeological record — Iron Age settlement, agriculture, and megalithic burial traditions dated to roughly 1000–600 BCE — carefully distinguished from the much later legendary settlement narratives preserved in Pali chronicle literature.

The chapter traced Anuradhapura's long, cumulative growth from a Protohistoric Iron Age occupation around 900–500 BCE, through an Early Historic transitional phase beginning around 500 BCE, into a substantial urban and political center by the third century BCE. It examined the close, though not simplistically deterministic, relationship between Sri Lankan kingship and the sponsorship of dry-zone irrigation infrastructure, insisting that royal reservoirs built upon generations of accumulated local water-management practice rather than emerging from royal will alone.

The chapter then addressed, with particular source-critical care, the introduction of Buddhism to the island. It distinguished precisely between what Ashoka's Major Rock Edicts II and XIII actually claim — that his dhamma-oriented outreach and medical provisions reached Tamraparni — and what the much later Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa narrate about a specific mission led by Mahinda to a specific king, Devanampiya Tissa. This distinction, the chapter argued, is essential: Mauryan awareness and claimed outreach are contemporary and well attested; the chronicle's specific narrative is not.

The chapter examined Buddhism's subsequent institutional embedding through three named monastic complexes at Anuradhapura — the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana — distinguishing their traditional foundation narratives from the longer, more gradual pattern of construction and expansion that archaeology documents. It showed that patronage drew on both royal and non-royal donors, as the early Brahmi cave inscriptions demonstrate.

The chapter devoted sustained attention to the vamsa chronicle tradition itself, dating the Dipavamsa to roughly the fourth century CE and the Mahavamsa to roughly the fifth, and associating both with the Mahavihara specifically. It argued that this literature is simultaneously valuable historical evidence and purposeful, retrospective monastic composition, produced by a community with identifiable institutional and religious interests, requiring careful, undogmatic interpretation rather than either wholesale acceptance or wholesale dismissal.

The chapter then positioned Sri Lanka within its wider regional context. It examined sustained connection with South India across the Palk Strait, using the Elara-Dutthagamani tradition as a concrete case study in why later Sinhala-Tamil national categories should not be projected backward onto the second century BCE, while showing how that same episode became, in later Buddhist memory, a celebrated model of righteous kingship whose later political uses the chapter explicitly flagged for treatment in later volumes.

The chapter examined Sri Lanka's participation in Indian Ocean commercial networks, anchoring that discussion in the concrete archaeological and epigraphic evidence from Godavaya — a shipwreck assemblage and a second-century-CE inscription dedicating port revenue to a monastery — while reserving the fuller regional synthesis of Indian Ocean trade for Chapter 25.

Finally, the chapter argued that Sri Lanka's early historic civilization was distinctive without being isolated — an island society built through the convergence of indigenous institutional development and sustained external connection, a pattern consistent with this volume's broader treatment of early historic South Asia's regional diversity since Chapter 19.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled a single recurring methodological discipline: archaeology, contemporary epigraphy, and retrospective chronicle literature each answer different historical questions, and none can substitute for the others. Ashoka's inscriptions establish awareness, not narrative; Brahmi donative inscriptions establish an organized sangha, not a missionary's itinerary; the Godavaya inscription establishes monastic revenue from trade, not the circumstances of Buddhism's original arrival two centuries earlier; and the Mahavamsa supplies a coherent, richly detailed narrative whose specific claims require constant testing against these other, more contemporary sources.

An artificial water-storage structure formed by an embankment across a drainage line, the basic unit of tank irrigation.
Cascade system
A network of linked reservoirs along a drainage basin allowing water released from an upstream tank to be reused by tanks farther downstream.
Devanampiya Tissa
Sri Lankan ruler associated by chronicle tradition with the royal reception of Buddhism.
Mahinda
Buddhist missionary figure credited by chronicle tradition with introducing Buddhism to Sri Lanka.
Tamraparni
The ancient name for Sri Lanka used in Ashoka's Major Rock Edicts II and XIII.
Palk Strait
The narrow, shallow channel separating Sri Lanka from Tamil-speaking South India, a corridor of connection rather than a barrier.
Megalithic tradition
Iron Age burial practice involving stone-lined graves, attested in Sri Lanka with connections to South Indian megalithic cultures.
Mahavihara
The earliest of Anuradhapura's major monastic foundations according to chronicle tradition and the institutional home of the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa; it coexisted with powerful competing fraternities, especially Abhayagiri and Jetavana.
Abhayagiri
A major Anuradhapura monastic complex that developed as an important, at times rival, institution to the Mahavihara.
Jetavana
A major Anuradhapura monastic complex housing one of the largest brick stupas of the ancient world.
Elara
A ruler of South Indian origin remembered by the Mahavamsa as having held power over part of Sri Lanka in the second century BCE.
Dutthagamani
Sri Lankan prince credited by chronicle tradition with defeating Elara and reunifying the island, later remembered as a model of Buddhist kingship.
Godavaya
An early historic port on Sri Lanka's southern coast, documented by shipwreck archaeology and a second-century-CE inscription recording monastic revenue from port tolls.
Retrospective composition
The historical circumstance in which a text is composed well after the events it describes, requiring careful source-critical interpretation.
Monastic land grant
Land or villages donated to support a Buddhist monastic institution, a key mechanism of royal and non-royal patronage.
Indian Ocean exchange
The maritime commercial system connecting Sri Lanka to South India, the Arabian Sea, and the wider Indian Ocean world, examined further in Chapter 25.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold together two things that can feel, at first, like they pull in opposite directions: genuine respect for one of South Asia's richest historical literatures, and genuine discipline about what that literature can and cannot establish. The Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa are not simply myths to be discarded, nor are they transparent windows onto the third century BCE. They are both — valuable evidence and purposeful memory — and learning to read them as both, alongside contemporary epigraphy such as Ashoka's edicts, Sri Lanka's own Brahmi donative inscriptions, and site-specific evidence such as the Godavaya shipwreck and inscription, is this chapter's central lesson.

Sri Lanka's early historic civilization, built around Anuradhapura's irrigation-fed agrarian economy and its Buddhist monastic institutions — the Mahavihara, Abhayagiri, and Jetavana — developed through sustained connection to South India and the wider Indian Ocean world, not in spite of the sea that surrounds the island but very often because of it. The Elara-Dutthagamani tradition, examined carefully in this chapter, shows both the value and the danger of ancient historical memory: valuable because it likely reflects a genuine pattern of cross-strait political involvement, and dangerous when later communities read modern ethnic categories back into a period that did not recognize them.

Students who understand Sri Lanka as neither an isolated island nor a simple extension of the Tamil mainland, and who can distinguish precisely what different kinds of evidence — archaeological, epigraphic, and literary — actually establish, are well prepared for Chapter 25, which now draws Sri Lanka, Tamilakam, the Deccan, and the wider early historic world into a single connected regional history.
