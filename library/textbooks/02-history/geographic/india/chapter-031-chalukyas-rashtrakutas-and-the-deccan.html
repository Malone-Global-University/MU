Part VII — Regional Kingdoms and the Early Medieval Order
31

Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, and the Deccan

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

At Pattadakal, on the banks of the Malaprabha River in present-day Karnataka, stand temples built side by side in two distinct architectural languages — one associated with traditions of the north, one with traditions of the south — commissioned within a few decades of one another by royal patrons of a single dynasty, including, in the case of the Virupaksha temple, a queen, Lokamahadevi, consort of Vikramaditya II. The coexistence of these two architectural languages at a single royal site is itself historical evidence. It indicates a dynasty willing to draw deliberately on both northern and southern building traditions rather than committing exclusively to one, a pattern that developed over centuries of Chalukya engagement — through warfare, diplomacy, marriage alliance, and direct architectural borrowing — with courts to the north and south of the Deccan plateau they ruled.

Chapter 30 left the narrative of post-Harsha South Asia at a moment of apparent fragmentation: the substantial Kannauj-centered political configuration Harsha had assembled did not survive intact after his death, and no immediate successor preserved it, so that power across the subcontinent reorganized into a landscape of competing regional kingdoms rather than a single paramount authority. That chapter introduced the Early Chalukyas of Badami only briefly, chiefly through the figure of Pulakeshin II and his celebrated check on Harsha's ambitions at the Narmada River, and it handed the fuller story of Deccan political development forward to the present chapter. This chapter now takes up that story in its own right, tracing more than four centuries of Deccan history from the rise of the Early Chalukyas in the sixth century through the eventual revival of Chalukya power in the Deccan in the tenth and eleventh centuries, by way of the extraordinary Rashtrakuta ascendancy of the eighth through tenth centuries.

The chapter's governing argument is that the Deccan plateau was never merely a transitional zone separating a supposedly more historically significant Gangetic north from a supposedly more historically significant Tamil south. It was an independent and repeatedly decisive center of state formation. Dynasties based in the Deccan — first the Chalukyas of Badami, then the Rashtrakutas of Manyakheta, then a restored Chalukya line at Kalyani — built durable administrative systems, patronized architecture and literature on an ambitious scale, extracted agrarian and commercial resources across a wide territorial base, and, crucially, repeatedly projected military and political power both northward into the Gangetic world and southward into Tamil country. Understanding how this happened requires moving beyond a simple list of kings and battles toward an explanation of how regional power, once established, could be converted into something approaching imperial reach without erasing the deeper multipolar structure of early medieval South Asia that Chapter 30 described.

This chapter also asks students to hold two ideas in tension that might otherwise seem contradictory. On one hand, the political history that follows is full of conquest, rivalry, and the violent overthrow of one ruling house by another: Pulakeshin II's wars against Harsha and the Pallavas, the bitter and recurring Chalukya-Pallava frontier conflict, the Rashtrakuta seizure of power from the very Chalukya line that had dominated the Deccan for two centuries, and the eventual Chalukya recovery that displaced the Rashtrakutas in turn. On the other hand, this same period produced some of early medieval South Asia's most significant achievements in temple architecture, religious patronage, administrative organization, and literary culture — achievements that depended on stability, resources, and cross-regional exchange rather than on warfare alone. The Deccan's early medieval history cannot be reduced to either story by itself. It must be understood as both at once: competitive state formation and cultural flowering, each feeding the other.

A further theme running through this chapter concerns the nature of the evidence historians use to reconstruct Deccan political history. Unlike the richly documented Mauryan edicts examined earlier in this textbook, Chalukya and Rashtrakuta history depends heavily on royal copper-plate land grants and stone inscriptions, most of which were commissioned by kings or their officials to record specific acts of religious donation, and which therefore combine genuine administrative and genealogical information with formal panegyric — praise poetry written to exalt a ruling king and his ancestors. Historians must read these inscriptions carefully, extracting reliable chronological and administrative detail while remaining alert to exaggeration, omission, and the political purposes such documents served. This chapter will model that method repeatedly, beginning with one of the most important inscriptions for reconstructing Pulakeshin II's reign, examined in the Primary Evidence Window below.

Finally, this chapter situates Deccan history within the wider geography introduced in Chapter 1 and developed throughout this textbook. The Deccan Plateau sits between the Narmada and Tapti river systems to its north and the Tungabhadra and Krishna river systems to its south, bounded by the Western Ghats along the Arabian Sea coast and shading gradually into the eastern coastal plains toward the Bay of Bengal. This position gave Deccan rulers access, by both land and river corridor, to the agriculturally rich Gangetic world, to the wealthy Tamil kingdoms of the far south, and to the Arabian Sea's maritime commerce. Geography alone did not make the Chalukyas or the Rashtrakutas powerful, but it gave ambitious Deccan rulers unusually good reasons, and unusually good routes, to try.

By the chapter's end, students should understand not simply who ruled the Deccan between the sixth and eleventh centuries, but how a regional kingdom could become powerful enough to humble a north Indian emperor, how a usurping dynasty could inherit and expand an existing political and administrative system, how temple-building functioned as a form of royal communication as consequential as any military campaign, and how the alternating fortunes of Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas reveal a durable regional political tradition that persisted across dynastic change rather than rising and falling with any single ruling house.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the territorial, political, and institutional foundations of Early Chalukya power in the sixth and seventh centuries.
  2. Analyze the reign of Pulakeshin II as a case study in Deccan imperial ambition, including his conflicts with Harsha and the Pallavas.
  3. Trace the long Chalukya-Pallava rivalry and explain why control of the southern frontier mattered to both dynasties.
  4. Interpret the temples of Pattadakal as evidence of the relationship among kingship, religious patronage, and cross-regional cultural exchange.
  5. Describe the rise of the Rashtrakutas and explain how a new ruling house displaced and absorbed an established Chalukya political order.
  6. Assess the scale and character of Rashtrakuta imperial ambition, including their interventions in both northern and southern politics.
  7. Explain how Deccan states were administered below the level of the king, including subordinate rulers, officials, and land grants.
  8. Connect agrarian expansion and commercial exchange in the Deccan to the political power of its ruling dynasties.
  9. Trace the later Chalukya recovery of Deccan supremacy and explain what this reveals about continuity beneath dynastic change.
  10. Evaluate the reliability and limitations of royal inscriptions and copper-plate grants as historical evidence.
  11. Distinguish between political rivalry and cultural isolation, explaining how warring Deccan and Tamil states nonetheless shared and exchanged architectural and religious traditions.
  12. Articulate why the Deccan should be understood as an independent center of early medieval state formation rather than a transitional zone between north and south.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How should historians weigh royal inscriptions, written to praise a ruling dynasty, as evidence for political and military events?
  3. Why did control of the Deccan's northern and southern frontiers matter so much to Chalukya and Rashtrakuta rulers?
  4. In what sense was the confrontation between Pulakeshin II and Harsha a turning point, and in what sense was it only one episode in a longer Chalukya political program?

  7. How did Deccan rulers govern a large and ethnically and linguistically diverse territory without the administrative apparatus of a modern state?
  8. What was the relationship between agrarian and commercial expansion and the military and political power of Deccan dynasties?
  9. Why does the alternation of Chalukya and Rashtrakuta supremacy suggest the persistence of a regional political tradition rather than a simple story of rise and fall?

Historical Context

Chapter 30 traced the fragmentation of the Gupta imperial order and the rise of regional kingdoms across northern India, culminating in the Kannauj-centered political configuration Harsha assembled in the seventh century and its dissolution after his death. That chapter also introduced, as a deliberate counterpoint to the northern narrative, the Early Chalukyas of Badami, who by the early seventh century had built one of the Deccan's most formidable regional states under Pulakeshin II. Chapter 30 used Pulakeshin II chiefly to demonstrate a single, important point: Harsha's authority, however extensive in the Gangetic plain, did not extend across all of South Asia, and his attempt to expand southward across the Narmada River was decisively checked. This chapter now turns that relationship around, treating Pulakeshin II and the Chalukya state not as a foil for northern Indian history but as the center of its own historical narrative.

The Deccan Plateau, the geographic stage for this chapter, occupies the broad triangular landmass of peninsular India south of the Narmada and Vindhya ranges and north of the Tamil country drained by the Kaveri. It is a region of black volcanic soils well suited to certain crops, of major rivers — the Godavari, Krishna, and Tungabhadra among them — flowing generally eastward toward the Bay of Bengal, and of long-established overland routes connecting the western coast near present-day Goa and the Konkan to the Gangetic world via corridors through Malwa and Gujarat, and to the Tamil south via corridors along both the eastern and western sides of the plateau. Earlier chapters of this textbook have already traced important Deccan history: the Satavahanas of Chapter 22 built one of the peninsula's earliest major states in this same general region centuries before the Chalukyas, and Chapter 30 has already shown the Vakatakas and other post-Gupta Deccan powers operating as regional players in their own right. This chapter's dynasties inherit a region with a long prior history of state formation, agrarian development, and commercial connection — not an undeveloped frontier awaiting its first kingdom.

By the sixth century, the Deccan's political landscape included a number of competing regional powers, among them the Kadambas of Banavasi in the western Deccan and various Pallava-affiliated or Pallava-adjacent polities along the eastern and southern margins of the plateau. Into this competitive environment, a family identifying itself in inscriptions as the Chalukyas established a base at Vatapi, or Badami, in present-day northern Karnataka, and began a process of regional consolidation that would, within a few generations, produce one of early medieval South Asia's most consequential ruling houses. Understanding how this consolidation occurred, and how it eventually gave way first to the Rashtrakutas and then to a restored Chalukya line, is the central task of this chapter.

Historians working on this period depend overwhelmingly on epigraphic evidence: stone inscriptions recording royal grants, boundary settlements, and commemorations, and copper-plate charters recording land donations to Brahmins, temples, or religious institutions. These documents, discussed further below and in the Primary Evidence Window, provide genuine and often precisely dated administrative and genealogical information, but they were composed to serve the interests of the rulers who commissioned them. A victory recorded in a copper-plate charter may be accurately dated even when its description of the victory itself is rhetorically inflated. This chapter asks students to read such evidence with the same combination of confidence and caution that earlier chapters have applied to Ashokan edicts, Gupta pillar inscriptions, and Sangam poetry.

Section 31.1

Early Chalukyas

According to Chalukya inscriptions composed generations after the fact, a ruler named Pulakeshin I established an independent base at Vatapi, a naturally fortified site set among red sandstone cliffs in present-day northern Karnataka, sometime in the mid-sixth century CE. Inscriptions describe him performing the hiranya-garbha ritual, a Brahmanical rite of royal consecration associated with claims to elevated or renewed social and political status, a detail that suggests the Chalukyas, whatever their precise origins, sought from an early point to present themselves as legitimate Kshatriya rulers fully integrated into Brahmanical political culture rather than as regional upstarts. Students should treat this self-presentation as a political claim recorded by the dynasty itself, not as a neutral report on the family's actual social origins, which remain genuinely uncertain and debated among historians.

The Chalukyas emerged into a Deccan political landscape already populated by established regional powers, including the Kadambas of Banavasi to the southwest and various smaller chiefdoms across the western plateau. Early Chalukya expansion proceeded through the familiar early medieval pattern traced already in Chapter 30: successful military campaigns against neighboring powers, followed by the absorption of defeated rulers as subordinate chiefs, or samantas, who retained local authority while acknowledging Chalukya overlordship and providing military support when called upon. This layered structure of a paramount king surrounded by subordinate, locally rooted rulers was not unique to the Chalukyas; it was the characteristic form of state organization across much of early medieval South Asia, and it will recur throughout this chapter and the chapters that follow.

Pulakeshin I's successors extended Chalukya authority across an increasingly wide swath of the western and central Deccan over the following decades, bringing them into direct contact and eventual conflict with powers to the north, including Malava and Gujarat chiefdoms, and powers to the south and east, including Pallava-affiliated territories. By the opening years of the seventh century, the Chalukya kingdom centered on Vatapi had become one of the Deccan's most formidable regional states, possessing a secure capital, an expanding network of subordinate rulers, and growing ambitions that would be realized dramatically under the dynasty's most celebrated ruler, Pulakeshin II, examined in the next section.

It is important for students to recognize the chronological significance of this consolidation. Chalukya state-building at Vatapi was occurring during precisely the same decades that northern India was reorganizing itself after the Gupta collapse, and the Chalukya kingdom had already achieved substantial regional power well before Harsha assembled his own Kannauj-centered political order in the Gangetic plain. The Chalukyas were not a late-arriving regional power reacting to northern developments; they were contemporaries of that northern reorganization, building, independently and on their own timeline, one of the Deccan's most significant early medieval states.

Section 31.2

Pulakeshin II

Pulakeshin II, who came to power around 610 CE after a period of succession dispute within the Chalukya royal family, is the dynasty's most historically significant ruler and the figure through whom Chapter 30 first introduced Deccan history to students. That chapter used Pulakeshin II chiefly to illustrate the limits of Harsha's northern authority. This section now examines his reign as a historical subject in its own right, revealing a ruler whose ambitions and achievements extended across virtually the entire breadth of the Deccan and well beyond its traditional boundaries.

Pulakeshin II's early campaigns, recorded chiefly in the Aihole inscription examined in the Primary Evidence Window below, describe a sequence of military successes against neighboring powers: the Kadambas of Banavasi, the Gangas of southern Karnataka, the Alupas of the coastal region near present-day Mangalore, the Mauryas of the Konkan coast (a regional dynasty unrelated to the earlier imperial Mauryas examined in Chapters 15 through 18), and the Latas, Malavas, and Gurjaras of the region north of the Narmada. This sequence of campaigns, if the inscription's claims are read as reflecting a genuine, if rhetorically inflated, pattern of military activity, suggests a ruler engaged in sustained, multi-directional expansion rather than a single decisive war.

The confrontation with Harsha, dated by most historians to approximately 618 to 620 CE, occurred within this broader pattern of expansion rather than as an isolated event. Harsha, having consolidated substantial authority across the Gangetic plain, apparently sought to extend his reach southward across the Narmada River, a natural and long-recognized boundary between the Gangetic world and the Deccan. According to the Aihole inscription, Pulakeshin II's forces checked this advance, and Harsha did not succeed in extending his authority south of the Narmada. Chapter 30 treated this episode chiefly as evidence of Harsha's limits; this section treats it as evidence of Chalukya strength, achieved through the same pattern of military organization, subordinate alliance, and territorial control that Pulakeshin II had already demonstrated against Deccan rivals.

Pulakeshin II also extended Chalukya influence eastward, installing a branch of the family, later known as the Eastern Chalukyas, in the Vengi region of the eastern Deccan along the Godavari-Krishna delta, a development that would produce a long-lived subsidiary dynasty with its own significant later history. Toward the south, Pulakeshin II's ambitions brought the Chalukyas into direct and escalating conflict with the Pallava dynasty of Kanchipuram, a rivalry introduced briefly here and examined at greater length in the next section. This southern conflict ultimately proved costly: in or around 642 CE, the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I invaded Chalukya territory, sacked the capital at Vatapi, and, according to most historical reconstructions, brought about Pulakeshin II's death, either in battle or shortly thereafter.

Students should draw two lessons from Pulakeshin II's reign. First, the Narmada confrontation with Harsha, however celebrated in later historical memory and however significant as a marker of the Deccan's political independence from the north, was only one episode within a far larger program of Chalukya expansion conducted simultaneously on multiple frontiers. Second, that same expansionist program carried genuine risk: the aggressive southern campaigns that extended Chalukya reach toward Pallava territory ultimately provoked a devastating retaliation that nearly ended the dynasty altogether, a reversal of fortune examined further below.

Section 31.3

Chalukya-Pallava Rivalry

The conflict between the Chalukyas of Vatapi and the Pallavas of Kanchipuram, briefly introduced in the preceding section, was not a single war but a recurring, multi-generational contest that shaped Deccan and northern Tamil politics for well over a century. This section examines that rivalry from the Chalukya and Deccan perspective; the fuller history of the Pallava dynasty itself — its origins, its capital at Kanchipuram, its agrarian and religious transformation of the northern Tamil country, and its own architectural achievements at Mahabalipuram — belongs to Chapter 32 and is not duplicated here.

The strategic stakes of the rivalry centered on control of the fertile frontier zone in the northern Tamil country and southern Deccan borderlands, a region of agriculturally valuable river valleys and strategically important routes connecting the Deccan plateau to the Tamil south. Whichever dynasty controlled this frontier could project influence into the other's core territory, extract tribute from border chiefdoms, and claim the prestige associated with successful campaigns against a powerful rival. Neither side could simply ignore the other: Chalukya ambitions toward the south, visible already in Pulakeshin II's reign, inevitably collided with Pallava ambitions toward the north, and the resulting conflict proved remarkably durable across successive generations of rulers on both sides.

Pulakeshin II's southern campaigns against the Pallavas, which apparently achieved some success during the earlier part of his reign, provoked the devastating Pallava retaliation of 642 CE described above, in which Narasimhavarman I sacked Vatapi itself. This reversal demonstrates an important pattern in early medieval Deccan warfare: campaigns of conquest, even when initially successful, could expose a kingdom's own capital to retaliation once the campaigning ruler's attention and resources were absorbed elsewhere. The sack of Vatapi was a genuine, if temporary, eclipse of Chalukya power, and Chalukya inscriptions from the following decades are notably less boastful about southern affairs than the Aihole inscription of Pulakeshin II's own reign.

Chalukya power recovered under Pulakeshin II's successors, and the rivalry with the Pallavas continued intermittently across the following decades, with campaigns and counter-campaigns recorded in inscriptions from both sides, each naturally claiming victory. Later Chalukya rulers, including Vikramaditya I in the later seventh century and Vikramaditya II in the early eighth century, led further campaigns against Pallava territory, with Vikramaditya II's forces reportedly reaching and occupying Kanchipuram itself on more than one occasion, though without permanently annexing Pallava territory outright. This pattern — repeated invasion, temporary occupation of the rival capital, and eventual withdrawal rather than permanent conquest — reflects the characteristic limits of early medieval military power discussed in Chapter 30: even a decisively victorious campaign rarely produced permanent territorial annexation of a well-established, agriculturally wealthy, and politically organized rival kingdom.

Historians should be cautious about treating either side's inscriptional claims of victory as straightforwardly reliable. Both Chalukya and Pallava inscriptions describe triumphant campaigns against the other dynasty across the same decades, and it is not always possible to reconcile these competing claims into a single, agreed sequence of events. What can be said with confidence is that the rivalry itself was sustained, consequential, and mutually shaping: both dynasties organized significant resources around this frontier conflict for more than a century, and both dynasties' political cultures, including their religious patronage and architectural ambitions examined in the next section, were shaped in part by this ongoing competition with a formidable southern or northern neighbor.

Section 31.4

Pattadakal and Temple Culture

Warfare was not the only, or even the primary, instrument through which Chalukya kings asserted authority and legitimacy. Religious patronage, and above all the construction of monumental stone temples, served as an equally important form of royal communication, one that advertised a king's piety, wealth, and connections to both local and translocal religious and cultural traditions. Nowhere is this clearer than at Pattadakal, a ceremonial complex on the Malaprabha River near Vatapi, where successive Chalukya rulers sponsored an unusually concentrated cluster of major stone temples across the late seventh and early eighth centuries.

What makes Pattadakal especially significant for this chapter's argument is its deliberate architectural duality. Several temples at the site, including the Papanatha temple, were built in a style associated with architectural traditions of northern and central India, generally termed the nagara style and characterized by a curvilinear tower, or shikhara. Other temples at the same site, including the Virupaksha and Mallikarjuna temples, were built in a style associated with Tamil and southern architectural traditions, generally termed the dravida style and characterized by a stepped, pyramidal tower. According to its own inscriptions, the Virupaksha temple was commissioned around 740 CE by Queen Lokamahadevi, consort of Vikramaditya II, reportedly to commemorate her husband's southern victory over the Pallavas, and its dravida-style architecture and sculptural program show direct evidence of influence from, or deliberate emulation of, Pallava temple traditions in the Tamil south, including the Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram.

This architectural choice carries real historical significance. It demonstrates that military rivalry between the Chalukyas and the Pallavas did not translate into cultural isolation or mutual rejection. Quite the opposite: Chalukya patrons appear to have studied, admired, and deliberately incorporated architectural and artistic traditions associated with their most persistent military rival, likely bringing craftsmen or craft knowledge from the Tamil south northward in the aftermath of successful campaigns. Political competition and cultural borrowing operated simultaneously rather than as contradictory impulses. This pattern — regional kingdoms fighting fiercely over territory and tribute while sharing, adapting, and competitively emulating one another's religious architecture — recurs throughout early medieval South Asian history and should not be read as paradoxical. It reflects a shared elite religious and architectural culture that transcended the immediate boundaries of any single kingdom, even as those kingdoms competed intensely for territorial and political supremacy.

Pattadakal's significance extends beyond any single temple. Taken as a whole, the site documents a Chalukya pattern of drawing on both northern and southern building traditions side by side rather than committing architecturally to either alone. Some later scholars have associated this Deccan building tradition with the development of a distinctive hybrid style, sometimes termed vesara; UNESCO's own description of the Pattadakal complex, by contrast, characterizes its temples in terms of a northern rekha-nagara-prasada idiom, a southern dravida-vimana idiom, and local Kadamba-Chalukya forms existing alongside one another, rather than requiring a single fused "vesara" category. The precise classification and chronology of any such hybrid style remain subjects of ongoing art-historical discussion, and this textbook presents the vesara label with that caution attached. For this chapter's purposes, the important point is not the technical classification of styles but what the pattern reveals about Deccan political culture: the ability to absorb, adapt, and project cultural authority through religious architecture was as central to Chalukya kingship as military success.

Temple patronage also served practical political functions beyond religious merit and architectural prestige, including the establishment of endowments that supported temple priesthoods, ritual specialists, and associated economic activity, themes examined more fully in Section 31.7 and taken up at greater length in Chapter 37's dedicated treatment of temple-centered economies across early medieval South Asia. This chapter's treatment of Pattadakal is deliberately limited to its significance for Chalukya kingship, religious patronage, and cultural exchange, rather than a full economic analysis of temple endowment, which belongs to that later, more thematically comprehensive chapter.

Section 31.5

Rise of the Rashtrakutas

The Badami Chalukya dynasty, having recovered from the 642 CE sack of Vatapi and continued its rivalry with the Pallavas for another century, was itself overthrown not by an external rival but by a family that had previously served as one of its own subordinate chiefs. Around 753 CE, a Rashtrakuta chief named Dantidurga, whose family had held a subordinate, samanta-level position under Chalukya overlordship in the Deccan, defeated Kirtivarman II, the last significant Badami Chalukya ruler, and established Rashtrakuta supremacy over most of the Deccan. This transition was not completed in a single year, however. Inscriptional evidence, including a grant dated 757 CE, indicates that Kirtivarman II retained a measure of authority over a reduced domain in the southern part of the former Chalukya kingdom for several years after Dantidurga's initial victory, and it was Dantidurga's successor, Krishna I, who completed the elimination of remaining Chalukya sovereignty by around 757 CE. The Rashtrakuta takeover of the Deccan is therefore better understood as a process spanning roughly 753 to 757 than as a single decisive event.

Students should understand that this transition represents a different kind of political change than the external invasions and frontier wars examined in the preceding sections. Dantidurga did not conquer the Deccan from outside its existing political system; he seized control from within it, using a position of established, if subordinate, authority to displace his former overlord. This pattern — an internal subordinate rising to supplant the dynasty it had previously served — recurs across early medieval South Asian political history and illustrates one of the characteristic vulnerabilities of the layered samanta system described in Section 31.1: a system that relied on subordinate rulers for military support and regional administration also, by its nature, created subordinate rulers with the resources, local authority, and military experience eventually to challenge their superiors.

Dantidurga's successor, Krishna I, consolidated and expanded Rashtrakuta authority across the Deccan during the later eighth century, completing the final conquest of remaining Chalukya resistance and extending Rashtrakuta power into the eastern Deccan and toward the northern Karnataka region previously dominated by the Badami line. Krishna I is also closely associated with one of early medieval South Asia's most extraordinary architectural achievements: the excavation, beginning during his reign, of the monolithic Kailasa temple at Ellora, a vast rock-cut temple carved downward out of a single mass of basalt rock rather than built up from constructed masonry, representing one of the most ambitious religious construction projects undertaken anywhere in the premodern world. The Kailasa temple demonstrates that the new Rashtrakuta dynasty inherited, and was fully capable of extending, the tradition of monumental religious patronage already well established by the Chalukyas at sites such as Pattadakal.

The Rashtrakuta rise to power therefore illustrates an important continuity beneath apparent dynastic rupture. The new ruling house did not dismantle the political, administrative, and cultural order it inherited from the Chalukyas; it absorbed, adapted, and in important respects expanded that order, including its system of subordinate rulers, its patterns of agrarian and religious land grants, and its tradition of monumental temple patronage. The following section examines how, under Dantidurga's and Krishna I's successors, this inherited Deccan political and institutional base became the foundation for a still more ambitious program of territorial expansion and political influence extending well beyond the Deccan's traditional boundaries.

Section 31.6

Rashtrakuta Imperial Ambitions

Under rulers such as Dhruva, who reigned in the final decades of the eighth century, and his son Govinda III, who reigned into the early ninth century, Rashtrakuta power expanded well beyond consolidation of the Deccan itself into direct military intervention in the political affairs of both northern India and the Tamil south. This section establishes the scale of that ambition without narrating every campaign in full detail, since the most significant northern intervention — Rashtrakuta involvement in the so-called Tripartite Struggle for control of Kannauj, alongside the Gurjara-Pratiharas and the Palas — is examined comprehensively in Chapter 34, which is specifically devoted to the political contest for supremacy in northern India during this period.

What students should understand at this point in the narrative is the basic shape and significance of Rashtrakuta northern intervention: Dhruva and, more decisively, Govinda III led major military campaigns into the Gangetic heartland, on more than one occasion defeating or humbling both Gurjara-Pratihara and Pala forces and briefly asserting a degree of influence over the prestigious city of Kannauj, long recognized as a symbolic seat of paramount authority in northern India since the time of Harsha. These campaigns did not result in permanent Rashtrakuta territorial annexation of the Gangetic plain; like the Chalukya campaigns against Kanchipuram examined earlier, they were episodes of successful raiding and temporary political humiliation of rivals rather than durable conquest. Their significance lies elsewhere: they demonstrate that a Deccan-based dynasty could project military power across the full length of the subcontinent, intervening decisively in a northern Indian political contest among dynasties whose core territories lay hundreds of miles from the Rashtrakuta capital.

Toward the south, Rashtrakuta rulers continued and in some respects intensified the pattern of military engagement with Tamil and southern Deccan powers already established by the Chalukyas, including recurring conflict with the Western Gangas of southern Karnataka and with Pallava and, in later decades, early Chola forces in the Tamil country, themes that connect forward to Chapter 33's account of Chola imperial expansion. The Rashtrakutas, in other words, inherited and expanded the dual-frontier strategic position the Chalukyas had already occupied: a Deccan base capable of threatening both the Gangetic north and the Tamil south, and willing to do so as political opportunity allowed.

The reign of Amoghavarsha I, which extended across much of the ninth century and stands among the longest of any early medieval South Asian ruler, illustrates a different and equally important dimension of Rashtrakuta imperial ambition: the conversion of military success into durable institutional authority, achieved only after a genuinely difficult beginning. Amoghavarsha I came to the throne as a minor, and the early years of his reign were marked by serious rebellions among Rashtrakuta feudatories and, according to the surviving evidence, a temporary loss of the throne itself before his restoration to power by around 821 CE. It was from Amoghavarsha I's reign onward, following this period of instability, that Manyakheta was developed as the dynasty's principal capital, a status it retained for the remainder of Rashtrakuta rule. Once his authority was secured, his long reign became associated with extensive religious patronage across multiple traditions, including Jain institutions that received significant royal favor during this period, substantial literary activity at the Rashtrakuta court, and a general consolidation of administrative structures examined in the next section. Later medieval Arab geographical writers, whose accounts of South Asia derive from secondhand report and merchant testimony rather than direct eyewitness experience of the Rashtrakuta court, nonetheless describe the Rashtrakuta kingdom among the major powers of contemporary Asia, a detail that, while requiring the same source-critical caution appropriate to any secondhand account, suggests that Rashtrakuta prestige and reputation extended well beyond South Asia's own borders during this period.

Students should draw from this section the central lesson that "regional kingdom" did not mean "small or weak kingdom" in the early medieval Deccan context. The Rashtrakutas, operating from their capital at Manyakheta far from either the Gangetic plain or the Tamil heartland, nonetheless achieved a degree of supraregional hegemony — the ability to intervene decisively, if not permanently, in the affairs of distant and powerful rival kingdoms — that places them among the most consequential polities of early medieval South Asia, fully comparable in ambition, if not always in permanent territorial reach, to the northern dynasties examined in earlier chapters. At the same time, Amoghavarsha I's early rebellions are a useful reminder that even a dynasty's most celebrated reign need not have been stable from its outset.

Section 31.7

Deccan Administration

Military success and religious patronage alone cannot explain how Chalukya and Rashtrakuta kings sustained authority across large and diverse territories for generations at a time. This section moves below the level of kings and campaigns to examine how these states actually governed, insofar as the evidence, drawn chiefly from copper-plate land grants and stone inscriptions, allows reconstruction.

Both dynasties governed through the layered system of subordinate authority already introduced in Section 31.1. At the apex stood the paramount king, who in inscriptions typically claimed grand titles asserting universal or near-universal sovereignty, such as maharajadhiraja ("great king of kings") or similar formulations, even though actual authority rarely extended uniformly across the full territory such titles implied. Below the paramount king stood a hierarchy of subordinate rulers, often termed samantas or, at a more elevated or extensive level of authority, mahasamantas, who governed their own territories with considerable local autonomy in exchange for acknowledging the paramount king's overlordship, providing military contingents when summoned, and, in many cases, making their own religious donations in a manner that echoed, while remaining visibly subordinate to, royal patronage patterns.

Below this tier of subordinate rulers, inscriptions reveal a further layer of administrative officials responsible for specific territorial divisions, variously named in different inscriptions as vishayas, rashtras (a term reflected in the Rashtrakuta dynastic name itself, suggesting origins connected to regional territorial administration), or other locally specific units, each overseen by officials charged with revenue collection, local justice, and the maintenance of order. Village-level administration, as in earlier periods examined in Chapters 27 and 37, appears to have rested substantially on local assemblies and headmen whose authority predated and in many cases persisted alongside the overarching royal and subordinate hierarchy, though the precise relationship between village-level institutions and higher administrative tiers varied by region and period and should not be reconstructed as a single, uniform bureaucratic pyramid.

Copper-plate land grants, the single most important category of surviving administrative evidence for this period, typically recorded the donation of land, and the revenue rights associated with it, to a Brahmin individual or family, a temple, or another religious institution. Such grants reveal a great deal about Deccan administrative practice: they identify specific territorial divisions and their boundaries, name the officials responsible for implementing the grant, specify the revenue obligations from which the granted land was exempted, and, through their genealogical preambles, provide crucial chronological and dynastic information historians use to reconstruct royal succession. At the same time, students should recognize that these grants document a particular kind of transaction — royal or elite religious donation — rather than the full range of everyday administrative activity, and that historians must be cautious about generalizing from the specific terms of a land grant to the full structure of taxation and governance across an entire kingdom.

The conversion of military success into durable authority depended heavily on this administrative apparatus. A victorious campaign that did not result in the incorporation of defeated rulers into this layered hierarchy of subordination, or in grants that bound local elites and religious institutions to the new ruling dynasty through shared interest in its continuation, was unlikely to produce lasting political change, a lesson illustrated repeatedly by the pattern of successful-but-temporary campaigns examined in Sections 31.2, 31.3, and 31.6. The Rashtrakutas' ability to absorb and extend the Chalukya administrative system, rather than simply replacing it with an entirely new apparatus, helps explain why their dynasty, despite beginning as an internal usurpation, was able to sustain supraregional authority for more than two centuries.

Section 31.8

Trade and Agrarian Expansion

The political and military achievements examined in the preceding sections rested on a material foundation of agricultural production, expanding settlement, and commercial exchange specific to the Deccan's geography and resources. This section examines that foundation directly, while leaving the broader South Asia-wide treatment of agrarian expansion, land grants, temple economies, and the historiographical debate over early medieval "feudalism" to Chapter 37, which addresses these themes comprehensively across the entire early medieval period and region.

The Deccan's characteristic black volcanic soils, formed from ancient basalt flows and particularly well suited to cotton cultivation among other crops, together with its major river valleys — the Krishna, Godavari, Tungabhadra, and Malaprabha among them — supported substantial agricultural settlement across the period examined in this chapter. Copper-plate grants repeatedly reference the clearing and settlement of new agricultural land, the construction or maintenance of irrigation tanks and reservoirs particularly important in a region of lower and more variable rainfall than the Gangetic plain, and the granting of revenue rights over specific, named villages, evidence that, taken together, suggests steady agrarian expansion across the Chalukya and Rashtrakuta centuries, even though the precise scale of that expansion is difficult to quantify from inscriptional evidence alone.

Commercially, the Deccan occupied a strategically valuable position connecting the Arabian Sea coast to the interior and, via established routes, to both the Gangetic north and the Tamil south. Ports along the western coast, including sites in the Konkan region that came under at least nominal Chalukya and later Rashtrakuta influence, connected Deccan agricultural and craft production to Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean trade networks already well established in earlier periods, as Chapters 22 and 25 have shown for the Satavahana-era Deccan. Arab geographical and travel writers of the ninth and tenth centuries, discussed briefly in the previous section, describe active maritime commerce passing through ports associated with Rashtrakuta authority, further evidence, read with appropriate source-critical caution regarding secondhand testimony, of the kingdom's commercial as well as political significance.

Inland, merchant communities and craft producers operated within towns that grew up around important temple complexes, administrative centers, and route junctions, with inscriptional evidence occasionally referencing merchant guilds, or nigamas, and associated commercial taxes and tolls that formed part of royal and subordinate revenue systems. The relationship between temple patronage, discussed in Section 31.4, and local commercial activity deserves particular note: major temple complexes such as Pattadakal and Ellora required sustained economic activity to construct and maintain, including skilled labor, quarried stone, metalwork, and provisioning for resident priesthoods and pilgrims, activity that in turn stimulated local settlement, craft production, and exchange in the surrounding region.

This chapter presents Deccan agrarian and commercial expansion as a necessary material precondition for the political and military achievements examined throughout the chapter, without attempting the fuller thematic synthesis — covering land grants, jati and occupational communities, peasant production, merchant guilds across the whole of early medieval South Asia, and competing historiographical interpretations of the period's economic character — that Chapter 37 undertakes once the chronological narrative of regional kingdoms has been more fully developed in subsequent chapters.

Section 31.9

Later Chalukya Power

Rashtrakuta supremacy in the Deccan, so impressively built and extended across the eighth and ninth centuries, began to weaken during the tenth century amid succession disputes, renewed pressure from subordinate and rival powers, and, around 973 CE, outright overthrow. A claimant named Taila II, asserting descent from the earlier Badami Chalukya line, defeated the last significant Rashtrakuta ruler, Karka II, and established what historians generally term the Later Chalukya, or Western Chalukya, dynasty. Taila II initially continued to govern from Manyakheta, the Rashtrakuta capital he had just inherited; it was only in the mid-eleventh century, under Someshvara I, that Kalyani became the dynasty's principal capital, the city from which the dynasty's common modern name, "Chalukyas of Kalyani," derives.

Students should resist a tempting but misleading way of summarizing this sequence of events: that the Chalukyas "rose," the Rashtrakutas "replaced" them, and then the Chalukyas simply "returned," as though the Deccan's political history over four centuries formed a closed loop ending where it began. A more accurate reading recognizes several important continuities and changes across this sequence. The Later Chalukya dynasty governed first from Manyakheta and only later from Kalyani, neither of which was the original Chalukya base at Vatapi, reflecting shifts in the regional balance of power and settlement over the intervening centuries rather than a simple restoration of earlier arrangements. The Later Chalukyas inherited, much as the Rashtrakutas had inherited from the Badami line before them, an established administrative system of subordinate rulers, land-grant practices, and religious patronage traditions, adapting rather than reinventing the institutional framework of Deccan kingship examined in Section 31.7.

The Later Chalukya dynasty's own claim of direct descent from the earlier Badami Chalukyas, asserted prominently in their inscriptions, should be read with the same source-critical caution this chapter has applied throughout to dynastic genealogical claims: such assertions served an obvious legitimizing function, allowing Taila II and his successors to present their new authority as the restoration of a rightful, ancient line displaced by Rashtrakuta usurpation, regardless of the precise and now largely unrecoverable details of actual descent. What matters historically is not whether this genealogical claim can be verified in full, but that a new Deccan ruling house found it strategically valuable to present itself this way, confirming that the memory of Badami Chalukya authority remained a significant source of political legitimacy more than two centuries after that dynasty's own overthrow.

Later Chalukya rule across the late tenth and eleventh centuries involved continued regional consolidation and recurring conflict on multiple fronts, including with surviving Rashtrakuta-affiliated powers, with other Deccan regional states, and, of increasing significance as the eleventh century progressed, with the rapidly expanding Chola Empire pressing northward from the Tamil south under rulers such as Rajaraja I and Rajendra I, whose dramatic imperial expansion is examined fully in Chapter 33. This emerging Chalukya-Chola rivalry, structurally similar in many respects to the earlier Chalukya-Pallava conflict examined in Section 31.3, demonstrates the persistence of the Deccan's characteristic strategic position: a powerful regional state, based on the plateau, contesting its southern frontier against an equally powerful and expansionist Tamil state, even as the specific dynasties involved had changed across four centuries.

The Later Chalukya recovery of Deccan supremacy, in short, demonstrates that the regional political order this chapter has traced — layered subordinate authority, extensive religious patronage, agrarian and commercial foundations, and contested northern and southern frontiers — proved durable across repeated dynastic change. Individual ruling houses rose and fell, sometimes dramatically and violently, but the underlying structure of Deccan regional power, and its characteristic relationship to both northern and southern South Asia, persisted and would continue to shape the subcontinent's political history well beyond the chronological close of this chapter.

Section 31.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced more than four centuries of Deccan history, from the rise of the Early Chalukyas at Vatapi in the mid-sixth century through the Later Chalukya recovery of Deccan supremacy in the late tenth century. Its central historical argument, developed across the preceding nine sections, is that the Deccan functioned throughout this period as an independent and repeatedly decisive center of early medieval state formation, not as a peripheral corridor between a more historically significant Gangetic north and Tamil south.

Sections 31.1 and 31.2 established the foundations of that argument through the Early Chalukyas. Pulakeshin I's mid-sixth-century consolidation at Vatapi, and the subsequent expansion of Chalukya authority through the familiar early medieval pattern of conquest and subordinate incorporation, produced by the early seventh century one of the Deccan's most formidable regional states. Pulakeshin II's reign, examined as a historical subject in its own right rather than merely as the ruler who checked Harsha, revealed a sustained, multi-directional program of military expansion across the Kadambas, Gangas, Alupas, Konkan Mauryas, and northern powers, of which the celebrated Narmada confrontation with Harsha was only one episode, albeit a historically significant one that confirmed the Deccan's political independence from northern paramountcy.

Section 31.3 traced the long Chalukya-Pallava rivalry as a sustained, multi-generational contest over the fertile and strategically vital southern frontier, demonstrating that early medieval warfare between powerful, well-organized kingdoms typically produced cycles of temporary conquest and retaliation — including the devastating 642 CE Pallava sack of Vatapi — rather than permanent territorial annexation of a well-established rival.

Section 31.4 showed that this military rivalry coexisted with, and in important respects stimulated, cultural exchange and emulation, nowhere more visible than in the Chalukya temples at Pattadakal, where northern nagara and southern dravida architectural traditions stood side by side, the latter directly informed by the very Pallava tradition the Chalukyas had fought for generations. Political competition and cultural borrowing, this section argued, were not contradictory impulses but simultaneous features of early medieval elite culture.

Sections 31.5 and 31.6 traced the Rashtrakuta displacement of the Badami Chalukyas across roughly 753 to 757 CE — Dantidurga's initial victory over Kirtivarman II followed by Krishna I's completion of Chalukya elimination a few years later — as an internal usurpation by a formerly subordinate chief rather than an external conquest, and showed how Krishna I's successors — Dhruva, Govinda III, and above all the long-reigning Amoghavarsha I — converted an inherited Deccan political and institutional base into a still more ambitious program of supraregional hegemony, intervening decisively, if not permanently, in both northern Indian politics (a theme Chapter 34 will examine fully through the Tripartite Struggle) and southern Deccan and Tamil affairs.

Sections 31.7 and 31.8 moved beneath the level of kings and campaigns to examine how this power was actually exercised and sustained: through a layered hierarchy of paramount kings, subordinate samantas, and territorial officials documented chiefly through copper-plate land grants, and through an agrarian and commercial base of black-soil cultivation, irrigation investment, temple-centered settlement, and Arabian Sea maritime commerce specific to the Deccan's geography.

Section 31.9 completed the chapter's dynastic arc by tracing the Later Chalukya overthrow of Rashtrakuta power in 973 CE, arguing that this sequence of dynastic rise, displacement, and recovery should not be read as a closed loop but as evidence that the underlying structure of Deccan regional power — its administrative institutions, religious patronage traditions, and contested double frontier against both north and south — persisted and adapted across repeated and sometimes violent dynastic change, setting the stage for the Later Chalukyas' own eleventh-century rivalry with the rising Chola Empire.

Chapter 32 now turns to the Pallavas directly, examining their own origins, their transformation of the northern Tamil agrarian and religious landscape, their capital at Kanchipuram, and their architectural achievements at Mahabalipuram — the southern side of the rivalry this chapter has examined from the Deccan's perspective. Students should carry forward from this chapter a central lesson applicable to the regional kingdoms examined throughout Part VII: a state's regional character did not limit the scale of its ambition, and the Deccan, positioned between the Gangetic world and the Tamil south, repeatedly used that position to project power, absorb influence, and shape the political and cultural history of early medieval South Asia as a whole.

Primary Evidence Window

The Aihole Inscription

Evidence type: Sanskrit stone inscription (praśasti, or royal panegyric) · Composed by: Ravikirti, court poet of Pulakeshin II · Approximate date: c. 634–635 CE · Location: Inscribed on the Jinendra (Jain) temple at Aihole, near Badami, Karnataka, which the inscription itself records that Ravikirti founded

Context: The Aihole inscription is among the most important surviving sources for reconstructing the reign of Pulakeshin II. Composed in ornate Sanskrit verse by Ravikirti, the inscription identifies its author as both the composer of the praśasti and the founder of the Jain temple on which it is inscribed. The text recounts the Chalukya royal genealogy and then narrates, in celebratory language, a sequence of Pulakeshin II's military campaigns, including his conflicts with the Kadambas, Gangas, Mauryas of the Konkan, Latas, Malavas, Gurjaras, and, most famously, his confrontation with Harsha near the Narmada. The inscription's presence on a temple that its own Jain author founded is suggestive of a Jain presence within the Chalukya courtly world, including among the dynasty's literary circle, but by itself it is not sufficient evidence from which to reconstruct a specific royal policy toward Jainism; students should distinguish what the inscription actually documents (a Jain poet's standing at court and his personal religious foundation) from a broader claim about royal patronage that would require additional evidence to establish.

Nature of the evidence: The inscription describes Harsha's ambitions toward the Deccan as having been frustrated, stating in substance that Harsha's joy departed from him after his encounter with Pulakeshin II's forces near the Narmada — language typical of the triumphant, rhetorically elevated style court poets used to celebrate royal patrons, rather than a neutral battlefield report.

Analysis: As a praśasti, the Aihole inscription was composed explicitly to glorify Pulakeshin II and his ancestors, and its account of his campaigns should be read as a political and literary performance as much as a factual military record. The sequence and approximate dating of campaigns it describes are generally treated by historians as broadly reliable, since court poets had strong incentives to be accurate about verifiable recent events even while embellishing their significance. Importantly, the Aihole inscription is no longer the only epigraphic evidence for the Harsha encounter: a copper-plate grant found in the Bijapur-Mumbai region and dated April 4, 619 CE independently references Pulakeshin II's success against Harsha, and scholars working from that grant have used it to place the encounter in approximately the winter of 618–619 CE. This independent corroboration strengthens confidence that some genuine Chalukya success against Harsha's forces occurred around this date. It does not, however, resolve the separate and still uncertain questions of the encounter's exact scale, its specific battlefield circumstances, or its longer-term political consequences, all of which still depend heavily on the Aihole inscription's own rhetorically elevated account.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a court poet have strong incentives to be accurate about the sequence of a king's campaigns while still exaggerating their outcomes?
  2. What does Ravikirti's dual role as praśasti author and temple founder suggest about the position of Jain literary figures within the Chalukya court, and what can this single inscription not tell us about royal policy more broadly?
  3. How does the existence of an independent, precisely dated copper-plate grant referencing the same event change how confident historians can be about the Harsha encounter, compared to relying on the Aihole inscription alone?
  4. How should historians distinguish between what this inscription (and the corroborating 619 CE grant) can establish about chronology and what they cannot establish about the actual scale of military events?
Historical Debate

Who "Won" at the Narmada?

The question: Granting that some genuine Chalukya success against Harsha's forces occurred near the Narmada River around the winter of 618–619 CE, how decisive was that encounter, and how significant was it for the longer-term political relationship between the Deccan and the Gangetic north?

Historians can now distinguish two separate questions that earlier generations of scholarship, relying on the Aihole inscription alone, sometimes blurred together. The existence and approximate date of a Chalukya success against Harsha is now corroborated by more than one epigraphic source: alongside the Aihole inscription, a copper-plate grant from the Bijapur-Mumbai region, precisely dated April 4, 619 CE, independently references the Chalukya triumph over Harsha, allowing scholars to place the encounter with reasonable confidence in approximately the winter of 618–619 CE. The scale, exact battlefield circumstances, and long-term political consequences of that success, however, remain considerably less certain and depend almost entirely on the Aihole inscription's own rhetorically elevated account.

Interpretation A — A decisive and historically significant Chalukya success, with durable but not permanent consequences: On this view, the corroborated dating strengthens confidence that Pulakeshin II's forces achieved a genuine and significant check on Harsha's southward ambitions, halting the advance of the most powerful north Indian ruler of the age and confirming, for the seventh-century political configuration then in place, the Deccan's independence from Gangetic paramountcy. This interpretation treats the episode as a real and important turning point, while stopping short of claiming that it fixed the Narmada as a permanent, unbreachable political boundary for all later centuries — a claim this chapter's own later Rashtrakuta narrative, in which Deccan armies repeatedly campaigned well north of the Narmada, would not support.
Interpretation B — A real but militarily modest episode whose later significance was constructed retrospectively: On this view, even with the corroborating 619 CE grant confirming that something happened and roughly when, the surviving evidence still does not allow historians to reconstruct whether the encounter was a major pitched battle, a smaller frontier skirmish, or an extended stand-off that never produced a single decisive clash. On this view, the episode's greatest significance lies not in its military scale but in what Chalukya court tradition, beginning with Ravikirti's own praśasti, made of it afterward: a symbolically important marker of Deccan political independence, regardless of the precise military reality on the ground.

Evidence: Both interpretations now draw on two independent, closely dated epigraphic sources — the Aihole inscription and the 619 CE Bijapur-Mumbai copper-plate grant — for the encounter's approximate timing, but neither source, nor any surviving Harsha-era or Xuanzang account, independently verifies the engagement's scale or decisiveness.

Why it matters: How historians weigh this question shapes how they characterize the broader relationship between northern and Deccan political power in the early seventh century, and how much weight to place on a single, politically interested inscription even once its basic chronology has been independently corroborated.

Student task: Using the Aihole inscription excerpt above, the corroborating 619 CE grant described here, and the broader pattern of Chalukya campaigns described in Section 31.2, evaluate which interpretation the available evidence better supports, and explain precisely which claims are now well supported and which remain genuinely uncertain.

Historical Vocabulary
Samanta, Mahasamanta, and Early Medieval Overlordship

A samanta was a subordinate ruler who retained local authority over his own territory while acknowledging the overlordship of a more powerful king, typically by providing military support when summoned, participating in court ceremonies, and sometimes paying tribute. A mahasamanta held a more elevated or extensive version of this same subordinate status. This system of layered, negotiated authority, rather than direct centralized administration, was the characteristic form of political organization across early medieval South Asia, examined already in Chapter 30 and central to this chapter's account of both Chalukya and Rashtrakuta governance.

Students should understand that overlordship of this kind was inherently unstable in a specific sense: a samanta's local authority, military resources, and administrative experience, all granted or tolerated by the paramount king for practical reasons, could eventually become the foundation for that same samanta's bid for independent or paramount power, exactly as occurred when the Rashtrakuta chief Dantidurga overthrew his former Chalukya overlords in 753 CE.

Historical Vocabulary
Agrahara and the Copper-Plate Land Grant

An agrahara was a village or tract of land granted, typically by royal or elite donation and recorded on inscribed copper plates, to a Brahmin individual, family, or group, often exempting the grant from certain taxes and obligations in recognition of the recipients' ritual and scholarly functions. Comparable grants endowed temples and other religious institutions. These copper-plate charters form the single most important category of surviving administrative evidence for Chalukya and Rashtrakuta history, combining genealogical preambles praising the granting king's ancestry with specific, often precisely dated administrative detail about the land granted, its boundaries, and the officials responsible for implementing the grant.

Historians value these grants for their administrative specificity while remaining cautious about their genealogical and laudatory sections, which served the political purpose of legitimizing the granting dynasty rather than simply recording verifiable fact.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Chalukya to Rashtrakuta to Chalukya

What Changed

  • The ruling dynasty itself changed twice across the period this chapter examines: Chalukya to Rashtrakuta in 753 CE, and Rashtrakuta to Later Chalukya in 973 CE
  • The location of the Deccan's political capital shifted from Vatapi to Manyakheta (from Amoghavarsha I onward) to, eventually, Kalyani under Someshvara I
  • The identity of the principal southern rival shifted over time, from the Pallavas toward the emerging Cholas by the Later Chalukya period
  • The scale of northern intervention expanded considerably, from Pulakeshin II's defensive check on Harsha to Rashtrakuta offensive campaigns reaching Kannauj itself

What Stayed the Same

  • The layered system of paramount kings and subordinate samantas persisted across all three dynastic configurations
  • Copper-plate land grants to Brahmins and religious institutions remained the characteristic instrument of royal patronage and administrative record-keeping
  • Monumental temple construction remained a central expression of royal legitimacy from Pattadakal through Ellora
  • The Deccan's strategic "double frontier" position, inviting intervention both north and south, remained constant regardless of which dynasty held power

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic dynastic ruptures and the underlying institutional continuity allows students to see early medieval Deccan history as neither a simple story of stable, unbroken dynastic rule nor a chaotic sequence of unrelated usurpations, but as a durable regional political tradition capable of surviving, and even strengthening through, repeated changes of ruling family.

Cause and Effect

The Rashtrakuta Displacement of the Chalukyas

Long-term causes

The samanta system of subordinate authority, while administratively useful, steadily equipped loyal subordinate families such as the Rashtrakutas with military experience, local resources, and regional prestige over successive generations of service.

Short-term causes

Weakening Badami Chalukya authority under Kirtivarman II, combined with Dantidurga's own ambition and accumulated military strength, created a specific opportunity for a decisive challenge to Chalukya rule around 753 CE.

Effect

The Rashtrakutas inherited, rather than destroyed, the existing Deccan administrative and religious-patronage order, using that inherited base to launch an even more ambitious program of supraregional expansion under Dhruva, Govinda III, and Amoghavarsha I.

This textbook's response

This chapter treats the Rashtrakuta rise not as an external disruption of Deccan history but as a demonstration of how durable that history's underlying institutions had become — stable enough to survive a change of ruling family largely intact.

Historical complexity: The same structural vulnerability that allowed the Rashtrakutas to displace the Chalukyas in 753 CE would, in turn, allow the Later Chalukyas to displace the Rashtrakutas in 973 CE, a pattern worth bearing in mind as a recurring feature of early medieval Deccan — and more broadly South Asian — political life, rather than a peculiarity of any single dynasty's decline.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village, Temple, and Workshop in the Early Medieval Deccan

Beneath the dynastic narrative of kings, campaigns, and inscriptions, most people living in the Chalukya and Rashtrakuta Deccan experienced these centuries chiefly through the rhythms of village agriculture, local religious practice, and craft production. Copper-plate grants, though composed to document elite donations, incidentally preserve valuable information about village life: the names of local landmarks and boundary features used to define a grant's territory, the agricultural and non-agricultural dues from which granted land might be exempted, and occasional references to local assemblies or headmen responsible for implementing a royal order at the village level.

The construction of major temple complexes such as Pattadakal and Ellora, discussed above chiefly as evidence of royal patronage and cultural exchange, also represented major local economic events, requiring sustained employment of quarry workers, stonemasons, sculptors, and associated laborers over years or decades, along with the provisioning of workers and, once completed, resident priesthoods and visiting pilgrims. Such projects likely drew labor and resources from a wide surrounding region, temporarily or permanently reshaping local economic life in ways that inscriptions, focused as they are on royal donors and religious recipients, do not directly describe but that historians can reasonably infer from the sheer scale of the architectural achievement involved.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined more than four centuries of Deccan history, from the mid-sixth-century rise of the Early Chalukyas at Vatapi through the Later Chalukya recovery of Deccan supremacy at the close of the tenth century. Its governing argument has been that the Deccan functioned throughout this period as an independent and repeatedly decisive center of early medieval state formation, capable of shaping political and cultural developments across South Asia rather than merely separating a more historically significant north from a more historically significant south.

The chapter began with the foundations of Early Chalukya power, tracing Pulakeshin I's mid-sixth-century consolidation at Vatapi and the dynasty's expansion through the characteristic early medieval pattern of conquest and subordinate incorporation. It then examined Pulakeshin II's reign in its own right, revealing a ruler engaged in sustained, multi-directional military expansion across the Deccan and beyond, of which the celebrated confrontation with Harsha near the Narmada River, examined previously in Chapter 30 chiefly from Harsha's perspective, represented only one significant episode.

The chapter then traced the long Chalukya-Pallava rivalry as a sustained, multi-generational contest over the Deccan's southern frontier, marked by cycles of invasion, temporary occupation, and retaliation rather than permanent conquest, including the devastating 642 CE Pallava sack of Vatapi. It showed, through the architecture of Pattadakal, that this military rivalry coexisted with genuine cultural exchange, as Chalukya patrons incorporated southern dravida architectural traditions directly informed by Pallava precedent even while continuing to fight the Pallavas on the battlefield.

The chapter's central dynastic transition came with the Rashtrakuta overthrow of Badami Chalukya rule across roughly 753 to 757 CE, examined as an internal usurpation by a formerly subordinate chief rather than an external conquest, completed in stages between Dantidurga's initial victory and Krishna I's elimination of residual Chalukya authority, and as evidence of a structural vulnerability inherent in the layered samanta system of early medieval governance. It then traced the Rashtrakutas' conversion of this inherited Deccan base into an even more ambitious program of supraregional hegemony, including military intervention in northern Indian politics under Dhruva and Govinda III and the institutional and cultural consolidation achieved during the long and, in its early years, genuinely turbulent reign of Amoghavarsha I, who made Manyakheta the dynasty's imperial capital.

Moving beneath the dynastic narrative, the chapter examined how Deccan states were actually governed, through a layered hierarchy of paramount kings, subordinate samantas and mahasamantas, and territorial officials, documented chiefly through copper-plate land grants whose genealogical and laudatory content must be read with appropriate historical caution. It connected this administrative structure to the Deccan's agrarian and commercial foundations, including black-soil cultivation, irrigation investment, temple-centered settlement, and Arabian Sea maritime commerce.

The chapter closed by tracing the Later Chalukya overthrow of Rashtrakuta rule in 973 CE, noting that Taila II initially continued to govern from Manyakheta, the former Rashtrakuta capital, before Kalyani became the dynasty's principal seat under Someshvara I in the mid-eleventh century. This final dynastic transition should not be read as a simple restoration closing a historical loop, but as further evidence that the underlying structure of Deccan regional power — its administrative institutions, its religious patronage traditions, and its contested double frontier against both the Gangetic north and the Tamil south — proved durable across repeated and sometimes violent dynastic change, setting the stage for the Later Chalukyas' own eleventh-century rivalry with the rising Chola Empire examined in Chapter 33.

Taken together, these transitions caution against reading Deccan political chronology too neatly. The Chalukya-to-Rashtrakuta transition was a multi-year process rather than a single clean break; Rashtrakuta Manyakheta only became a true imperial capital a generation or more into the dynasty's rule; and the Later Chalukyas governed for nearly a century before Kalyani, the city that gives the dynasty its common name, became their actual seat of power. Precision about such details matters not for its own sake, but because it keeps students from imposing a false tidiness on a history that was, in reality, gradual, contested, and often incompletely documented.

Throughout, the chapter has modeled careful use of inscriptional evidence, distinguishing the reliable chronological and administrative information copper-plate grants and stone inscriptions provide from the rhetorically inflated claims of royal panegyric, a method students should carry forward into subsequent chapters covering other early medieval regional kingdoms.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Samanta
A subordinate ruler who retained local authority while acknowledging the overlordship of a more powerful king, providing military support and tribute in exchange for continued local rule.
Mahasamanta
A subordinate ruler holding a more elevated or extensive version of samanta status within the early medieval hierarchy of overlordship.
Agrahara
A village or tract of land granted, typically through royal donation, to a Brahmin individual or family, often with associated tax exemptions.
Copper-plate charter
An inscribed land-grant document, the single most important category of surviving administrative evidence for Chalukya and Rashtrakuta history.
Praśasti
A royal panegyric, or formal inscription composed to praise a ruling king and his ancestors, combining genuine administrative and genealogical content with rhetorical exaggeration.
Nagara style
An architectural tradition associated with northern and central India, characterized by a curvilinear temple tower, or shikhara.
Dravida style
An architectural tradition associated with Tamil and southern India, characterized by a stepped, pyramidal temple tower.
Vesara style
A term some scholars apply to a proposed hybrid Deccan architectural style blending nagara and dravida elements; its classification and chronology remain debated, and some authoritative descriptions of sites such as Pattadakal instead treat the northern, southern, and local idioms as existing side by side rather than fused into one style.
Hiranya-garbha ritual
A Brahmanical rite of royal consecration associated with claims to elevated social and political status, performed by Pulakeshin I according to Chalukya inscriptions.
Digvijaya
Literally "conquest of the directions," a term and concept associated with royal claims of extensive, multi-directional military campaigning.
Monolithic temple
A temple carved downward from a single mass of rock rather than constructed from assembled masonry, exemplified by the Kailasa temple at Ellora.
Rock-cut architecture
Architecture created by excavating and carving directly into natural rock formations, a tradition extended significantly under Rashtrakuta patronage at Ellora.
Deccan trap
The black volcanic basalt geological formation underlying much of the Deccan Plateau, producing soils well suited to certain crops including cotton.
Nigama
A merchant guild, referenced in some early medieval Deccan inscriptions in connection with commercial activity and taxation.
Vatapi (Badami)
The fortified capital of the Early (Badami) Chalukya dynasty.
Manyakheta
The Rashtrakuta capital from the reign of Amoghavarsha I onward, later also the initial capital of the Later Chalukya dynasty after 973 CE.
Kalyani
The principal capital of the Later (Western) Chalukya dynasty from the mid-eleventh century under Someshvara I onward; the dynasty initially governed from Manyakheta after 973 CE.
Pattadakal
A ceremonial and religious complex where Chalukya rulers sponsored temples in both nagara and dravida architectural styles.
Ellora
A major rock-cut religious complex, including the Rashtrakuta-sponsored Kailasa temple.
Aihole inscription
A Sanskrit praśasti composed by the poet Ravikirti, the principal surviving source for reconstructing the reign of Pulakeshin II.
Tripartite Struggle
The multi-decade contest among the Rashtrakutas, Gurjara-Pratiharas, and Palas for control of Kannauj and northern Indian paramountcy, introduced here and examined fully in Chapter 34.
Overlordship
A form of hierarchical political authority in which a paramount ruler claims the allegiance of subordinate rulers who retain local autonomy.
Feudatory
A term sometimes used by historians to describe a subordinate ruler bound to a paramount king through obligations of military service and tribute, roughly equivalent to samanta.
Maharajadhiraja
A grand royal title meaning "great king of kings," claimed by paramount rulers including the Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas, often asserting broader authority than was uniformly exercised in practice.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to see the Deccan plateau not as a connecting corridor between two more significant Indias but as a stage on which some of early medieval South Asia's most consequential political and cultural history actually unfolded. A ruler at Vatapi could check the ambitions of the most powerful king in the Gangetic plain. A dynasty based far from either the Ganges or the Kaveri could march its armies to Kannauj and back. A temple complex on a minor Deccan river could stand as a confident synthesis of architectural traditions drawn from across the subcontinent. None of this was incidental to South Asian history; it was central to it.

The developments traced in this chapter — the rise and expansion of the Early Chalukyas, their long and costly rivalry with the Pallavas, the cultural confidence expressed at Pattadakal, the internal usurpation that produced Rashtrakuta supremacy, the extraordinary reach of Rashtrakuta imperial ambition, the administrative and economic foundations beneath all of this political drama, and the eventual Later Chalukya recovery — together demonstrate that regional power, properly organized and ambitiously deployed, could rival and repeatedly intersect with the history of the Gangetic empires and Tamil kingdoms examined in neighboring chapters.

Students who understand that the Deccan's dynastic changes, however dramatic and sometimes violent, took place atop a durable and adaptable regional political tradition, and who recognize that military rivalry and cultural exchange operated simultaneously rather than as opposites, are well prepared to encounter the Pallavas of Chapter 32 not as a separate story but as the other half of a relationship this chapter has already begun to tell.

Chapter 32 now turns south, to Kanchipuram and the Pallava transformation of the Tamil country — the dynasty whose long rivalry with the Chalukyas has run through much of this chapter, and whose own history deserves the same full, independent treatment this chapter has given the Deccan.
