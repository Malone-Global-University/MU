Chapter Opening

On 9 December 1946, a few hundred men and a small number of women gathered in the Constitution Hall of the Council House in New Delhi, under the oldest member present as temporary chairman, to begin writing a constitution for a country that did not yet exist as a sovereign state. Britain still ruled. The Muslim League’s seats stood empty. Most of the princely states had sent no one. The members had been chosen not by the people but by provincial legislators, who had themselves been elected by a restricted electorate. Just over three years later, on 26 January 1950, the document they produced came fully into force, and the same country held, within two further years, a national election in which every adult citizen was entitled to vote.

Chapter 87 explained how India secured its territorial sovereignty after 15 August 1947: how the Dominion succeeded the British Raj, how hundreds of princely states were brought into the Union, and how Jammu and Kashmir became the subject of the first war between India and Pakistan. This chapter asks a different question. Once a state existed and its borders were, for the moment, defined, how was its power organized, limited, legitimized, and made accountable? Chapter 88 therefore opens Volume VI by examining how India turned independence into a constitutional republic and, within that republic, a functioning electoral democracy.

The central historical question is this: how did India establish a constitutional democracy amid the upheavals of independence and Partition, and what institutions, principles, compromises, and political arrangements defined the early Nehruvian republic? The chapter’s central argument is that the settlement of 1946–1952 combined representative democracy, a powerful Union government, enforceable fundamental rights, a distinctive form of religious pluralism, social-reform commitments, and the leadership of the Indian National Congress. These elements were bound together by a shared nationalist inheritance, but they also contained tensions from the outset: between political equality and social inequality, between a strong centre and federal autonomy, between individual liberty and state security, and between the promise of reform and the protection of property and existing hierarchies.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I follows the Constituent Assembly from its first sitting to the commencement of the Constitution. Division II examines the institutions of parliamentary government, federalism, and the judiciary. Division III turns to secularism, fundamental rights, caste, and the Directive Principles, concluding with the First Amendment of 1951. Division IV explains how the constitutional promise of universal adult franchise was carried out through the Election Commission and the first general elections of 1951–1952. Division V assesses Congress dominance, the opposition, and the political arrangement historians call the Nehruvian settlement.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually official. The Constituent Assembly Debates record the arguments of members in print; the original constitutional text, the statutes of the Provisional Parliament, the First Amendment, and the Election Commission’s published statistical reports allow historians to check claims against documents. But official records have limits. They tell us what members said and what institutions decided far better than they tell us what illiterate voters, Dalit villagers, or women who were registered only as someone’s relative understood the new order to mean. The chapter’s Documentary Source Windows and Everyday Life studies are designed to keep both kinds of evidence in view, and each states plainly whether a passage is an authenticated brief quotation or an editorial paraphrase.

Students should keep several cautions in mind. First, the Constitution has been amended many times since 1950; its present text is not the text of 1950, and this chapter always dates the provision under discussion. Second, the Constituent Assembly was not elected by universal adult franchise, and the elections of 1951–1952 were. Third, “Nehruvian settlement” is an analytical label used by historians and political scientists, not the name of a document or a formal agreement. Fourth, this chapter explains the constitutional authorization of social and economic reform and leaves the execution of that reform (planning, land reform, the Hindu Code Bills, and the linguistic reorganization of states) to Chapter 89. Pakistan’s parallel constitutional struggles are the subject of Chapter 90, and the other South Asian states are taken up in Chapter 91.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why the Republic of India was both a remarkable institutional achievement and an unfinished one: a constitutional democracy created in a country in which most adults could not read, in which caste hierarchy remained powerful, and in which a single party commanded overwhelming parliamentary strength, yet one that established durable procedures through which power could be contested and, in time, transferred.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Constituent Assembly was formed, whom it represented, and how Partition altered its membership and responsibilities.
  2. Analyze the Objectives Resolution and the Assembly’s committee system as expressions of negotiation among competing constitutional visions.
  3. Assess Ambedkar’s role as Chairman of the Drafting Committee alongside the contributions of B. N. Rau, other members, and constitutional administrators.
  4. Distinguish among adoption (26 November 1949), signing (24 January 1950), and general commencement (26 January 1950) of the Constitution, and explain the significance of Republic Day.
  5. Describe how the Constitution adapted parliamentary government, bicameral legislature, and cabinet responsibility to Indian conditions.
  6. Evaluate the constitutional distribution of powers between the Union and the states and the significance of emergency provisions.
  7. Explain the role of the Supreme Court and judicial review, and trace early conflicts between legislation and rights.
  8. Interpret India’s original constitutional approach to religion, distinguishing it from both strict separation and religious establishment, and from later textual changes to the Preamble.
  9. Compare justiciable Fundamental Rights with non-justiciable Directive Principles, and analyze the First Amendment of 1951 as a case study in constitutional conflict.
  10. Analyze the constitutional treatment of untouchability, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and reserved representation.
  11. Reconstruct how universal adult franchise was administered through the Election Commission and the first general elections, and distinguish votes cast from seats won.
  12. Evaluate Congress dominance, the opposition, and the strengths and limits of the Nehruvian settlement, separating constitutional intention, institutional implementation, and political outcome.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could an indirectly selected Constituent Assembly claim the authority to write a constitution “for the people”?
  2. In what ways was constitution-making a process of negotiation rather than the implementation of one leader’s philosophy?
  3. Why did Ambedkar insist that political democracy required social democracy, and how did the Constitution attempt to respond to that argument?
  4. Why did India adopt parliamentary rather than presidential government, and with what consequences for executive power?
  5. Was the Constitution of 1950 best described as federal, unitary, or something distinct from both?
  6. How did India’s original constitutional approach to religion differ from the model of strict separation of church and state?
  7. What was the difference between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, and why did the difference matter in 1951?
  8. Why was universal adult franchise adopted before widespread literacy or economic equality?
  9. What does the difference between Congress’s vote share and seat share reveal about the first general elections?
  10. What did it mean to call early India a democracy in which one party dominated, and what evidence distinguishes dominance from the absence of competition?

Historical Context

The constitution-makers did not begin from nothing. Over almost a century, colonial rule had created legislatures, provincial governments, an elaborate civil service, a codified legal system, and a professional army, as Chapters 71 and 81 explained. The Government of India Act of 1935 had given the provinces a measure of self-government and enfranchised roughly one in ten of the population in British India, with property, tax, and educational qualifications and separate electorates for religious and other communities. Nationalists had meanwhile developed their own constitutional ideas: the Nehru Report of 1928 and the Karachi resolution on fundamental rights in 1931 had both promised civil liberties and adult franchise in a future free India. The independent Republic therefore combined real inheritance with deliberate innovation.

The political circumstances were exceptional. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 had envisaged a Constituent Assembly for undivided India, but the failure of that plan, the Direct Action Day violence, and the decision for Partition (Chapters 85 and 86) meant that the Assembly worked amid communal violence, mass displacement, the assassination of Gandhi on 30 January 1948, and war over Kashmir. Chapter 87 described the integration of the princely states, a process that was still unfolding while the Constitution was being drafted. Drafters knew that a fragile state needed a strong centre, and they also knew that Partition had been produced in part by the failure to agree on how communities would be represented.

Social conditions shaped the arguments. The 1951 census recorded a population of roughly 361 million, with an overall literacy rate of about 18 percent, far lower for women than for men. Most people lived in villages, agriculture dominated the economy, and caste hierarchy governed access to land, water, temples, education, and public life. Constitutional equality would be proclaimed in a society where such equality did not exist in practice. That gap lay at the centre of Ambedkar’s warnings and of the debates about franchise, rights, and the Directive Principles.

Finally, the Indian National Congress, which had led the national movement, entered 1947 with a vast organization, enormous prestige, and few comparable rivals. The leaders who dominated the Assembly (Nehru, Patel, Prasad, Azad) were Congress leaders; so were most of the members. Yet the Assembly also included Ambedkar, who had long opposed Congress, representatives of minorities and princely states, women members, and a number of independent-minded lawyers. This chapter treats Congress’s dominance as a historical fact that must be explained and assessed, not as a substitute for the constitutional history itself.

Section 88.1

The Constituent Assembly: Formation, Representation, and the Challenge of Constitution-Making

The Constituent Assembly of India met for the first time on 9 December 1946. Its origin lay in the Cabinet Mission’s proposals of May 1946 (Chapter 85), which envisaged a body that would frame a constitution for an undivided India. Its members were not chosen by voters directly. Instead, the members of the provincial legislatures, elected in the early months of 1946 on a limited franchise, voted in July and August 1946 to choose representatives, using the single transferable vote. Seats were allotted to the provinces roughly in proportion to population, and within each province to the main communities (General, Muslim, and Sikh). Representatives of the princely states, who accounted for a fixed share of the total, were to be chosen through arrangements negotiated with the states, which in practice meant that many were nominated by rulers or their governments, and that state representation grew only gradually as states joined.

The Assembly that met in December 1946 was therefore incomplete. The Congress had won the large majority of the general seats, and the Muslim League had won nearly all the Muslim seats; but the League, having withdrawn from the Cabinet Mission arrangements, boycotted the Assembly, and the princely states were initially thinly represented. The oldest member present, Sachchidananda Sinha, served as temporary chairman, and on 11 December the Assembly elected Rajendra Prasad as its President. A body formally meant to write a constitution for a united country was, in effect, dominated by one party and uncertain whether the whole of India would remain within its scope.

Partition transformed it. Once the plan of 3 June 1947 was accepted, members representing areas that became Pakistan ceased to sit in the Indian Assembly, and Pakistan’s own Constituent Assembly, discussed in Chapter 90, began its work in Karachi. The Indian Assembly was reconstituted at the end of October 1947 with about 299 members, drawn from the provinces and, increasingly, from the princely states as they acceded or merged. Some Muslim League members from provinces that remained in India rejoined its proceedings, and the League’s members who stayed in India contributed to debates on minority safeguards. With independence, the Assembly also became a sovereign body: it was free of British parliamentary control, and from the Dominion’s creation it also functioned as the country’s central legislature, meeting in that capacity under a separate chair, G. V. Mavalankar. For about two and a half years it therefore wrote the constitution while legislating for a country in crisis.

The Assembly’s indirect selection poses a legitimate historical question. It lacked a direct electoral mandate from adult citizens; the provincial legislators who chose its members had themselves been elected by an electorate that excluded most adults. Critics, both contemporary and later, have noted that the Assembly was dominated by lawyers, landowners, and educated professionals, and that peasants, workers, and the poor were not represented by their own members in any proportion to their numbers. Defenders have argued that the Assembly drew authority from a different source: the national movement. Its leading members had spent decades in the struggle against colonial rule, and their authority rested on that record as much as on the electoral procedure. Both statements are historically true, and students should resist reducing the question to one or the other. Most of all they should avoid describing the Assembly as having been elected by universal adult franchise; that would not occur until the general elections of 1951–1952 (§§88.13–88.16).

The Assembly was nonetheless more varied than a purely Congress body. Besides the principal leaders (Prasad, Nehru, Patel, Maulana Azad, and Sarojini Naidu), it included Ambedkar, who had first entered the Assembly from Bengal with Muslim League support and, after Partition cost him that seat, returned from Bombay in July 1947. There were Christian, Parsi, Sikh, Anglo-Indian, and Muslim members, among them H. C. Mookerjee (elected Vice-President), Frank Anthony, and Begum Aizaz Rasul. Jaipal Singh Munda spoke for Adivasi communities. Fifteen women sat in the Assembly: Hansa Mehta, Durgabai Deshmukh, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Ammu Swaminathan, Dakshayani Velayudhan, Renuka Ray, Purnima Banerji, Sucheta Kripalani, and others. Their numbers were small relative to the male membership, but their contributions to debates on rights, personal law, and education were substantial. Women had been prominent in the national movement, and they argued for equality without separate reserved seats.

The task they faced was unusually complex. The Assembly had to decide on the form of government, the relationship between the Union and the states, the rights of citizens, the position of religious and linguistic minorities, the place of historically oppressed castes, the status of the princely states, and the language of government, while drawing on British, American, Irish, Canadian, Australian, and other models, and on India’s own nationalist and colonial experience. It had to do so while the country was convulsed. Its procedures, with committees preparing proposals, a published draft, open debate, thousands of proposed amendments, and three readings, gave the process a deliberative character that the circumstances might not have suggested.

Historical Vocabulary
Constituent Assembly

A constituent assembly is a body convened to write or adopt a constitution, as distinct from an ordinary legislature that makes laws under an existing constitution. India’s Constituent Assembly was both: after August 1947 it also legislated for the Dominion. The distinction matters because a constituent body claims to speak for the people as a whole, and the question of what justified that claim (an indirect election, the authority of the nationalist movement, later popular acceptance) is a central issue in this chapter.

Section 88.2

The Objectives Resolution, Constitutional Committees, and Competing Visions of India

On 13 December 1946, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution, which the Assembly adopted on 22 January 1947 after a debate shaped by the League’s absence. Nehru acknowledged that the Assembly was incomplete and framed the resolution as a pledge. The text declared the intention to proclaim India an independent sovereign republic and to draw up a constitution for a Union comprising British Indian territories, the Indian states, and other parts willing to join. It stated that all power and authority derive from the people. It promised social, economic, and political justice; equality of status and opportunity; and freedom of thought, expression, belief, worship, and association, subject to law and public morality. It pledged adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes, and it affirmed the integrity of territory and a contribution to world peace.

The resolution did not use the words “socialist” or “secular,” and it did not spell out the details of the political system. It was, rather, a statement of principles that later became the foundation for the Preamble drafted in 1949. Its debate nevertheless revealed disagreements. Some members, notably M. R. Jayakar, urged postponement until the Muslim League joined; Ambedkar, speaking on 17 December, urged conciliation and cautioned that a constitution that did not carry all major communities would be fragile. The Assembly rejected postponement, but the exchange foreshadowed a recurring theme: the new order would be judged by whether it could command the allegiance of those who had not chosen it.

The Assembly then worked through committees. A Steering Committee and a Rules of Procedure Committee managed business. A Union Powers Committee and a Union Constitution Committee, both chaired by Nehru, considered the central government; a Provincial Constitution Committee, chaired by Vallabhbhai Patel, considered the provinces; and a States Committee dealt with the princely states. An Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities, and Tribal and Excluded Areas, also chaired by Patel, worked through sub-committees (J. B. Kripalani chaired the Fundamental Rights sub-committee; H. C. Mookerjee, the Minorities sub-committee). Their reports went to the full Assembly, whose debates produced revisions. The Constitutional Adviser, B. N. Rau, supported the committees with comparative research and draft texts.

Behind these procedures lay competing visions. Nehru’s outlook combined parliamentary democracy, civil liberty, secular citizenship, and state-led development, and he spoke of an India that would be both modern and rooted in its history. Ambedkar’s emphasis was on legal remedies, social democracy, and the destruction of caste hierarchy through constitutional means. Patel, as Home Minister and chair of the minorities committee, emphasized order, integration, and a strong Union, and in the Advisory Committee he guided the compromise by which separate electorates were abandoned. Gandhians, notably those who argued for a village-based political order, criticized the draft for ignoring the village; socialists wanted explicit economic commitments; some Hindu members wanted constitutional protection for cow protection and for Hindi and Sanskrit; minority members sought safeguards, though many of them in 1949 chose to give up reserved seats and rely on joint electorates and fundamental rights. Sikh, Christian, Anglo-Indian, tribal, and Muslim leaders each pursued different strategies.

One important decision was made in the Advisory Committee’s work of 1947–1949. In 1947 the committee voted against separate electorates, which had been a feature of British constitutional arrangements since 1909 and which many, including nationalists, held responsible for deepening political division. In May 1949 the Assembly accepted the abolition of reserved legislative seats for religious minorities, retaining reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes within joint electorates for a limited period, and special provisions for Anglo-Indians. Historians such as Rochana Bajpai have shown that the minority members themselves were divided and that the outcome reflected negotiation, not simple imposition. This is a vital point for students: constitution-making involved bargaining among interests with unequal power, and the final text cannot be read as the realization of any single leader’s philosophy.

The Assembly also accepted that its Union would include very different units. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules created special arrangements for tribal areas, and the Sixth Schedule (based on the work of the Bordoloi Committee) provided autonomous district councils in parts of Assam. Provisions on language, discussed in §88.7, reflected the same pattern of compromise. Taken together, the Objectives Resolution and the committee system show a Constituent Assembly that worked from broad principles toward detailed negotiated settlements.

Section 88.3

B. R. Ambedkar and the Drafting Committee: Constitutionalism and Social Democracy

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar, born in 1891 into the Mahar community, which was classed as untouchable, had become one of the most formidable intellectuals in India. Educated at Columbia University and the London School of Economics, and called to the bar at Gray’s Inn, he had led the Mahad water satyagraha of 1927, contested Gandhi over separate electorates and the Poona Pact of 1932 (Chapter 81), written Annihilation of Caste in 1936, and served as Labour Member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council in 1942–1946 (Chapter 80). He was an opponent of Congress. Yet when independence came, Nehru invited him to become Law Minister, and the Assembly, on 29 August 1947, appointed him Chairman of the Drafting Committee.

The Committee had seven members: Ambedkar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K. M. Munshi, Syed Muhammad Saadulla, and two others who changed during its work. B. L. Mitter was replaced by N. Madhava Rau, and D. P. Khaitan, who died, was succeeded by T. T. Krishnamachari. In November 1948, Krishnamachari told the Assembly that illness, absence, and death meant that the burden had fallen chiefly on Ambedkar. The Committee did not begin from a blank page. B. N. Rau, the Constitutional Adviser, prepared a draft in October 1947 from the Assembly’s committee reports and his own research, which included consultations in the United States, Canada, Ireland, and Britain. S. N. Mukherjee, the chief draftsman, gave the text its legal precision. The Committee’s draft, published in February 1948, was circulated for comment, revised in light of responses, and then presented to the Assembly in November 1948.

The accurate view is that the Constitution was a collective product. It is misleading to call Ambedkar “the father of the Constitution” as if he were its sole author, and it is equally misleading to diminish his role. He introduced and defended the draft, answered critics clause by clause, and made decisions about wording and structure, and the Assembly’s adoption of many of his arguments (on emergency powers, on a strong Union, on the primacy of judicial remedies) shaped the outcome. Some figures are commonly cited for the scale of the debate: several thousand amendments were tabled and about two and a half thousand were moved. The process lasted from November 1948 into November 1949, with a third reading beginning on 14 November 1949.

Ambedkar’s distinctive contribution was to insist that formal political equality was not enough. A Constitution could grant every adult one vote, but if the social order remained deeply unequal, democracy would be fragile. He drew on the English historian George Grote’s idea of “constitutional morality,” the habit of respecting constitutional forms and procedures even when they are inconvenient, and argued that it was not natural in India and had to be cultivated. He defended the parliamentary form because it provided continuous accountability. He described the Constitution as establishing a Union rather than a federation in the sense of a compact among sovereign states, so that states had no right to secede. He was skeptical of the Gandhian idea of village republics, which he regarded as bastions of localism and caste prejudice, though in 1948 the Assembly added a directive principle on village panchayats (Article 40) as a concession to Gandhian critics. And he prized Article 32, the right to constitutional remedies, as the essential guarantee of every other right.

His closing speech on 25 November 1949, the day before adoption, drew these themes together. He thanked the Assembly, acknowledged Rau and Mukherjee, and then offered what he called warnings: that the country should abandon methods of agitation that belonged to an era of struggle against an alien ruler (a reliance on unconstitutional methods) and use constitutional means; that it should avoid hero-worship in politics; and that it should recognize that political democracy must rest on social democracy, defined as a way of life recognizing liberty, equality, and fraternity. He foresaw that India was about to enter a contradiction between equality in politics and inequality in social and economic life. These arguments form the intellectual core of §88.11 and §88.12 and of the second Historical Debate in this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional Morality

Ambedkar borrowed from Grote the idea of constitutional morality: a reverence for constitutional forms and a willingness to resolve disputes through them rather than force. He argued it was not an instinct and had to be taught. The concept is not equivalent to the legal text; it names the habits, expectations, and restraint that give institutions life. Historians use it to ask whether the Republic’s institutions worked because of design or because of the practices of those who operated them.

Section 88.4

The Constitution of 1950: Adoption, Republic Day, and the New Constitutional Order

Three dates must be kept distinct. The Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, and at that moment certain provisions (those on citizenship, elections, the provisional Parliament, and a few others) took effect at once, so that the state could begin the preparations for its own new order. On 24 January 1950, members signed the Constitution, and the Assembly chose Rajendra Prasad as the first President; Jana Gana Mana was accepted as the national anthem the same day. The Constitution as a whole came into force on 26 January 1950. A handful of members declined to sign, and the record notes that the poet-politician Hasrat Mohani was among them.

The date of commencement was chosen for its history. On 26 January 1930, following the Lahore Congress’s declaration of Purna Swaraj (Chapter 81), Indians had been asked to take a pledge of independence, and the day had been observed as Independence Day each year until 1947. Choosing the same date for the Republic tied the new order to the movement’s own symbolism, although the actual independence had come on 15 August 1947. In 1949 the political leadership also had to resolve another question: could a republic remain within the Commonwealth? At the Commonwealth meeting of April 1949, the “London Declaration” accepted India’s continuing membership as a republic, with the monarch recognized only as a symbol of the association, not as India’s head of state. Thus the Dominion of India, whose last Governor-General was C. Rajagopalachari (who succeeded Mountbatten in June 1948), became a sovereign republic on 26 January 1950, and Prasad took the oath of office as President before the Chief Justice-designate, H. J. Kania.

As enacted, the Constitution had 395 articles, 22 parts, and eight schedules. It opened with the Preamble, which declared the people’s resolve to constitute India into a sovereign democratic republic and to secure to all citizens justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity, with the dignity of the individual and the unity of the nation. Part I, Article 1, said that “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.” The text drew on many sources, and Ambedkar made no apology for the fact: the Government of India Act of 1935 supplied much of the administrative and emergency structure; the British system provided the model of parliamentary government; the United States, the idea of a written bill of rights and judicial review; Ireland, the Directive Principles; Canada, a strong centre with residuary powers; and Australia, the Concurrent List. The result was not a copy of any one model.

The Constitution repealed the Government of India Act of 1935 and the Indian Independence Act of 1947, ended the office of Governor-General, and provided for continuity of existing laws (Article 372), so that British-era statutes such as the Indian Penal Code continued until amended. It created a President as head of state, a Prime Minister and Council of Ministers responsible to the lower house, a bicameral Parliament, state legislatures, a Supreme Court and High Courts, an Election Commission, and an independent auditor. Rajendra Prasad, as President, and Nehru, as Prime Minister, thus became holders of new constitutional offices.

The text is best understood as a framework and not as a completed social program. It specified institutions and rights, set out aspirations, and left for ordinary legislation and politics the work of land reform, economic planning, and social change. This is why the chapter distinguishes carefully between constitutional authorization and subsequent implementation, a distinction that organizes the division between this chapter and Chapter 89. It is also why historians have asked whether a document written by an indirectly elected Assembly could claim legitimacy. India did not hold a referendum. The answer that emerged in practice was acceptance through use: the 1951–1952 elections, in which citizens took part under the new constitutional framework, retrospectively gave the Republic a popular foundation.

The Constitution of 1950 should not be confused with its current text. It has been amended more than a hundred times, its articles and schedules have expanded to roughly 450 articles and twelve schedules, the Preamble was changed in 1976, and the fundamental right to property, the voting age, and the names of the two Houses have all been altered. Section 88.5

Parliamentary Democracy: The President, Prime Minister, Cabinet, and Responsible Government

The Constitution of 1950 established a parliamentary system of government, adapted from British practice, at both the Union and state levels. The executive power of the Union was vested in the President, but the Constitution also provided that a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head would aid and advise the President. The Council was collectively responsible to the House of the People, as the Lok Sabha was then called (Article 75). In constitutional terms, the President was the head of state, formally appointing the Prime Minister and other ministers, summoning and proroguing Parliament, giving assent to bills, and serving as supreme commander of the armed forces. In political terms, the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, who commanded a majority in the lower house, held the real executive power.

The principle of responsible government rests on the confidence of the legislature. A ministry that lost the confidence of the House of the People had to resign. Ministers were required to be members of Parliament, or to become members within six months. Collective responsibility meant that ministers publicly supported Cabinet decisions or resigned. The Constitution did not spell out many of the conventions that governed these relationships. As in Britain, practice supplied what text did not: the Cabinet’s agenda, the role of the Cabinet Secretariat, committees of ministers, the Prime Minister’s duty to keep the President informed (Article 78), and the question-and-answer procedures in the House.

The most interesting early constitutional question concerned the President’s powers. The original text of Article 74 said that the President would be aided and advised by ministers; it did not say in explicit words that the President was bound by that advice. (An amendment in 1976 made the advice binding, and a later amendment allowed the President to ask the Council to reconsider it once.) Ambedkar had said in the Assembly that the President would occupy the position of the British monarch: head of the state, not of the executive. Yet the first President, Rajendra Prasad, held strong views and was a respected senior Congress figure, and in the early years he and Nehru sometimes disagreed. Prasad’s attendance at the consecration of the restored Somnath temple in May 1951, against Nehru’s reservations about a head of state taking part in a religious ceremony, is one famous example; his concerns about the proposed Hindu Code Bill, discussed in Chapter 89, are another. In 1951 Prasad also raised questions about the extent of his own discretion. The settled view that emerged, supported by the Attorney-General and, in practice, by Prasad’s conduct, was that the President was bound by ministerial advice. This outcome was established by convention and by the persuasive authority of Nehru and the Cabinet, not by clear constitutional text.

The Constitution also designated a Vice-President, elected by members of both Houses, who would be ex officio chairman of the upper house. The President was elected indirectly by an electoral college of the elected members of Parliament and of the state legislatures (Articles 54–55), a design intended to give the office national authority without creating a rival to the Prime Minister on the basis of a separate popular mandate. Governors of states were appointed by the President and thus, in practice, by the Union government, with a role analogous to the President’s at the state level.

Why did India choose parliamentary rather than presidential government? The Assembly considered the question and, after the first drafts of 1947, chose the British model. Members noted that the country’s political leaders and civil servants had worked within it under the Government of India Act of 1935, which had introduced a form of responsible government in the provinces in 1937. Ambedkar argued that a parliamentary system offered more continuous accountability, since the executive could be questioned and removed at any time, whereas a presidential system offered periodic accountability but more stability. In a country of such scale, with so many communities, a presidential figure with direct popular election could also seem dangerous. The framers were also aware of the failures of other new constitutions in the interwar period and preferred a model that produced accountable governments through party competition in legislatures.

The decision had consequences. It made the Prime Minister dependent on a legislative majority and on party discipline, thus shifting power to party organizations. Under a dominant party, as §88.17 explains, the executive could be powerful in practice despite constitutional accountability. And it assigned to the largely ceremonial President a constitutional guardianship function (for example, in appointing a Prime Minister when no party held a majority) that would matter more in the era of coalitions discussed in Chapter 98. In the early 1950s, none of this was tested: Nehru commanded an overwhelming majority, and the question of presidential discretion remained largely theoretical.

Section 88.6

Parliament, Bicameralism, and the Institutions of Representative Government

The Parliament of the Union consisted of the President and two Houses: the Council of States, soon known as the Rajya Sabha, and the House of the People, the Lok Sabha. (The Hindi names were adopted formally in 1954; this chapter uses the original English titles where it refers to the Constitution’s text and the later names where it is discussing the institutions over time.) The Lok Sabha was designed to be directly elected by adult franchise from territorial constituencies, with a maximum of 500 members chosen from the states and a small number of additional members from the centrally administered territories; the President could nominate two members of the Anglo-Indian community if it was not otherwise represented. The Rajya Sabha was designed as a chamber of the states, with a maximum of 250 members, of whom 12 were nominated by the President for distinction in literature, science, art, and social service, and the rest elected by the state legislative assemblies through proportional representation using the single transferable vote. It was a permanent body, with one-third of its members retiring every two years.

The two Houses were not equal. Money bills could be introduced only in the lower house, and the upper house could delay but not block them; on ordinary legislation, a disagreement could be settled by a joint sitting in which the more numerous Lok Sabha would normally prevail. Only the Lok Sabha could remove the government by withdrawing confidence. The upper house thus acted as a revising and representative chamber, giving some voice to the states and to expertise, though in the early decades it was strongly shaped by the same party majorities.

There was also a transitional institution. When the Constitution commenced, the Constituent Assembly became the Provisional Parliament, which continued until the first elected Parliament met in 1952. This meant that the country’s legislature during the critical early period (which passed the Preventive Detention Act, the Representation of the People Acts, and the First Amendment) was not elected by universal franchise but consisted of the same indirectly selected members. Critics at the time, including Shyama Prasad Mookerjee and H. N. Kunzru, pointed out that rights-changing legislation by an unelected body raised questions of propriety; supporters answered that the Provisional Parliament was a necessary stage before the general elections could be held. G. V. Mavalankar, who had presided over the Assembly in its legislative capacity, served as its Speaker.

Parliamentary procedure was inherited largely from Britain and from the pre-independence legislatures. Members could ask questions of ministers, introduce adjournment motions, speak in debate on bills and on the budget, and serve on committees. The Public Accounts Committee, which scrutinized expenditure after the fact, continued from the colonial period; the Estimates Committee was added in 1950 on the recommendation of the Finance Minister, John Mathai. The Speaker, who presided over the lower house, was expected to be impartial, though also a party member, and Mavalankar’s rulings were influential in setting rules for debate and the relationship between the House and the government. Budget approval, the annual cycle of demands for grants, and the requirement that the government obtain the House’s consent to taxation gave Parliament its basic power.

The record of debates in the Provisional Parliament shows how far this model was operating in practice. In February 1950, for example, the debate on the Preventive Detention Bill saw members of different persuasions, including Congress members, argue against allowing detention without trial in peacetime; the government maintained that the situation, with communal violence, the communist insurgency in Telangana, and war in Kashmir, required it. The Bill passed, but the debate and the dissent were real. Similarly, the debates of 1951 on the First Amendment and on the Representation of the People Bill involved extended criticism by members of the opposition, including Mookerjee, Kripalani, and communist members led by A. K. Gopalan, and the government responded in detail.

Yet constitutional design and political practice differed. The Provisional Parliament was overwhelmingly Congress. A government with a secure majority controlled the legislative timetable; most bills passed; and the opposition, though vocal, could not defeat legislation. Parliamentary accountability therefore depended less on the defeat of the government than on the pressure of debate, publicity, the independence of the Speaker, and the Prime Minister’s own readiness to answer questions. Nehru, who attended the House regularly and treated it as an essential arena, set a norm of engagement that later leaders did not always follow. This is a theme for §88.19 and §88.20.

Section 88.7

Federalism: Union Powers, State Governments, and the Constitutional Distribution of Authority

Article 1 describes India as a Union of States, and Ambedkar made a point of this wording. In introducing the draft in November 1948, he explained that the framers had chosen “Union” to make clear that the federation was not the result of an agreement among states and that no state had the right to secede. The Constitution was federal in structure, since it divided powers between the Union and the states, but it was also weighted toward the centre. It may be described as federal in form with strong unitary features, or, in a phrase used by later scholars, as a system that could operate as federal or unitary depending on circumstances.

The distribution of legislative powers appears in the Seventh Schedule. The Union List, with 97 entries, covered defence, foreign affairs, railways, currency, and other national matters. The State List, with 66 entries, covered public order, police, local government, agriculture, and other matters. The Concurrent List, with 47 entries, covered subjects such as criminal law, marriage, and economic planning, on which both could legislate, with the Union’s law prevailing in case of conflict. Residuary powers, that is, subjects not listed anywhere, went to the Union (Article 248), unlike the United States, where they remain with the states. The Union could also legislate on State List subjects in specified circumstances, such as when the Council of States declared this to be in the national interest, and states were required to exercise their executive powers in ways that did not impede the Union’s.

Other provisions strengthened the centre. Governors were appointed by the President, not elected. Article 3 allowed Parliament, by ordinary law, to form new states, alter boundaries, or change names, which was the legal basis of the reorganization of states in 1956 discussed in Chapter 89. Emergency provisions, drawn largely from the Government of India Act of 1935 and, in part, from the Weimar Constitution, allowed the Union to take over wide powers. Article 352 allowed the President to proclaim an emergency on grounds of war, external aggression, or (in the original text) internal disturbance. Article 356 allowed the President, on a Governor’s report or otherwise, to take over a state’s government if its constitutional machinery had failed, a power that came to be called President’s Rule. Article 355 made it the Union’s duty to protect each state against external aggression and internal disturbance. The first use of Article 356 came in 1951 in Punjab. These provisions were defended as essential in a country that had just endured Partition and in which the integration of princely states was incomplete; critics warned they could be misused for partisan advantage, a concern that history would justify, as later chapters, especially Chapter 93, show.

The financial arrangements further showed the character of the system. The Constitution provided for the sharing of certain taxes and for grants to states, and for a Finance Commission, appointed by the President every five years, to recommend the distribution; the first, chaired by K. C. Neogy, was constituted in 1951. States relied on the Union for a large share of their finances. The extra-constitutional Planning Commission, established in March 1950, would soon become a central institution of Union influence (Chapter 89).

The original constitutional geography itself reflected the circumstances of integration. The First Schedule divided the Union into four categories. Part A states were the former governors’ provinces of British India. Part B states were princely states or unions of states, headed by a Rajpramukh, who was often a former ruler. Part C states were centrally administered units under chief commissioners, and Part D consisted of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. These categories meant that different parts of the Union had different degrees of self-government, a distinction removed in 1956. Jammu and Kashmir, listed among the Part B states, was governed by a special provision, Article 370, which was framed as a temporary arrangement; the circumstances of its accession belong to Chapter 87, and its later history to Chapter 105.

One must distinguish constitutional federalism from political centralization. On paper, the Union’s powers were great, but in practice the early Republic’s centralization depended also on the fact that Congress governed at the centre and in nearly all states, and that state leaders such as Govind Ballabh Pant in Uttar Pradesh and B. C. Roy in West Bengal were powerful in their own right. Nehru wrote regular letters to the chief ministers, which served as a channel of influence but also as a form of consultation. Language policy illustrated the bargaining: the Constitution made Hindi in the Devanagari script the official language of the Union, with English continuing for fifteen years, a compromise reached in 1949 by the so-called Munshi–Ayyangar formula, and it listed fourteen languages in the Eighth Schedule. The question of whether to organize the states on a linguistic basis had been deferred after the Dar Commission and the JVP Committee advised caution; its resolution is Chapter 89’s subject.

Section 88.8

The Judiciary, Constitutional Supremacy, and the Protection of the Republic

The Constitution established an integrated judicial system topped by the Supreme Court of India, which was inaugurated on 28 January 1950, two days after the Constitution commenced. It replaced the Federal Court created in 1937, and in 1949 Parliament abolished appeals to the Privy Council in London, ending the last formal judicial link with Britain. The first Chief Justice was H. J. Kania; his early colleagues included Fazl Ali, M. Patanjali Sastri, Mehr Chand Mahajan, B. K. Mukherjea, and S. R. Das. Below the Supreme Court were the High Courts of the states, inherited from the colonial system, and subordinate courts.

The Court’s authority rested on several provisions. Article 13 declared that laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights were void to the extent of the inconsistency, thereby creating the basis for judicial review of legislation. Article 32 gave citizens the right to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, with power to issue writs, and Article 226 gave the High Courts a similar power. The Court had original jurisdiction in disputes between the Union and states (Article 131), appellate powers, and an advisory role at the President’s request (Article 143). Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution, because without a remedy a right was only a declaration. Judges of the Supreme Court were appointed by the President after consultation, held office until age 65, and could be removed only by an address of both Houses on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, by a special procedure that made removal very difficult. A Directive Principle (Article 50) called for the separation of the judiciary from the executive.

The Constitution could be amended (Article 368), most provisions by a special majority of Parliament, and some federal provisions with ratification by half the state legislatures. The Assembly thus tried to combine flexibility with protection, and the question of whether there were limits on Parliament’s amending power was not answered in 1950; the answer, which came in later decades, belongs to later chapters.

The early Court heard important cases that exposed these tensions. In A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), a Communist leader challenged his detention under the Preventive Detention Act, and the Court, interpreting “procedure established by law” in Article 21 narrowly, largely upheld the law. In Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950), by contrast, the Court struck down the ban on the circulation of a left-wing journal, Cross Roads, holding that restrictions on speech had to fall within the grounds the Constitution permitted; a similar case from Delhi involved the RSS journal Organiser. In 1951 the Madras case State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan struck down caste-based quotas in educational admissions as inconsistent with Article 29(2), and in the same year the Patna High Court held a Bihar land-reform law invalid under the equality clause, while other High Courts reached different conclusions about similar laws. Historian Rohit De has shown that many early rights cases were brought by ordinary citizens, such as traders, small publishers, and those affected by regulation, and that the Court’s work therefore reflected the ordinary conflicts of citizens with a regulatory state.

These decisions mattered because they placed in the open a tension that Division III explores. Parliament had an overwhelming majority and a mandate for social reform; the courts had power to strike down laws that violated rights; and the rights included, at that point, an express guarantee of property. The Court’s decisions on speech, preventive detention, and reservation, together with the High Court decisions on land reform, prompted the government to introduce the First Amendment in 1951, discussed in §88.12. The mixture of judicial independence and a powerful legislature was, thus, no abstraction: within eighteen months of the Constitution’s commencement it produced the first major conflict.

An independent judiciary was intended to restrain governmental power even where a single party controlled the legislature. The courts were a source of institutional continuity: colonial judges and lawyers became Indian ones, and the language and practice of common-law reasoning carried over. But a court enforcing rights in a poor society also faced limits. It was remote from most villages, its processes were expensive and slow, and the justiciable rights of 1950 were skewed toward liberty and property more than toward welfare.

Historical Debate 1

Did the Constitution Establish a Federal Union or a Centralized State?

The question: Was the Constitution of 1950 essentially federal, with a strong centre justified by circumstances, or was it in substance a centralized system with federal features?

Interpretation A — A necessary strong Union: After Partition, with princely states still being integrated, with violence and a war in Kashmir, the framers needed a state capable of holding the country together. The Union’s residuary powers, emergency provisions, and the power to reorganize states were safeguards for cohesion. Ambedkar defended the arrangement as flexible: the system could be federal in normal times and operate with greater central authority in emergencies. The Constitution also protected states’ authority over the State List and gave them independent legislatures and courts.
Interpretation B — A centralized system with limited autonomy: Critics, then and since, have argued that the combination of appointed governors, President’s Rule, central control of finance, residuary powers, and the Union’s ability to alter state boundaries by ordinary law left states with limited autonomy. They also note that the party that controlled the centre controlled nearly every state, so that Article 356 could be used for political ends. The first use in Punjab in 1951 seemed to some observers to confirm this concern.

Evidence: Constitutional provisions; the Constituent Assembly Debates on the Union, emergency provisions, and finance; early practice in Centre–State relations, including the 1951 Punjab case and the pattern of Congress control.

Why it matters: The question underlies later conflicts: the reorganization of states (Chapter 89), the use of President’s Rule (Chapters 93 and 94), and demands for regional autonomy.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Government of India Act of 1935 to the Constitution of 1950

What changed? Sovereignty moved from Parliament in Westminster to the people of India; the Governor-General and Crown were replaced by a President; princely states ceased to be separate entities; adult franchise replaced a narrow electorate; separate religious electorates were abolished; a bill of enforceable rights was added; and the Privy Council’s jurisdiction ended.

What remained continuous? A large part of the administrative structure, the emergency provisions, the federal division of powers in its basic outline, the civil service and police, the legal codes, and the courts’ common-law traditions. Article 372 preserved existing law.

Who experienced change most directly? Citizens previously excluded from the franchise, residents of the princely states, and religious and caste minorities.

Who experienced greater continuity? Civil servants, police officers, and lawyers, whose institutions persisted; many villagers also found the day-to-day contact with the state much as it had been.

Why does the pattern matter? The Republic combined a break in the source of authority with continuity in the machinery of government, which helps explain both its stability and the persistence of colonial-era practices.

Division III

Secularism, Fundamental Rights, and the Social Purposes of the Republic

§§88.9–88.12 · 1947–1951

Section 88.9

Secularism: Religion, Citizenship, and the Constitutional State

No question was more charged in 1947–1950 than the place of religion in the new state. Partition had been defended by its proponents as a means of protecting a religious minority, and it had been accompanied by massacre and displacement (Chapter 86). The Constitution’s answer was to define citizenship without reference to religion, to guarantee religious freedom, and to prohibit discrimination on grounds of religion, while not erecting a wall between the state and religious life. The word “secular” did not appear in the Constitution’s original text. This point needs to be stated with care. In November 1948, K. T. Shah proposed to describe India in Article 1 as a “secular, federal, socialist” Union; the Assembly did not accept the amendment, and Ambedkar and others said that such matters should be left to the people and their legislatures. A proposal to begin the Constitution “in the name of God” was also defeated. The original Preamble thus described India as a sovereign democratic republic. The words “socialist” and “secular” were added by the Forty-second Amendment in 1976, an event discussed in Chapter 93. That later addition did not mean that the original Constitution lacked secular protections; it meant that these were found in specific articles rather than in a declared label.

The principal provisions were these. Citizenship (Articles 5–11) was defined by domicile, birth, or descent, and by rules for those who had migrated across the new border: those who went to Pakistan after March 1947 generally lost the claim unless they returned under a permit system. Article 14 guaranteed equality before the law, and Article 15 prohibited discrimination by the state on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Article 16 guaranteed equality of opportunity in public employment. Article 25 guaranteed freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, and health. At the same time, it allowed the state to regulate economic, financial, political, or other secular activity associated with religious practice, to provide for social welfare and reform, and to throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus; an explanation treated Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists as included in references to Hindus for that purpose, and recognized the carrying of the kirpan as part of the Sikh profession of religion. Article 26 allowed religious denominations to manage their own religious affairs; Article 27 barred compulsory taxation for the promotion of a particular religion; Article 28 restricted religious instruction in state-funded institutions. Articles 29 and 30 protected the cultural and educational rights of minorities, including the right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.

India’s approach thus differed from strict separation on the American model. It permitted the state to reform religious institutions and practices in the name of social welfare (Article 25(2)), as in temple entry for those formerly barred, while also protecting minority educational autonomy. Scholars such as Rajeev Bhargava have described this as a model of principled distance, in which the state engages with religion selectively, neither establishing it nor excluding it from public life. It also differed from a model of establishment: no religion was designated a state religion. The Constitution did, however, retain personal laws, those governing marriage, inheritance, and the family, which varied by religious community. A Directive Principle (Article 44) said that the state should endeavour to secure a uniform civil code. When the Assembly debated this in November 1948, some Muslim members, among them Mohammad Ismail and Naziruddin Ahmad, argued that personal law should be exempt from state legislation, while others, including Ambedkar, replied that the Constitution did not require an immediate uniform code and that legislation on personal law was already common. The compromise left an issue that would recur (Chapters 93, 99, and 107).

The tension between reform and religious freedom was apparent immediately. The state’s power to reform Hindu institutions was exercised first through measures on temple entry and, later, the Hindu Code Bills (Chapter 89). Within the Congress, views differed: Nehru was a committed advocate of a secular state; Patel was more cautious but insisted on non-discrimination and was equally firm about the protection of minorities; Prasad, as President, attended the Somnath ceremony and had a more public religiosity; others, such as Maulana Azad, spoke for a composite nationalism. Meanwhile, Hindu nationalist organizations, including the Hindu Mahasabha and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, argued for a different understanding of the nation. The RSS had been banned after Gandhi’s assassination, and the ban was lifted in July 1949 on conditions; the Jana Sangh, founded in 1951, would bring a version of this outlook into electoral politics.

In practice, the experience of minorities was uneven. The Constitution protected them in law; the state generally maintained order after the violence of 1947–1948; and Muslim, Christian, Sikh, and other communities took part in the elections of 1951–1952. Yet Muslims in north India faced the consequences of Partition: many elite families had left, communities were frightened, and suspicion of their loyalty was voiced. Questions about displaced property, citizenship, and the permit system affected families who found themselves on different sides of the border. The Nehru–Liaquat Pact of April 1950, which committed both governments to protect minorities, was prompted by renewed violence in Bengal, and Mookerjee’s resignation from the Cabinet over it shows how contested the issue was.

Historical Vocabulary
Secularism

In India, secularism is not simply a translation of the European concept of separating church and state. Its constitutional meaning developed through the articles on equality, non-discrimination, religious freedom, and minority rights, and later through judicial interpretation and the Preamble’s revision in 1976. In the Assembly’s era, speakers used the idea to mean that the state would not discriminate among citizens on religious grounds and would protect freedom of belief. Historians avoid reading the later, contested political meanings of the word back into 1950.

Section 88.10

Fundamental Rights: Equality, Liberty, Religious Freedom, and Constitutional Remedies

Part III of the Constitution, Articles 12–35, set out the Fundamental Rights. Their roots lay in the nationalist movement’s demands: the Nehru Report of 1928 and the Karachi resolution of 1931 had both listed rights, and the Assembly’s Fundamental Rights sub-committee, chaired by J. B. Kripalani, drew on these along with the American Bill of Rights and other models. Article 12 defined the “State” against which the rights operate, including the central and state governments, legislatures, and local authorities. Article 13 declared void any law, past or future, that was inconsistent with or took away these rights.

Equality (Articles 14–18) was the first group. Article 14 guaranteed to every person equality before the law and the equal protection of the laws. Article 15 prohibited discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth in access to shops, restaurants, wells, and public places, while allowing special provisions for women and children. Article 16 provided equality of opportunity in public employment, with an exception allowing reservation for backward classes inadequately represented. Article 17 abolished untouchability (§88.11). Article 18 abolished titles.

Freedoms (Articles 19–22) came next. Article 19 guaranteed to citizens freedom of speech and expression, peaceable assembly, association, movement, residence, property, and any occupation, trade, or business, each subject to reasonable restrictions on stated grounds. Article 20 protected against retrospective punishment, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination. Article 21 stated that no person could be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. The Assembly had at one stage used the language of “due process,” but after B. N. Rau consulted American jurists, including Felix Frankfurter, who warned of the unpredictability of judicial review under that clause, the Assembly adopted “procedure established by law,” which proved decisive in the Gopalan case. Article 22 provided safeguards on arrest, including the right to be informed of the grounds and to consult a lawyer, but allowed preventive detention under laws made by Parliament, subject to limits. The Preventive Detention Act, passed in February 1950, used that power, a striking choice in a constitution that had just guaranteed liberty. Ambedkar presented Article 22 as a compromise; critics said it nullified the liberty it described.

Other rights addressed forms of exploitation. Articles 23–24 prohibited traffic in human beings and forced labour, and child labour in factories and mines. Articles 25–28 secured religious freedom (§88.9), and Articles 29–30 protected the cultural and educational rights of minorities. Article 31 dealt with property, protecting individuals against deprivation of property except by authority of law and providing for compensation in compulsory acquisition; Article 19(1)(f) guaranteed the right to acquire and hold property. These provisions were removed from the chapter on Fundamental Rights in 1978. Students should not assume that the 2026 text of Part III is the same as that of 1950: the property right, in particular, was a major subject of dispute in the early years, and it was altered repeatedly. The final group, Article 32, gave the right to constitutional remedies, and Articles 33–35 dealt with the application of rights to the armed forces and with martial law.

Two kinds of provision should be distinguished. Many rights protect individuals against state action: free speech, protection against arbitrary arrest, religious freedom. Others address social structures. Article 15(2) and Article 17 apply to private conduct by prohibiting exclusion from public spaces and untouchability, and Article 23 bars forced labour whoever imposes it. These provisions show that the framers recognized that tyranny in India could come from society as well as from the state. But the rights also embodied limits. The restrictions allowed under Article 19, the carve-out for preventive detention, and the express protection of property meant that rights could be restricted or could obstruct reform, as the next sections show.

Finally, rights in a country of low literacy and limited legal aid depended on who could reach a court. The early cases were brought by newspaper owners, traders, landlords, and activists who knew the system; the person who benefited most from a formal remedy was the one who could afford it. Historians therefore evaluate the Fundamental Rights both as constitutional commitments and as claims that took time to become effective for the poor.

Section 88.11

Caste, Social Equality, and the Constitutional Abolition of Untouchability

Article 17 declared that “Untouchability” was abolished and its practice in any form forbidden, and that enforcing any disability arising from it was an offence punishable by law. The article was remarkable in two ways. It named the practice outright, and it applied to society as well as to the state. Parliament enacted a statute giving effect to it only later, in 1955 (Chapter 89). The Constitution’s other provisions on caste included Article 15(2) on access to public places, Article 16(2) and (4) on employment, Article 23 on forced labour, Article 25(2)(b) on temple entry, Article 46, which directed the state to promote the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections and Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and Articles 330–342 on reserved representation and the identification of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

The treatment of representation grew out of the earlier history. Under the Communal Award of 1932, the British had proposed separate electorates for the “Depressed Classes”; Gandhi’s fast in Yerwada prison produced the Poona Pact between Gandhi and Ambedkar, which replaced separate electorates with a larger number of reserved seats within general electorates (Chapter 81). The Constituent Assembly took the Poona Pact approach. Seats were reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the House of the People and the state assemblies, to be filled by election in common electorates (that is, all voters in a reserved constituency, whatever their caste, voted), for an initial period of ten years (Article 334). Separate electorates for religious minorities, abolished in 1949, were not reintroduced. The difference between reserved representation within a common electorate and a separate communal electorate was substantial: in the first, candidates had to seek votes from the whole electorate; in the second, only members of the group voted for its representatives. Ambedkar, who had in earlier years pressed for separate electorates, accepted the joint-electorate formula in the Assembly.

The identification of Scheduled Castes was left to presidential orders under Articles 341 and 342. The Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order of 1950 listed castes by state and, in its original form, limited Scheduled Caste status to persons professing the Hindu religion; Sikhs were included in 1956 and Buddhists in 1990. Because many Dalits had converted to Christianity or Islam to escape caste, this restriction left them outside the benefits, a limitation that scholars have criticized. The Constitution also provided for the special treatment of tribal areas through the Fifth and Sixth Schedules, and for the reservation of a proportion of public posts, subject to administrative efficiency (Article 335). Dalit and Adivasi members of the Assembly, such as Dakshayani Velayudhan, Jagjivan Ram, and Jaipal Singh Munda, argued for these protections.

Ambedkar’s distinctive concern was that legal citizenship would coexist with social domination. He believed that caste was not merely a form of discrimination but a hierarchy that structured control of land, labour, education, and dignity, and that a constitution could abolish its legal sanction without ending its social power. His closing speech warned that equality in politics would sit uneasily with inequality in social and economic life (§88.3). Gandhi, who had called untouchables Harijans and campaigned for temple entry, saw the evil as a moral wrong to be remedied by change of heart within Hindu society; Ambedkar rejected that frame, and in 1956 he would lead a mass conversion to Buddhism (Chapter 94). The Constitution reflected both traditions: a legal prohibition and a commitment to representation, but it did not undertake an economic redistribution.

It is important to avoid an anachronistic reading. The constitutional abolition of untouchability did not automatically end it. In many villages, access to wells, temples, and tea shops remained restricted; Dalit labourers faced bonded labour, social boycott, and violence when they asserted claims; and the state’s own officials shared the prejudices of the society. The Constitution created a standard to which Dalits could appeal, and the standard became an instrument in later struggles, but it was not self-executing. The implementation of reservation in education and employment, of land reform, and of anti-discrimination legislation belongs to Chapter 89, and the later politics of caste (the Mandal Commission, OBC reservations, and Dalit political movements) to Chapters 94, 99, and 107.

Consider an agricultural labourer in a village in the 1950s whose family belongs to a Scheduled Caste. In January 1950 the Constitution declared untouchability abolished. In his village, however, he might still be required to draw water from a separate well, eat apart at a village feast, or sit on the ground in front of landowning castes; he might owe a debt to a landlord that bound him to labour at low wages. If he went to the police to complain of an insult, the officer might belong to the same dominant community as the offender.

The Constitution gave him a claim and a language of rights, but not an immediate remedy. A court case was costly; a political party or a Dalit organization such as the Scheduled Castes Federation, with its limited reach, was a possible ally. The distance between the promise and the practice illustrates Ambedkar’s concern, and the historian’s evidence for it comes from official reports, memoirs, and later oral histories rather than from the constitutional text.

Section 88.12

Directive Principles, Constitutional Compromise, and the First Amendment of 1951

Part IV of the Constitution, Articles 36–51, contained the Directive Principles of State Policy. Modelled in part on the Irish Constitution, they set out goals that the state should pursue but made them, in Article 37, not enforceable by any court. They were nonetheless declared fundamental in the governance of the country, and the state was to apply them in making laws. The list was wide. The state was to strive to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order informed by justice (Article 38); to direct policy toward an adequate means of livelihood for all, an equitable distribution of material resources, and the avoidance of a concentration of wealth (Article 39); to organize village panchayats (Article 40, the Gandhian element); to secure the right to work, education, and public assistance (Article 41); to provide humane conditions of work and maternity relief (Article 42); a living wage (Article 43); a uniform civil code (Article 44); free and compulsory education for children up to the age of fourteen within ten years (Article 45); protection of the weaker sections (Article 46); improvement of nutrition and public health, and the prohibition of intoxicating drinks (Article 47); the organization of agriculture and animal husbandry, including prevention of the slaughter of cows and calves (Article 48); separation of the judiciary from the executive (Article 50); and the promotion of international peace (Article 51).

The relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles reflected the Assembly’s compromise between liberal constitutionalism and social democracy. Fundamental Rights were justiciable, that is, enforceable in courts, and operated as limits on state power. Directive Principles were not, because the framers believed that the state’s resources were limited, that elected governments should decide priorities, and that courts should not order a government to implement a program. Yet in an undeveloped country with a large poor population, the framers were reluctant to omit the social commitments. Granville Austin, in an influential study, described the Constitution’s aims as a “seamless web” of national unity, democracy, and social revolution, and the Directive Principles were the clearest textual expression of the last. Critics, from the left, argued that unenforceable rights were hollow; critics from the right argued that they invited governmental interference with property.

The tension came to a head in the spring of 1951. Several developments coincided. The Madras High Court and then the Supreme Court in Champakam Dorairajan had struck down caste-based quotas in educational admissions, holding that Directive Principles could not override Fundamental Rights. The Patna High Court had struck down Bihar’s Land Reforms Act. The Supreme Court’s decision in Romesh Thappar had narrowed the government’s power to restrict speech. For a government committed to abolishing zamindari, supporting backward classes, and preserving public order, these decisions seemed to block its programme.

Nehru introduced the Constitution (First Amendment) Bill on 10 May 1951, and it became law in June 1951. Its main changes were these. It recast the limits on freedom of speech and expression by adding grounds such as public order, friendly relations with foreign states, and incitement to an offence, and it made restrictions on speech subject to a test of reasonableness. It added Article 15(4), allowing the state to make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, a direct response to Champakam. It added Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule, which protected laws for the acquisition of estates and a listed group of land-reform statutes from being challenged on the ground that they violated Fundamental Rights; the schedule initially listed thirteen Acts. It also amended the provisions on state trading. The Statement of Objects and Reasons from the Legislative Department sets out the government’s case.

The amendment was passed by the Provisional Parliament, not an elected one, and was strongly opposed. Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, H. N. Kunzru, and Kripalani objected to the restrictions on speech and to the amending of rights by a body without a fresh popular mandate. Nehru defended the amendment as a necessary correction, arguing that a constitution should be capable of adapting to meet social needs. The episode is revealing for several reasons: it showed that a government with a dominant majority could change the Constitution quickly; that courts and legislature could clash over the meaning of rights; that the property right was a real barrier to agrarian reform; and that the Constitution’s amending procedure was flexible. It also meant that, from 1951, the structure of rights was open to alteration in the interest of reform, which became the subject of decades of constitutional dispute. Chapter 89 explains the legislation the amendment protected.

Historical Debate 2

Could Constitutional Rights Produce Social Equality without Structural Reform?

The question: Could the guarantees of liberty and equality in the Constitution by themselves produce a more equal society, or did they require structural reforms of land, caste, and economic power?

Interpretation A — Rights as the foundation of transformation: A written guarantee of equality, an independent judiciary, and a remedy such as Article 32 gave marginalized groups a legal language and institutions with which to press claims. Historians such as Rohit De have shown how ordinary citizens used constitutional rights in the courts from the first years. On this view, rights created the conditions for gradual reform, and the amendments of 1951 show that the system could adapt.
Interpretation B — Rights without structural change: Ambedkar himself warned that political equality would exist alongside social and economic inequality, and that democratic institutions might be undermined unless the latter was addressed. The justiciable rights favoured liberty and property; the social goals were non-enforceable; and caste hierarchy and landholding structures remained powerful. On this reading, the Constitution offered an authorization for reform rather than reform itself.

Evidence: The text of Parts III and IV; Ambedkar’s speeches; early cases on property, speech, and reservation; the First Amendment; accounts of village life; the later record of land reform and caste violence (Chapters 89 and 94).

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians evaluate the Constitution as an instrument of social change, and it should not be reduced to blame on one leader. Nehru, Ambedkar, Patel, the courts, and the state legislatures each had a role.

Division IV

Universal Adult Franchise and the Creation of Electoral Democracy

§§88.13–88.16 · 1949–1952

Section 88.13

Universal Adult Franchise: Political Equality in a Deeply Unequal Society

Under British rule, the vote had always been a privilege. The reforms of 1909, 1919, and 1935 had successively widened the electorate, but always on the basis of property, tax payment, or education, and always with separate electorates dividing voters by religion and other categories. The 1935 Act enfranchised about thirty million people in British India, roughly one in ten of the population, and the elections of 1937 and 1945–1946 were conducted on that basis. In the princely states, which covered about a third of the subcontinent’s territory, there were almost no elections of this kind. A constitutional commitment to every adult citizen’s right to vote was, therefore, a break with the colonial past.

It also fulfilled a nationalist commitment. The Nehru Report of 1928 had endorsed adult suffrage, and the Karachi Congress of 1931 had promised it in its resolution on fundamental rights. When the Constituent Assembly took up the question, the dominant view, expressed by members across parties, was that independence meant political equality, and that it would be inconsistent to continue to treat illiterate peasants, women, and the poor as unfit to vote. The Assembly debated whether the franchise should be limited by literacy, property, or education, and a few members expressed doubts about the preparedness of the electorate. The Assembly overwhelmingly rejected restrictions. Article 326 provided that elections to the House of the People and the state assemblies would be on the basis of adult suffrage: every citizen not less than 21 years of age, and not otherwise disqualified by non-residence, unsoundness of mind, crime, or corrupt or illegal practice, would be entitled to be registered as a voter. Article 325 added that no person would be ineligible for inclusion in the electoral roll, or claim inclusion in a special roll, on grounds of religion, race, caste, or sex. The original voting age was 21; it was lowered to 18 only in 1989 by the Sixty-first Amendment, and students must not apply the modern age to the 1950s.

Three features distinguished India’s decision. Universal adult suffrage was introduced at the time of independence rather than being extended in stages, as it had been in Britain, where women had gained equal franchise in 1928, or in France, where women voted from 1944. It covered women on the same terms as men, and it covered groups previously excluded, including Dalits, tribal communities, and the landless. And it was introduced when the country’s literacy rate, according to the 1951 census, was only about 18 percent (around 9 percent for women), and when per capita income was among the lowest in the world. It was therefore a bet on citizens’ capacity, and some outside observers regarded it as an act of reckless idealism.

The concept has distinct elements that students should keep separate. Citizenship was a legal status defined by Articles 5–11. Eligibility to vote depended on citizenship, age, residence, and the absence of disqualification. Enrolment required that a person’s name appear on a compiled electoral roll, a bureaucratic act dependent on enumerators, record-keeping, and, in the case of women, on whether they would give their own names. Participation meant the act of voting, shaped by distance, fear, information, and local power. The constitutional guarantee dealt with the first two. The next sections show how the Election Commission addressed the third, and how the fourth turned out.

The central argument is that India granted voting rights on an egalitarian constitutional basis before the achievement of widespread literacy or socioeconomic equality. This shifted the logic of political authority: the legitimacy of governments would derive from the votes of poor and illiterate citizens, even if power in the village and in the party organizations remained unequally distributed. Scholars have debated whether the early franchise was transformative or merely formal. It is better to say that it created a framework within which previously excluded groups could, over time, organize politically, as the later politics of caste and region would show (Chapters 94 and 99).

Cause and Effect

The Decision for Universal Adult Franchise

Long-term causes

Nationalist commitments from the Nehru Report and Karachi resolution; the colonial franchise’s narrowness and communal basis; mass mobilization from the 1920s; the principle that sovereignty belongs to the people.

Short-term causes

The need to legitimize an indirectly chosen Assembly; Partition, which made distinctions between communities politically dangerous; the Congress leadership’s belief that exclusion would be hard to defend.

Event

Adoption of Articles 325–326 and the commencement of the Constitution in 1950.

Immediate effects

The need to enrol some 170 million electors; creation of the Election Commission; planning of the 1951–1952 election.

Long-term consequences: A mass electorate that made elections the main source of political authority and gradually shifted attention to groups previously on the margins. Historical complexity: The decision also depended on assumptions about Congress’s own electoral strength and on a conviction, shared across much of the political class, that adult franchise was a natural consequence of national independence; it was not a single leader’s act.

Section 88.14

The Election Commission: Electoral Rolls, Constituencies, Ballots, and Administrative Capacity

Article 324 vested “the superintendence, direction and control” of electoral rolls and elections in an Election Commission, and the Commission was established on 25 January 1950, the day before the Constitution fully came into force (later observed as National Voters’ Day). The Commission’s independence was meant to protect elections from the control of the party in office: its members were to be appointed by the President, and the Chief Election Commissioner could not be removed except in the way a Supreme Court judge could. Until 1989 the Commission had a single member. The first Chief Election Commissioner, appointed in March 1950, was Sukumar Sen, a senior civil servant who had been Chief Secretary of West Bengal; he would hold the post until 1958 and later advised on elections in Sudan.

The practical task was daunting. Voter lists had to be prepared for a population of over 360 million, spread across about three million square kilometres of varied terrain, with no existing record of adults’ names and ages in most of the country. Rolls had to be drawn up house by house by enumerators who often had to explain the exercise to people who had never heard of it. Literacy was low; transport was limited; the territories of the former princely states had little electoral machinery; and the disruption of Partition meant that millions of refugees had to be registered in new places, which required special arrangements. Voters could not simply sign for ballots, so the Commission devised a system in which each candidate had a separate ballot box, marked with the candidate’s party or independent symbol, such as a pair of bullocks for the Congress or a hut for the Socialists, and voters would drop a blank paper into the box of their choice. This avoided the need to read names.

Two statutes provided the legal framework. The Representation of the People Act of 1950, passed by the Provisional Parliament, dealt with the allocation of seats, the delimitation of constituencies, the qualifications of voters, and the preparation of rolls. The Representation of the People Act of 1951 dealt with the conduct of elections, the qualifications and disqualifications of candidates, corrupt practices, and the machinery for deciding election disputes. The constituencies for the first election were drawn on the basis of the 1950–1951 legislation and the Commission’s orders, in consultation with the state governments. The 1951 election used 401 parliamentary constituencies to fill 489 seats: most were single-member, but 86 elected two members, one from the general category and one reserved for the Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes, and one elected three. The system was meant to ensure reserved representation while preserving a common electorate, as discussed in §88.11.

Women’s registration illustrates the difficulty of establishing individual political identity. In many parts of the country, women did not give their own names to enumerators; they were recorded as the wife of, or mother of, a named man. Sen regarded this as a violation of the principle of individual franchise and ordered the enumerators to return and record women’s own names; where women refused, their entries were in many places removed. According to Chief Election Commissioner Sukumar Sen’s contemporary estimate, nearly 2.8 million women ultimately failed to provide individual names and their entries were deleted from the electoral rolls. This was a registration exclusion, not a statutory denial of women’s voting rights. The episode shows both the Commission’s insistence on formal equality and the practical resistance of social custom, and it was widely discussed in the press at the time.

Preparing a national election also required mobilizing a vast temporary workforce. The Election Commission’s contemporary report counted 132,560 polling stations containing 196,084 polling booths; some later Commission summaries label the latter total as polling stations. These are distinct counting conventions, not evidence of more than 200,000 physical station sites. The operation also required large numbers of steel ballot boxes and several hundred thousand polling officers, many of them teachers and clerks, together with police. The Commission conducted publicity through radio, film, and posters to explain the process. The first election was staged over about four months, so that officials and security forces could be moved from one region to another. These arrangements suggest that the Election Commission’s achievement was administrative as much as political: it created the capacity for a mass election by a state that had previously rarely dealt directly with most of its citizens in this way. Its independence from party direction, protected by the Constitution and exercised by Sen, became one of the central institutions of the Republic.

Section 88.15

India’s First General Elections, 1951–1952: The Democratic Experiment

The first nationwide elections to the House of the People and the state legislative assemblies were held over the winter of 1951–1952. The first votes were cast on 25 October 1951 at Chini in the Himachal Pradesh hills, where polling was held early because the area would be inaccessible once the snows came; the main polling took place in January and February 1952, and the exercise ended in the latter month. The Election Commission’s records list about 173.2 million registered electors, the largest electorate assembled to that date. The elections filled 489 seats in the Lok Sabha and, simultaneously, about 3,200 seats in the state assemblies; more than fifty parties contested, along with large numbers of independent candidates. Jammu and Kashmir, with its own constitutional arrangements, was not part of the general election in the same form; the state elected a Constituent Assembly of its own in 1951.

The campaign reflected the scale and diversity of the country. Nehru, as Congress president and Prime Minister, travelled extensively, by air, car, train, and on foot, and by commonly cited estimates addressed millions of people, often introducing basic ideas of voting to audiences who had never seen a ballot. The Congress manifesto emphasized planning, secular citizenship, and social reform. Other parties campaigned on their own platforms: the Socialist Party on economic redistribution, the Communists on land and workers’ rights, the Jana Sangh on a cultural nationalism and a strong state, the Scheduled Castes Federation on Dalit rights, and regional parties on local and linguistic demands. In some regions, caste, kinship networks, and the influence of former princely rulers or large landowners shaped voting; in others, party organization mattered.

The polling arrangements had to adapt to conditions: boats in the backwaters of Travancore-Cochin, camels and elephants in remote areas, special measures in hilly and forested regions. The Commission’s system of separate boxes meant that the voter’s choice was secret even if the voter could not read. Violence was limited relative to the fears that had been expressed, though there were incidents and disputes, and the election petitions that followed went before tribunals established by the 1951 Act. Observers noted the queues of voters, including many women who came in groups, and the generally orderly conduct.

The Election Commission’s historical turnout series records participation at about 45.7 percent of registered electors; this voter-participation measure must not be equated with the larger sum of candidate-votes across multi-member constituencies. Statistics of participation by gender, caste, or region were not systematically recorded in the first election; the scholarly estimates that exist suggest that women’s turnout was lower than men’s, that participation varied greatly among states, and that rural voters came out in large numbers. Historians therefore caution against assuming that a single voting experience represented all communities. A woman in a village in Madras might vote for the first time under her own name, while one in a conservative district might have been omitted from the roll. For Dalits, the reserved constituencies gave the possibility of candidates from their own communities, but their votes were distributed among parties; Ambedkar himself contested a reserved seat in Bombay and lost, as §88.16 shows. The experience of adivasi and forest communities, many of whom lived at a distance from polling stations, was also distinct.

The completion of the elections mattered internationally. Newly independent states in Asia and Africa were watched for signs of whether parliamentary democracy would take root outside its European and North American homes, and some foreign commentators had predicted that a poor, largely illiterate electorate could not manage the exercise. The successful holding of the elections, with a peaceful transfer of seats in many states, strengthened India’s prestige and underwrote Nehru’s claim, in a Cold War context, that a different model of political development from both the Western and the Communist was possible (Chapter 92). Domestically, it conferred on the Republic the popular legitimacy that the indirect Constituent Assembly had lacked. As Sen’s Commission recognized, the election was proof of administrative capacity, and the experience would be repeated in 1957, 1962, and thereafter.

Section 88.16

Electoral Results, Representative Legitimacy, and the First Lok Sabha

The Indian National Congress won 364 of the 489 elected seats in the first Lok Sabha, about 74 percent of the seats, with 44.99 percent of the candidate-votes recorded in the Election Commission’s party returns. This difference is the key to interpreting the result. India used the first-past-the-post system, under which the candidate with the most votes in a constituency wins, regardless of the share. In a field of many parties and independents, a party with a plurality in most constituencies can win a vast majority of seats. Congress’s 364 seats did not represent 364/489 of the recorded candidate-votes: roughly 55 percent of those candidate-votes went to other candidates. Because some constituencies elected more than one member, these party-vote totals cannot be treated as a count of distinct people who voted. The opposition vote was divided among many parties and large numbers of independents, which meant that it translated into few seats.

The distribution of opposition support reveals a pattern. The Socialist Party won about a tenth of the vote but only twelve seats, because its support was spread thinly. The Communist Party of India, banned or restricted in some regions before it abandoned its insurrectionary strategy in 1951, won sixteen seats, concentrated in Madras and in the Telangana area of Hyderabad and in Travancore-Cochin, where Communist-led alliances were strong. Independents, who ran in large numbers, won 37 seats—7.57 percent of the 489 elected seats—although they received 15.90 percent of the recorded candidate-votes in the official party summary. The Jana Sangh won three seats, including that of Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, and Ambedkar’s Scheduled Castes Federation won only two, with Ambedkar himself defeated in the reserved seat in Bombay by a Congress candidate who had previously been his associate. These results demonstrate that even where Congress dominated, opposition forces existed and could win seats in particular regions.

The state results were more varied than the national total. Congress did not win a majority in several states, including Madras, Travancore-Cochin, and PEPSU, and in some of these the formation of governments required coalitions or the support of independents; in Madras, C. Rajagopalachari became Chief Minister after the election by assembling a majority. This variety is important because it shows that the dominance of Congress was not uniform: it was strongest in the Hindi heartland and in much of the west and south, and weaker at the margins.

Nehru was elected from Phulpur in Uttar Pradesh, the result of a campaign in which the Congress contested through his prestige. The first elected Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha convened on 13 May 1952; G. V. Mavalankar was elected Speaker, Nehru led the government, and Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, as Vice-President, chaired the upper house. Rajendra Prasad was elected President by the new electoral college. Because no opposition party held enough seats to qualify, the Lok Sabha did not at first have a formally recognized Leader of the Opposition, although A. K. Gopalan of the Communist Party was the most prominent opposition parliamentary voice. The Provisional Parliament, with its unelected members, was replaced by a body with a direct popular mandate.

The conclusion for historians is twofold. First, the elections provided the Republic with democratic legitimacy: citizens had voted in great numbers, under their own constitution, in elections administered by an independent commission. Second, legitimacy was not the same as balanced competition. Under first-past-the-post, a party with 45 percent of the votes could hold three-quarters of the seats, which magnified Congress’s advantage and weakened the fragmented opposition’s ability to scrutinize government. The political scientist Rajni Kothari later described this pattern as the “Congress system,” in which opposition operated mainly through factions inside the dominant party and through small parties outside it. The question of how to describe an electoral system that was open but uncompetitive in effect is taken up in Division V.

Section 88.17

Congress Dominance: Organization, Electoral Authority, and the National Movement’s Legacy

The Indian National Congress entered independence as the most powerful political organization in the country. Founded in 1885 and transformed by Gandhi into a mass movement (Chapter 79), it had run provincial governments in 1937–1939, led the struggles of 1920–1922, 1930–1934, and 1942, and spoken for the nationalist movement in negotiations with the British. Its leaders were household names; its organization reached from provincial committees to district and village units; and its claim to have won independence gave it enormous prestige. The challenge in 1947 was to turn a movement that had united many interests against a colonial ruler into a party of government.

There was an early question about whether it should do so at all. In January 1948, shortly before his death, Gandhi drafted a proposal to dissolve the Congress as a political party and reconstitute it as a body for social service, the Lok Sevak Sangh. The leadership did not adopt it. Instead, the party reorganized itself as the governing party and, in 1948, adopted a constitution that limited organized groups within it; the Congress Socialist Party left to form the Socialist Party. Congress presidents in these years included J. B. Kripalani, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, and Purushottam Das Tandon, who defeated Kripalani in 1950 with Patel’s backing. Tandon’s socially conservative and Hindu-traditionalist leanings clashed with Nehru’s, and in September 1951, under pressure from Nehru, he resigned, and Nehru himself became Congress president while remaining Prime Minister. The combination of the two roles gave Nehru both governmental and organizational authority and marked a shift in the party’s balance after the death of Patel in December 1950.

The Congress’s strength lay in several features. It had a widely recognized leadership with national prestige. Its organizational network, from the Pradesh Congress Committees to local units, gave it capacity to campaign across the country. It had a broad social coalition: upper-caste elites, landowners, merchants and industrialists, peasants, Dalits and Muslims in certain regions, workers in the Indian National Trade Union Congress, and women, who had participated in the nationalist movement. It also accommodated competing ideologies, from Gandhian constructive workers and religious traditionalists to socialists, liberals, and conservatives. This internal diversity meant that many political disagreements took place inside the party, a feature that later political scientists described as the party’s capacity to absorb opposition. State and local Congress leaders, such as Pant in Uttar Pradesh, B. C. Roy in West Bengal, and Ravishankar Shukla in Madhya Pradesh, had their own bases of power and were not merely agents of the centre.

The advantages of dominant-party government were considerable. It provided administrative continuity in a period of crisis, stability for the new institutions, and the capacity to pass reforms. The party’s leaders had experience of governing in 1937. Yet the dangers were also real. A party that won most elections could treat the state as its own and could hold power without much fear of defeat; patronage and factional struggles within the party could replace competition between parties; and opposition could be weak and reduced to a protest role. Critics from the left and the right noted that the party’s dominance made it difficult to hold governments to account.

Congress dominance should not, however, be equated with the absence of democratic competition. The elections were open; opposition parties contested and sometimes won; Congress lost control of several states in the first election; and factional struggles inside the party were intense and sometimes decided policy. The “Congress system,” as Kothari described it in the 1960s, meant a dominant party surrounded by opposition parties that acted as pressure groups and by factions within the Congress that acted as the opposition. This analytical description was later challenged by those who argued that it understated the conflicts among parties. For the early 1950s, it is a useful label for a feature of the Republic, but one that historians should test against the evidence.

Section 88.18

Opposition Parties, Social Movements, and Political Pluralism

The opposition in the early Republic was diverse. The Socialist Party, formed after the Congress Socialists left the Congress in 1948, was led by Acharya Narendra Dev, Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, and Asoka Mehta. It criticized the Congress for conservatism on land, wages, and economic inequality and advocated democratic socialism. The Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party was founded in 1951 by Kripalani, who had left the Congress, and was joined by others; in 1952 it merged with the Socialists to form the Praja Socialist Party. The Communist Party of India, which in 1948–1950 had followed an insurrectionary line and supported the Telangana peasant uprising against the Nizam’s regime and the Indian state, was banned in some areas and had many of its leaders imprisoned; in 1951 it changed course and decided to contest elections. In the first Lok Sabha it was the largest opposition group.

The Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded on 21 October 1951 by Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, who had left Nehru’s Cabinet after the Nehru–Liaquat Pact of 1950, drew on the organizational support of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and advocated a nation defined by Hindu cultural identity, a strong central state, and a firm stance toward Pakistan. The Hindu Mahasabha, discredited by association with Gandhi’s assassination (although V. D. Savarkar was acquitted in 1949), and the Ram Rajya Parishad also appealed to traditionalist Hindu sentiment. The Scheduled Castes Federation, led by Ambedkar, represented Dalit interests, though its electoral showing was weak. In Tamil-speaking areas the Dravidian movement, whose organizations included the Dravida Kazhagam and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (founded 1949), challenged north Indian and Brahmin dominance, though the latter did not contest the 1952 election. The Akali Dal in Punjab, the Jharkhand Party among tribal communities in Chota Nagpur, and the Ganatantra Parishad in Orissa represented regional or community interests. Large numbers of independents ran as well.

These organizations challenged Congress on several axes: economic policy and the pace of land reform; caste and the position of Dalits; the relationship of religion and the state; civil liberties, including preventive detention and the First Amendment; the treatment of princes and landlords; and the shape of the federal system. They took part in legislative debate, as in the Provisional Parliament; in elections, where they won seats in particular regions; and in social movements. Vinoba Bhave’s Bhoodan movement, which began at Pochampalli in Telangana in April 1951 and asked landowners to donate land to the landless, was a Gandhian effort to achieve land redistribution by moral persuasion. Trade unions, divided among the Congress-linked INTUC, the socialist Hind Mazdoor Sabha, and the Communist-led AITUC, organized workers. The press, including newspapers that criticized the government, formed part of the public sphere, though the Press cases of 1950 show governmental unease.

The Congress system created opportunities for disagreement while also placing structural disadvantages on its competitors. Opposition parties lacked the Congress’s national prestige, its organization, its access to the resources of government, and, often, its capacity to reach rural voters through local leaders. First-past-the-post magnified the effect of division among them. They also differed fundamentally with one another, which made cooperation difficult: the Socialists and Communists distrusted each other; the Jana Sangh and the Communists stood at opposite ends; and Ambedkar’s federation was separate from all. Even so, the opposition made use of every available forum, and Nehru, who often respected debate and responded to criticism, took opposition speakers seriously.

The overall picture is of a politically pluralistic early Republic despite unequal party strength. The range of parties, the vitality of debate, and the existence of regional strongholds meant that politics was not a one-party monopoly. It was also a system in which, as §88.16 showed, pluralism in outlook did not translate into balanced competition for office.

Section 88.19

Nehru, Constitutional Norms, and the Early Republic’s Political Contradictions

Jawaharlal Nehru, Prime Minister from August 1947, played a central role in establishing the conventions of the new system. He attended Parliament regularly, answered questions, and treated the legislature as the forum in which government justified itself. He conducted Cabinet government through committees and with a considerable measure of consultation, though he was by far the dominant figure. From 1947 he wrote fortnightly letters to the chief ministers, setting out his views on national and international questions, a practice that served both as instruction and as a means of maintaining national leadership across states. He used the platform of the Prime Minister’s office to make the case that India could be a democracy, secular in citizenship, committed to planning, and independent in foreign affairs, and he made that identity internationally recognizable (Chapter 92).

His constitutional relationships were complex. With Prasad, as noted in §88.5, he disagreed over the President’s role, Somnath, and the Hindu Code. With Patel, he differed on temperament and policy, though they cooperated closely on integration and administration until Patel’s death; the old picture of a permanent rivalry has been qualified by historians who note their shared commitment to a united India. With Ambedkar, he worked on the Law Ministry and the Constitution, but Ambedkar resigned in September 1951 in frustration at the delay of the Hindu Code Bill (Chapter 89). With state governments he exercised influence through party and through the letters. With the judiciary he showed respect for judicial independence but pushed through the First Amendment when he believed courts were obstructing reform.

The early Republic’s contradictions are evident in these relationships. The first was between centralization and federalism: the Constitution gave the Union great powers, Congress controlled most states, and yet state leaders had their own bases. The second was between civil liberty and public order: the same Parliament that enacted guarantees of freedom passed the Preventive Detention Act, imposed restrictions on speech, and used Article 356 in Punjab in 1951. The third was between representative democracy and inherited social hierarchy, which the first elections, the reserved constituencies, and the continued power of landlords and dominant castes all illustrated. The fourth was between constitutional continuity and reform: the Republic kept the colonial civil service (now the Indian Administrative Service and other all-India services, which the Constitution recognized in Article 312), the police, and the legal codes, and employed them to carry out a program of reform that many of their members had little sympathy for.

The term “Nehruvian settlement” requires careful use. It is an analytical description applied by later scholars to a cluster of arrangements and commitments that emerged in the early years: parliamentary democracy and civil liberties within a constitutional framework; secular citizenship; a strong Union with a federal structure; a mixed economy with state planning (Chapter 89); non-alignment in foreign policy (Chapter 92); and Congress predominance. It was not a single written agreement, and not all its elements were Nehru’s alone. Others, including Patel, Ambedkar, Prasad, and Azad, made their own contributions, and some features, like the dominance of Congress or the continuity of the colonial state, were not chosen by Nehru so much as inherited. Nor was the settlement uncontested: it was criticized from the Hindu nationalist right, from the Marxist left, from Gandhians, and from liberals who later formed the Swatantra Party (Chapter 94), and it has been the subject of continuing political and historiographical dispute.

Section 88.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: The Foundations and Limits of India’s Constitutional Democracy

This chapter has traced the movement from a Constituent Assembly that met in December 1946 under colonial rule to a Republic that held the largest democratic election the world had seen by 1952. Its themes converge on a set of institutions and a set of tensions. The Assembly produced a Constitution through negotiation, committees, public drafts, and extended debate, with Ambedkar, Rau, Prasad, Nehru, Patel, and many others contributing. The document established parliamentary government, a bicameral legislature, a strong Union with federal features, an independent judiciary with powers of review, a distinctive approach to religious freedom and minority rights, a chapter of enforceable fundamental rights, a statement of non-enforceable social aims, constitutional measures against untouchability, and universal adult franchise administered by an independent Election Commission. The first general elections gave the system popular legitimacy, and Congress dominance and the opposition’s pluralism shaped its operation.

Why did Indian leaders commit to mass electoral democracy despite Partition, poverty, low literacy, and severe social inequality? Several answers emerge. Nationalist commitments over decades had made representative government and rights a central claim against the colonial state, and to renounce them at independence would have undermined the movement’s own justification. A mass franchise offered a way to bind together a diverse population and to give the new state a legitimacy that did not rest on a single religion, language, or caste. The Congress leadership, confident of its popularity, saw few risks. And the framers viewed universal suffrage as a matter of principle, a recognition of equal citizenship. There were also contrary pressures: concern with order after Partition, distrust of an unlettered electorate, and the wish for a strong state. The outcome was a compromise in which rights and elections coexisted with detention powers, a strong centre, and emergency provisions.

The durability of the institutions was impressive: the Constitution survived, elections recurred, courts continued to hear cases, and the Election Commission established a record of administrative capacity. But tensions were evident from the beginning. Political equality coexisted with entrenched social hierarchy, as Ambedkar warned. A strong Union was balanced against states whose powers could be overridden. Rights to liberty and property were checked by the power of preventive detention and the pressures for reform, leading to the First Amendment. Secular citizenship coexisted with religiously defined personal laws and with continuing suspicion toward minorities. And a dominant party shaped the operation of the system in ways that the constitutional text did not foresee.

The final historical argument of the chapter distinguishes three levels: constitutional intentions, institutional implementation, and actual political outcomes. At the level of intention, the framers aimed to combine liberty, equality, and social justice within a democratic framework. At the level of institutions, they built bodies that could operate (a Parliament, a Supreme Court, an Election Commission) while borrowing from colonial administrative structures. At the level of outcome, the Republic of the early 1950s was a democracy with free elections and an independent judiciary, but also a dominant-party system with strong executive power and limited transformation of the social order. Historians should resist both celebratory and dismissive judgments, and should note that the settlement was the product of particular people in particular circumstances.

Chapter 89 turns from the design of the Republic to its use. It examines how Nehru’s government and the states used the constitutional authorization (the Directive Principles, the Ninth Schedule, Article 15(4), Article 3) to pursue economic planning, land reform, affirmative action, the Hindu Code Bills, and the linguistic reorganization of states, and how far those efforts succeeded.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how India converted independence into a constitutional democracy. It began with the Constituent Assembly, convened on 9 December 1946 under the Cabinet Mission framework and chosen by indirect election through provincial legislatures, with the Muslim League absent and princely-state representation incomplete. Partition reduced and reconstituted the Assembly, and independence made it a sovereign body that also served as the Dominion legislature. Its legitimacy rested on indirect election and on the authority of the nationalist movement, and historians must keep it distinct from the universal-franchise elections of 1951–1952.

The Objectives Resolution of December 1946 and January 1947 stated the Assembly’s guiding aims, and the committee system turned them into detailed proposals. The process was negotiated among competing visions: Nehru’s parliamentary and secular modernism, Ambedkar’s social democracy, Patel’s emphasis on integration and order, Gandhian criticism of the neglect of the village, socialist demands, and minority representatives’ differing strategies. The abandonment of separate electorates in 1947–1949 was one of the key compromises.

Ambedkar’s work as Chairman of the Drafting Committee was central, but the Constitution was the product of many hands, including B. N. Rau, S. N. Mukherjee, other Committee members, and the Assembly as a whole. His closing speech of 25 November 1949 set out the argument that political democracy requires social democracy, and warned of the contradiction between political equality and social and economic inequality.

The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949, signed on 24 January 1950, and brought into force on 26 January 1950, a date tied to the Purna Swaraj pledge of 1930. As enacted it had 395 articles, 22 parts, and eight schedules. Its original Preamble described India as a sovereign democratic republic; the words “socialist” and “secular” were added in 1976. The text borrowed from many sources and retained much of the colonial administrative and legal structure.

The institutions it created were a parliamentary executive with a President as head of state and a Council of Ministers responsible to the House of the People; a bicameral Parliament; a federal system with a strong Union, residuary powers at the centre, and emergency provisions; and an independent Supreme Court with powers of judicial review. The early cases on speech, detention, reservation, and land reform exposed conflicts between legislative reform and judicial protection of rights.

The Constitution’s approach to religion combined citizenship without regard to religion, freedom of conscience, non-discrimination, minority cultural and educational rights, and the state’s authority to reform religious institutions, without separating state from religion on the American model. It retained personal laws and listed a uniform civil code as a Directive Principle. Part III’s Fundamental Rights were enforceable, Part IV’s Directive Principles were not, and the original text protected property as a fundamental right. The First Amendment of 1951 modified speech restrictions, added Article 15(4), and shielded land-reform laws through the Ninth Schedule, in a conflict between a dominant legislative majority and the courts.

Article 17 abolished untouchability, and the Constitution provided reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in joint electorates, but legal equality did not end caste discrimination, as Ambedkar had foreseen. The framework of reform, including affirmative action and land reform, was authorized in the Constitution but belongs, in its implementation, to Chapter 89.

Universal adult franchise, set at age 21, gave all adult citizens the vote at independence, in a society with about 18 percent literacy. The Election Commission, established on 25 January 1950 under Sukumar Sen, prepared rolls for about 173.2 million electors, designed a system of symbols and separate ballot boxes, and ran the first general elections of 1951–1952. Women’s registration revealed the difficulty of establishing individual political identity. Turnout was about 45.7 percent.

Congress won 364 of 489 Lok Sabha seats with about 45 percent of the votes, a disproportion produced by the first-past-the-post system and a fragmented opposition. The results gave the Republic democratic legitimacy but not balanced competition. The opposition parties were diverse and active, and Congress itself was a coalition, but its dominance shaped how the system functioned. “Nehruvian settlement” is an analytical term for a cluster of arrangements, not a single agreement.

The chapter’s central lesson is the distinction between constitutional intentions, institutional implementation, and political outcomes. The Constitution established durable democratic institutions while leaving unresolved questions about social equality, economic transformation, religious pluralism, and the balance of central and state power. Chapter 89 examines how the new state tried to use its authority to transform society and the economy.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Constituent Assembly
The body, first meeting on 9 December 1946, that drafted and adopted the Constitution; indirectly elected and later also the Dominion legislature and Provisional Parliament.
Objectives Resolution
Nehru’s statement of aims, moved 13 December 1946 and adopted 22 January 1947.
Drafting Committee
Committee appointed 29 August 1947, chaired by Ambedkar, that prepared the Draft Constitution.
Constitutional Adviser
Office held by B. N. Rau, who prepared the preliminary draft and comparative research.
Preamble
Introductory statement of purposes; in 1950 it described a sovereign democratic republic, and was amended in 1976.
Union of States
Article 1’s description of India, indicating an indestructible Union rather than a compact among states.
Dominion
Status of India, under the Crown, between August 1947 and January 1950.
Republic
A state whose head is not a hereditary monarch; India became one on 26 January 1950 while remaining in the Commonwealth.
Parliamentary government
System in which the executive is drawn from and responsible to the legislature.
Collective responsibility
Principle that the Council of Ministers answers jointly to the House of the People (Article 75).
Bicameralism
A legislature with two Houses; here the House of the People and the Council of States.
Provisional Parliament
The Constituent Assembly in its legislative role from January 1950 until the elected Parliament met in 1952.
Federalism
Constitutional division of powers between a central government and states, here with a strong Union.
Residuary powers
Powers not listed in the Union, State, or Concurrent Lists, assigned to the Union by Article 248.
President’s Rule
Takeover of a state’s government under Article 356 when constitutional machinery has failed.
Part A, B, C, D states
Original First Schedule categories reflecting British provinces, princely-state unions, centrally administered units, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
Judicial review
Courts’ power to invalidate laws that violate the Constitution, grounded especially in Articles 13, 32, and 226.
Fundamental Rights
Enforceable rights in Part III, including equality, freedoms, religion, minority rights, and constitutional remedies.
Directive Principles
Non-enforceable social and economic goals in Part IV that guide state policy.
Secularism
In the Constitution’s original form, a bundle of protections for religious freedom and non-discrimination, without the term in the Preamble.
Untouchability
Practice of social exclusion of certain castes, abolished by Article 17.
Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes
Groups listed by presidential order for constitutional protections and reserved representation.
Reserved constituency
Constituency in which seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes but all voters take part.
Universal adult franchise
Right of all adult citizens to vote; set at age 21 in 1950.
First-past-the-post
Electoral system in which the candidate with the most votes in a constituency wins.
Electoral roll
Register of eligible voters, prepared under the Election Commission.
Election Commission
Body created under Article 324 on 25 January 1950 to supervise elections.
First Amendment (1951)
Constitutional change that altered speech restrictions, added Article 15(4), and created the Ninth Schedule.
Congress system
Kothari’s term for a pattern of dominant-party politics with opposition operating partly inside the dominant party.
Nehruvian settlement
Analytical term for the political arrangements and commitments of the early Republic.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a country with a very uncertain beginning, a divided population, and great inequality built institutions through which power could be debated, limited, and changed by votes. These institutions shaped nearly everything that follows in Volume VI, from planning and land reform to the Emergency and the rise of coalition politics.

It also shows the value of separating levels of analysis. A constitution states intentions; institutions translate them into practice; politics produces outcomes that no one fully planned. Ambedkar’s warning about the distance between political and social equality remains an enduring question of Indian history, and the first elections demonstrated that a mass electorate could participate while hierarchy persisted.

The chapter connects the end of Volume V to the beginning of Volume VI: the national movement’s promises of rights, franchise, and sovereignty were partly fulfilled in 1950, and the colonial state’s machinery was both inherited and reshaped. Beyond India, the Republic’s experiment was watched across the decolonizing world, and Chapters 90 and 91 show how neighbouring states took different constitutional paths.

Chapter 89 now turns to the use of state power: the Five-Year Plans, land reform, affirmative action, the Hindu Code Bills, and the linguistic reorganization of states, each authorized or constrained by the framework this chapter has described.
