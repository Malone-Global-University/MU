Chapter Opening

Within the span of roughly three years, between 1950 and 1953, the new Indian Republic created a Planning Commission, launched its First Five-Year Plan, passed legislation to abolish landlord intermediaries, opened the first of thousands of Community Development blocks, and was forced by the death of a Telugu activist after a long fast to concede the principle of a linguistic state. None of these steps was inevitable, and none of them did what its authors hoped in the form they first imagined. Together, though, they show what independence was supposed to mean to its leaders: not only the end of foreign rule, but the use of a democratic state to change how a poor agrarian society produced, owned, worshipped, married, and governed itself.

Chapter 88 examined the constitutional framework within which that effort took place: the Constituent Assembly, the Constitution of 1950, parliamentary democracy, federalism, fundamental rights, universal adult franchise, and the political dominance of the Indian National Congress. A constitution, however, is a statement of possibilities. This chapter asks what the governments of the Republic, above all that of Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and those of the states, actually tried to do with those possibilities, and how far their ambitions were realized. Its principal coverage runs from 1950 to Nehru's death in May 1964, with necessary background from 1947–1949 and a limited look forward to the end of the Third Five-Year Plan in 1966.

The inheritance was daunting. India in 1950 was overwhelmingly rural, its agriculture dependent on the monsoon and burdened by unequal landholding, tenancy, and debt. Its industrial base, though not negligible, was thin in capital goods and engineering. Literacy was low, life expectancy short, and the Partition of 1947, examined in Chapter 86, had displaced millions of people, divided irrigation systems and jute and cotton economies, and strained public finances. Colonial rule had left behind railways, a civil service, a legal system, and some modern industry, but also an economy that leaders across the political spectrum regarded as stunted by the priorities of an external power. Debates over the nature of that colonial economic legacy are discussed in Chapters 66, 72, and 76; here what matters is that Indian leaders overwhelmingly believed that political freedom would be hollow without economic transformation.

The chapter's argument unfolds across five divisions. The first examines planning and industrial development: the Planning Commission, the three Five-Year Plans, the mixed economy, and the heavy-industry strategy of the Second Plan. The second turns to agrarian reform, the most direct test of whether a democratic government could alter rural power: the abolition of zamindari, tenancy regulation, land ceilings, the Bhoodan movement, and the Community Development and Panchayati Raj programs. The third examines caste, affirmative action, and the Hindu Code legislation, showing how legal equality met entrenched social hierarchies and family structures. The fourth traces linguistic reorganization, from the Andhra agitation to the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 and the division of Bombay in 1960. The fifth assesses the achievements, contradictions, and competing interpretations of Nehru-era development.

Three connected arguments run through these divisions. First, planned development expanded state capacity and built industrial, scientific, and infrastructural foundations that outlasted the era. Second, redistributive and social reforms collided with entrenched inequalities and with the limits of administrative implementation, so that legal change and social change moved at very different speeds. Third, linguistic reorganization, which Nehru and many Congress leaders initially feared, became an important method of accommodating regional demands within Indian federalism. Different instruments operated through different institutions and met different kinds of resistance, and the chapter's task is to explain why results varied so substantially across sectors, classes, regions, and communities.

The chapter relies on an unusually rich documentary record. Planning Commission publications, parliamentary debates, statutes and amendments, court judgments, commission reports, census and national-accounts statistics, village surveys, newspapers, and the personal papers and speeches of leading figures permit historians to examine both intentions and outcomes. But these sources have characteristic limitations. Official plan documents are statements of aspiration written by planners; contemporary statistics were gathered by an administrative system whose own reach was limited, and have since been revised; and the voices of tenants, laborers, women, and members of Scheduled Castes appear in the record chiefly through the filters of officials, investigators, and reformers. Students should treat targets, statutes, and announcements as evidence of what the state intended, and treat implementation evidence separately.

A final caution concerns hindsight. Later developments, including the Green Revolution of the late 1960s, the Mandal Commission of 1979, the economic liberalization of 1991, and the amendments to Hindu inheritance law in 2005, are discussed in later chapters. They should not be read back into the 1950s. The leaders of the 1950s did not know that a new agricultural technology would arrive in the next decade, or that their reservation policies would be debated in entirely different terms thirty years later. This chapter therefore tries to understand the period on its own terms before assessing it, and it avoids treating Nehruvian development as either an unqualified success or a comprehensive failure.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the economic inheritance of independent India and the intellectual and institutional foundations of planning, including the National Planning Committee, the Bombay Plan, and the Planning Commission.
  2. Trace the priorities, financing, and results of the First, Second, and early Third Five-Year Plans, distinguishing targets from achieved outcomes.
  3. Distinguish the Indian mixed economy from both Soviet-style command planning and laissez-faire capitalism, using the Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956.
  4. Analyze the Mahalanobis strategy of heavy industrialization and its relationship to India's continued dependence on agriculture and foreign exchange.
  5. Differentiate among the abolition of intermediaries, tenancy reform, land ceilings, redistribution, and rural development programs, and explain why each met different obstacles.
  6. Evaluate the legal and political significance of the First Amendment of 1951, Articles 31A and 31B, and the Ninth Schedule in their original context.
  7. Assess the Community Development Programme, Bhoodan, and the Panchayati Raj movement, and ask who benefited from rural institutions.
  8. Explain why constitutional equality and the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955 did not by themselves end caste discrimination.
  9. Distinguish Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe, and other backward-class policy categories and interpret the work and fate of the Kalelkar Commission.
  10. Describe the Hindu Code controversy and the original provisions and limits of the four Hindu law enactments of 1955–1956.
  11. Trace the movement from the Andhra agitation to the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, the division of Bombay in 1960, and the creation of Nagaland in 1963.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Nehru-era development and linguistic federalism using evidence from the 1950s and from later scholarship.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did leaders of very different political outlooks agree that national independence required state-directed economic development?
  2. What did "mixed economy" mean in practice, and how did it differ from both complete state ownership and an unregulated market?
  3. Why did the Second Plan shift toward heavy industry, and what were the consequences for agriculture, employment, and foreign exchange?
  4. Why could the abolition of zamindari succeed in ending one kind of landlord power without redistributing land to those who farmed it?
  5. What explains the very different outcomes of tenancy and ceiling legislation across Indian states?
  6. Why did legal equality and the prohibition of untouchability coexist with persistent caste discrimination?
  7. How did debates over the Hindu Code reveal competing visions of family, religion, gender, and the authority of the state?
  8. Why did Nehru and many Congress leaders initially resist linguistic states, and why did they eventually concede the principle?
  9. How should historians weigh the building of institutions, industry, and infrastructure against persistent poverty and inequality?
  10. In what sense was early postcolonial India's transformation substantial but uneven, and what does that pattern reveal about state capacity and social power?

Historical Context

The economic and social arguments of the 1950s drew on a long history. Nineteenth-century nationalist writers such as Dadabhai Naoroji and R. C. Dutt, examined in Chapter 76, had argued that colonial policy drained resources from India and obstructed industrial growth. The Swadeshi movement of 1905 and the interwar growth of Indian-owned cotton, jute, steel, and sugar industries, discussed in Chapters 77 and 80, added a business constituency to this argument. By the 1930s the Indian National Congress, under pressure from socialists within its ranks and impressed by Soviet planning and by Depression-era state intervention elsewhere, had accepted planning as a goal. The National Planning Committee under Nehru's chairmanship, appointed in 1938, and the Bombay Plan of 1944 prepared by a group of leading industrialists and economists, show that support for state-guided development ran from the left of the nationalist movement to its commercial elite.

Agrarian structure was equally the product of history. Colonial revenue settlements, examined in Chapters 63 and 66, had created sharply different arrangements across regions. In zamindari areas such as Bengal, Bihar, and much of Uttar Pradesh, intermediaries collected rent from cultivators and paid a revenue to the state. In ryotwari areas such as much of Madras, Bombay, and parts of the Deccan, individual cultivators were recognized as the principal revenue payers. Mahalwari arrangements in parts of northern India assigned revenue responsibility to village communities. In each system, however, there were tenants, sharecroppers, and landless agricultural laborers beneath the recognized landholder, and these groups were disproportionately drawn from lower castes. The Congress of the 1930s and 1940s had promised agrarian reform, and the Kumarappa Committee of 1949, a Congress agrarian-reforms body, recommended abolition of intermediaries and ceilings on holdings.

Independence also brought immediate pressures. The Partition of 1947 had divided Punjab and Bengal and disrupted irrigation, jute processing, and trade. Refugees needed housing, land, and employment. The integration of the princely states, examined in Chapter 87, added territories with their own revenue and landholding systems, including large jagirs in Hyderabad. The Telangana peasant uprising in Hyderabad State, from 1946 to 1951, showed that agrarian conflict could turn violent. A food crisis in the late 1940s and continued dependence on imports reinforced the sense that agricultural production could not be left to chance.

Constitutionally, the Republic had adopted a federal structure with a strong center, as Chapter 88 explained. Land, agriculture, and local government fell under the states; planning, however, was a concurrent concern, and the Planning Commission was not a constitutional body but was created by executive resolution. Many of the reforms examined here therefore required cooperation among a central government that formulated national priorities and state governments that held legislative authority over land and local administration. That division of responsibility helps explain why the same national commitment produced different outcomes across states, and why the courts, whose power to review legislation Chapter 88 also discussed, became a venue for conflict over property.

Students should also note the international context. The Cold War, introduced in Chapter 92, shaped the availability of foreign technology, aid, and credit. India's leaders drew on Soviet experience of planning, on British and American economic thought, and on the development debates of the early postwar years, while insisting on a distinctive democratic path. At the same time, the evidence for this period is better for official intentions than for rural practice, and statistics from the era were collected, revised, and sometimes disputed. Readers should expect figures to be approximate and should compare series carefully.

Section 89.1

Development after Independence: Economic Conditions and the Planning Commission

India's economic starting point in 1947 was one of widespread poverty. Agriculture employed the great majority of the population, yields were low, irrigation covered only a fraction of cropped land, and food output was vulnerable to the monsoon. The Bengal famine of 1943, examined in Chapter 84, remained a recent memory. Industry existed, notably cotton textiles, jute, iron and steel at Jamshedpur, sugar, and cement, but it was concentrated in a few regions and produced few machines, machine tools, or heavy chemicals. Railways were extensive but oriented toward ports and export, and electricity generation per head was very low. The census of 1951 counted about 361 million people, of whom roughly 18 percent could read and write.

Partition added specific damage. The division of Punjab separated canal colonies from their headwaters and markets, and the loss of the jute-growing regions of East Bengal left Calcutta's mills short of raw material. The rehabilitation of millions of refugees, discussed in Chapter 86, absorbed public funds and land. Food imports were needed in the late 1940s, and the government also faced inflationary pressure. Leaders therefore approached development as both a long-term aspiration and an emergency.

The intellectual case for planning had been prepared well before independence. The National Planning Committee, set up by the Congress in 1938 with Nehru as chairman and including scientists, industrialists, and economists, produced reports on sectors ranging from industry to public health. The Bombay Plan of 1944, written by a group of prominent industrialists and economists including J. R. D. Tata, G. D. Birla, and Purshotamdas Thakurdas, proposed a fifteen-year program to double per-capita income and argued that the state should take a major role in investment and basic industries. Other proposals of the period included the People's Plan associated with M. N. Roy's followers and the Gandhian Plan, which stressed village industry. These plans differed on ownership and method, but they shared a premise that markets alone would not produce rapid industrialization in a poor, late-developing country. International thinking, from wartime economic mobilization to emerging development economics, reinforced that premise.

The Planning Commission was established by a resolution of the Government of India on 15 March 1950. It was not created by the Constitution, nor was it a statutory body. Its remit was to assess national resources, formulate plans for their effective use, determine priorities, and appraise progress, and it was to advise rather than command. Nehru served as chairman, which gave the body substantial political weight, while a deputy chairman and members, including economists and public figures, did much of the drafting. The Commission worked with ministries and, through the National Development Council established in 1952, with chief ministers, because land, agriculture, education, health, and many other subjects fell constitutionally to the states.

This institutional design mattered. The Commission could set priorities and influence the allocation of central assistance, but projects were executed by ministries, state governments, public enterprises, and local bodies, each with its own incentives and limits. Planning in India was thus a negotiated process within a federal system, not the issuing of production orders. It also differed from the Soviet model in being carried out under a parliamentary democracy with a free press and private property.

An important distinction in this early debate concerned the purposes for which scarce public money should be used. The Bombay Plan made clear that several leading industrialists accepted extensive public investment, but they did not agree with every socialist argument for public ownership. Gandhian critics, meanwhile, worried that an industrial strategy organized around cities, machinery, and distant administrative experts could neglect employment and self-reliance in villages. These alternatives were not merely intellectual positions: they implied different investment priorities, types of technology, and measures of success. A program that increased steel output could strengthen future productive capacity without immediately raising the earnings of a landless laborer. The Planning Commission therefore faced a political as well as a technical task: to turn incompatible expectations into priorities that could claim national support. In a federal democracy, the public legitimacy of those choices could matter as much as their mathematical consistency.

Why did leaders regard development as inseparable from independence? Colonial economic dependence, in their view, had consisted not only of foreign political control but of reliance on imported manufactures and foreign capital goods and on a pattern of trade serving metropolitan interests. Economic sovereignty meant building the capacity to produce steel, machinery, fertilizers, and power domestically, to finance investment from domestic savings, and to reduce vulnerability to external suppliers. Nehru gave this argument a strongly moral and symbolic form, treating large dams and factories as expressions of national purpose. Such rhetoric should be read as part of a political culture seeking legitimacy as well as an economic program, and it coexisted with real technical arguments about capital formation.

Analysis and limitations: These documents record intentions, assumptions, and the official view of conditions. The Plan's growth and output figures reflect the data then available and later were revised. The documents are authored by planners and political leaders and say little about how cultivators or local officials understood the Plan. The resolution's advisory language also understates the Commission's later political influence.

Section 89.2

The First Five-Year Plan, 1951–1956: Agriculture, Irrigation, and Reconstruction

The First Five-Year Plan ran from April 1951 to March 1956. Its modest design reflected the limits of the time: the economy had just emerged from the disruptions of the 1940s, savings were low, and administrative experience of planning was limited. The Plan therefore emphasized rehabilitation, agricultural production, irrigation, power, and transport rather than a dramatic reorientation of industry. Influenced by growth models such as the Harrod–Domar framework that related investment to output, planners aimed for a modest annual growth rate in national income, around 2 percent, and an increase in savings and investment.

Public-sector outlay was in the order of Rs 2,000 crore, a figure revised as the Plan proceeded, with the largest shares going to irrigation and power, agriculture and community development, and transport and communications. Financing relied on domestic revenue, borrowing, some deficit financing, and foreign assistance, which was small at this stage but began to grow. The Plan operated through the cooperation of central and state governments: the center provided grants and loans for approved state schemes, and states implemented them, so that outcomes depended on state capacity as well as national design.

The Plan's best-known projects were multipurpose river-valley schemes. Several were not new. The Damodar Valley Corporation had been created in 1948, and the Bhakra and Hirakud dams were begun before the Plan. What the Plan did was fund and expand them, with Bhakra-Nangal on the Sutlej, Hirakud on the Mahanadi, and others in the south and west. Students must distinguish initiation from completion: Hirakud was formally inaugurated in 1957 and Bhakra's major works were completed in the early 1960s. The announced benefits of such projects, including irrigation, flood control, and electricity, came after years of construction, resettlement of displaced villagers, and the later building of canals and distribution systems. Displacement of rural communities, often poorly compensated and often drawn from tribal and marginal groups, was an under-recorded cost.

Other First Plan investments included fertilizer production at Sindri, locomotive production at Chittaranjan, the Integral Coach Factory at Perambur, shipbuilding and aircraft enterprises, and the expansion of railways and roads. The Plan also funded refugee rehabilitation and the launching of the Community Development Programme examined in §89.8.

Results were, by official accounts, better than the Plan's own targets. National income grew at a rate above the planned figure, and foodgrain output rose from about 51 million tonnes in 1950–51 to about 67 million tonnes in 1955–56, helped by favorable monsoons and by the recovery and extension of cultivated area as well as irrigation. Historians caution that good rainfall explained a considerable part of the improvement and that the early 1950s should not be read as showing that planned investment alone had transformed agriculture. Official evaluation, by the Planning Commission itself, was also naturally positive, and later reconstructions of the national accounts differ in detail. The more careful conclusion is that the First Plan achieved stabilization and a measurable increase in output and created infrastructure, while leaving structural problems such as low productivity, unemployment, and rural inequality largely intact.

The Plan's results mattered for what came next. Their relative success encouraged planners to expand ambitions in the Second Plan, and the experience of the first years revealed that agricultural gains, unlike industrial ones, depended on the weather and on the behavior of millions of farm households. It also revealed that the Plan's funds were administered through state machinery of uneven quality, a problem that recurs throughout this chapter.

Section 89.3

The Mixed Economy: Public Enterprise, Private Capital, and State Regulation

India's chosen economic model combined public and private enterprise, a system usually called the mixed economy. It contrasted with Soviet-style planning, in which the state owned virtually all productive assets and directed output administratively, and with an economy in which investment decisions were left primarily to private markets. In India, agriculture, most trade, and the great majority of small manufacturing remained in private hands, as did substantial large industry. The state's role was to build basic and heavy industry, direct investment through licensing, and provide infrastructure, finance, and some services.

The first formal statement was the Industrial Policy Resolution of April 1948. It divided industry into categories: a small group of industries, such as arms and ammunition, atomic energy, and railways, reserved to the state; a group of basic industries in which the state would have exclusive right to new undertakings but allowed existing private units to continue for a period; a group of regulated private industries; and the remainder open to private enterprise. The resolution also made clear that the private sector would have a place, partly to reassure industrialists and partly because the state lacked capital and managers to run everything.

In the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, Parliament established the machinery of industrial licensing. Entrepreneurs in scheduled industries needed government permission to establish new units, expand capacity, or change products. The rationale was to channel scarce capital and foreign exchange into planned priorities, prevent concentration, and encourage regional dispersal. Licensing would later become the center of critiques, as discussed in §89.19.

The 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution, adopted in April 1956 as the Second Plan began, revised the framework in light of the Parliament's declared goal of a "socialistic pattern of society" (a goal associated with the Congress's Avadi session of 1955). It listed seventeen industries in a first schedule whose future development would be the exclusive responsibility of the state, a second schedule of twelve industries to be progressively state-owned but in which private enterprise would be expected to supplement state effort, and a residual category left to private initiative within the licensing system. It also affirmed the role of small-scale and cottage industries. Students should resist the common misreading that 1956 created a state monopoly of industry: the great majority of industrial activity remained open to private firms, and the Resolution explicitly acknowledged their role.

Historical Vocabulary
Mixed Economy

An economy in which public and private ownership coexist and the state steers investment through planning and regulation. In India it meant public enterprise in strategic and capital-intensive sectors, private enterprise in much of industry and trade, and state control through licensing and import regulation. It was neither complete nationalization nor a free market.

The term matters because both supporters and critics later described the period as "socialist." Historians should distinguish the rhetoric of a socialistic pattern from the actual structure of ownership and regulation.

Why did leaders favor state leadership in capital-intensive sectors? The case rested on several arguments. Private capital in India was limited and risk-averse regarding projects with long gestation periods and low initial returns, such as steel, heavy machinery, and power. The state could mobilize resources, including foreign aid, on a scale private firms could not. State ownership of basic industries was thought to prevent concentration of economic power in a few business houses and to align investment with national priorities rather than with private profit alone. Many industrialists themselves had supported state action in infrastructure and heavy industry in the Bombay Plan, though they were wary of full nationalization.

Critics, then and later, raised concerns. Licensing gave officials broad discretion, creating risks of delay, favoritism, and rent-seeking. Restricting entry and expansion could reduce competition and encourage inefficiency, and public enterprises were sometimes burdened by political direction, overstaffing, and weak cost discipline. Some contemporary figures, such as C. Rajagopalachari, who founded the Swatantra Party in 1959, argued that the permit system threatened economic freedom, while others from the left argued that the state was not going far enough in curbing private wealth. Debates about how far these problems were visible in the 1950s, as opposed to later decades, are discussed in §89.19.

Section 89.4

The Second and Third Five-Year Plans: Heavy Industry, Self-Reliance, and the Mahalanobis Strategy

The Second Five-Year Plan (1956–1961) marked a decisive shift toward heavy industry. Its intellectual author was Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis, a statistician who had founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta and who headed the Planning Commission's work on the Plan frame. Mahalanobis drew on a two-sector growth model, later elaborated into a four-sector version, that divided the economy into a capital-goods sector and a consumer-goods sector. His argument was that, if the aim was to raise the rate of growth over the long run, investment should be directed disproportionately to the production of machines that make other machines, because the capacity to produce capital goods domestically would determine how fast the economy could expand without relying on imported equipment. In the short term, this meant accepting slower growth of consumer goods; in the long term, it promised faster growth and independence from foreign suppliers.

The Plan accordingly increased public-sector outlay to roughly Rs 4,800 crore as originally framed and gave the largest shares to industry and minerals, power, and transport. It envisioned a substantial increase in national income and a sharp expansion of steel, coal, machine-building, and electricity. Small and village industries were assigned the role of providing employment and consumer goods, a compromise between the heavy-industry strategy and Gandhian and employment concerns.

Three public-sector steel plants became symbols of the Plan: Bhilai in Madhya Pradesh, built with Soviet cooperation; Rourkela in Odisha, with West German firms; and Durgapur in West Bengal, with British firms. Other projects included the Heavy Engineering Corporation at Ranchi, heavy electrical equipment at Bhopal, machine-tool manufacture, expansion of coal mining, new fertilizer and chemical factories, and thermal and hydroelectric power stations. Technical education expanded in parallel, as §89.17 discusses.

International contributions were significant but should not suggest that industrialization was foreign-directed. India obtained equipment, technicians, and credit from the Soviet Union, West Germany, Britain, the United States, and others, partly because it practiced nonalignment and could bargain among suppliers, a theme taken up in Chapter 92. But decisions about priorities, project location, and ownership were made by Indian planners, engineers, and politicians, and Indian engineers, workers, and public-sector managers carried out construction and operation. Foreign collaboration was a means to build domestic capacity.

The Plan's execution soon met difficulties. By 1957–58 India faced a serious foreign-exchange crisis, as imports of capital goods and food rose faster than export earnings and reserves fell. The government tightened import controls and sought additional external credit, and the Plan's public outlay was scaled back and its priorities reordered. Food prices rose in some years, and agricultural output did not keep pace with the Plan's assumptions. Nevertheless, industrial capacity expanded: steel plants began producing in the late 1950s and early 1960s, and the production of machinery, power equipment, and chemicals grew.

The Third Plan (1961–1966) sought to build on this base with an emphasis on self-reliance and a renewed focus on agriculture, including the introduction of intensive agricultural district programs. It set higher growth targets, but its implementation coincided with the border war with China in 1962 and the war with Pakistan in 1965, examined in Chapter 92, which raised defense expenditure, and with poor harvests, notably the droughts of 1965 and 1966. Growth fell well below target, food imports, including under American PL-480 arrangements, rose, and the Plan period ended with an economic crisis and the devaluation of the rupee in 1966.

The shortage of foreign exchange was not a temporary accounting inconvenience; it exposed a structural weakness of the strategy. Steel mills and engineering works required imported machinery long before they could produce domestic substitutes, so an investment intended to reduce dependence could first intensify it. Export earnings from established products did not grow rapidly enough to pay for all the necessary equipment. Delaying a project could waste prior expenditure, while completing it required credit, aid, or a reduction in other imports. Foreign loans also introduced questions about repayment and the timing of future earnings. Policymakers therefore had to prioritize among projects that looked equally important on the original Plan's industrial map. The effects of this adjustment were not uniform: a mill already under construction was more likely to receive protection than a rural school or an irrigation scheme whose benefits were harder to quantify. The episode illustrates why a plan's announced outlay, its revised outlay, and expenditure actually realized should never be treated as the same statistic.

The section's central tension is clear. Investment in industrial capacity was meant to create long-term independence, but the economy depended on agricultural productivity for food, raw materials, wage goods, and exports, and a stagnant farm sector constrained industrial growth. Planners were aware of this, and agricultural programs expanded, but the Second Plan's priorities and the weak performance of agricultural policy left the economy exposed. The following divisions turn to the countryside, where this dependence was most acute.

Division II · §§89.5–89.8 · Late 1940s–early 1960s

Land Reform, Rural Development, and Agrarian Inequality

India remained overwhelmingly rural, and agrarian reform was the principal test of whether constitutional democracy could alter material conditions.

Section 89.5

Land Reform and the Colonial Agrarian Inheritance

The landholding systems of independent India were inherited from colonial and precolonial arrangements, examined in Chapters 55, 63, and 66, and they varied greatly. In zamindari areas, concentrated in Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, much of Uttar Pradesh, and parts of Madras and central India, the state recognized intermediaries as proprietors who collected rent from cultivators and paid a fixed or periodically revised revenue. In ryotwari areas, covering much of Madras, Bombay, and parts of Assam and the Deccan, the cultivator, or the registered occupant, was the formal revenue payer to the state. In mahalwari areas, chiefly in parts of the northwest, revenue was assessed on estates held by village communities. The princely states, including Hyderabad and Travancore, had their own systems, including large jagirs and temple and religious endowments.

These formal categories concealed a layered reality. Beneath proprietors and registered occupants stood tenants with differing degrees of security, from occupancy tenants with hereditary rights to tenants-at-will who could be evicted; sharecroppers who gave a fraction of the crop, often one half, to the landowner; and agricultural laborers who owned no land and worked for wages or in bonded arrangements. A single household might combine several of these positions. Large landowners also frequently leased land out rather than cultivating it, so that ownership and cultivation diverged. Conditions varied by region: in parts of Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh intermediary power was strong, in Bengal sharecropping was widespread, and in some southern districts tenants held land under complex customary arrangements.

Caste and landholding overlapped. Upper-caste groups, such as Brahmins, Rajputs, Bhumihars, and Kayasthas in the north, and Reddys, Kammas, Nairs, and others in the south, were disproportionately landlords or substantial occupants, while Scheduled Castes made up a large share of agricultural laborers and many tenants. Intermediate peasant castes, such as Jats, Yadavs, and Kurmis, often held land as cultivating owners or tenants and would later become politically significant. This overlap meant that agrarian reform was not only an economic matter but touched ritual and social hierarchy, a point the chapter returns to in Division III.

It helps to distinguish several concepts that are often confused. Ownership referred to proprietary rights over land; tenancy to the right to cultivate land owned by another on specified terms; occupancy rights to a secure, often hereditary, claim by a tenant; revenue obligations to payments owed to the state; and agricultural employment to wage labor, which gave no rights over land at all. Reforms targeting one of these did not necessarily change the others.

Why was land reform considered essential? Several arguments converged. As a matter of justice, many leaders regarded extractive intermediary rents and insecure tenancies as relics of colonial exploitation. As a matter of productivity, planners argued that tenants with insecure tenure and high rents lacked incentive to invest, and that a more equal distribution of land would encourage intensive cultivation. As a matter of democratic politics, a rural society dominated by landlords seemed incompatible with universal adult franchise and citizenship. The Congress had made abolition of intermediaries a commitment, and the First Plan and the Second Plan both endorsed it. The difficulty was that land was a state subject, that landowners were politically influential in state legislatures and in the Congress itself, and that records of rights were often incomplete or inaccurate.

Historical Vocabulary
Zamindar and Intermediary

A zamindar was a landholder recognized under colonial revenue settlements, especially in northern and eastern India, with rights to collect rent from cultivators and an obligation to pay revenue to the state. Intermediary is the wider legal term for those standing between the state and the actual cultivator, including zamindars, jagirdars, and others.

The terms matter because zamindari abolition ended the intermediary tier, not necessarily the unequal distribution of land among those who remained. Zamindars retained land they cultivated themselves, called sir or khudkasht land in some states.

Section 89.6

Abolition of Zamindari: Legislation, Compensation, and Constitutional Conflict

The first great agrarian measure of independent India was the abolition of intermediary interests. Because land was a state subject, abolition proceeded through separate state laws, and their dates, terms, and effectiveness differed. Some provinces had begun before 1947: Madras passed an Estates Abolition Act in 1948, and Hyderabad abolished jagirs in 1949. Uttar Pradesh enacted the Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act in 1950, put into effect in 1952 after considerable preparation, and it became one of the most thoroughgoing measures: it transferred proprietary rights of zamindars to the state and conferred rights on tenants and cultivators who paid to the state, though zamindars retained their directly cultivated lands. Bihar passed a Land Reforms Act in 1950, Madhya Pradesh abolished proprietary and jagir rights in the early 1950s, and West Bengal enacted an Estates Acquisition Act in 1953. Other states followed in various forms.

Compensation was a central issue. The Constitution as adopted in 1950 guaranteed property rights, and Article 31 required that property taken by the state be acquired for a public purpose and with compensation. Congress governments thought that compensation should be limited, but landlords argued that it should be at market value. State laws specified compensation by formulas, generally multiples of net income from the land, often payable in bonds and in installments, with scales that favored smaller intermediaries over the largest. In Bihar, where the Maharaja of Darbhanga, Kameshwar Singh, was among the largest zamindars, the Act and its compensation provisions were challenged. In 1950 the Patna High Court struck down the Bihar law on the ground that it violated the equal protection of laws because of its differential treatment of classes of intermediaries, while other high courts upheld similar laws.

The government's response was the Constitution (First Amendment) Act of 1951. It inserted Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule. Article 31A protected laws providing for the acquisition of estates and rights therein from challenge on the ground that they violated fundamental rights such as equality or property. Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule validated specified state land-reform statutes, which were listed in the Schedule, regardless of any judicial finding of inconsistency with fundamental rights. The same amendment inserted Article 15(4) permitting special provision for backward classes, discussed in §89.10, and made other changes, including to free-speech provisions, that provoked separate controversy. The Supreme Court in later years addressed the amendment's validity and the Ninth Schedule's scope, but those later developments belong to the history of constitutional law after the period examined here; for the 1950s, the essential point is that Parliament chose to remove agrarian-reform statutes from judicial challenge in order to protect a political priority.

What did abolition achieve? Its most clear outcome was the end of the legal category of intermediary landlord in much of the country. Millions of tenants who had paid rent to intermediaries came to hold their land directly from the state, and the largest landed estates and jagirs, which had been politically powerful, lost their legal basis. In some states, such as Uttar Pradesh, large numbers of former tenants became landholders with rights of transfer. For the state, abolition increased direct contact with cultivators and revenue records, and it removed a class that had been a pillar of the colonial order.

The legal and administrative sequence of abolition deserves closer scrutiny. Legislatures first identified which intermediary interests would vest in the state; governments then had to issue notifications, calculate compensation, survey estates, settle disputes, and recognize the rights of cultivators beneath former intermediaries. A landholder's loss of the right to collect revenue did not necessarily mean the disappearance of his social influence or ownership of land retained for personal cultivation. Some households continued to dominate local credit, irrigation, and village institutions even after formal abolition. Conversely, a cultivator who acquired an enforceable title could gain a new degree of security against arbitrary rent demands. Those different results explain why the abolition statutes could be legally far-reaching yet distributively modest. The courts mattered, but so did district-level land-record officers whose decisions determined which names appeared in records of rights and which claims could be pursued without an expensive lawsuit.

But the essential distinction must be kept in view: ending intermediary revenue rights did not necessarily redistribute agricultural land to the people who worked it. Zamindars kept their personal cultivation lands, often extensive, and many retained land by claiming that it was under their own cultivation, by transferring land to relatives, or by resuming land from tenants before the laws took effect. Sharecroppers and tenants-at-will, who were often not recorded in official records, frequently failed to gain rights. Landless laborers received nothing. The beneficiaries were often those intermediate cultivators who held recorded occupancy, and the biggest landlords were weakened as a legal class while many remained economically and socially powerful. Historians have debated whether abolition mainly strengthened middle peasant proprietors or reproduced agrarian hierarchy under new forms. The best-supported view is that it did both in different places.

Section 89.7

Tenancy Reform, Land Ceilings, Redistribution, and Unequal Implementation

After the abolition of intermediaries, reform moved to the next layers. Tenancy reform sought to regulate rents, give tenants security against eviction, and in some cases allow tenants to acquire ownership. Rent regulation generally capped rents at some fraction of the produce, often between one-fifth and one-fourth in the laws of several states, though sharecropping rents ran higher in practice. Security of tenure laws limited the grounds for eviction, often permitting a landowner to resume land for "personal cultivation," a provision that became a common route for evasion. Ownership laws, such as the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act of 1948 and its amendments, were more ambitious: they provided for tenants to become owners on payment of compensation, with the "tillers' day" of 1957 in Bombay marking the date from which tenants would be deemed purchasers. Land-ceiling legislation imposed an upper limit on the amount of land that one household could hold, with the surplus to be taken by the state and distributed to landless or marginal cultivators. The Second Plan endorsed ceilings, and by around 1960 most states had enacted laws, though ceilings were often set high and subject to many exceptions.

Outcomes varied. In Kerala, a Communist-led ministry under E. M. S. Namboodiripad took office in 1957 after the state's first elections and introduced agrarian legislation to secure tenants against eviction and set ceilings. The ministry was dismissed in 1959 under Article 356 after a political agitation, an episode that raised questions about center–state relations, and the Kerala Agrarian Relations Act of 1960, passed under a different coalition, modified the original proposals. Fuller tenancy abolition and redistribution in Kerala came after 1969, which is beyond the period of this chapter. In West Bengal, the 1953 and 1955 legislation abolished intermediaries and set ceilings, but implementation was weak, and the more effective registration of sharecroppers through Operation Barga belongs to the late 1970s. In Uttar Pradesh, abolition was relatively thorough, but ceilings were weak and later reforms limited. In parts of southern India, Madras's protection acts of the 1950s shielded some tenants but left many sharecroppers outside protection.

Implementation failed for identifiable reasons. Landowners often evicted tenants before laws took effect or converted them into "voluntary" surrenders. Land was transferred to relatives and dependents, or held in fictitious names, to remain under the ceiling, a practice called benami holding. Many laws included exemptions for orchards, plantations, cooperative farms, and religious trusts, and ceilings were set at generous levels. Land records were incomplete and often controlled by village accountants, often of dominant castes. Tenants who had informal oral arrangements could not prove their status, and fear of retaliation deterred claims. Local legislators and officials were often drawn from or allied with landowning groups. Courts and revenue tribunals moved slowly.

The consequences fell unevenly. Tenants with recorded rights, often from intermediate castes, gained security and sometimes ownership. Sharecroppers and unrecorded tenants, disproportionately lower-caste, often lost land or access. Agricultural laborers, among the poorest groups and heavily Scheduled Caste, were largely outside the reforms and, if tenants were evicted and land was resumed for personal cultivation, they sometimes were pushed into laboring status. Women's rights in land were rarely recognized: records typically named male household heads, and customary and personal law, as discussed in §89.11, limited inheritance. The overall effect was to expand the category of peasant proprietors and reduce the power of the largest absentee landlords, without creating a broadly egalitarian distribution. Students should treat land reform not as a single national program but as a set of state-level measures with different content, timing, and enforcement, and should not assign to the 1950s outcomes, such as the Left Front's reforms in West Bengal, that came much later.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Tenant Household Facing Land Reform (Composite Reconstruction)

This study is an illustrative composite, not an account of a named family, and it contains no archival quotations. Its circumstances are drawn from state tenancy acts, village surveys, census and rural-credit survey data, and later scholarship on land records and eviction.

Imagine a household of five in a village of eastern Uttar Pradesh or northern Bihar in the early 1950s. The household cultivates about two acres, held on an oral arrangement from a landowner of a dominant landowning caste, to whom it gives a half share of the crop. It belongs to an intermediate peasant caste and supplements income through seasonal labor on larger farms. The village accountant keeps the records of rights, and the landowner's family has long been prominent in local affairs.

When abolition and tenancy laws are announced, the household's position depends on whether its status is recorded. If the accountant lists the household as a tenant with occupancy rights, it may gain a legal claim. If it is listed as a laborer, or if it is not listed, its claim is weak. The landowner may declare the plot as his own cultivation, may offer the household a "voluntary" surrender with a small payment, or may evict it before the law takes effect. The household's options depend on whether it can afford legal help, whether caste or kin networks offer protection, and whether local officials or political leaders will back it. A household of lower caste faces additional risk of social retaliation. Meanwhile, a woman in the household has no recorded right to the land in her own name.

The scenario shows why the meaning of legal change depended on records, power, and caste, and why the same statute could secure one household and dispossess another.

Section 89.8

Community Development, Cooperatives, Bhoodan, and Rural Modernization

Alongside legal reform, the government sought to raise rural productivity and welfare through administrative programs. The Community Development Programme was inaugurated on 2 October 1952, the anniversary of Gandhi's birth, with 55 projects. It drew on pilot experiments such as the Etawah project in Uttar Pradesh, begun in 1948 with the American planner Albert Mayer, and aimed to bring a coordinated package of agricultural extension, irrigation, health, education, and village industry to rural blocks. In 1953 the National Extension Service extended a more basic structure of extension workers across the country, and blocks were gradually added so that by the early 1960s the program covered most of the rural areas. Each block, with about a hundred villages, was staffed by a block development officer and specialists, with village-level workers as the base. S. K. Dey was an influential minister in its early years.

Cooperative credit was another strand. The All-India Rural Credit Survey of 1951–54, conducted by the Reserve Bank of India, found that cooperatives supplied only a very small share of rural credit, most of which came from moneylenders and landlords at high interest. The survey recommended a state-partnered cooperative system, and the Plans promoted cooperative credit, marketing, and processing societies. Results were uneven: cooperatives flourished in some regions, such as parts of Maharashtra and Gujarat with strong sugar and dairy cooperatives, but elsewhere were dominated by larger cultivators and offered little to poor borrowers.

A very different approach came from the Gandhian tradition. Vinoba Bhave began the Bhoodan ("land gift") movement in April 1951 in the Telangana region, then affected by the peasant uprising, when a landowner at Pochampalli responded to his appeal for land for the landless by offering a hundred acres. Bhave then walked through villages across India, asking landowners to donate a sixth of their holdings, and set a target of 50 million acres. The movement later broadened into Gramdan, the donation of whole villages, from 1952 onward. Bhoodan attracted wide moral attention, and Jayaprakash Narayan joined it in the mid-1950s. The amount of land pledged was large, several million acres by the late 1950s, but a considerable share of it was poor, disputed, or encumbered, and much less was actually distributed to landless families. The movement showed the appeal of a voluntary approach but also its limits against entrenched ownership, and it could not substitute for legal redistribution.

Evaluation of participation produced mixed findings. Panchayati bodies enlarged the number of elected offices and gave rural leaders access to development funds and patronage, and over time they became arenas for political training. But studies found that influential landowning and dominant-caste families often captured these bodies, that officials retained financial and technical control, and that women and Scheduled Castes had little voice, a situation that later reforms, including constitutional amendments of 1992, attempted to address. The voluntary character of Bhoodan raised a different problem from that posed by statutory ceilings. Vinoba Bhave's appeals could persuade donors who would have opposed compulsory redistribution, and the movement gave public prominence to the moral question of unequal land ownership. But a pledge made at a meeting was not equivalent to a completed transfer of cultivable land with an undisputed title. Some promised plots were encumbered, distant, of poor quality, or difficult to register, and distribution required legal and administrative work that a spiritual appeal could not replace. Community Development encountered a parallel difficulty. Blocks received personnel and funds for agriculture, roads, sanitation, and local organization, but the groups most able to attend meetings or control cooperatives could be those already influential in the village. These cases help explain why participatory language in official programs needs to be tested against actual control over land, credit, and institutions.

These shortcomings had consequences for national policy. By the early 1960s the Third Plan acknowledged that agricultural growth was lagging and began to concentrate resources on districts and farmers with irrigation and better assets, as in the Intensive Agricultural District Programme. This selective approach, together with the food crises of 1965 and 1966, set the stage for the agricultural strategy of the later 1960s, examined in Chapter 94.

Division III · §§89.9–89.12 · 1950–early 1960s

Caste, Affirmative Action, and Social Reform

The limits of legal equality when deeply rooted social and economic hierarchies remained intact.

Section 89.9

Caste and the Republic: Constitutional Equality versus Social Practice

Chapter 88 examined how the Constitution established equality before the law, prohibited discrimination on grounds of caste, and in Article 17 abolished untouchability and made its practice in any form an offense. This section does not repeat that constitution-making process. Its question is what happened when those principles met village and urban life in the 1950s.

The answer was that discrimination persisted widely. Dalit communities, a term in political use that referred to groups legally listed as Scheduled Castes, continued to face exclusion in many villages from wells and water sources, temples, tea shops, barbers' and washermen's services, and common burial grounds, and often lived in separate hamlets. Their occupations, such as agricultural labor, scavenging, leather work, and sweeping, were frequently tied to dependence on landowners and to ritual stigma. In schools, children from Scheduled Castes might be seated separately or discouraged, and access to employment in factories and offices depended on networks and prejudice as much as on law. Violence and social boycott were sometimes used against those who claimed rights. Regional variation was real: in parts of the south and west, reform and anti-caste movements had altered conditions, and in cities anonymity softened some barriers, while in many northern and central villages exclusion was severe.

Parliament responded in 1955 with the Untouchability (Offences) Act. It made it an offense to prevent a person on grounds of untouchability from entering a place of worship, using a well, tank, or other source of water, entering shops, restaurants, and hotels, using public conveyances, or being denied goods and services, with penalties of imprisonment and fines. The Act came into force in June 1955. Its original form was limited, however: penalties were modest, enforcement depended on police and magistrates who were often reluctant, and prosecutions were rare and convictions fewer. Parliament later amended and renamed it the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976, strengthening it, and a further Act in 1989 addressed atrocities, both outside this chapter's period. Students should therefore distinguish the 1955 original from its later versions.

The Dalit political landscape in the 1950s was shaped by B. R. Ambedkar's leadership. After his resignation from the cabinet in 1951, discussed in §89.11, he continued to organize through the Scheduled Castes Federation and sought a broader political party, which became the Republican Party of India, founded in 1957 after his death. On 14 October 1956 at Nagpur, Ambedkar embraced Buddhism together with a large crowd of followers, an act that he presented as a path to equality and dignity beyond the Hindu social order, and he died on 6 December 1956. The conversion movement, known as Navayana or neo-Buddhism, became a significant social and cultural movement, especially in Maharashtra, though many Scheduled Caste communities in other regions followed different paths, including Congress politics and the Gandhian Harijan welfare institutions.

Other networks also worked on social reform: Gandhian organizations such as the Harijan Sevak Sangh, Christian missions, Buddhist and Sikh groups with their own approaches, labor and Communist organizations, and regional anti-caste traditions such as the Self-Respect Movement in Tamil Nadu and the legacy of Jotirao Phule in Maharashtra, discussed in Chapters 74 and 80. These groups differed in their analysis. Gandhians stressed moral reform and the transformation of attitudes, Ambedkarites stressed political power and legal rights, and Communists emphasized class and land. The disagreements among them are part of the history of the period.

Why did a legal prohibition not guarantee transformation? Caste operated through economic dependence, village power, ritual practice, and everyday social relations that law reached unevenly. A laborer who depended on a landowner for wages and loans could not easily file a complaint against him. Local police and magistrates shared the social assumptions of the community. The reforms examined earlier in this chapter, which left laborers without land, also left a major source of dependence untouched. The law nevertheless mattered: it established a public norm, gave activists a legal instrument, and signaled that discrimination lacked the sanction of the state, even if its enforcement lagged far behind its declaration.

Section 89.10

Affirmative Action, Reservations, and the Politics of Social Justice

The Constitution authorized several forms of special provision. Seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies were reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to population, for an initial period of ten years that was later extended. Article 16(4) permitted reservation in public employment for backward classes not adequately represented in state services, Article 335 required that the claims of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes be taken into consideration consistently with administrative efficiency, and Article 46 directed the state to promote their educational and economic interests. The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes were listed by Presidential orders in 1950, building on colonial-era schedules, and the lists could be modified by Parliament.

Education policy led to a legal conflict. In 1951 the Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan held that the Madras government's communal order, which reserved college seats by caste and community, violated the Constitution's equality guarantee because the Constitution then had no provision allowing it. Parliament responded in the First Amendment of 1951 by adding Article 15(4), which allows the state to make special provision for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. Together with Article 31A and the Ninth Schedule, it shows how the First Amendment altered the constitutional balance in favor of social-reform legislation.

The policy categories should be kept distinct. Scheduled Castes were groups listed because of historical untouchability, concentrated in the lowest ritual and economic positions. Scheduled Tribes were listed communities, often forest and hill peoples, identified by geographic isolation, distinct culture, and marginal economic position, and their relationship to the caste order was different and varied by region. The category of other backward classes, a name used later, referred to a large and heterogeneous set of socially and educationally disadvantaged groups that were not Scheduled Castes or Tribes, whose identification was far more contested because it depended on criteria, and states used different lists.

To address the wider question, Article 340 empowered the President to appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of backward classes. The first such body, the Backward Classes Commission under Kaka Kalelkar, was appointed in January 1953 and submitted its report in March 1955. It listed more than 2,300 communities as socially and educationally backward, recommended reservations in education and public employment, and used caste as the principal basis for identification, with additional indicators such as low literacy and poor representation in government. The report's reception was complicated. Kalelkar himself, in a covering letter, expressed doubt about the caste-based criterion and urged consideration of other tests, and the government regarded the report as unworkable, in part because of the difficulty of defining and enumerating the categories and out of concern that caste-based classification would perpetuate caste. The central government did not adopt its recommendations, and the issue was left largely to states, several of which, such as Madras (later Tamil Nadu), Mysore, and Kerala, pursued their own backward-class policies. The question returned at the national level with the Mandal Commission in 1979 and its implementation in 1990, which belongs to Chapter 99 and should not be read back into the 1950s.

Section 89.11

The Hindu Code Bills: Marriage, Divorce, Adoption, and Inheritance

Personal law, the rules governing marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, and guardianship, was administered in British India according to the religion of the parties. For Hindus, the rules came from a mix of texts, commentaries, custom, and judicial decisions, differing by region and by the two main schools, Mitakshara and Dayabhaga, with consequences for property in joint families and for women's limited rights. Reformers had pressed for modernization since the nineteenth century, as Chapters 67 and 74 describe, and had achieved statutes such as the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856 and the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929. In 1941 the government appointed the Hindu Law Committee under B. N. Rau to examine codification, and its work led to a draft code.

The Hindu Code Bill was introduced in the Constituent Assembly acting as the legislature in 1947 and referred to a select committee. B. R. Ambedkar, as law minister, took charge of the Bill, which proposed a unified code covering marriage, divorce, succession, adoption, and guardianship and providing for monogamy, divorce, and improved property rights for women, including daughters' shares in inheritance. It met strong opposition. Orthodox Hindu organizations, including the Hindu Mahasabha, the Ram Rajya Parishad led by the religious figure Swami Karpatri, and many traditional scholars and Congress members, argued that Parliament, which they considered unrepresentative of Hindu opinion, should not interfere with religious law, that divorce was alien to Hindu marriage, and that equal inheritance would fragment property. Some opponents objected that only the Hindu community was being singled out. President Rajendra Prasad voiced reservations. Supporters included Nehru, a group of women legislators and women's organizations, notably Hansa Mehta, Renuka Ray, and Durgabai Deshmukh, and reformist lawyers.

In September 1951, after the Bill had been taken up in Parliament and its consideration had been cut short, Ambedkar resigned from the cabinet. His resignation letter and statement criticized the government's failure to carry through the Hindu Code, and he cited other grievances as well, including the handling of provisions for backward classes, foreign policy, and his own position in the ministry, so it should not be reduced to a single cause. Nehru then made the Hindu Code a theme of the 1951–52 general election campaign, and after Congress won decisively, the government returned to reform but in a different form: rather than a single code, it brought separate bills, passed after extended debate.

Four statutes resulted. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 established monogamy and made bigamy an offense, provided for judicial separation and divorce on specified grounds such as cruelty, desertion, adultery, conversion, and certain illnesses, set a minimum marriage age (originally 15 for brides and 18 for grooms, later raised), and recognized inter-caste marriage as valid. The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 reformed intestate succession by making daughters equal heirs with sons in the separate property of a father who died without a will, giving widows and mothers shares, and converting women's limited estates into absolute ownership. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 set rules for guardianship of minors, with the father as natural guardian and the mother next. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 permitted adoption by Hindu women meeting conditions and set obligations to maintain wives, children, parents, and widowed daughters-in-law. The Acts applied to Hindus broadly defined to include Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs, and to others not governed by another personal law.

Proceedings required information, resources, and a judicial forum, while family pressure could discourage recourse to the law. Section 89.12

Women, Family Law, Education, and the Limits of Social Reform

The Hindu law reforms were major, but their effect on women depended on who could use them. Divorce and monogamy provisions primarily affected women in educated and urban families with access to courts, while most rural women, who faced informal community councils and limited legal knowledge, were little touched. Inheritance reforms gave daughters a share only in property that a father held separately, and joint family property under the Mitakshara system remained with male coparceners until much later, so that the majority of land held in joint families passed to sons. Many fathers also made wills or arranged transfers to exclude daughters, and custom and social pressure discouraged daughters from claiming shares, often with the expectation that dowry or marriage expenses had already settled their claims. The Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961 attempted to curb the dowry system but was weakly enforced.

India did not adopt a single law for all communities. Muslims remained under personal law for marriage, divorce, and inheritance, based on the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act of 1937 and the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act of 1939; Christians were governed by statutes such as the Indian Divorce Act of 1869 and the Indian Succession Act of 1925; and Parsis by the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act of 1936. The Special Marriage Act of 1954 provided a civil marriage option available to couples regardless of religion. The Constitution's Article 44, a Directive Principle, said that the state should endeavor to secure a uniform civil code, and debates in the Constituent Assembly had shown disagreement. In the 1950s Nehru and others argued that a uniform code was desirable in principle but that reform should begin with Hindu law and that change in other communities should come with their consent, a position shaped by the aftermath of Partition and concern for minority confidence. Critics have since argued that this approach left the principle of uniformity unfulfilled and made reform appear selectively directed at Hindus; defenders respond that Hindu law reform was within the community's own reform tradition and that the Special Marriage Act offered a secular alternative. This debate continued in later decades, as Chapter 107 shows.

Education was another channel of change. The literacy rate among women was about 9 percent in 1951 and about 15 percent in 1961, compared with about 27 and 40 percent for men, so that gains were real but the gap remained wide. The Constitution's directive to provide free and compulsory education to children until age 14 within ten years was far from achieved by 1960. Girls' enrollment grew, especially at primary level and in urban areas, and women entered teaching, nursing, medicine, and clerical work in growing numbers, while the great majority of women worked in agriculture and household production, often unpaid or underpaid. Women's political participation was visible, with women among Constituent Assembly members and ministers such as Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, but women made up fewer than 5 percent of members of the first Lok Sabha.

Women's organizations were historical actors in these changes. The All India Women's Conference, founded in 1927, campaigned on education and legal reform, including the Hindu Code. The National Federation of Indian Women, founded in 1954 with links to the Communist movement, worked on working women's rights and social issues. The Central Social Welfare Board, established in 1953 under Durgabai Deshmukh, funded voluntary welfare organizations for women and children. Women legislators pressed in Parliament for the reform bills. Presenting reform solely as male leaders' initiative omits these advocates.

Effects differed by caste, class, religion, and region. Upper-caste urban women, benefiting from education and legal literacy, gained most. Scheduled Caste and tribal women faced both caste and gender disadvantage, and, in the countryside, women of landed families had inheritance rights on paper but little power to claim them. Muslim women remained subject to personal law that was itself being debated within the community. Throughout, statutory change and transformation in social practice occurred at different speeds: the law could shift quickly, while family authority, property control, and women's economic status shifted slowly.

Division IV · §§89.13–89.16 · 1948–1963

Linguistic Reorganization and the Remaking of Indian Federalism

How regional language movements challenged inherited provincial boundaries and how the government reorganized the federation instead of rejecting linguistic claims.

Section 89.13

Language, Regional Identity, and the Demand for Linguistic States

The provinces inherited from British rule had been drawn by the accidents of conquest, administration, and treaty rather than by language. The Madras Presidency combined Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, and Kannada speakers; Bombay combined Marathi, Gujarati, and Kannada areas; the Central Provinces and Berar joined Hindi and Marathi regions; and the princely state of Hyderabad contained Telugu, Marathi, and Kannada districts under an Urdu-using administration. Meanwhile, the spread of vernacular print and education, discussed in Chapters 73 and 80, had strengthened regional-language identities, and by the early twentieth century activists in many regions demanded provinces in which their language would be the language of government and schooling.

The Indian National Congress had embraced this idea well before independence. At its Nagpur session in 1920 it reorganized its own provincial committees on linguistic lines, and the Nehru Report of 1928 endorsed linguistic provinces in principle. Congress leaders had thus promised that a free India would redraw provinces along linguistic lines. Linguistic claims were not inherently separatist: most movements wanted a state within India, and activists regarded loyalty to a regional language and to the nation as compatible. It is important to distinguish language from ethnicity, religion, region, and political identity. Speakers of one language might belong to several religions and castes and live in several provinces, and the same person could hold several identities at once.

After independence, however, the context changed. Partition, achieved on the basis of religious nationalism and accompanied by catastrophic violence, as Chapter 86 described, left leaders anxious that any new principle of territorial division might encourage fragmentation. The integration of princely states was still under way, and administrative capacity was stretched. In 1948 the Constituent Assembly appointed the Linguistic Provinces Commission under S. K. Dar, a retired judge, which reported in December 1948 that reorganization on a purely linguistic basis was not advisable at that time and that administrative convenience and other considerations should take priority. The pressure from linguistic activists led the Congress to appoint its own committee of Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, and Pattabhi Sitaramayya, the JVP Committee, which reported in 1949 that the issue should be postponed because of the risks of the moment, while conceding that if the strong feeling of the people was clear, the question could be reconsidered, and that the Andhra case, subject to the question of Madras city, was the most advanced.

The problem was also administrative, not only symbolic. A citizen expected to petition a district office, attend a school, or follow a legislative debate needed an intelligible language of public business. A province covering several linguistic regions might retain an older administrative center and appoint officials whose language was not that of every locality. Yet linguistic reorganization could produce fresh minorities inside the proposed new boundaries, and borders could not perfectly separate speakers in market towns and mixed districts. Demands were therefore about access to government, educational opportunity, employment, and cultural recognition, but also about the location of capitals, the division of revenues, and control over offices. Acknowledging these competing interests helps explain both the attraction of a linguistic principle and the resistance to applying it mechanically. It also prevents the incorrect inference that a population sharing a language necessarily favored the same party, territorial boundary, or economic program.

Nehru's reluctance reflected specific worries. He feared that reorganization might intensify separatism, distract governments from economic development, harm minorities within new states, and weaken national cohesion at a moment of acute vulnerability. Others, such as Patel, shared the caution. Nehru was not opposed to the idea of language as a basis of cultural life; he was concerned about timing, administrative disruption, and the possibility that linguistic claims would multiply without limit. Yet the activists' case drew on democratic logic: representative government and public education would function best in the language of the people. The tension between these positions framed the next decade.

Historical Vocabulary
Linguistic State and the Part A, B, C, D Classification

A linguistic state is a state whose boundaries are drawn so that the speakers of a major regional language form the majority and the language becomes the language of administration and education. The Constitution of 1950 classified states into Parts A (former British provinces), B (princely states or unions), C (centrally administered states), and D (the Andaman and Nicobar Islands). The 1956 Act abolished this classification.

The term matters because linguistic reorganization was only one of the criteria used in 1956, and several states remained multilingual.

Section 89.14

The Andhra Movement: Potti Sriramulu and the Creation of Andhra State in 1953

The Telugu-speaking movement for a separate province was the oldest and strongest of the postwar linguistic movements. Telugu speakers were the largest group in the Madras Presidency after the Tamils, living in the northern coastal districts, the Rayalaseema region, and, in a separate political unit, the Telangana districts of Hyderabad State. Telugu cultural associations and an Andhra Mahasabha had pressed for a province since the early twentieth century, and Andhra University was founded in 1926. Telugu leaders felt that the Tamil-dominated Madras administration neglected their region.

The central difficulty was Madras city. Telugu leaders claimed it, citing the Telugu-speaking population and the role of Telugu migrants in its life; Tamil leaders insisted that it was historically a Tamil city. The dispute undermined the JVP Committee's offer, divided Telugu leaders, and led to prolonged argument over the capital of a new state. Congress politicians in Andhra, such as T. Prakasam, were themselves divided, and the Communist Party of India, strong in the Telugu region, supported the demand and used it against Congress in the 1952 elections, in which Congress did poorly in the Telugu districts.

The crisis came with Potti Sriramulu, a Gandhian who had worked in Harijan welfare and Gandhi's ashram. He undertook a fast unto death in Madras in October 1952, demanding a separate Andhra state with the question of Madras city set aside. As the central government hesitated, his condition worsened, and he died on 15 December 1952. His death provoked widespread violence and demonstrations in Andhra: crowds attacked railways, government buildings, and other property, and police firing caused deaths. Within days, on 19 December, Nehru announced in Parliament that Andhra State would be formed. The speed of the reversal showed how popular mobilization, and the possibility of disorder, could force a reconsideration of federal policy.

Andhra State was formed on 1 October 1953 under the Andhra State Act of 1953, out of the Telugu-speaking districts of Madras State, with Kurnool as the temporary capital and T. Prakasam as its first chief minister. Madras city remained in Madras State. It was the first state created after independence on a linguistic basis and the first in which the central government yielded to a language movement. Students should distinguish this Andhra State of 1953 from Andhra Pradesh, which was established in 1956 through the addition of the Telugu-speaking territories of Hyderabad State, discussed in the next section.

Andhra's creation also exposed a tension within the principle it seemed to vindicate. Telugu-speakers were not located in a single province: the coastal and Rayalaseema areas that entered Andhra State in 1953 differed in political experience and economy from Telugu-speaking Telangana, then part of Hyderabad State. The loss of Madras city sharpened debates over what a new state needed in order to function, and the choice of Kurnool as capital reflected political bargaining rather than a geographically inevitable outcome. After 1953, advocates of a broader Telugu-speaking state and opponents of an immediate merger advanced rival arguments about employment safeguards, public spending, irrigation, and the position of the less-developed region. These disputes became important when Andhra Pradesh was formed in 1956. Thus the successful campaign for Andhra State established a precedent for linguistic reorganization without answering where every boundary should lie or how regional inequalities within a language community would be addressed.

The Andhra case had two lessons. It strengthened the argument that other language movements might succeed through agitation, and it showed that the Congress could not resolve the issue by delay. It also illustrated the role of a martyr's death in democratic politics, with Sriramulu's Gandhian method of the fast used to put pressure on a government that claimed Gandhi's legacy. The experience encouraged similar movements, among Marathi, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, and Punjabi speakers, and made the government's appointment of a commission in 1953 almost unavoidable.

Section 89.15

The States Reorganisation Commission and the States Reorganisation Act of 1956

On 29 December 1953, the Government of India established the States Reorganisation Commission, with Fazl Ali, a former judge and governor, as chairman, and H. N. Kunzru, a Servants of India Society leader, and K. M. Panikkar, a historian and diplomat, as members. It was directed to examine the whole question of reorganization of the states, with an objective and impartial approach, taking into account the historical background, the existing situation, and the needs of the country as a whole. The Commission toured the country, received a very large number of memoranda, and interviewed thousands of witnesses, and the process itself drew popular attention to boundaries.

Its criteria were not purely linguistic. The Commission emphasized four sets of considerations: the preservation and strengthening of the unity and security of India; linguistic and cultural homogeneity; financial, economic, and administrative considerations; and the successful working of the national plan. It treated language as an important but not the sole factor and was skeptical of the "one language, one state" formula, favoring states large enough to be administratively and financially viable. It also recommended the abolition of the distinctions between the classes of states. It submitted its report in 1955.

The report recommended sixteen states and three centrally administered territories. It proposed that the Telugu-speaking districts of Hyderabad be formed into a separate Hyderabad State, with the question of their merger with Andhra to be considered after the 1961 elections, and that Bombay remain a bilingual state with a separate Vidarbha state carved from Marathi-speaking areas. It rejected the demand for a Punjabi-speaking state, and it proposed the unification of the Malayalam-speaking areas into Kerala and the enlargement of Mysore as a Kannada-speaking state. The recommendations were debated in Parliament, the press, and the streets. The government did not adopt them unchanged. It moved to merge Telangana with Andhra to form Andhra Pradesh, supported by a political agreement among Telugu leaders in 1956, often called the Gentlemen's Agreement, which promised safeguards for Telangana, and it retained a bilingual Bombay for the time being.

The States Reorganisation Act of 1956, together with the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, came into effect on 1 November 1956. The Seventh Amendment abolished the Part A, B, C, and D classification and created a two-tier arrangement of states and Union Territories. The new structure comprised fourteen states and six Union Territories. The states were Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Bombay, Jammu and Kashmir, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Mysore, Orissa (Odisha), Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal; the Union Territories were Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Tripura, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and the Laccadive, Minicoy, and Amindivi Islands.

Section 89.16

Linguistic Federalism after 1956: Political Accommodation and Continuing Regional Demands

The reorganization of 1956 left unresolved the largest case. The decision to keep Bombay as a large bilingual state, with Bombay city a major commercial center, enraged Marathi speakers, who wanted a state of their own with Bombay as its capital, and alarmed Gujaratis, who feared domination or wanted their own state. Marathi activists formed the Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti, a broad coalition that included Communists, socialists, and others, and a Mahagujarat movement, led by Indulal Yagnik among others, demanded a Gujarati state. In January 1956, after the announcement that Bombay city might be treated as a centrally administered territory, riots broke out in Bombay, and police firing caused several dozen deaths. The Union Finance Minister C. D. Deshmukh resigned in protest. In the 1957 elections, Congress performed badly in Bombay city and Maharashtra, underlining the strength of the movement.

Parliament passed the Bombay Reorganisation Act of 1960, dividing Bombay State into Maharashtra and Gujarat, effective 1 May 1960, with Bombay city in Maharashtra and Ahmedabad as the first capital of Gujarat. Y. B. Chavan became the first chief minister of Maharashtra and Jivraj Mehta of Gujarat. The division showed that, after initial resistance, the Congress accepted that persistent popular agitation indicated a durable demand, and that a bilingual compromise was less stable than separate states.

Not every demand followed a linguistic pattern, and Nagaland illustrates the point. In the Naga Hills, the Naga National Council under A. Z. Phizo had demanded independence from the time of Indian independence, and from the early 1950s a violent conflict developed, in which the Indian army was deployed and special powers legislation was enacted in 1958. The Naga hills had a distinct history, since the area had been administered separately under British rule as an excluded area, and its population consisted of many tribes with different languages and a significant Christian community. A group of Naga leaders formed the Naga People's Convention and negotiated with the central government, leading to an agreement in 1960 and the creation of Nagaland as a state on 1 December 1963, with special constitutional safeguards under Article 371A for Naga customary law and land. This was a political accommodation of a regional and ethnic claim by creating a separate state within the Union, but insurgents rejected the settlement, and conflict continued. Nagaland therefore requires an analytical framework involving regional political conflict, ethnic and tribal claims, and special constitutional arrangements, rather than linguistic reorganization alone.

Language conflict also arose over the national language. The Constitution provided that Hindi in the Devanagari script would be the official language of the Union, with English continuing for fifteen years, until 1965. Non-Hindi regions, especially Tamil-speaking areas, feared disadvantage in employment and education. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, founded in 1949 and led by C. N. Annadurai, raised a demand for a separate Dravida Nadu, which it abandoned in 1963 after the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act of 1963 permitted restrictions on advocacy of secession, and it shifted to a regional-autonomy position. The Official Languages Act of 1963 provided for the continued use of English alongside Hindi after 1965, and the issue, along with anti-Hindi agitation in Madras in 1965, shows that linguistic politics was not confined to state boundaries.

The reorganization's effect on political representation was also ambiguous. A language-based administration could make a state legislature more accessible to local voters, encourage publication and schooling in a regional language, and create new opportunities for local political leaders. It did not follow that smaller linguistic minorities automatically received equal influence within the new state. Questions about schooling for minority-language children, recruitment to public service, and the location of universities continued to generate disputes. Nor did the creation of a new state settle economic divisions between prosperous and poorer districts. The central government retained major fiscal and constitutional powers, while new state governments inherited significant responsibilities for education, agriculture, and local development. For this reason, linguistic federalism should be assessed as an adjustment in the structure of political accommodation, not as proof that cultural identity had ceased to be contested.

The consequences of reorganization were wide. Linguistic states made regional languages the languages of schooling and administration, enhanced representation of regional elites, and encouraged regional parties, such as the DMK in Tamil Nadu and later others, which competed with the Congress. They changed the working of federalism: chief ministers became powerful regional figures, and bargaining between center and states grew. But linguistic states did not end disputes over boundaries, minorities, languages, and resources. Border areas such as Belgaum between Maharashtra and Mysore remained contested, minorities within states, including speakers of other languages and religious groups, sought protection, and river-water and capital disputes arose. Demands continued after Nehru's death: the Punjabi-speaking state demanded by the Akali Dal, which the 1956 reorganization had not granted, was conceded in 1966 when Punjab was divided into Punjab and Haryana, with some hill areas transferred to Himachal Pradesh, and Chandigarh designated as a Union Territory. Later demands, including Telangana, are examined in later chapters.

Historical Debate 2

Did Linguistic Reorganization Strengthen or Weaken Indian National Unity?

The question: Did the creation of states on linguistic lines promote fragmentation, or did it accommodate regional identities within the Union and thereby strengthen it?

Interpretation A: Reorganization risked fragmentation. Contemporary critics, including Nehru for much of the early period, feared that linguistic states would encourage regional chauvinism, weaken national feeling, strengthen demands for further division, and create tension with minorities. After Partition, such fears seemed grounded in recent history, and later conflicts over water, borders, and migrants were cited as vindication.
Interpretation B: Accommodation strengthened the Union. Other historians argue that recognizing regional languages gave citizens a stake in the Indian state, channeled movements into constitutional politics, and prevented the development of secessionist movements on the model of other multilingual states. They point to the Andhra and Bombay cases, where concessions followed agitation without the disintegration feared, and to the DMK's abandonment of secession.

Evidence: The evidence includes the Commission's reasoning, the record of the Andhra and Bombay agitations, electoral results, the DMK's change of position, and the continuing disputes over boundaries and resources. Nagaland and other cases show that accommodation did not always succeed where movements demanded independence, not merely a state.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians view the role of democratic concession in holding a diverse state together. The weight of evidence from the period examined here favors the view that linguistic federalism accommodated rather than destroyed national unity, while recognizing that it did not eliminate conflict.

Student task: Use the Andhra movement, the 1956 settlement, and the Maharashtra–Gujarat division to test each interpretation, and identify additional evidence, such as later regional conflicts, that would help decide between them.

Section 89.17

Building the Developmental State: Infrastructure, Science, Education, and Public Health

Beyond the Plans' headline projects, the Nehru governments built institutions intended to sustain development over decades. The infrastructure was large: irrigation capacity expanded through the multipurpose projects and many smaller schemes; installed electricity capacity multiplied several times between 1951 and 1965; railways were extended and modernized, with new locomotive and coach factories; and roads and ports expanded. Public-sector enterprises, created in steel, heavy engineering, machine tools, fertilizers, oil exploration, and other sectors, became major employers and investors. Students should distinguish the announcement or construction of a project from its completion, operating performance, and measurable benefits: dams took years to build, canal networks lagged behind reservoirs, power stations ran below capacity, and some plants suffered delays and cost overruns.

Scientific and technical institutions formed a distinctive part of the state's program. The Council of Scientific and Industrial Research, established in 1942, expanded into a chain of national laboratories in the 1950s, including the National Physical Laboratory and the National Chemical Laboratory. The Atomic Energy Commission, created in 1948 under Homi Bhabha, led to the establishment of the Atomic Energy Establishment at Trombay, where India's first research reactor began operation in 1956; the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research had been founded in 1945. Nehru personally promoted a "scientific temper" as a national goal. Agricultural research expanded through the Indian Council of Agricultural Research and the Indian Agricultural Research Institute, and in 1960 Pantnagar in Uttar Pradesh became the site of the first agricultural university modeled on the American land-grant system, with international collaboration.

Technical education grew with the Indian Institutes of Technology. The first, at Kharagpur, was established in 1951 on the site of a former detention camp and given statutory status in 1956; others followed at Bombay (1958), Madras (1959), Kanpur (1959), and Delhi (1961), each built with assistance from a different country, namely the Soviet Union through UNESCO, West Germany, the United States, and Britain. The University Grants Commission was set up in 1953 and given statutory authority in 1956, and the All India Institute of Medical Sciences was created by an Act of 1956. Universities, engineering colleges, and polytechnics multiplied. These institutions produced engineers, scientists, and managers who would staff public enterprises and, in later decades, private firms and foreign employers.

Public health and primary education received less attention than heavy industry but changed significantly. The National Malaria Control Programme of 1953 and the National Malaria Eradication Programme from 1958 sharply reduced malaria incidence, and vaccination and sanitation programs expanded, with a pilot smallpox eradication program from 1962. Life expectancy at birth, estimated at roughly 32 years in 1951, rose to about 41 years by 1961, though estimates vary. Primary school enrollment grew substantially, and the Constitution's goal of universal elementary education was pursued through state programs, but the goal of free and compulsory education to age 14 was far from achieved, and dropout rates were high, especially among girls and Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe children.

Institution building also required a long interval between expenditure and visible benefits. Training engineers or establishing a research laboratory could create capacities that only became economically significant when firms, farms, and government departments were prepared to use them. Regional concentration mattered: a technical institute or heavy-industrial plant generated direct employment near its location, while a distant village might see only indirect effects through the national budget or later supply chains. Public health and primary education posed a different challenge because their benefits depended on recurrent staffing, maintenance, and access, not merely construction. A history of state capacity must therefore compare the impressive ability to establish institutions with the more uneven ability to make their services available to the population at large.

The distribution of these investments was uneven. Steel, heavy industry, and major river projects were concentrated in particular regions, and states with stronger administration and political influence did better. Irrigation and power served some regions, such as Punjab and the Gangetic delta, far more than dry areas. Urban areas and better-off communities obtained a larger share of educational and medical facilities than villages and poor groups. Displacement by dams and mines imposed costs on tribal and rural communities that were rarely compensated adequately. A balanced assessment recognizes that the institutions of this era were among the state's most durable creations while acknowledging that their benefits were distributed unequally.

Several patterns follow. National income grew faster than in the late colonial decades, and industrial production grew at a much higher rate than agriculture, by an official index at roughly 7 percent a year in the 1950s, led by capital goods and basic industries. Per-capita income, however, grew by only about 2 percent a year in the 1950s because the population was growing at close to 2 percent a year, with the census recording 21.6 percent growth between 1951 and 1961, and per-capita growth fell well below 1 percent in the early 1960s. Foodgrain output rose strongly across the 1950s, helped by new irrigation, expanded cultivation, and favorable weather, but food imports rose in the later 1950s and early 1960s, reaching several million tonnes a year under PL-480 arrangements and over ten million tonnes in 1966. Foreign-exchange shortages recurred.

The growth figures in the quantitative study require more precise interpretation than a ranking of three Plans. The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation's historical summary reports target and achieved annual growth rates of 2.1 and 3.6 percent for the First Plan, 4.5 and 4.3 percent for the Second, and 5.6 and 2.8 percent for the Third. These are retrospective official summary figures, not a claim that every contemporary estimate or later national-accounts reconstruction yields identical rates. A different Planning Commission statistical series gives slightly different outcomes. Moreover, an aggregate growth rate cannot tell us whether output rose because more land was cultivated, yields improved, or new machinery increased industrial capacity. The favorable harvests of the early 1950s contributed to the First Plan's results, while war and drought affected the Third. Economic interpretation must connect rates with the changing composition of production and the conditions under which investment occurred.

Agriculture illustrates the limitations of judging policy by an endpoint alone. Official foodgrain figures rise from about 50.82 million tonnes in 1950–51 to 66.85 million in 1955–56 and 82.02 million in 1960–61, but the movement was not steady: production fell in some intervening years. The 1965–66 drought brought output down to about 72.35 million tonnes from 89.36 million the preceding year. These numbers establish substantial expansion alongside pronounced vulnerability, not continuous food security. They also describe total production in an economy whose population was increasing. To estimate improvement in material welfare, researchers would need to examine per-person supplies, prices, regional access to food, and how much agricultural income actually reached tenants and wage laborers. Similarly, estimates of literacy require explicit census years and definitions rather than treating educational expansion as automatically equivalent to full access.

Industry poses another measurement problem. Steel capacity and machine-building investment represented important new productive assets, but capital-intensive industries generated fewer immediate jobs per rupee invested than many labor-intensive activities. That difference did not make industrial investment futile: a domestic machine-making sector could reduce future import dependence and support infrastructure or agriculture. It did, however, mean that increases in industrial output could coexist with persistent rural underemployment. The central test is not whether industry or agriculture 'won' the planning debate, but how decisions about investment affected employment, foreign-exchange demand, consumer supplies, and public revenue together. These trade-offs help explain why official growth targets were politically important yet insufficient as indicators of development.

Employment was a persistent problem. Capital-intensive industries generated relatively few jobs, and the labor force grew faster than the opportunities available, so that planners acknowledged a growing backlog of unemployment and underemployment. Small-scale and village industries, supported by protection and subsidy, absorbed some labor but at low productivity. Most workers remained in agriculture. Poverty estimates for the period are contested because of differing methods, and researchers generally find no sustained, large decline in the proportion of people below poverty lines in the 1950s and early 1960s, though they differ on exact magnitudes. Industrial output could rise without producing comparable improvements for rural households because the largest share of the poor depended on agriculture, where productivity changed slowly, and on wage labor, where employment was limited.

Section 89.19

The Achievements and Limits of Nehruvian Development: Competing Historical Interpretations

Historians and economists have evaluated the Nehruvian record in different ways, and the differences reflect not only different evidence but different standards. One interpretation emphasizes achievement. State planning, on this view, created an industrial base, with steel, machinery, power, chemicals, and engineering, that India lacked at independence; raised the savings and investment rate substantially; built scientific and technical institutions; and supported an economic independence that allowed foreign policy autonomy. Economists such as Sukhamoy Chakravarty defended the logic of the strategy given the constraints of a poor economy, and supporters stress that the growth rate, though modest by later East Asian standards, was well above that of the colonial period and was achieved under a democracy without large-scale coercion.

A second interpretation emphasizes limits. Critics point to slow growth of agriculture and of productivity, continuing mass poverty, insufficient employment generation, agrarian inequality left largely intact, the neglect of primary education and public health relative to heavy industry, and an industrial licensing system that created delay and protected inefficient firms. Jagdish Bhagwati and Padma Desai, writing in 1970, analyzed the system of controls and its costs, and later work by economists such as Raj Krishna, who coined the phrase "Hindu rate of growth" in the late 1970s, popularized the view that India's growth was held back by policy. Pranab Bardhan offered a political-economy argument that the dominant proprietary classes, namely industrial capitalists, rich farmers, and the professional bureaucracy, constrained the state's ability to extend reform and to mobilize resources, and that public investment was shaped by their competing claims. From the left, other critics argued that planning had strengthened the position of big business and that land reform was insufficiently radical.

It is important to distinguish criticisms made at the time from retrospective evaluations influenced by later debates. Contemporary dissent existed: B. R. Shenoy, a member of a panel of economists advising on the Second Plan, wrote a note of dissent warning about deficit financing and inflation; the Swatantra Party, founded in 1959, campaigned against controls and for greater economic freedom; Gandhian economists urged village-based development; and Communists and socialists urged more radical redistribution. By contrast, judgments that licensing was responsible for the slow growth of the 1970s, or that East Asian export-led growth offered an obvious alternative, rely on decades of subsequent evidence and on the comparative success of other economies that planners in the 1950s could not know.

Historical Debate 1

Was State-Led Industrialization the Most Effective Development Strategy for Independent India?

The question: Given India's resources and constraints in the 1950s, was a strategy emphasizing heavy industry under state leadership the best way to raise living standards and secure independence?

Interpretation A: The strategy built essential long-term capacity. Because capital goods were largely imported, a poor country with a small export base could not grow quickly without developing its own capacity to produce machines, steel, and power. Private capital could not or would not finance such projects, and heavy industry created technical skills and reduced vulnerability to foreign suppliers. The foreign-exchange crisis of 1957–58 supports the claim that dependence on imports was a real constraint.
Interpretation B: The strategy underweighted agriculture, employment, and efficiency. Because most Indians were poor farmers, critics argue that more investment in agriculture, irrigation, rural infrastructure, primary education, and labor-intensive small industry would have raised incomes and employment faster, and that licensing and public-sector management reduced efficiency. The food crises of the mid-1960s illustrate the risk of neglecting agriculture.

Evidence: Evidence includes the Plan documents' assumptions, growth and employment data, the pattern of food and capital-goods imports, the record of public-enterprise performance, and comparisons with other economies, though the last depends on hindsight.

Why it matters: The question affects the broader assessment of postcolonial state-building and the explanations of India's later turn to agricultural technology in the 1960s and to liberalization in 1991, discussed in Chapters 94 and 98.

Student task: Separate the constraints that were identifiable in the 1950s from judgments that depend on subsequent evidence, and explain which evidence would most help decide the question.

The gap between intended outcomes and what administrative institutions could deliver explains much of the variation in results. Planners' models assumed that policies could be executed by competent administrations and responded to by predictable behavior; in practice, the state's capacity was strong in building concentrated projects staffed by engineers and weak in changing the conduct of millions of farmers, landlords, and local officials. Results varied across sectors, with industry and infrastructure advancing faster than land reform, social legislation, or basic education, and across states, with those with stronger administrations or distinctive political histories, such as Punjab, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, and Kerala, showing different patterns from others.

A disciplined comparison of interpretations should also ask what the economy might plausibly have achieved under alternative policies available at the time. Critics of heavy industry argued that greater spending on irrigation, primary schools, public health, and small enterprises might have reached more people sooner. Supporters replied that dependence on imported equipment and the absence of basic industries would impose severe limits on future growth and strategic independence. Neither argument can be settled by selecting a single later growth rate: each rests on assumptions about scarce capital, technology, labor skills, administrative effectiveness, and world markets. Regional differences complicate the national story further. States varied in tenancy arrangements, schooling, local political organization, and administrative records, so formally similar policies could produce very different outcomes. Historical assessment should therefore connect national planning choices to the local institutions that mediated them.

By 1961 the Third Plan faced new pressures. The border war with China in 1962 raised defense expenditure and diverted resources, the war with Pakistan in 1965 added to the strain, and food shortages and the droughts of 1965 and 1966 reduced agricultural output and increased dependence on imports and on aid. The extended treatment of these wars belongs to Chapter 92; here they matter as constraints on economic policy. By the time Nehru died, in May 1964, the first phase of planning was widely seen as having reached its limits, and the question of how to revise strategy, particularly in agriculture, became central.

Review and Historical Synthesis: Development, Social Reform, and Federal Accommodation

This chapter has examined three major historical processes of the first postcolonial decades. The first was planned economic development: the creation of the Planning Commission, the three Five-Year Plans, the mixed economy defined by the industrial policy resolutions, and the construction of steel plants, dams, power stations, laboratories, and technical institutes. The second was a set of efforts to alter agrarian and social hierarchy: the abolition of zamindari, tenancy and ceiling legislation, Bhoodan and Community Development, the constitutional and statutory measures on caste and reservations, and the Hindu Code legislation. The third was the linguistic reorganization of the federation, from the Andhra agitation to the Act of 1956 and the division of Bombay in 1960, with Nagaland as a different kind of accommodation.

These programs demonstrated the expanding ambitions of democratic government. A state with limited resources and a vast, poor, diverse population undertook to industrialize, reform landholding, prohibit untouchability, reform family law, and redraw its internal boundaries, all within a parliamentary system and without suspending elections. That ambition itself, and the willingness to use law and planning to reshape society, was a distinctive feature of the early Republic.

Yet the chapter has also shown why a state capable of building large infrastructure projects could struggle to redistribute land, enforce caste protections, or deliver social services equally. The difference lay in the institutions and relationships through which each measure operated. A steel plant could be built by a public corporation with engineers, foreign collaboration, and central funds, in a concentrated location. Land reform required cooperation from state legislatures and revenue officials who were often drawn from or allied with landowning groups; caste protection required police and courts to act against locally dominant communities; social services required teachers, health workers, and funds distributed across hundreds of thousands of villages. Where implementation depended on local social power, the state's capacity fell short of its declared intentions. Where, as in the linguistic case, political mobilization was strong and accommodation was possible through redrawing boundaries, the state could respond more effectively.

The conclusion is that India's early postcolonial transformation was substantial but uneven. It produced institutions and capabilities that shaped later development, including a base of heavy industry, scientific and technical institutions, a federal structure that accommodated regional identities, and a legal framework of social reform. It also left unresolved inequalities in land, income, caste, gender, education, and political representation, and exposed vulnerabilities in agriculture and foreign exchange that the mid-1960s crises made plain. These unresolved tensions help explain the changes examined in later chapters: the agricultural strategy of the Green Revolution, the rise of caste-based and regional politics, and, eventually, the liberalization of the economy.

Three comparisons make the chapter's conclusion more precise. First, industrial planning often concentrated resources and authority in specialized national agencies; land reform had to pass through state legislatures, local officials, and village relations of power. Second, statutes concerning marriage or untouchability could establish rights immediately in legal language, but their everyday value depended on information, enforcement, and the ability of households to use legal institutions. Third, linguistic reorganization altered the territorial structure of representation in response to organized regional demands, yet it could not eliminate disputes among communities within the new boundaries. These examples show why a single judgment that the state was either powerful or weak is inadequate. Its capacity was strongest in some functions and markedly constrained in others. The resulting achievements and limits arose not simply from Nehru's intentions but from the interaction of federal institutions, organized interests, administrative capacity, and social inequality.

Finally, India's developmental experience can be contrasted with that of its neighbor. Pakistan, examined in Chapter 90, faced a different set of institutional problems, including constitutional instability, the early dominance of the civil-military bureaucracy, and the widening political and economic divide between its eastern and western wings, and these produced a different trajectory from India's parliamentary politics and its continuity of leadership. Comparing the two cases helps students see that the outcomes examined here depended not only on policy choices but on the institutions through which states governed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how independent India tried to convert political freedom and constitutional democracy into economic development, social change, and a workable federal union between 1950 and Nehru's death in 1964. Its central question was how the Republic attempted to overcome colonial underdevelopment and inherited inequality through planning, industrialization, agrarian reform, social legislation, and linguistic federalism, and what those efforts achieved and failed to achieve.

Planning drew on pre-independence traditions, including the National Planning Committee and the Bombay Plan, and was institutionalized in the Planning Commission of March 1950, an advisory body whose plans were executed by ministries and state governments. The First Plan (1951–1956) concentrated on agriculture, irrigation, and reconstruction and, helped by favorable monsoons, exceeded its modest growth target. The mixed economy, defined by the Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956 and the licensing regime of 1951, combined public enterprise in basic industries with a regulated private sector.

The Second Plan, shaped by P. C. Mahalanobis's argument for capital-goods investment, shifted resources toward heavy industry and produced the steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela, and Durgapur, but it ran into a foreign-exchange crisis, and the Third Plan, hit by wars and drought, fell far below its targets. The tension between industrial capacity and agricultural dependence was never resolved in this period.

Agrarian reform was the principal test of democratic reform. The abolition of zamindari, carried out by state laws and protected by the First Amendment, Article 31A, 31B, and the Ninth Schedule, ended the legal category of intermediary landlord but did not necessarily redistribute land to cultivators. Tenancy regulation and ceiling laws were weakened by evictions, benami transfers, exemptions, poor records, and local political influence, and agricultural laborers and sharecroppers gained little. Bhoodan, Community Development, and Panchayati Raj produced new institutions but were often captured by rural elites.

Legal equality met entrenched social hierarchy. Article 17 and the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955 prohibited untouchability, and reservations and Article 15(4) provided special measures for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and backward classes, but discrimination persisted and the Kalelkar Commission's report was not adopted. The Hindu Marriage Act, Succession Act, Guardianship Act, and Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1955–1956, which replaced the single Hindu Code that Ambedkar had championed, reformed marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, and maintenance, while leaving daughters outside the coparcenary and leaving other communities' personal laws unchanged.

Linguistic reorganization began with resistance from Nehru and the Dar and JVP committees, but the death of Potti Sriramulu in December 1952 forced the creation of Andhra State on 1 October 1953. The States Reorganisation Commission of 1953–1955 used language as one criterion among several, and the Act that took effect on 1 November 1956 created 14 states and six Union Territories. The division of Bombay in 1960 and the creation of Nagaland in 1963 showed continuing accommodation of regional demands, and the Punjab reorganization of 1966 showed that it was not finished.

Infrastructure, scientific institutions, technical education, and public health campaigns expanded the state's developmental capacity, though unevenly. Measured performance included national income growth that exceeded colonial rates, rising industrial output, and rising foodgrain production, alongside rapid population growth, modest per-capita gains, persistent poverty, and unemployment. Historians disagree about whether the strategy was effective, and the debate must distinguish contemporary constraints from hindsight.

The chapter's overall conclusion is that early postcolonial transformation was substantial but uneven. The state could build steel plants and redraw state boundaries more readily than it could redistribute land, enforce caste protections, or deliver social services equally, because the former depended on central institutions and mobilized politics and the latter on local social power. The unresolved inequalities and constraints shaped the later agricultural strategy, the politics of caste and region, and the economic changes taken up in Chapters 94 and 98. Chapter 90 turns to Pakistan, whose institutional path contrasted sharply.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what a democratic state tried to do with its newly won sovereignty, and it reveals the difference between legislating change and producing it. The same government that built steel plants and redrew the map of the federation found that land reform, caste equality, and women's legal rights were slower and more contested, because they depended on thousands of local relationships of power.

It also shows how historical change comes from interaction between planners and publics. The Andhra movement and the Bombay agitations compelled a reluctant leadership to change course, and women's organizations and Dalit leaders pressed reforms that officials might otherwise have delayed. Reform was not only a gift from above.

Many of the chapter's themes continue in later volumes: the agricultural strategy of the later 1960s, the rise of caste-based and regional politics, the reopening of personal-law debates, and the liberalization of 1991 all grew from the achievements and limits examined here. Comparison with Pakistan, the subject of Chapter 90, and with the smaller states of Chapter 91, shows that institutions and political settlements shaped different outcomes in different parts of South Asia.

Within the six-volume sequence, the chapter marks the shift from founding the Republic to governing it. Chapter 88 established the framework; this chapter asked what it could do. The answer, substantial but uneven, is the beginning of a long conversation about the state's role that continues to shape South Asian history.
