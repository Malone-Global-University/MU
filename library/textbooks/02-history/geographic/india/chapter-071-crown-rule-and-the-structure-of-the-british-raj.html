Part XIII — The Rebellion of 1857 and the British Raj

Crown Rule and the Structure of the British Raj

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 1 November 1858, a proclamation in the name of Queen Victoria was read at Allahabad announcing that the government of India had passed from the East India Company to the Crown. A proclamation, however, does not collect revenue, try a theft case, or keep a road open. Consider, schematically, how any significant decision of the new order had to travel. A dispatch drafted in the India Office in London reached the Viceroy and his Council in Calcutta, or, in the hot weather, in the hill station of Simla. From there it passed to a provincial government, then to a divisional commissioner, then to a district collector's office. There it finally reached a tahsildar, a village accountant, and a village headman who knew the fields and the families concerned. Reports, petitions, and complaints traveled back along the same chain. This chapter is about that chain: who held each link, what authority they possessed, and why the whole structure worked as well, and failed as often, as it did.

Chapter 70 explained how the Rebellion of 1857 was defeated and how the Government of India Act of 1858 ended Company rule. This chapter begins where that account ends. The question it asks is institutional rather than narrative: once the Crown had assumed responsibility, how was a subcontinent of roughly a quarter of a billion people governed? The 1881 census, the first systematic all-India enumeration, counted approximately 254 million people. The covenanted civil service that nominally directed much of this administration numbered only about a thousand men. Even adding the large European army, the British presence was minute in proportion to the governed.

The answer developed in this chapter is that the Raj was a layered system. At the top stood the Secretary of State and a Council in London, answerable to Parliament but not to any Indian electorate. Below them sat the Viceroy and his Executive Council, the provincial governments, and the district officers who touched the lives of ordinary people. Alongside this directly administered territory stood several hundred princely states, governed by Indian rulers under British supremacy. Beneath and through all of these ran the armies, police forces, and courts that gave institutional form to coercion, and a small, carefully restricted system of consultative councils that gave limited voice to a narrow slice of Indian society.

The chapter's central thesis is that British rule after 1858 was sustained through central imperial direction, provincial and district bureaucracy, indirect authority over princely states, military and police power, codified law, and restricted political consultation, and that its effectiveness depended heavily on Indian institutions and intermediaries even while the system excluded most Indians from sovereign political authority. The Raj was powerful, but it was not omnipotent. Its power lay in organization, in the ability to command revenue and force, and in the cooperation, willing or reluctant, of enormous numbers of Indian clerks, police constables, village officials, landholders, lawyers, and soldiers.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and requires careful handling. Statutes, parliamentary papers, dispatches, provincial administration reports, district gazetteers, census volumes, and civil-service memoirs survive in great quantity. Almost all of them were produced by the state itself, to describe, justify, or improve its own operation. Petitions, vernacular newspapers, the writings of Indian officials, and later memoirs offer some counterweight, but historians must constantly ask who wrote a document, for whom, and what it leaves unsaid. The scholarship of Thomas R. Metcalf, C. A. Bayly, Bernard S. Cohn, David Gilmour, and others, discussed especially in Section 71.19, has shown how much of the Raj's administrative knowledge was itself gathered, filtered, and sometimes shaped by Indian informants and subordinates.

The chapter's principal chronological emphasis is 1858 to approximately 1900. Later institutional developments are noted only where needed to complete the picture. Several topics are reserved elsewhere and are treated here only as far as the institutional analysis requires. Chapter 72 examines railways, agriculture, famine, and the economic debates over colonial rule. Chapter 73 examines caste, census, and race as social classification. Chapter 76 examines the Indian National Congress and organized nationalism. Chapter 77 treats the Partition of Bengal, Muslim political organization, and separate electorates. Chapter 71 provides the administrative framework within which all of those developments took place.

  3. Analyze the recruitment, power, and social character of the Indian Civil Service, and distinguish it from the provincial, subordinate, and specialized services.
  4. Distinguish among presidencies, lieutenant-governorships, chief commissionerships, and non-regulation provinces, and explain why British India lacked a uniform administrative system.
  5. Describe the roles of the district collector-magistrate and of Indian subordinate officials in revenue, order, and justice.
  6. Explain how colonial government was financed and how fiscal decentralization shaped provincial administration.
  7. Assess the scope and limits of municipal and district-board self-government, including Lord Ripon's resolution of 1882.
  8. Compare direct and indirect rule, and explain paramountcy as an evolving doctrine rather than a fixed legal relationship.
  9. Analyze the functions of Residents and Political Agents and the special administration of frontier regions.
  10. Evaluate the army, police, codified law, and courts as instruments of both bureaucratic regularity and colonial coercion.
  11. Interpret the Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892 as examples of consultation without responsible government.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of how the Raj functioned, and explain why formal sovereignty did not equal unlimited administrative control.

Historical Context

The Government of India Act of 1858 (21 & 22 Victoria, c. 106) abolished the East India Company's governing role and vested its territories and powers in the Crown. Chapter 70 narrates the surrounding events: the suppression of the rebellion, the proclamation read at Allahabad, and the immediate reorganization of the army. Chapter 63 explains the Company state that Crown rule inherited, including its district collectorates, revenue machinery, and courts. Chapter 71 does not retell either story. It asks what the inherited machinery became when placed under new constitutional authority.

Much of that machinery was unchanged in 1858. The district collector, the revenue settlement, the subordinate courts, and the dependence on Indian clerks and village officials were all products of the Company's decades of rule, as were the subsidiary alliances and treaty relationships with princes described in Chapter 64. What changed was the political framework above them. The Company's Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished and replaced by a Cabinet minister responsible to Parliament. Parliament's formal supremacy, and the Crown's symbolic position, gave the government of India a new claim to legitimacy, and it was soon extended by the Royal Titles Act of 1876 and the Delhi Durbar of 1877, when Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.

The rebellion also left strong marks on administrative thinking. British officials emphasized security, the loyalty of the army, the cultivation of princes and landholders as supports of order, and caution about interfering with religious custom. At the same time, the postwar decades saw ambitious codification of law, the growth of a regular police, the building of public works, and extensions of consultation that the Queen's Proclamation of 1858 had seemed to promise. These tendencies were not always consistent with one another. The tension between reassuring Indian elites, extracting revenue, and maintaining racial and political hierarchy runs through every section of this chapter.

Technology also changed the conditions of government. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 shortened the sea route between Britain and India, and a direct telegraph cable between Britain and Bombay began operating in 1870, so that instructions could cross in hours rather than weeks. Railways, discussed more fully in Chapter 72, moved troops and officials. These developments strengthened London's capacity to supervise the Government of India, and the Government of India's capacity to supervise the provinces, and thus formed part of the context of increasing administrative centralization.

Section 71.1

The Secretary of State for India and Imperial Authority

The Government of India Act of 1858 replaced the Company's double structure of Board of Control and Court of Directors with a single office: the Secretary of State for India, a member of the British Cabinet. The first holder was Lord Stanley, followed by Sir Charles Wood, the Duke of Argyll, Lord Salisbury (twice, in 1866–1867 and 1874–1878), Lord Kimberley, Lord Hartington, and others, and later by Lord George Hamilton, who served from 1895 to 1903. The office was supported by the India Office in London, a department of clerks and specialists who prepared the dispatches and records through which Indian government was supervised.

Constitutionally, authority flowed from Parliament and the Crown. The Secretary of State was responsible to Parliament, which retained the power to legislate for India, and the Government of India acted under his "superintendence, direction, and control." In practice the Secretary controlled senior appointments, issued policy directives, reviewed the Government of India's legislation, and supervised its finances. He could also instruct the Viceroy on matters of foreign policy, military expenditure, and relations with princes, and the Viceroy's freedom of action was correspondingly limited. Yet the Secretary of State did not personally administer Indian government. He depended on the Viceroy's reports and on advisers, most of whom had long Indian experience, to shape decisions.

Parliamentary accountability was real but weak. India's annual accounts were presented to Parliament, but the "Indian budget" debate usually took place late in the session before a thin House. Members rarely challenged detailed Indian policy unless a scandal, war, or famine drew attention. The salaries of the Secretary of State and the India Office staff were charged to Indian revenues rather than to the British Treasury, a feature criticized by Indian nationalists and some British Radicals as a burden on the Indian taxpayer. Most importantly, the people governed had no vote for the Parliament that ruled them. The exception that illustrates the rule is Dadabhai Naoroji, elected to the House of Commons for Finsbury Central in 1892 as a Liberal, who campaigned on behalf of Indian grievances yet remained a lone voice in a chamber whose majority remained indifferent. His career is examined further in Chapter 76.

For historians, the Secretary of State's position illustrates a basic feature of the Raj. Sovereignty was located in London, but day-to-day knowledge and initiative were located in India. The tension between these two facts, sharpened by the telegraph after 1870, explains many of the disagreements described in Sections 71.2 and 71.3.

Section 71.2

The Council of India and Decision-Making in London

To assist the Secretary of State, the 1858 Act created the Council of India, originally of fifteen members. Eight were appointed by the Crown and seven were initially elected by the Company's Court of Directors, and at least nine of the fifteen were required to have substantial Indian experience. The Council was largely composed of retired Indian civil servants, military officers, and former governors. Its members, popularly nicknamed "the old Indians" by critics, brought deep local knowledge but also a conservative attachment to existing practice. A later Act of 1869 transferred the filling of vacancies to the Secretary of State and limited terms to ten years, strengthening the minister's control over its composition.

The Council's powers were advisory and financial rather than executive. The Secretary of State presided and could overrule it on most matters, but the Act required the consent of a majority of the Council for the expenditure of Indian revenues. This gave the Council a particular role in restraining fiscal initiatives and was intended as a safeguard for Indian finances against British political pressure. The Secretary could, however, send "secret" or "political" dispatches to the Viceroy without prior Council discussion, for example in matters of war and diplomacy. Committees of the Council, organized by subject, reviewed the great volume of correspondence from India and prepared recommendations.

Decision-making followed a recognizable path. A Government of India dispatch, often accompanied by pages of minutes written by officials in Calcutta or Simla, reached the India Office, where it was digested, circulated, and answered. In the era before the telegraph, a dispatch could take weeks to cross, and a reply and a reconsideration could consume months. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the cable link of 1870 changed this. Telegraphic instructions allowed Secretaries of State to intervene more promptly, and Viceroys, notably those with strong personalities such as Lytton, sometimes chafed at what they regarded as supervision by telegraph. Disagreement between London and Calcutta was not unusual, whether over Afghan policy, military costs, or reform. Lord Ripon's liberal initiatives, including the Ilbert Bill and local self-government discussed in Sections 71.8 and 71.16, met uneven support in London and strong opposition from the European community in India.

The institutional distinction between British parliamentary supervision and Indian political accountability is therefore central. Government of India decisions were scrutinized, in principle, by British ministers and a British Parliament. No Indian body had comparable authority. The structure was a system of imperial accountability to the metropolitan state, not accountability to the governed.

Section 71.3

The Viceroy, Governor-General, and Executive Government

In India, supreme authority rested with the Governor-General, who after 1858 was commonly called the Viceroy, a title emphasizing his role as the Queen's personal representative, especially in relations with princes. The first to hold the office under the Crown was Charles Canning, who had served as Governor-General since 1856. The Viceroy was appointed by the Crown on the advice of ministers for a term normally of about five years. Most were British aristocrats or politicians with little or no previous Indian experience; Sir John Lawrence (1864–1869), a veteran of the Punjab administration, was an exception. The Viceroy governed British India "in Council," meaning with an Executive Council of senior members, though he held special powers to override Council decisions in exceptional circumstances.

Canning's reforms of 1859 introduced the portfolio system, assigning individual Council members responsibility for departments such as finance, public works, home affairs, military affairs, and law. The Councils Act of 1861 gave this arrangement statutory form. The Foreign Department, headed by the Viceroy himself in practice, handled relations with princes and neighboring states. A Department of Revenue and Agriculture was created in 1871. The central government functioned through secretaries, notes, and minutes, and the "file" with its sequence of signed comments became the characteristic instrument of administration. The capital was Calcutta until 1911, but from the 1860s the government spent each summer in Simla, a hill station that thereby became a seat of imperial decision-making.

The Viceroys differed in style and priorities, and their differences illustrate how personality interacted with structure. Canning emphasized reconstruction and conciliation after the rebellion and presided over the initial settlement. Lawrence, a strong advocate of economy, favored frontier restraint. Lord Mayo (1869–1872) pursued public works and financial decentralization and was assassinated by a convict in the Andaman Islands in 1872. Lord Lytton (1876–1880) cultivated imperial pageantry, presided over the Delhi Durbar of 1877, and pursued an activist frontier policy that led to the Second Anglo-Afghan War; his government also enacted the Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act of 1878. Lord Ripon (1880–1884) repealed the Vernacular Press Act, promoted local self-government, and sponsored the Ilbert Bill. These are not intended as biographies. They show that the Viceroy's office had a considerable range of discretion within the limits set by the Secretary of State, the Council, and the requirement of maintaining imperial order.

The central government's relations with the provinces were not those of a simple chain of command. The governors of Madras and Bombay were appointed in London, often from British politics, and they corresponded with the Secretary of State and enjoyed considerable standing. Lieutenant-governors, drawn typically from the senior ranks of the civil service, ran the larger provinces. The Government of India coordinated and sometimes overruled them, but it also depended on them for information and execution, an interdependence discussed further in Section 71.19.

Section 71.4

The Indian Civil Service: Recruitment, Training, and Administrative Power

The Indian Civil Service, or ICS, was the name commonly given to the covenanted civil service, whose senior members held the principal posts in district, provincial, and central administration. Competitive examination for entry had been introduced in 1855 following the Charter Act of 1853, and the 1858 Act confirmed the principle. The East India College at Haileybury had closed in 1858. Candidates admitted through the open competitive examination had varied educational backgrounds, although the London examination, its curriculum, and the cost of travel disproportionately favored well-resourced British applicants. Successful candidates completed prescribed probationary study and further examinations, including preparation in Indian languages and law, before entering a service whose prestige, salary, and security were exceptional. For roughly a thousand European officers, it was the centerpiece of imperial administration.

The 1858 Proclamation promised that Indians would be admitted to office irrespective of race or creed. In practice, formal openness met substantial obstacles. The examination was held only in London until the 1920s, requiring Indian candidates to travel to Britain at great expense, in some cases violating caste or family norms regarding overseas travel. The syllabus favored Classical education. The maximum age for candidates was reduced in stages, from 23 in 1855 to 22 in 1860, to 21 in 1866, and to 19 for the examination beginning in 1878, in practice disadvantaging Indians who needed time to acquire English-language schooling and reach Britain. Satyendranath Tagore passed the competitive examination in 1863 and arrived to begin his Indian service in December 1864, becoming the first Indian admitted through this route. In 1869 Surendranath Banerjee and Romesh Chunder Dutt passed the examination. Banerjee's subsequent career is especially well known: his candidacy was challenged over his age and then upheld on appeal, and he was dismissed from the service in 1874 following an inquiry into his conduct, a decision he and many Indians regarded as unjust. He later became a prominent nationalist leader. Dutt completed a distinguished administrative career and later wrote influential studies of Indian economic history.

Historical Vocabulary
Covenanted Civil Service and "Uncovenanted" Service

The term covenanted derives from the contract, or covenant, that Company servants originally signed with their employer. After 1858 it continued to designate the elite service whose members, recruited by examination in Britain, held the highest civil posts. The uncovenanted service comprised the much larger body of officials, many of them Indian, recruited in India and not bound by such covenants, who staffed lower and middle positions.

The distinction matters because the modern phrase "the ICS" can suggest that the British governed India directly through a few dozen administrators. In reality the covenanted service was a small senior leadership, and the bulk of administrative work was done by the uncovenanted, provincial, and subordinate services described below. After the Public Service Commission of 1886–1887 (the Aitchison Commission) the term "Provincial Civil Service" came into wider use for the middle ranks.

Efforts to widen access met resistance. The Statutory Civil Service, created in 1879 under Lord Lytton, allowed nomination of Indians of good family to posts otherwise reserved for the ICS, but it was seen by many Indians as a poor substitute for open competition and was abandoned in 1891. The Public Service Commission of 1886–1887 recommended the creation of a Provincial Civil Service for Indians in the middle ranks, which was subsequently organized. Indian demands for simultaneous examinations in India, one of the earliest issues in organized nationalism (see Chapter 76), were not met in this period.

The ICS must be distinguished from the broader administrative establishment. Indians performed the great majority of administrative work as deputy collectors, subordinate judges, tahsildars, police inspectors, clerks, and revenue accountants. Specialized services such as the Public Works Department, the Forest Department (organized after 1864), the Indian Medical Service, the Post and Telegraph, and the educational services formed further layers. The ICS exercised leadership and supervision, but the ranks below it, and the Indians who filled them, made colonial administration feasible.

Section 71.5

Presidencies, Provinces, and Territorial Administration

Directly administered British India was not a uniform territory. It was a patchwork of provinces whose forms reflected the order and circumstances of conquest, as discussed in Chapters 62 through 65. The three older presidencies, Bengal, Madras, and Bombay, each retained distinct traditions. Madras and Bombay were headed by governors appointed from London with their own executive councils. Bombay, for example, included Sindh after its conquest in 1843. The Bengal presidency, by contrast, was governed by a lieutenant-governor from 1854, a recognition that the Governor-General could no longer personally supervise so large a territory, and it included Bihar and Orissa as well as Bengal proper.

Elsewhere, the structure varied again. The North-Western Provinces were headed by a lieutenant-governor. Awadh, annexed in 1856 and a center of the rebellion, was administered after 1858 by a chief commissioner and in 1877 was joined under one officer who served as lieutenant-governor of the North-Western Provinces and chief commissioner of Oudh; the two were formally combined as the United Provinces in 1902. Punjab, annexed in 1849 (Chapter 65), had its own lieutenant-governorship from 1859. The Central Provinces, formed in 1861 from Nagpur and neighboring territories, were headed by a chief commissioner, as was Assam after its separation from Bengal in 1874. Burma was added in stages: Lower Burma after the Second Anglo-Burmese War of 1852, and Upper Burma after the Third Anglo-Burmese War of 1885–1886. It was governed as a province of British India under a chief commissioner and, from 1897, a lieutenant-governor. British Baluchistan, Ajmer-Merwara, Coorg, and the Andaman Islands, where a penal settlement was established in 1858, were smaller jurisdictions under chief commissioners or other arrangements. In Berar, territories assigned to the British in 1853 remained under the Nizam's nominal sovereignty while being administered by British officials.

A particularly important distinction was that between regulation and non-regulation provinces. In older regions the administration followed formal regulations and a division between executive and judicial authority. In newly annexed provinces such as Punjab, a simpler system developed, in which a district officer might combine revenue, magisterial, and judicial functions and decisions were made with less formality and more personal discretion. The "Punjab model," associated with John and Henry Lawrence, influenced the thinking of an entire generation of officials and was often cited as an alternative to the rigid procedures of the older provinces.

Section 71.6

District Collectors, Magistrates, and the Everyday Machinery of Rule

The district was the fundamental unit of colonial administration. By the later nineteenth century British India contained well over two hundred districts, each typically with a population of around a million people and an area of several thousand square miles, though sizes varied greatly. Above the district stood the division, headed by a commissioner who supervised several districts. Below it lay the subdivision, the tahsil or taluka, and the village. The principal British official in the district was the collector-magistrate, often called simply the collector or, in non-regulation provinces, the deputy commissioner.

Historical Vocabulary
Collector-Magistrate

The collector was originally the official charged with collecting land revenue, an office created in the Company period (see Chapter 63). In many regions the same officer was also the district magistrate, responsible for criminal jurisdiction and the maintenance of order, and in some provinces a district judge or sessions judge handled higher judicial functions separately. The combination of revenue and magisterial powers in one official was the distinctive feature of district government.

The term should not be taken to mean that a single British officer personally supervised a million people. The collector headed an office and a hierarchy of Indian subordinates, and the title denoted a position at the top of that structure rather than the whole of it.

The collector's responsibilities were broad. He supervised the assessment and collection of land revenue and other taxes, kept records of landholding and crops, and managed the revenue and treasury. As magistrate he oversaw police and heard criminal cases, and he was expected to prevent disorder and respond to famine or epidemic emergencies. He inspected schools and public works, and supervised local boards and municipalities as they developed. During the cold-weather touring season, collectors moved through the district with their camps, hearing complaints, inspecting records, and interviewing village officials, a practice that was valued as a means of acquiring local knowledge. Annual administration reports, district manuals, and gazetteers collected and published the results.

Authority traveled down through the system in a chain of subordination. The Government of India issued resolutions and instructions to provincial governments, which passed them to divisional commissioners and then to collectors. Collectors transmitted them to deputy collectors and tahsildars in charge of subdivisions, and then to the village. Information moved in the opposite direction, from the village accountant's records through the tahsildar's office to the collector's office and upward. At each stage, summaries and abstracts condensed what had been reported, which meant that decisions at the top were based on extensively filtered information.

The people who made this system work were overwhelmingly Indian. The collector's office employed clerks, record-keepers, translators, and accountants, many of them drawn from literate castes or communities with long traditions of service to earlier regimes. Deputy collectors and tahsildars carried out much of the revenue and magisterial work. Police inspectors and constables enforced order. In the villages, headmen (known by many regional names, including patel, lambardar, and malguzar) and village accountants (patwari, kulkarni, karnam, and others) collected revenue, kept land records, and reported to the authorities. These Indian officials were more than passive instruments. They controlled the flow of information, mediated between officials and villagers, and could profit from their positions, so that the effective operation of district government depended on their cooperation and could be shaped by their interests. Bernard Cohn and C. A. Bayly have both emphasized how such intermediaries influenced what the colonial state could know, as discussed in Section 71.19.

The records of a revenue dispute show why this chain mattered. In an illustrative case, not a reconstructed individual lawsuit, a cultivating household might contest a measurement, a recorded tenancy, or a demand for arrears. It could begin by approaching a village headman or patwari, whose account of fields and rights shaped what was sent to the tahsil. A clerk translated a petition or copied it into the appropriate register; a revenue officer compared it with the settlement papers; and a disputed finding could eventually reach the collector or an appellate authority. This process created potential remedies, but also expense, delay, and opportunities for record-keepers to influence the facts before a European officer ever saw them. Regional revenue systems changed both the kind of claim and the offices handling it.

In Punjab and much of northern India, settlement and record-of-rights procedures made village papers central to disputes over occupancy and revenue obligations. In parts of Madras, ryotwari assessment made the recorded cultivator and the individual holding especially important; in Bengal, permanent-settlement estates produced different relationships among zamindars, tenants, and government officials. These were not interchangeable systems. Their comparison shows why a general model of collectorate authority must be modified by the law and social organization of particular provinces. The same officer who appeared to embody centralized power was frequently dependent on Indian specialists who could explain local tenure and documentary practice.

A district gazetteer or an annual report converted this administrative work into statistical descriptions of territory. Reports might aggregate rainfall, police cases, criminal convictions, school attendance, revenue balances, and the maintenance of roads. Such numbers allowed comparison across years and provinces, but were never independent of how categories had been defined and how consistently local returns were collected. A student examining one of these reports should ask what it meant to be counted as a cultivator, tenant, criminal case, or school attendee, and which acts of negotiation, coercion, or omission disappeared between the village record and the published total.

Everyday Life and Social History

Meeting the State at the District Office

For most people, the colonial state was not the Viceroy or Parliament but a clerk's desk, a tahsildar's court, or a visiting official's camp. A cultivator might approach the district office to petition about the assessment on his land, to resist an eviction, to dispute a boundary, or to complain about the conduct of a constable. Petitions were written, often by professional petition-writers, in the language of the district or in Urdu, Persian, or English, and the district's formal records show that petitioning was an extremely common form of interaction with officials. The mere fact of petitioning suggests that many people believed the state could be persuaded, shamed, or pressured by formal appeals, even when most petitions were denied.

Not every encounter was initiated by the governed. People met the state when revenue was demanded, when cattle were impounded, when a census enumerator or a forest guard appeared, or when police investigated a crime. These encounters were uneven. A landholder with influence could often secure favorable treatment, while a poor tenant or a member of a marginalized community might have little recourse. Officials, themselves embedded in local networks, were open to influence, and complaints about corruption, delay, and extortion by lower functionaries appear throughout the official record. Historians must read the documents of the district office as the record of a negotiation between power and need, rather than a simple register of command.

Section 71.7

Revenue Administration, Budgets, and the Financing of Government

Colonial government had to be financed from Indian resources, and the structure of revenue shaped the structure of administration. In the mid-nineteenth century the largest single source of public income was land revenue, collected under settlements discussed in Chapter 66. Other significant sources included the opium monopoly, whose exports to China contributed substantial revenue until the late nineteenth century, the salt tax, customs duties, excise on liquor and drugs, and stamp duties. Income taxes were introduced in 1860, abandoned and revived in various forms, and permanently established in 1886 on the basis of a narrow band of higher incomes. The bulk of the burden fell on agriculture and on consumption taxes such as salt, which affected the poor.

Expenditure was dominated by the army, by the cost of civil administration and public works, and by sterling payments abroad known as the Home Charges. These covered interest on debt, pensions for retired officials, stores bought in Britain, the expenses of the India Office, and guaranteed returns on railway investment. The rebellion had increased the public debt by many millions of pounds, and the heavy military establishment left little flexibility for other spending. The relationship between rupee revenue and sterling obligations also made the government sensitive to exchange rates, particularly when the value of silver fell in the later nineteenth century. The question whether these payments constituted an economic "drain" is a major historical debate examined in Chapters 72 and 76. For present purposes the essential point is institutional: revenue demands and fiscal control profoundly shaped what government could do.

The administration of finance was initially highly centralized. Provincial governments requested funds from the Government of India for roads, police, jails, education, and other services, and the central government controlled the budget. Lord Mayo's resolution of December 1870 began a process of fiscal decentralization by assigning fixed grants to provincial governments for specified services and allowing them to manage savings and, to a limited extent, raise local taxes. Subsequent arrangements in 1877 and 1882 gave provinces shares of certain revenue heads. The system's logic was administrative economy and efficiency rather than political devolution: provincial officials, it was hoped, would spend more carefully when working with a defined budget, and local taxes could pay for local services.

Fiscal administration thus linked every level of government. The collector's revenue statement fed into provincial accounts, which fed into the imperial budget presented each year by the Finance Member, and the Secretary of State's Council supervised the whole. Indian critics increasingly noted that the people who paid the taxes had no control over how the revenue was spent. Questions of agricultural conditions, trade, famine, and economic criticism of colonial policy are reserved for Chapter 72.

Lord Mayo’s financial decentralization illustrates how the relationship between central and provincial governments was adjusted without creating autonomous fiscal sovereignty. Selected spending responsibilities and revenue assignments were transferred or allocated to provincial administrations under agreements designed to improve budgeting and administrative incentives. Provinces could arrange expenditures within those constraints, but they remained subject to central oversight and lacked the taxing powers of independent governments. A district official facing a request to repair a road or support a dispensary could therefore encounter limits set by provincial allocations, local rates, and the priorities of an imperial government that also funded a costly army.

Section 71.8

Municipalities, District Boards, and Local Self-Government

Municipal bodies had existed in India's presidency towns since the eighteenth century, and during the 1860s and 1870s the government extended municipal and local administration to many other towns and districts, often as a way of passing the cost of sanitation, roads, lighting, and schools to local taxpayers. Provincial legislation such as the Madras Local Boards Act of 1871 and the Bengal Municipal Act of 1876 created committees of nominated members who administered limited funds under official supervision. These early bodies were instruments of administrative convenience, with official chairmen and official control.

The next stage came under Lord Ripon, whose government issued a resolution on local self-government on 18 May 1882. The resolution encouraged provincial governments to establish networks of local boards, to expand the elective principle where suitable, to appoint non-official chairmen wherever possible, and to reduce the degree of official control from above. The subdivision or taluka was to become the basic unit for rural boards, and the boards were to receive funds and defined responsibilities. Provincial legislation followed in the mid-1880s, including Local Boards Acts in Madras and Bombay and the Bengal Local Self-Government Act of 1885. Municipal reforms also gave elected members a larger place in some cities, though the later Calcutta Municipal Act of 1899 reduced the elected element in the Calcutta Corporation.

The restricted nature of representation requires emphasis. Local participation gave some Indians experience in public finance, sanitation, and education, and it created arenas for emerging political leadership, including figures who later entered nationalist politics. It also provided a way for urban professionals and landed elites to influence local affairs. But local boards were subordinate bodies, dependent on official grants, subject to official veto, and responsible for matters seen as local rather than political. The difference between local consultation and meaningful democratic autonomy was therefore large. Urban social history, including the changing character of cities, is explored in Chapter 75.

Ripon’s proposals did not produce a single standardized municipal constitution. The principle of non-official participation was filtered through provincial legislation, the judgments of district officials, local property qualifications, and the willingness of influential residents to serve. A town might gain an elected component while its chairman and principal administrators remained under strong governmental control. Rural boards had still narrower sources of income and were often compelled to choose among roads, dispensaries, waterworks, and education without powers comparable to those of a provincial government. This makes “self-government” a term requiring examination rather than a description to accept at face value.

The Calcutta Municipal Act of 1899 provides a concrete test of how reversible reform could be. Before that change, the corporation had seventy-five commissioners, fifty of whom were elected; the new arrangement reduced the body to fifty commissioners, only twenty-five elected, and placed important executive powers in a committee and a government-appointed chairman. Indian elected commissioners protested the proposed curtailment. Both the expansion of local participation and its subsequent restriction formed part of the institutional history of the Raj. The dispute also shows why Indian officials and public figures could defend municipal rights as a political principle even while debates formally concerned sanitation and the management of urban services.

Section 71.9

Princely States and the Geography of Indirect Rule

Roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent's area, and perhaps a quarter of its population by early-twentieth-century counts, lay in territories governed by Indian rulers rather than by British provincial administrations. These princely states, numbering several hundred, ranged from large states such as Hyderabad and Mysore to estates of a few villages. The British applied to them the language of treaty and alliance developed in the Company period (Chapters 64 and 65), but after 1858 the relationship was reorganized around the Crown's supremacy.

The states differed enormously. Hyderabad, ruled by the Nizam, was the largest in area and population, a Muslim dynasty presiding over a majority Hindu population, with a long tradition of Persianate court culture and a capable administrative elite. Its minister Salar Jung I, who served from 1853 until 1883, undertook administrative reform, while the British held the Berar districts under a separate arrangement noted in Section 71.5. Mysore, restored to the Wodeyar dynasty in 1881 after half a century of direct British administration, was gradually established as a model state with a reputation for administrative efficiency. Baroda, ruled by the Gaekwad dynasty, was a leading Maratha state in western India. Gwalior, under the Scindia dynasty, was another major Maratha state, whose ruler had remained generally loyal during 1857. Kashmir, created through the Treaty of Amritsar of 1846 (Chapter 65) under a Dogra dynasty, governed a population predominantly Muslim and was strategically situated at the northern edge of the subcontinent. Travancore, in the far south, had a distinctive Hindu temple-centered political culture and a record of notable administrative and educational development.

Princes retained internal government, including their own revenue systems, courts, and armies in some cases, but accepted limits imposed by the paramount power. These typically concerned foreign relations, which were handled exclusively by the Government of India; defense, in that large armies were discouraged or placed under British supervision; succession, which required British recognition; and certain matters of imperial interest such as railways, telegraphs, and extradition. Each state's rights were governed by its own treaties, engagements, and customary practice, so that the category "princely state" conceals wide variation in legal status and actual autonomy. The Government of India dealt with them through the Foreign (later Foreign and Political) Department under the Viceroy.

Why did Britain maintain indirect rule? The rebellion had shown the value of princely loyalty: several rulers had assisted the British in 1857, and Lord Canning is reported to have described them as "breakwaters" in a storm. The Queen's Proclamation promised respect for the rights and dignity of princes. Preserving states cost less than annexing them, gave imperial rule a veneer of traditional legitimacy, and avoided the administrative burden of governing more territory. For the princes, the arrangement offered security of dynasty and territory in exchange for subordination. Historians such as Bernard Cohn and Thomas Metcalf have emphasized that after 1858 the British promoted an image of princes as "natural leaders" of Indian society, a theme reflected in the ceremonial culture of durbars and honors, including the Order of the Star of India (1861), and in the creation of chiefs' colleges such as Rajkumar College at Rajkot (1868) and Mayo College at Ajmer (1875). Later developments in princely politics, including integration into independent India, are examined in Chapter 87.

Indirect rule was therefore neither an absence of government nor a uniform constitutional category. Mysore’s administration after the 1881 rendition could be celebrated as the restoration of an Indian court, but its ruler exercised authority subject to an explicit instrument of transfer and continuing British supervision. Hyderabad maintained extensive ministries and a major court, yet its relationships with the Resident and its financial obligations restricted policy choices. Smaller Rajputana and Central India states experienced still other arrangements. The decisive questions for a historian are which powers a ruler exercised in practice, which matters required British consent, and how disputes were settled when these authorities conflicted.

Section 71.10

British Residents, Political Agents, and Relations with Indian Rulers

The practical contact between the Government of India and the princely states was carried by Residents and Political Agents, officers of the Political Department, many of them recruited from the ICS and the army. A Resident was stationed at a major court, such as Hyderabad, Mysore, Baroda, Gwalior, and (from 1885) Kashmir, where he served as the Viceroy's representative, channel of communication, and observer. In regions with many small states, such as Rajputana and Central India, Political Agents supervised groups of states, with an Agent to the Governor-General at the head of each agency.

Their functions combined diplomacy, supervision, and advice. They communicated British policy and demands, mediated disputes between states and between rulers and subjects, reported on the conduct of courts, and oversaw matters such as succession and the education of minor heirs. The line between advice and command could be thin. A Resident at a powerful court might simply offer counsel, while one at a weaker state, or during a minority, might direct administration closely. Residents also gathered information, and their reports to the Foreign Department are a major source for the history of the states. Like district reports, however, they reflect the perspective of the supervising power and should be read critically.

The Resident's position differed fundamentally from that of the district collector. The collector administered British territory under British law. The Resident dealt with a sovereign ruler, at least in form, and operated through persuasion, pressure, and the ultimate threat of intervention. Princes had their own ministers, courts, and officials, and many were skilled in managing relations with the Residency. Some states cultivated Residents; others resisted. The princely courts could also shelter political activity, patronize artists and scholars, and pursue their own reforms, so that "indirect rule" never meant simple British dictation.

The institutional division between the residency system and provincial administration was also a division of knowledge and legal order. British Indian courts did not ordinarily have jurisdiction in princely territory, and subjects of states did not share the formal legal status of British subjects. This created a fragmented political geography in which individuals, goods, and crimes crossed borders whose significance varied by case, and in which cantonments, railway lands, and similar enclaves added further complexity.

The Political Department’s correspondence also formed a parallel archive of government. Residents recorded interviews at court, ceremonial precedence, disputes among ministers, succession claims, and proposed public works; agents transmitted requests upward to the Viceroy’s Foreign Department. A ruler seeking recognition for an heir might therefore need not only the support of his own court but also a favorable evaluation from a Resident. Conversely, a court with effective ministers and secure alliances could use diplomatic language, appeals to precedent, and competing channels to secure room for maneuver. The relationship was structured by inequality yet was still conducted through negotiation, not simply by decrees copied from a district office.

Section 71.11

Paramountcy, Succession, Intervention, and Imperial Sovereignty

Historical Vocabulary
Paramountcy

Paramountcy denotes the claim of the British Crown to supreme authority over the Indian states, which it exercised through treaties, engagements, custom, and policy. The term came into use in the later nineteenth century and was never defined in a single, precise legal instrument. Britain asserted that paramountcy entitled it to intervene in a state's affairs when imperial interests or good government were thought to require it, while the princes generally argued that it was limited by their treaties.

The concept matters because it shows how imperial sovereignty operated: it was not a fixed constitutional formula but an evolving doctrine that expanded, contracted, and was reinterpreted according to circumstances and the balance of power.

Because paramountcy had no precise legal definition, its content emerged from practice. After 1857 the British abandoned the Doctrine of Lapse, the policy of annexing states whose rulers died without natural heirs, which had contributed to the discontent of 1857 (Chapter 69). In 1860–1862 Lord Canning issued sanads, or grants, to major rulers guaranteeing the recognition of adoptions in default of natural heirs, subject to good conduct. This secured the continuity of dynasties, while reaffirming that succession was recognized by the Crown and conditional on loyalty. The decision removed a major source of princely insecurity and made rulers more willing supporters of the regime.

Yet intervention continued. Succession disputes, minority administrations, and misgovernment gave frequent occasions for it. When a ruler died leaving a minor heir, the British typically established a regency council or direct administration under an official until the heir came of age, with a British-supervised education. Mysore was administered by the British until 1881, when Chamaraja Wodeyar was installed. Hyderabad's regency during the minority of the Nizam Mahbub Ali Khan, who succeeded in 1869 and was formally invested in 1884, was supervised by the Resident. In 1875 the Gaekwad of Baroda, Malhar Rao, was deposed following an inquiry into charges of misgovernment and an alleged attempt to poison the Resident, and a young collateral, Sayajirao III, was installed in his place. In Kashmir, Maharaja Pratap Singh was in 1889 deprived of most of his powers and the state was placed under a British-supervised council, with powers returning to him in 1905. In 1891, in Manipur, a palace dispute led to the killing of a British Chief Commissioner and several officers, and Britain responded by sending troops, executing one of the leaders, and installing a boy ruler under British supervision.

The evidence shows both the retreat from annexation and the persistence of intervention. After 1858 the British largely avoided extinguishing states, but they claimed and exercised rights to depose, to supervise administration, and to shape succession. Princes also gained and lost in other ways. They were drawn into imperial ceremonies, including the Delhi Durbar of 1877, and into military cooperation through the Imperial Service Troops, a program begun in 1889 under which states maintained units available for imperial service. The effect was to bind princes to the empire as partners and subordinates simultaneously.

The limits of princely sovereignty should therefore be stated clearly. Rulers possessed real authority in internal governance and could exercise it vigorously, but their external relations were controlled, their succession was subject to recognition, and their conduct could be investigated and punished. In theory they were sovereign allies, and in practice they were subordinate to a superior power. The phrase "indirect rule" captures this situation, but does not remove the fact that the British retained the ultimate power to decide.

Baroda illustrates both the reach and the ambiguity of intervention. Following allegations against Malhar Rao Gaekwad, British authorities removed him in 1875 and recognized Sayajirao Gaekwad III as the successor. A decision represented as the correction of misrule also confirmed that British authorities could pronounce on the adequacy of a ruler’s conduct and determine who would occupy the throne. Mysore’s rendition in 1881 illustrates the other direction: after prolonged direct administration, authority was restored to the Wodeyar dynasty under stated conditions. Reading these cases together prevents a false choice between claims that the British always annexed troublesome states and claims that adoption guarantees removed their power to intervene.

The adoption sanads themselves should be understood as political instruments rather than unrestricted recognition of an inherent right. They reassured rulers that a lack of natural heirs need not lead to annexation, but the imperial authority still claimed to determine whether conditions for recognition were met. Regency arrangements, treaty obligations, the location of British troops, and access to the imperial political department could limit a ruler without formally extinguishing the state. The tension between promises of dynastic continuity and discretionary power was therefore built into paramountcy.

This structure also affected subjects within princely states. A merchant or petitioner might appeal to a ruler’s courts and then seek the intervention of a Resident, but there was no general entitlement to convert a state’s internal dispute into an appeal before a British Indian court. Social reform, land administration, policing, and public finance might be undertaken by princely ministers for their own purposes or under British pressure; identifying the source of initiative requires attention to each state’s records. The diversity of these experiences makes “princely India” a field of political history in its own right, not merely a margin of directly ruled India.

Section 71.12

Frontier Administration and the Uneven Reach of Colonial Sovereignty

The Raj's boundaries were not a simple line enclosing a uniformly ruled territory. In frontier regions, authority was exercised through a combination of districts, agencies, treaties, subsidies, and military posts. The borderlands in the northwest, the northeast, and the Himalayas each developed distinct systems because they were mountainous, thinly governed by earlier states, and strategically sensitive. Each is treated here only for its administrative form; the wider diplomatic history of the frontier belongs to later chapters.

In the northwest, the districts across the Indus, including Peshawar, Kohat, Bannu, and Dera Ismail Khan, were part of the Punjab and governed under special arrangements and a heavy military presence. The Frontier Crimes Regulation of 1872 allowed jirgas (councils of elders) to decide certain cases and permitted collective punishment and detention without ordinary trial, a framework later criticized as incompatible with equal justice. Beyond the administered districts lay tribal areas managed through subsidies, political officers, and periodic expeditions. British policy oscillated between "close border" restraint, favored by Lawrence, and a "forward policy" seeking influence beyond the frontier, particularly in the 1870s. The Treaty of Gandamak (1879), ending the first phase of the Second Anglo-Afghan War, and the Durand Line agreement with Amir Abdur Rahman in 1893, defined spheres of influence between British India and Afghanistan, which remained a separate state outside the Government of India's administration. In Baluchistan, Robert Sandeman developed a system of working through tribal chiefs with allowances and political officers, and British Baluchistan was organized in 1887; the Khan of Kalat remained a treaty ruler. Gilgit was supervised through a political agency from the 1870s and 1880s.

In the northeast, Assam and the hill districts presented a different pattern. The Garo Hills (1869) and the Naga Hills (1866) were organized as districts, the Lushai Hills were drawn into British administration through expeditions in 1871–1872 and 1889–1890, with the North and South Lushai Hills amalgamated under Assam administration in 1898, and the Inner Line Regulation of 1873 restricted movement between the plains and hill peoples, partly to protect commercial interests such as tea planting and partly to limit contact and conflict. Manipur remained a princely state, as noted in Section 71.11. Relations with the tribes of the hills are discussed further in Chapter 72 in connection with plantations and forests.

In the Himalayas, Nepal remained an independent kingdom governed by the Rana family, which controlled the state through hereditary prime ministers. A British Resident was stationed at Kathmandu, and Nepal's rulers had supported Britain in 1857, for which part of the Terai was returned to Nepal in 1860. Sikkim came under a treaty-based relationship of British protection and intervention after 1861, and Bhutan accepted a subsidy and ceded the Duars after the war of 1864–1865 under the Treaty of Sinchula. Ceylon, by contrast, was governed as a crown colony from London's Colonial Office and not from Calcutta, and the Maldives became a British protectorate in 1887 administered in association with Ceylon. These arrangements show why "British India," "the Indian Empire," and "South Asia" cannot be used interchangeably: the Government of India's authority varied from direct administration through treaty supervision to diplomatic influence over formally independent states.

One way to compare these arrangements is to ask how a legal dispute or armed confrontation would be handled at successive distances from a provincial capital. A settled district could be subject to ordinary criminal courts and revenue officers. A neighboring agency might instead rely on political officers who dealt with chiefs and jirgas and possessed exceptional security powers. Across a treaty boundary, officials had to invoke diplomacy or military pressure rather than claim ordinary district jurisdiction. Such differences were deliberate instruments of imperial policy, not simply unfinished stages on the way to one universal administrative model.

The northern and northeastern frontiers also demonstrate that the colonial category of a “tribe” did not describe a single political condition. Villages and chiefs differed in their forms of authority, their commercial ties to the plains, and their responses to official attempts to restrict travel, extract labor, regulate trade, or establish posts. The Inner Line, for example, determined where outsiders could cross into designated hill territories under permit restrictions, while the Naga Hills district created in 1866 expanded and changed unevenly. The merger of the North and South Lushai Hills in 1898 likewise did not mean that every community or route came under uniform day-to-day control. Such distinctions are essential when studying claims of territorial sovereignty on a map.

Section 71.13

The Imperial Army and the Military Foundations of the Raj

The army was the ultimate basis of imperial authority. Chapter 70 explained the reorganization that followed 1857: the absorption of the Company's European regiments into the Crown's forces, the adoption of a higher proportion of European troops, and the reservation of artillery to Europeans. This section examines the army as a permanent institution rather than retelling those changes. By the 1880s it comprised on the order of seventy thousand British and well over a hundred thousand Indian troops, and it was among the largest charges on the Indian budget.

Command was divided between civil and military authority. The Commander-in-Chief of the Indian Army and a Military Member of the Viceroy's Executive Council oversaw policy and finance. The old presidency armies of Bengal, Bombay, and Madras survived until 1895, when they were consolidated into four commands (Bengal, Madras, Bombay, and Punjab). British regiments were stationed in cantonments, usually separate from the existing towns and designed to provide secure and healthy quarters, and the distribution of troops reflected both internal security needs and frontier strategy. Indian regiments, recruited in different regions and communities, were deliberately mixed, and recruitment increasingly favored certain regions, especially the Punjab, a trend connected with the "martial races" theory discussed in Chapter 73.

The army had several functions. It guarded the northwestern frontier and supported expeditions beyond it, including the Second Anglo-Afghan War of 1878–1880 and the conquest of Upper Burma in 1885. Indian troops were also sent overseas in imperial campaigns, to places such as China (1860), Abyssinia (1868), and Egypt (1882), at the cost of India's revenues, an issue of continuing controversy because it burdened Indian taxpayers with imperial costs. And it provided a reserve of force for internal security: aid to the civil power in cases of riot or rebellion was a routine function of garrisons.

The relationship between army and civil administration was close. Magistrates could call on troops to maintain order, and the stationing of garrisons influenced the local economy and urban geography through cantonment bazaars. At the same time the army's size and cost shaped the whole fiscal system described in Section 71.7. The reliance on Indian soldiers is another example of the colonial state's dependence on the people it governed: the empire's most important coercive instrument consisted mainly of Indian men under British officers.

The army also imposed a heavy institutional commitment on colonial finance. Cantonments required land, transport, supplies, medical facilities, and communications infrastructure, while campaigns beyond the administered frontier could become disputes between the Government of India and London over who should pay. Indian soldiers served in formations whose organization and recruitment policies were shaped by the memory of 1857, but the army was not recruited through a general system of compulsory conscription. Its practical importance lay in the state’s ability to station, move, provision, and command forces—and in its reliance on Indian soldiers and support personnel to do so.

Section 71.14

Police, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Internal Security

While the army guarded the frontier and the last resort of order, the police handled routine coercion. The Police Act of 1861, based on the recommendations of a commission of 1860, established the framework of provincial police forces. Each province had an Inspector-General, and each district a Superintendent of Police, subordinate in matters of general order to the district magistrate. Below the superintendent were inspectors, sub-inspectors, head constables, and constables, mostly Indian, who staffed police stations (thanas). The design drew on the model of the Irish Constabulary, a paramilitary and centralized force, rather than the civilian police of London, and was intended to provide the state with a disciplined organization throughout the countryside. Senior police posts were reserved largely for Europeans and, from 1893, were filled by competitive examination in Britain.

In villages, much policing was still done by watchmen (chowkidars), who were paid by the community or the state and reported local crime. They were part of a long-standing tradition of village responsibility, but they now operated under the new framework. Police officers' main tasks included investigating crimes, arresting suspects, collecting information on local conditions, and supporting revenue officers. The subordination of police to the magistrate meant that policing and administration were connected, though officials debated whether this tie ensured discipline or enabled abuse.

The police were notorious for corruption and extortion, as official commissions and Indian critics repeatedly noted, and many ordinary people feared the constable. Low pay, limited supervision, and the pressure to produce convictions encouraged misconduct, including coerced confessions. The same system, however, was relied upon by many people who sought protection from theft and violence and who used the police and courts in their own disputes. Policing was therefore experienced very differently by officials, by property owners, and by poor and marginalized communities who were subject to surveillance and suspicion. Colonial legislation such as the Criminal Tribes Act of 1871, discussed in Chapter 73, extended this system of classification and control to whole communities.

Intelligence and surveillance expanded across the period. Officials collected information through district reports, informants, and the monitoring of the vernacular press, as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878 (repealed in 1882) illustrates. The Arms Act of 1878 restricted the possession of weapons. Fingerprint identification, developed by Edward Henry in Bengal, was adopted by the Government of India in 1897. Specialized intelligence organizations developed more fully after 1900. The sedition provision, Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code, was added in 1870, and older regulations, such as Bengal Regulation III of 1818, allowed detention without trial of "state prisoners." These powers distinguished ordinary public-order administration from exceptional security powers, and they would become central to the repression of nationalism discussed in later volumes.

The police system was not a simple ladder from constable to commissioner. In rural districts a station house, its officers, and its informants were often the nearest visible representatives of the state. Reports about theft, cattle disputes, labor mobility, or alleged disorder were transformed into written complaints and case records that might reach a magistrate. These processes could provide recourse for complainants; they could also expose villagers to intimidation, bribery, selective attention, or coercion in the recording of evidence. Examining the gap between the rules of police organization and the social relations surrounding a station is therefore as important as memorizing the provisions of the Police Act.

Section 71.15

Legal Codification and the Construction of Colonial Law

One of the most distinctive projects of the Raj was the codification of law. The idea had Company-era roots: the first Indian Law Commission, led by Thomas Babington Macaulay in the 1830s, produced a draft penal code in 1837, and further commissions continued the work. After 1858 the effort was renewed with the aim of creating clear, uniform, and accessible law. The Indian Penal Code, based on the 1837 draft, was enacted in 1860 and came into force in 1862. Criminal procedure was codified in 1861 and revised in 1872, 1882, and 1898, and civil procedure was codified in 1859 and revised later. The Indian Evidence Act of 1872, largely the work of James Fitzjames Stephen, who served as Law Member of the Viceroy's Council, set out rules for proof in courts. The Contract Act (1872) and the Transfer of Property Act (1882) extended codification into civil law.

Codification was defended on administrative grounds. Clear rules would make decisions predictable and reduce the arbitrary discretion of officials, and uniform procedure would allow a small body of judges to administer justice across vast territory. Officials also believed that law would serve as a symbol of imperial benevolence and of the rule of law, which they contrasted with what they described as arbitrary Indian rule. These claims were not purely rhetorical. Codes did standardize practice and gave lawyers and litigants documents on which to base arguments.

At the same time, law was an instrument of colonial coercion and exclusion. The codes were drafted by British lawmakers with little Indian participation, drawing on English law as a model, and they often substituted the logic of the state for local custom. Criminal law gave officials wide powers of arrest and punishment, and some provisions, such as the sedition clause and the Criminal Tribes Act, were applied to political and social control. The law also embedded distinctions of race and status in its procedures, as Section 71.16 explains. Historians therefore describe colonial legal codification as simultaneously creating bureaucratic regularity and consolidating a system of inequality.

The distinction between drafting, enactment, and commencement matters in this legal history. Macaulay’s penal-code project preceded the rebellion by decades; the enacted Indian Penal Code of 1860 did not come into operation until 1862. Likewise, successive versions of criminal and civil procedure reflected changes in the way judges, police, and litigants interacted. To describe the law as created suddenly by Crown rule would erase the Company-era work on which it depended. The transition after 1858 instead accelerated and reorganized an existing legislative enterprise.

The Evidence Act of 1872 attempted to make rules about relevance, admissibility, witnesses, and proof intelligible across different categories of legal proceeding. For a land dispute, the issue could become whether a registry, deed, or witness statement was admissible and what weight the court should attach to it. In a criminal prosecution, the handling of confessions, testimony, and official records mattered both to claims of fairness and to the police’s capacity to obtain convictions. A code specified rules, but the encounter between those rules, language translation, lawyers’ resources, and local power could produce unequal outcomes. Students should distinguish the formal text of an Act from its implementation in a particular court.

Codification also had limits. Personal law, regional custom, special frontier regulations, and the laws of princely states remained distinct. Not all territories were governed by the same courts or the same set of statutes. Consequently, the “uniform law of the Raj” is a useful description of an ambition in some fields, not an accurate inventory of everything applied throughout South Asia. The chapter’s treatment of personal law and jurisdiction in §71.16 should be read as part of that complex legal geography.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Company Practice to Crown Structures

What Changed

  • Constitutional authority shifted from a commercial corporation to a Cabinet minister responsible to Parliament, and a Viceroy acting in the Crown's name
  • Law was codified on a much larger scale, and High Courts replaced the older Supreme Courts and company appellate courts
  • Police were organized under a statute and provincial hierarchies, and the army was reorganized after 1858
  • The doctrine of lapse was abandoned, and princes were reassured and drawn into imperial ceremony
  • Legislative councils gave a limited consultative role to nominated non-officials

What Stayed the Same

  • The district collectorate, revenue settlements, and dependence on Indian subordinate staff remained the basis of administration
  • Executive and legislative authority remained in official hands with no responsible government
  • Subsidiary treaty relationships with princes continued in modified form
  • Land revenue remained a core source of income

Who experienced change most directly? Princes, who gained security of succession; litigants and defendants, who met new codes and courts; and educated Indians, who saw councils but not power. Who experienced greater continuity? Most villagers, who continued to deal with revenue and police officials much as before. Why the pattern matters: The Crown takeover is best understood as a reorganization and consolidation of an existing system rather than a complete break.

Section 71.16

Courts, Judicial Hierarchies, Personal Law, and Legal Inequality

The Indian High Courts Act of 1861 provided for High Courts to replace the Supreme Courts in the presidency towns and the Company's appellate courts. High Courts opened at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras in 1862, followed by Allahabad in 1866 and others later. Below them stood district and sessions judges, subordinate judges and munsifs for civil matters, and magistrates for criminal cases. The final appeal lay to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London. In the countryside beyond the presidency towns, known as the mofussil, the structure varied by province, and in non-regulation areas administrative officers often exercised judicial functions.

Indians participated heavily in this system. Lawyers, called vakils and pleaders, formed a growing profession; many of the early leaders of nationalism were trained lawyers. Indian judges were appointed to the High Courts, among them Shambhu Nath Pandit at Calcutta in 1863, T. Muthuswami Iyer at Madras in 1878, and Syed Mahmood at Allahabad in 1887. Large numbers of Indians, from landholders to peasants and women, used the courts as litigants, often pursuing disputes over land, debt, inheritance, and marriage, and the volume of litigation indicates that courts were a significant part of social life. They also sent petitions and appeals, sometimes successfully.

The colonial legal system recognized personal law for matters of family and inheritance. Following a policy that dated to the Company period, courts applied Hindu law to Hindus and Muslim law to Muslims in matters such as marriage, adoption, and succession, with other communities (such as Parsis and Christians) later governed by their own statutes. This was not a simple preservation of tradition. The courts relied on selected textual authorities and on English-trained judges' interpretations, and the abolition of the posts of court pandits and maulvis in the 1860s shifted authority from traditional experts to British-trained judges. The resulting "Anglo-Hindu" and "Anglo-Muhammadan" law was a hybrid that combined textual sources, precedent, and colonial assumptions. Regional custom, especially in Punjab, continued to matter, and the Punjab Laws Act of 1872 gave it a statutory standing.

Equality before the law was, in practice, limited. Under discriminatory rules then in force, Indian district magistrates and sessions judges faced restrictions on trying European British subjects outside the presidency towns. The racial qualifications and provisions for jury composition varied by procedure and changed after the Ilbert Bill compromise; they should not be described as identical rules for every charge and court. Historians, notably Elizabeth Kolsky, have documented cases in which Europeans charged with violence against Indians received light punishment, a pattern criticized by Indian newspapers and, at times, by officials. Access to justice was also unequal because litigation was expensive and slow, and the poor were disadvantaged against wealthier opponents.

The Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883 exposed these tensions. The Law Member, Courtenay Ilbert, proposed in 1883 to allow Indian district magistrates and sessions judges to try European British subjects in the mofussil, a power that Indian judges already held in the presidency towns. The measure, which sought to remove what was seen as a racial anomaly, provoked a fierce campaign by the European community, including planters and officials, who held public meetings and denounced the bill. The government compromised in 1884, allowing European defendants to demand a jury of which at least half the members were European. The affair demonstrated both the principle of racial privilege in the legal order and the willingness of educated Indians to organize in response, and it became an important background to the formation of the Indian National Congress in 1885 (see Chapter 76).

It is also essential to distinguish a formal right to sue from the practical ability to sustain litigation. A petitioner might have to obtain copies of records, pay fees, secure a translator or pleader, travel to hearings, and wait through adjournments and appeals. A wealthy landholder or commercial firm was better placed to absorb those costs than a small tenant or day laborer. At the same time, litigation sometimes allowed subordinated parties to challenge local officials or powerful neighbors, particularly when written title, tenancy, and procedural rules supplied a legally cognizable claim. This ambivalence helps explain why courts attracted substantial Indian participation even under an unequal regime.

Appointments of Indian High Court judges illustrate a related tension between institutional inclusion and racialized limits. Shambhu Nath Pandit took a judgeship at Calcutta in 1863; T. Muthuswami Iyer was appointed to the Madras High Court in 1878; Syed Mahmood began to serve at Allahabad in an officiating capacity in 1882 before his substantive appointment in 1887. These achievements should be understood against the much larger body of Indian subordinate judicial officers and legal practitioners. A few high appointments did not amount to equal access to senior judicial power, and the Ilbert controversy revealed how sharply European residents could resist proposals that challenged racial privilege in criminal jurisdiction.

Section 71.17

Legislative Councils and Consultative Government after 1861

The Indian Councils Act of 1861 reshaped the way law was made. For legislative purposes the Viceroy's Executive Council was enlarged by additional members, between six and twelve in number, nominated by the Viceroy, of whom at least half were to be non-officials. The Act also restored legislative powers to the Madras and Bombay governments and provided for a legislative council for Bengal (established in 1862); councils for the North-Western Provinces and Oudh followed in 1886, and for Punjab and Burma in 1897. In 1862 the first Indian nominees to the Viceroy's council included the Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala, and Sir Dinkar Rao of Gwalior, men chosen for their rank and loyalty.

The councils were not parliaments. They met to consider bills placed before them by the executive, and they had no power to discuss broader policy or to question the executive, and the Viceroy could veto legislation, which the Secretary of State could also disallow. In cases of emergency the Viceroy could issue ordinances with the force of law for a limited period without council approval. The nominated non-official members, whether British businessmen, landholders, or Indian notables, were chosen by the executive, and their role was advisory. The system followed a conception, often expressed by officials, that the government should be informed of public opinion and local conditions without being constrained by representative institutions. It also reflected the belief of many officials that Indians' natural leaders were landed aristocrats and princes, not the new English-educated professional class.

The Viceroy's executive responsibilities remained distinct from the legislative process. The same men who sat as the Executive Council also dominated the Legislative Council, with the nominated additional members added to them for legislative sessions, so that the executive controlled the agenda and the outcome. Nonetheless the councils had some significance. They provided a forum where draft laws were published and debated, where nominated Indians could voice objections that sometimes led to modification of measures, and where educated Indians began to see consultation as a precedent on which to build demands.

Specific nominations demonstrate the kind of information the government sought. In 1862 Canning selected the Maharaja of Patiala, Raja Deo Narain Singh of Benares, and Dinkar Rao, formerly a minister in Gwalior, for the enlarged imperial legislative council. They were recognizable figures within princely, landed, or administrative circles rather than representatives elected by a broad public. The Bengal Legislative Council, convened in 1862, also included nominated Indian notables. Their presence could introduce locally informed objections or lend prestige to a measure, but the executive retained control over who sat, what questions came forward, and what ultimately became law.

The councils’ design thus created a constrained route for influence. A member might criticize a draft provision affecting commercial practice or local government and potentially secure a modification. Yet the council could not direct the ordinary executive, force the resignation of ministers, or compel a general debate on administrative policy. In legal terms, legislative membership was not the same as responsible government; in political terms, the experience of sitting in a council could nonetheless expose the limitations of consultation and strengthen arguments for elected participation. The distinction between these two outcomes is a recurring theme in the transition toward Chapter 76.

Section 71.18

Limited Political Representation and the Indian Councils Act of 1892

By the late 1880s, educated Indians were pressing for a wider share in government, and the newly founded Indian National Congress (Chapter 76) called for the expansion of the councils and for elected members. The Indian Councils Act of 1892 responded cautiously. It enlarged the Viceroy's legislative council, to between ten and sixteen additional members, and the provincial councils, with Madras, Bombay, and Bengal given up to twenty and the North-Western Provinces and Oudh fifteen. It allowed members to discuss the annual financial statement, though not to vote on it or move amendments, and to put questions to the government on matters of public interest, subject to prior notice and with restrictions on supplementary questions.

The Act also introduced a limited and indirect method of selection. The Viceroy's council retained a majority of officials, and the Act spoke not of "election" but of nominations made on the recommendation of specified bodies. In the provinces these bodies included municipalities, district boards, universities, chambers of commerce, and associations of landholders; some members of the Viceroy's council were recommended by the provincial councils and by the Calcutta Chamber of Commerce. The practice was in effect a form of election by narrow constituencies, though officially denied that name. Those who participated were therefore a small circle of property-holders, professionals, and merchants, and the large majority of the population, including women, most peasants, and the poor, had no say.

The reform's significance was limited but real. Debate on the budget gave nominated and recommended members a regular forum for criticizing government finance, and the power of interpellation (questioning) allowed them to expose administrative practice. Gopal Krishna Gokhale, for example, later used the budget discussion in the Imperial Legislative Council as a platform for criticism of fiscal policy. Yet the official majority remained, the executive's decisions were unaffected, and the councils had no control over the government. The reforms of 1909, which expanded the councils further and introduced separate electorates for Muslims, belong to Chapter 77, which examines them alongside the political struggles that surrounded them.

The numbers in the 1892 Act require careful reading. Ten to sixteen referred to additional members of the Governor-General’s legislative council, not the total membership of the Viceroy’s executive and legislative establishment. The new upper limits for provincial additional members likewise sat alongside ex officio positions and depended on the province. Without this distinction, a table of council sizes can misleadingly compare unlike bodies. The Act expanded the space for questioning and discussion while preserving the executive’s ability to make decisions without obtaining an elected majority.

Indirect nomination was equally specific. Under the implementing arrangements, certain municipal bodies, district boards, universities, chambers of commerce, and associations could recommend candidates, but those arrangements were not a general adult franchise. Calcutta’s Chamber of Commerce enjoyed a channel into the imperial council that illustrates the privileged position of organized commercial interests. Indian landholders and educated professionals gained opportunities for participation, yet most laborers, cultivators, women, and many poorer townspeople had no comparable route. The politically important contrast was between the appearance of representation and the narrow social basis on which it operated.

Budget discussion made a difference because it put governmental choices into a public, although highly regulated, record. A member could criticize a proposed expenditure or ask why an administrative practice persisted, but could not vote down the budget, compel ministers to resign, or use unrestricted supplementary questions to demand an immediate answer. This limited power still created opportunities to document grievances. The framework is best understood as a small institutional opening whose importance became clearer when Indian participants began pressing for rights beyond the boundaries that the statute and its rules allowed.

Section 71.19

The Colonial State and Its Limits: Authority, Dependence, and Indian Agency

Taken together, the institutions described in this chapter formed a state that was formally supreme but practically constrained. Its formal sovereignty was never in doubt: Parliament could legislate for India, the Viceroy could issue orders, and the army could defeat any organized rival. But capacity to act on the ground depended on factors beyond law. The state was thin at the base, with a few thousand European officers and soldiers spread across a subcontinent. It had to rely on Indian clerks, accountants, police constables, and village officials; on landholders and local notables who collected revenue and maintained order; on Indian businesses and financiers who provided credit and supplies; and on Indian lawyers and litigants who gave the courts their business.

This dependence shaped what the state could know and do. C. A. Bayly, in work on information networks, argued that the East India Company and the Raj inherited and relied upon Indian systems of intelligence and record-keeping, and that gaps and distortions in this knowledge contributed to administrative failure and panic, as in 1857. Bernard Cohn emphasized that colonial knowledge, produced through surveys, censuses, language study, and ceremonial, was bound up with the exercise of authority. Thomas Metcalf, in Ideologies of the Raj, traced how British officials after 1857 emphasized difference and hierarchy, including the idea of governing through traditional Indian aristocracies. David Gilmour's study of the ICS, The Ruling Caste, shows how the service's members saw themselves and how their working lives were shaped by routine, isolation, and dependence on subordinates. These scholars differ in emphasis, and none argues that the Raj was merely a bureaucratic machine.

Tensions within the state compounded its limits. Centralization from London and Calcutta clashed with the discretion exercised by provincial and district officers. Policy statements, such as the promises of the Queen's Proclamation or the liberal language of Ripon's resolution, met the racial attitudes and self-interest of many British officials and the European community. Indian elites were courted as supporters but excluded from sovereign decision-making. The state's reliance on law and procedure restrained arbitrary behavior but also gave Indians instruments, such as petitions and lawsuits, that they used to defend their interests. Exclusion from real political power, meanwhile, produced the discontent that nationalist movements would organize.

For these reasons, the Raj should be described as powerful without being treated as omnipotent. It could levy taxes, recruit soldiers, imprison defendants under colonial law, and legislate, and it recruited and deployed armed forces and possessed considerable coercive capacity. But it often acted cautiously, and it operated through negotiated relationships with intermediaries whose interests did not coincide with its own. Indian agency, in the sense of the decisions, strategies, and cooperation of Indians in every position, was a condition of colonial power, whether in service, in negotiation, or in resistance. The distinction between formal sovereignty and administrative capacity is central to understanding why the Raj endured for nearly ninety years after 1858 and why it ultimately proved impossible to sustain against an organized and widely supported political movement.

The historian’s task is therefore to move among different scales of evidence. A statute specifies formal authority; an India Office dispatch records a decision in London; a provincial report describes implementation; and a district case file reveals the choices of subordinate officers and the efforts of residents to influence them. None substitutes for the others. An official report describing a successful revenue settlement, for example, might conceal a dispute over recorded rights that appears only in a petition or lawsuit. Conversely, an individual case cannot establish how frequently the same problem occurred elsewhere without comparative records.

This methodological distinction also prevents a caricature of the civil service. Some officers were conscientious and technically skilled; others were negligent, prejudiced, or abusive; and individual administrators could be more or less willing to hear Indian objections. But the system’s unequal distribution of authority was not a mere sum of personal behavior. London, Calcutta, provincial secretariats, and districts determined who could issue commands and whom they answered to. The army and courts supported that hierarchy, while the everyday work of government relied heavily on Indians who could pursue their own objectives through employment, petitioning, litigation, evasion, and negotiation.

The scholars introduced in this chapter should be read as offering complementary but distinct questions, not as five authorities making a single undifferentiated claim. C. A. Bayly emphasizes how intelligence and communication networks linked imperial administrators to existing Indian information systems; Bernard Cohn studies the categories and practices through which colonial institutions represented Indian society; Thomas R. Metcalf examines ideologies of hierarchy and difference; David Gilmour reconstructs the careers and culture of the senior administrative service; and Elizabeth Kolsky scrutinizes the relationship between racial violence and legal protections. The works and publication details are identified in the source guide following the chapter, allowing students to trace these arguments without mistaking a short summary for a page-collated quotation.

Cause and Effect

Imperial Centralization: Coordination and Exclusion

Long-term causes

The Company's evolution into a territorial state, the growth of a bureaucratic and revenue-based system, and a metropolitan tradition of supervision through Parliament and the Cabinet.

Short-term causes

The Rebellion of 1857, which created fear of insecurity and a desire for firmer control, and the technological changes of the 1860s and 1870s, including the telegraph, Suez Canal, and railways.

Immediate effects

A unified constitutional structure under the Secretary of State and Viceroy, portfolio-based central government, codified law, a statutory police, and a standard hierarchy from province to district.

Long-term consequences

Better administrative coordination, but also the exclusion of Indians from policy-making, resentment of racial hierarchy, and a growing demand for representative institutions.

Historical complexity: The same institutions that gave the Raj coherence also created the grievances on which nationalism fed. The outcome cannot be reduced to a single cause, such as the rebellion or technology, and it was not inevitable: officials debated the pace and limits of reform throughout the period.

Historical Debate

Did the Raj Function Primarily through Centralized Bureaucracy or through Negotiated Dependence on Indian Elites and Institutions?

The question: How should historians characterize the way the Raj worked in practice: as an efficient, centralized bureaucratic system, or as a state whose authority depended on negotiation with Indian intermediaries?

Interpretation A — Bureaucratic centralization: Emphasizes the formal structure: a hierarchy of command from the Secretary of State to the district officer, codified law and procedure, a professional elite service, a unified army and police, and communication technology that tightened supervision. In this view, the post-1858 state was more systematic and capable than any predecessor. The phrase "steel frame," used of the ICS by David Lloyd George in 1922, reflects an older and more admiring version of this view.
Interpretation B — Negotiated dependence: Emphasizes that British officials were few, that they relied on Indian subordinates, landholders, financiers, and local knowledge, and that formal rules were applied unevenly and bent by local circumstances. Work by Bayly, Cohn, and others on information, ritual, and collaboration supports this view, as does the evidence of petitions, litigation, and princely politics that show Indians shaping outcomes.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on statutes, official correspondence, and administration reports, and on accounts of the practical conduct of district and princely administration. They differ in how much weight they give to formal institutions as against informal relationships, and in how they interpret the same evidence, such as petitions, which may signal either the reach of the state or the ability of subjects to use it.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians explain both the endurance of the Raj and its eventual collapse, as well as how they understand Indian agency. Most scholarship now combines the two views, treating bureaucracy and negotiation as aspects of the same system.

Section 71.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined the political, territorial, administrative, military, and legal machinery through which Britain governed India after 1858. At the top, sovereignty was located in Parliament and the Crown and exercised through the Secretary of State, advised by the Council of India. In India, the Viceroy and Executive Council directed a central government organized by departments and supported by a small, elite civil service and a much larger Indian bureaucracy. Provinces differed in form and tradition, from the presidencies of Bengal, Madras, and Bombay to lieutenant-governorships, chief commissionerships, and non-regulation territories, and each in turn was divided into divisions, districts, subdivisions, and villages. The district collector-magistrate, supported by Indian subordinates and village officials, was the point at which the state met the people.

Alongside directly governed territory lay the princely states, whose rulers retained internal authority under a doctrine of paramountcy that was never precisely defined and that Britain used both to protect and to discipline them. Frontier regions were governed through special systems that reflected strategic priorities. The army and the police gave institutional form to coercion, and codified law and a hierarchy of courts provided a framework of procedure that regularized government while embedding racial privilege. Legislative councils and local boards offered limited consultation to a narrow band of Indian society but did not give them control over policy or the executive.

Four contradictions run through these institutions. Extensive bureaucratic power coexisted with reliance on Indian intermediaries, whose cooperation could never be taken for granted. Legal standardization coexisted with unequal status, visible in the privileges of European defendants and the Ilbert Bill affair. Consultative institutions existed without responsible government, so that Indians were heard but not obeyed. And princely autonomy existed under imperial supremacy, so that rulers were simultaneously partners and subordinates. These contradictions explain both the stability of the Raj through much of the later nineteenth century and the pressures that would later undermine it.

These institutions also created conditions for what followed. The financial structure described in Section 71.7, the dependence on revenue from agriculture and consumption taxes, and the burden of military and sterling obligations link directly to Chapter 72, which turns from the machinery of government to its material consequences: railways, agriculture, famine, and the debates over the economic effects of colonial rule. The social classifications used by the state, examined in Chapter 73, grew out of the administrative need to know and categorize those it governed. The councils and local boards provided arenas that Chapter 76 shows educated Indians using to organize. Chapter 71 therefore stands as the institutional foundation for the economic, social, and political history of the Raj in the chapters that follow.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how Britain governed India after the transfer of authority from the East India Company to the Crown in 1858. Its central argument is that the Raj was a layered system, combining central imperial direction, provincial and district bureaucracy, indirect authority over princely states, military and police power, codified law, and restricted political consultation, and that its effectiveness depended on Indian institutions and intermediaries even though it excluded most Indians from sovereign political authority.

At the summit of the system, the Government of India Act of 1858 created the Secretary of State for India, a Cabinet minister responsible to Parliament, and the Council of India, a body of experienced advisers with a particular role in supervising Indian expenditure. Parliamentary supervision was real in form but weak in practice, and it was a supervision on behalf of the metropolitan state rather than accountability to those governed. The opening of the Suez Canal and the telegraph link of 1870 strengthened London's ability to intervene and sharpened disagreements with Viceroys.

In India, the Viceroy and Governor-General headed an Executive Council organized by portfolios, a system introduced by Canning. Viceroys such as Lawrence, Mayo, Lytton, and Ripon pursued different priorities within the limits set by London and by the Council, showing that personal style mattered but operated inside structural constraints. The Indian Civil Service, recruited by examination in London, supplied the senior administrators. Barriers of examination location, age limits, and discrimination restricted Indian entry, while Indians performed most administrative work in provincial, subordinate, and specialized services.

Directly administered British India consisted of presidencies, lieutenant-governorships, chief commissionerships, and non-regulation provinces whose administrative forms reflected the circumstances of conquest. The district, headed by a collector-magistrate, was the central unit of government, and its functioning relied on Indian clerks, deputy collectors, tahsildars, police, and village officials. Authority moved downward and information upward through a hierarchy that filtered and condensed what officials knew.

Government finance rested on land revenue, opium, salt, customs, excise, and other taxes, and on heavy military and sterling obligations. Lord Mayo's resolution of 1870 and later arrangements decentralized financial responsibility to the provinces, mainly for reasons of administrative economy. Local self-government, notably under Ripon's resolution of 1882, offered limited elected participation in municipal and district bodies, but under property-based franchises and official supervision, and for the declared purpose of political education.

Indirect rule through the princely states reflected both the lessons of 1857 and the economy of preserving existing rulers. Paramountcy was an evolving doctrine rather than a fixed legal relationship. The abandonment of the Doctrine of Lapse and the adoption sanads of 1860–1862 gave princes security, but depositions, regencies, and interventions at Baroda, Kashmir, Manipur, and elsewhere showed that British supremacy was not nominal. Residents and Political Agents were the instruments of that supremacy. Frontier regions, from the northwest to the northeastern hills, were governed under special arrangements, and neighboring states such as Nepal, Bhutan, and Sikkim had differing relationships to the Government of India.

The army and the police gave institutional form to coercion. The army, reorganized after 1857 and heavily Indian in composition under British officers, guarded frontiers, served in imperial campaigns, and supported the civil power. The police, organized under the Act of 1861, handled routine order and surveillance and were often feared and distrusted. Exceptional powers, such as sedition law, detention under older regulations, and press and arms restrictions, extended the state's coercive reach.

Codification of law, in the Penal Code, procedure codes, and Evidence Act, provided bureaucratic regularity and a framework of standardized procedure, while embedding colonial priorities and racial privilege. High Courts, subordinate courts, and the recognition of Hindu and Muslim personal law structured the legal order, and Indian lawyers, judges, and litigants participated extensively. The Ilbert Bill controversy of 1883–1884 exposed the limits of legal equality and the political mobilization of the European community against reform.

Legislative councils created in 1861 and enlarged in 1892 gave limited consultation to nominated or recommended non-official members, including a small number of Indians, without control over the executive and with an official majority maintained. They represented consultation without responsible government.

The Raj's strengths and limits were two sides of the same structure. It commanded formidable force and revenue but was thinly staffed and dependent on Indian cooperation. Scholars such as Bayly, Cohn, Metcalf, and Gilmour have shown how knowledge, ritual, ideology, and the working lives of officials shaped that dependence. Chapter 72 now turns to the economic consequences of the system described here.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Secretary of State for India
The British Cabinet minister who, after 1858, held ultimate executive responsibility for Indian government and was answerable to Parliament.
Council of India
A body of experienced advisers created in 1858 to assist the Secretary of State; its consent was required for expenditure of Indian revenues.
Viceroy
The title of the Governor-General as the Crown's representative, especially in relations with princes; head of the Government of India.
Executive Council
The Viceroy's senior advisory and administrative body, whose members held departmental portfolios.
Portfolio system
The arrangement, introduced by Canning in 1859, by which individual Council members headed departments such as finance, law, or military affairs.
Indian Civil Service
The examination-recruited elite administrative service whose members held senior posts in districts, provinces, and the central government.
Covenanted service
The senior civil service bound by covenant, as opposed to the uncovenanted and provincial services that performed much of the administrative work.
Provincial Civil Service
The middle-ranking service, developed after the Public Service Commission of 1886–1887, in which many Indians served.
Presidency
One of the older divisions of British India (Bengal, Madras, Bombay), the latter two headed by governors with executive councils.
Non-regulation province
A recently annexed province, such as Punjab, governed through simpler procedures that gave officers combined powers and wider discretion.
Collector-magistrate
The principal British district official, responsible for revenue, order, and supervision of subordinates.
Tahsildar
An Indian revenue and administrative official in charge of a subdivision (tahsil or taluka) of a district.
Mofussil
The countryside and district towns outside the presidency capitals, used in colonial legal and administrative language.
Home Charges
Sterling payments made by the Government of India in Britain for debt interest, pensions, stores, and the India Office.
Provincial settlements
Arrangements, beginning with Mayo's 1870 resolution, that assigned fixed grants or revenue shares to provincial governments.
Local self-government
The system of municipal and district boards with a limited elected element, promoted by Ripon's 1882 resolution as political education.
Princely state
A territory ruled by an Indian prince under British paramountcy and outside direct provincial administration.
Paramountcy
The British claim to supreme authority over the Indian states, exercised through treaties, custom, and policy and never precisely defined.
Resident / Political Agent
British officers stationed at princely courts or supervising agencies, who conveyed British policy and reported on states.
Doctrine of Lapse
The pre-1857 policy of annexing states whose rulers died without natural heirs, abandoned after the rebellion.
Sanad
A formal grant or charter; the adoption sanads of 1860–1862 guaranteed recognition of adopted heirs to ruling chiefs.
Frontier Crimes Regulation
A law of 1872 allowing jirga adjudication and collective punishment in the northwest frontier districts.
Codification
The systematic organization of law into authoritative written codes, such as the Indian Penal Code of 1860.
Personal law
Law governing family matters such as marriage and inheritance, applied by communal identity, as in colonial Hindu and Muslim law.
Ilbert Bill
A 1883 proposal to let Indian judges try Europeans in the mofussil, modified in 1884 after European protest.
Legislative council
A consultative body of officials and nominated or recommended non-officials, established or expanded by the Acts of 1861 and 1892.
Interpellation
The right to put questions to the executive, granted in limited form to council members in 1892.
Ordinance
A temporary law issued by the Viceroy in emergencies without council approval.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how an apparently remote and thinly staffed imperial government exercised power over hundreds of millions of people. The answer lay less in the personal brilliance of viceroys than in layered institutions: a bureaucracy that reached the village, a police and army that enforced order, laws that standardized procedure, and princely and landed intermediaries who gave the system roots in Indian society.

It also reveals why the Raj contained the seeds of its own challenge. Education, law, and consultation created Indians with the skills and ambitions to claim a larger political role, while racial hierarchy and the denial of responsible government frustrated them. The district office, the courtroom, the council chamber, and the municipal board were places where many Indians learned how the system worked and how it might be argued against.

The institutions described here also shaped the postcolonial world. The Indian Civil Service, police, codified law, provincial structure, and princely integration were inherited, in modified forms, by independent India, Pakistan, and later by Bangladesh, as later volumes will show. Recognizing these continuities does not excuse colonial rule or dismiss the changes that followed independence. It helps explain why institutional legacies of the Raj remain subjects of debate.

Within the six-volume sequence, Chapter 71 stands between the military and constitutional story of Chapter 70 and the economic and social transformations of Chapters 72 through 75. Chapter 72 now examines the material consequences of the system described here: railways, agriculture, famine, and the competing interpretations of colonial economic change.
