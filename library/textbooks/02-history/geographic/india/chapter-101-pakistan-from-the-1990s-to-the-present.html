Pakistan from the 1990s to the Present

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Historical cutoff: 19 November 2026

Chapter Opening

On the evening of 12 October 1999, soldiers moved into the television station and the prime minister's residence in Islamabad, and a government that held the largest parliamentary majority in Pakistan's history ceased to exist within hours. Less than a quarter-century later, on 13 November 2025, a President of Pakistan signed into law an amendment to the Constitution that created a Chief of Defence Forces, a post to be held by the army chief, and a new Federal Constitutional Court. Between these two dates lie elections, constitutional amendments, a lawyers' movement, a war on terror, a devastating flood, a prime minister removed by parliament, and a former prime minister in prison. This chapter explains how these events were connected, and why none of them settled the question that runs through them: who holds final authority in Pakistan. The emergency imposed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in October 2026 offered an additional and direct test of federalism, emergency authority, and provincial electoral mandates.

Chapter 100 traced India's transformation from the Congress-led liberalization of the 1990s to the coalition politics of the mid-2020s. This chapter gives Pakistan an equally serious national history over the same period. The two countries began the 1990s with separate political institutions and a shared, and sharply contested, strategic environment, and their trajectories diverged. India's parliamentary system, despite crises, remained governed by regular elections and civilian control of the army. Pakistan's did not follow a straight line toward either democratic consolidation or authoritarian rule, which is the chapter's central interpretive claim.

The chapter's problem is the relationship among electoral legitimacy, military and intelligence institutions, constitutional authority, economic governance, provincial and regional differences, and ordinary social life. Its narrative is organized in five divisions: the decade of civilian governments that ended in nuclear tests, the Kargil conflict, and a coup (1990–1999); the Musharraf regime and the war on terror (1999–2008); the restoration of civilian government, the Eighteenth Amendment, and the counterinsurgency (2008–2018); the rise and fall of Imran Khan and the disputed election of 2024; and the coalition government, constitutional transformation, and economic position of 2024–2026. A final synthesis addresses why elections and reforms did not settle the distribution of authority.

The chapter relies on a mixture of evidence. Constitutional texts, court judgments, election commission records, census returns, and international financial documents are primary sources of unusual precision for this period. Newspaper reporting, memoirs by participants, and the reports of domestic and international monitors fill gaps, but the memoirs of military and political actors serve their authors' interests and must be read critically. For the most recent years, the chapter distinguishes carefully between documented facts, allegations, and provisional assessments, and it identifies the research-as-of date of 19 November 2026.

The chapter also avoids two simplifications. One treats Pakistan's history as a sequence of coups; the other treats the military as the sole political actor. Civilian leaders, courts, provincial governments, religious parties, social movements, and foreign governments all appear as actors with their own interests. Equally, the chapter does not reduce Pakistan to its politics: sections on the economy, the pandemic, floods, schooling, gender, and the experience of conflict in the western borderlands show how political choices affected households.

Chapter boundaries are respected. The strategic history of nuclear deterrence and Kashmir belongs to Chapter 105, and the chapter treats the 1998 tests and the Kargil war only as far as they explain Pakistan's domestic politics. The region-wide economic and demographic comparison belongs to Chapter 106, social institutions across South Asia to Chapter 107, environmental history to Chapter 109, and foreign relations to Chapter 110. Chapter 102 will turn to Bangladesh, which separated from Pakistan in 1971 (Chapter 92).

Students should finish the chapter able to explain the sequence of events from 1990 to 2026, to distinguish among militant organizations and among kinds of military influence, to evaluate competing interpretations of contested events, including Kargil and the 2024 election, and to understand why the question of authority remains open.

Historical Context

Pakistan came into being in 1947 (Chapter 86) as a state with two wings separated by about 1,600 kilometers of Indian territory, and the political quarrel between its eastern and western halves culminated in the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971 (Chapter 92). Chapter 90 examined Pakistan's early constitutional instability and the military takeover of 1958; Chapter 95 traced Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, the Constitution of 1973, Zia-ul-Haq's coup of 1977, Islamization, and the Afghan-Soviet war. This chapter begins when Zia's death in 1988 reopened competitive politics.

Four inheritances shaped the 1990s. The first was a constitutional order that, after the Eighth Amendment, gave the president power to dismiss governments. The second was a military that had ruled for a total of about three decades, had become a major economic and social institution, and regarded relations with India and Afghanistan as matters of national security under its charge. The third was the legacy of the Afghan war: an infrastructure of armed groups, the arms and drug economies of the border regions, and the intelligence services' networks. The fourth was a political economy marked by low tax collection, heavy defense and debt-service spending, and dependence on external assistance and remittances.

Geography matters as well. Punjab, home to more than half of Pakistan's population, dominates the National Assembly and the army's officer corps. Sindh, centered on the Indus delta and the port of Karachi, has its own political traditions. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the former tribal areas border Afghanistan along a frontier whose legitimacy Afghan governments have not accepted. Balochistan, the largest province by area and the least populated, holds minerals and a long coastline and has seen recurring insurgencies. The Indus and its tributaries underpin agriculture and make the country vulnerable to floods, as 2010 and 2022 showed.

Historians should also bear in mind that terms such as "the establishment" and "hybrid regime" are analytical shorthand. They describe a pattern in which the army, intelligence agencies, senior judiciary, and civil bureaucracy exercise influence over political outcomes without any single institution or individual directing them. This chapter uses the terms sparingly and defines them as it goes.

Division I

Democracy, Instability, and Nuclearization, 1990–1999

Why the return of elected governments did not resolve the struggle over political authority, and how a decade of civilian rule ended in nuclear tests, armed conflict, and another coup.

Section 101.1

Benazir Bhutto, Nawaz Sharif, and the Return to Electoral Politics

When Pakistan returned to elected government in December 1988, following the death of General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq (Chapter 95), it did so under a constitutional order that had been substantially reshaped during his rule. The most consequential inheritance was the Eighth Amendment, adopted in 1985, which added Article 58(2)(b) to the Constitution of 1973. That provision empowered the president to dismiss the prime minister and dissolve the National Assembly if, in the president's opinion, the government could not be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The amendment turned the presidency into a standing alternative to parliamentary authority. Alongside it stood an army that had ruled directly for eleven years, controlled a large share of public expenditure, and regarded foreign and security policy, especially toward India and Afghanistan, as its own domain. The elected governments of the 1990s therefore governed within a system of dyarchy, in which authority was divided among the prime minister, the president, and the army chief, and in which the balance among them was never settled by agreement.

The decade was dominated by two parties and two personalities. The Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP), founded by Zulfikar Ali Bhutto in 1967, was led after 1988 by his daughter Benazir Bhutto, the first woman to head the government of a Muslim-majority state. The PPP drew its strongest support from rural Sindh, from sections of Punjab's rural and urban poor, and from a political memory of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's execution in 1979 and of resistance to Zia. Its rival was a coalition that crystallized around Mian Muhammad Nawaz Sharif, an industrialist from a Lahore business family whose political career had been advanced under Zia in Punjab. Sharif led the Islami Jamhoori Ittehad (IJI), an alliance assembled in 1988 to contest the PPP, and from 1993 the Pakistan Muslim League (Nawaz), or PML-N. Its base lay in urban Punjab, in trading and industrial communities, and in conservative rural constituencies; its program combined market-oriented economic policy, infrastructure, and a more explicitly Islamic political language. The two parties were not ideological opposites in every respect, but each built its organization around a leader and a regional core, and each regarded the other as an illegitimate occupant of office.

The sequence of governments is easily summarized and instructive in its repetition. President Ghulam Ishaq Khan dismissed Bhutto's first government in August 1990 on grounds of corruption and misgovernment, and the elections of October 1990 brought Sharif to office. In April 1993 the same president dismissed Sharif. The Supreme Court, in a judgment under Chief Justice Nasim Hasan Shah, restored him in May 1993, a rare judicial check on the dissolution power. But the president and the prime minister could not work together, and in July 1993 both resigned under pressure brokered by the army chief, General Abdul Waheed Kakar. The elections of October 1993 returned Bhutto. Her second government fell in November 1996, when President Farooq Leghari, a PPP loyalist she had helped elect, dismissed her. Elections in February 1997 gave the PML-N a very large majority in the National Assembly, on a low turnout, and Sharif returned as prime minister. The decade thus saw two completed presidential removals (of Bhutto in 1990 and 1996), an attempted presidential removal of Sharif in April 1993 that the Supreme Court reversed before his negotiated resignation in July, and the military overthrow of Sharif in October 1999. Distinguishing these outcomes matters: judicial reinstatement did not resolve the underlying contest for authority.

What changed between the 1980s and the 1990s was less the formal structure than the political environment. Competitive party politics resumed, with a more active press and regular elections, and each party learned to govern in the shadow of the other's potential alliance with the presidency or the army. The military's role was usually indirect. Intelligence agencies, notably the Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI), were widely believed to have assisted in assembling the IJI in 1988 and 1990, a claim later given support by testimony in Pakistan's Supreme Court in the long-running Asghar Khan case, which examined the distribution of funds to politicians in 1990. Historians should distinguish this documented episode from the broader and more speculative claim that the army orchestrated every government's rise and fall: the evidence shows influence, pressure, and opportunism at critical junctures, not a single master plan.

The political economy of the two parties also shaped governance. Both governments promised development and both found their budgets constrained by debt service and defense spending. Bhutto's second government encouraged private power producers, whose long-term contracts later became a source of fiscal and political controversy. Sharif's governments favored privatization, motorway construction, and the expansion of business credit. In each case, the political need to reward supporters and the weakness of tax collection limited the state's capacity to invest in education and health, a pattern with long consequences examined in Section 101.6 and Section 101.14. The chapter's controlling problem emerges already in the 1990s: elected governments possessed electoral legitimacy but not undisputed authority over the instruments of coercion, the courts, or the economy.

Section 101.2

Political Instability and the Crisis of Governance in the 1990s

The repeated dismissals of the 1990s were not simply acts of presidential caprice, nor merely the consequence of corrupt politicians, as each side alleged. They arose from a structural conflict that the constitutional text had left open. Under Article 58(2)(b), an elected government could be removed by a single officeholder who was not himself elected by the public, and who could invoke a broad standard. Presidents cited corruption, mismanagement, the breakdown of law and order in Karachi, and conflict with the judiciary. In each dismissal the caretaker arrangement promised new elections within ninety days, and the caretaker governments of the decade often announced accountability measures aimed at the dismissed party. Accountability thus became a political weapon. Bhutto and her husband, Asif Ali Zardari, faced corruption cases that continued for years, while Sharif's family businesses attracted investigation under Bhutto's government. Whether particular allegations were justified is a matter for the legal records, many of which were never resolved in a final court judgment; what matters historically is that the language of corruption legitimated removal, while the process of accountability was selective.

Karachi provides the clearest illustration of how governance failed at the level of daily life. Pakistan's largest city and commercial center had been transformed by migration, first of Urdu-speaking refugees from India after 1947 (the muhajirs), then of Pashtuns, Punjabis, Sindhis, and Baloch seeking work. The Muttahida Qaumi Movement (MQM), founded in the 1980s to represent muhajir interests, won overwhelming support in the city's Urdu-speaking neighborhoods, and its relations with the state, the PPP, and rival ethnic and sectarian groups produced recurring violence. A military operation against the MQM began in 1992 under Sharif's first government and was succeeded by a second, police-led crackdown under Bhutto in 1995–96, with large numbers of killings in the city, including extrajudicial killings. The violence of these years should not be reduced to ethnic hatred. It involved competition over land, transport, extortion, party control of neighborhoods, and the use of armed party wings, with sectarian and criminal networks adding further layers. The 1990s left Karachi as an unresolved test of whether the federal government and the provinces could provide a common framework of security.

The judiciary's role became sharper in the second half of the decade. The 1993 restoration of Sharif had shown the Supreme Court's willingness to review dissolutions, though its rulings in other cases, including the validation of the 1990 and 1996 dismissals, showed that judicial independence was inconsistent. After the 1997 election Sharif used his large majority to amend the Constitution. The Thirteenth Amendment in April 1997 repealed Article 58(2)(b) and curtailed the president's power to appoint the service chiefs, restoring the balance toward parliament. The Fourteenth Amendment introduced penalties for members who defected from party discipline, which strengthened the prime minister against his own legislators at the cost of internal party debate. These reforms were substantial, and they also illustrated a theme that recurs through this chapter: constitutional change served institutional advantage as well as principle. A prime minister who had been dismissed under one set of rules removed those rules, then concentrated authority in his own office.

The confrontation with the Supreme Court followed. Sharif's government clashed with Chief Justice Sajjad Ali Shah over judicial appointments and contempt proceedings. In November 1997 a crowd of party supporters entered the Supreme Court building during a contempt hearing against the prime minister, and the Court's own bench divided, with other judges declaring the chief justice's appointment invalid. Sajjad Ali Shah was removed, President Leghari resigned in the same period, and Sharif's ally Rafiq Tarar became president in December 1997. Sharif had now eliminated the two principal institutional checks on his office, the dissolution power and the chief justice. The same logic produced attempts to control the press and to restrict the political space of critics, and in October 1998 his government introduced a Fifteenth Amendment that would have made the Shariah the supreme law and given the government broad powers to enforce it. The amendment passed the National Assembly but stalled in the Senate, where the PML-N lacked a majority, and critics regarded it as an attempt to concentrate authority through religious legitimacy.

By 1998 the government faced an economic crisis that political centralization could not solve. The economy had grown unevenly through the decade, with fiscal deficits, external debt, and weak revenue mobilization. The sanctions that followed the nuclear tests (Section 101.3) threatened a balance-of-payments emergency, and the freezing of foreign-currency bank accounts in May 1998 damaged confidence among depositors, including overseas Pakistanis. The failure of both major parties to complete a term in office, and the continuing influence of the army, appeared to many Pakistanis as evidence of a democratic system that produced neither stability nor accountability. This perception, widespread by 1999, helps explain why the October coup met little organized resistance and some public relief, a fact that does not mean it was inevitable or that the military's rule would be better, as Division II shows.

Section 101.3

Pakistan's Nuclear Tests of 1998

Pakistan's nuclear program had been pursued since the 1970s, after the loss of East Pakistan and India's 1974 test (Chapter 97). By the late 1980s Pakistan was widely believed to possess the capability to assemble nuclear weapons, a capability that successive governments neither confirmed nor renounced. The United States' tolerance of this ambiguity during the Afghan war ended with the Pressler Amendment of 1990, which cut off most American economic and military aid because the president could no longer certify that Pakistan lacked a nuclear device. The aid cutoff was one of the contexts for the dissatisfaction with American policy that shaped Pakistani opinion in the 1990s. Chapter 105 examines nuclear deterrence and strategic competition in detail. This section addresses the tests as a decision within Pakistani politics.

India conducted a series of nuclear tests at Pokhran on 11 and 13 May 1998, shortly after the election of a government led by the Bharatiya Janata Party (Chapter 99). Indian leaders emphasized the threat from China and, in some statements, from Pakistan. In Pakistan, the tests produced immediate pressure on Sharif's government to respond. Opposition parties, religious parties, sections of the press, and many officials argued that failure to test would show weakness and would invite coercion. Other officials, including some in the foreign and finance ministries, warned of the economic cost, and the United States, led by President Bill Clinton, offered inducements and threatened sanctions. The decision was made after a period of deliberation among the prime minister, the military leadership, and the nuclear scientists. On 28 May 1998 Pakistan announced five tests in the Chagai Hills of Balochistan, and on 30 May a sixth. The government declared 28 May a national day, later called Youm-e-Takbeer. The tests were popular across most of the political spectrum, and Sharif, who had been struggling in the aftermath of the Supreme Court crisis, gained temporary political benefit.

The costs followed. The United States and other governments imposed sanctions under non-proliferation legislation, and Japan and others suspended aid. Pakistan's foreign-currency reserves were low, and the government froze foreign-currency accounts, an emergency measure that Pakistan's financial sector remembered for years. A state of emergency was declared in the immediate aftermath, restricting fundamental rights, and the sanctions were not lifted for the Pakistani case until after September 2001. The tests also had effects on the regional security environment, including a more explicit arms race, and on the domestic politics of national identity, where nuclear capability became a symbol of sovereignty for a broad public. Historians sometimes contrast this popular pride with the persistent poverty of social indicators in the country. The contrast should be presented as a historical tension rather than a verdict: both the strategic rationale offered by officials and the opportunity costs argued by critics have to be recorded.

The tests also shaped civil-military relations. The nuclear and missile programs had always been under the control of the army and associated scientific institutions, whatever formal civilian oversight existed. The tests reinforced the military's claim to be the guardian of national security, while giving a civilian prime minister a legitimate role as the head of government who authorized them. Sharif's subsequent decision to pursue dialogue with India, discussed in the next section, depended on the strategic confidence that nuclear capability provided, but the army's leadership viewed that dialogue with suspicion.

Primary Source Window

The Lahore Declaration, 21 February 1999

Source type: Bilateral diplomatic declaration, with an accompanying Memorandum of Understanding on confidence-building measures · Signed: Lahore, 21 February 1999, by Prime Ministers Nawaz Sharif and Atal Bihari Vajpayee · Where to consult: the text is reproduced in the archives of the Government of India's Ministry of External Affairs and the Pakistan Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and in standard compilations of South Asian treaties.

Read the primary document: United Nations Peacemaker — Lahore Declaration (21 February 1999); original document record (external resource; access may vary by institution).

Context: Vajpayee traveled to Lahore by bus on the inaugural service of a new Delhi–Lahore route, nine months after both states had tested nuclear weapons. The visit, which included a visit to the Minar-e-Pakistan, was widely read as a political gesture. It occurred while elements of the Pakistani army were preparing the operation in the Kargil sector (Section 101.4).

Paraphrase of the content: The two governments reaffirmed their commitment to the Simla Agreement of 1972 and the United Nations Charter; recognized that nuclear capability added a dimension of responsibility to the relationship; agreed to take immediate steps to reduce the risk of accidental or unauthorized use of nuclear weapons; undertook to intensify consultations on security and non-proliferation; and committed to resolving all issues, including Jammu and Kashmir, through bilateral dialogue. The memorandum provided for advance notification of ballistic missile tests and other confidence-building measures.

Analysis: The declaration shows that the Sharif government, at least formally, sought to stabilize the relationship with India after the tests, and that the Indian government accepted language that included Kashmir. It also illustrates the problem of reading a diplomatic text as if it recorded the whole of state policy. Within months, the army's operation in Kargil contradicted its spirit, and Sharif's critics, and many Pakistanis, interpreted the declaration as evidence that he had been outmaneuvered or that he had misled the military. Sharif and his defenders have argued that the declaration represented the genuine policy of an elected leader and that the military's operation violated it. The text alone cannot establish who knew what, or when.

Questions for students:

  1. What commitments does the declaration make, and which of them concern nuclear weapons specifically?
  2. Who created this document, and what audiences were its authors addressing?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the policy of the Sharif government in early 1999?
  4. What can historians not establish from the declaration alone about the army's role or about the preparation of the Kargil operation?
Section 101.4

The Kargil War and the Collapse of Civilian-Military Cooperation

In the spring of 1999, units of the Pakistan Army, including the Northern Light Infantry and other forces, occupied positions on heights on the Indian-administered side of the Line of Control in the Kargil, Dras, and Batalik sectors of Ladakh. These positions overlooked the highway linking Srinagar and Leh. The ridges were normally unoccupied in winter by both armies, and the operation was designed to exploit that seasonal pattern. Pakistan's government initially described the occupants as Kashmiri militants, mujahideen acting independently, a claim that was contradicted by evidence of the participation of regular soldiers and was later effectively abandoned in Pakistani discussion. India discovered the intrusion in May, launched a major military and air response, and in June and July regained most of the positions, at significant cost in lives. Chapter 105 examines the war's role in Indian-Pakistani relations and nuclear signaling. Its domestic consequences concern this chapter.

The central domestic question is who authorized the operation. General Pervez Musharraf, appointed army chief in October 1998 after Sharif forced the resignation of General Jehangir Karamat, and a small group of senior generals planned and approved it. Musharraf has maintained that Sharif was briefed on the plan before it was executed, and that the prime minister supported its objectives. Sharif has maintained that he was not informed of the full scope until it was well underway, that he learned about its seriousness from the Indian prime minister's phone call, and that the operation sabotaged his diplomacy. Other senior officers and civilian officials have offered accounts that point in different directions, and the Pakistani military's own official history of the operation has not been fully available. The Historical Debate box at the end of this section presents the question as an unresolved historiographical problem. It is more productive for students to notice what is not disputed: that the army's operational planning was carried out within a small circle, that the civilian cabinet and defense committee did not exercise genuine control over it, and that the elected prime minister became responsible for its consequences.

Diplomatically, Pakistan was isolated. The United States and the other Group of Eight members called for the withdrawal of Pakistani forces. China, Pakistan's closest partner, urged restraint and did not endorse the operation. In early July, Sharif traveled to Washington and met President Clinton on 4 July. The resulting joint statement committed Pakistan to take concrete steps to restore the Line of Control, and Pakistani forces withdrew from the remaining positions. For the army, which had suffered casualties and considered the withdrawal a political defeat imposed by a civilian leader, the outcome was humiliating, and some officers blamed the prime minister. For Sharif, the army's operation had exposed him to blame for an outcome he said he had not designed. Each side built a narrative that placed responsibility on the other.

The deterioration of relations proceeded through the summer and early autumn. Sharif, now with a large majority and having already removed the president and the chief justice, tried to assert control over the army. His government also moved to bring the ISI more directly under his supervision. On 12 October 1999, while General Musharraf was returning from an official visit to Sri Lanka on a commercial flight, Sharif announced that he had been replaced as army chief by Lieutenant General Ziauddin Butt, the ISI director-general. Senior army commanders, many of whom had been informed in advance of the possibility, refused to accept the order; troops moved to take over the airport, the television station, and the prime minister's residence; and Musharraf's aircraft, which had been refused landing at Karachi, was allowed to land. By the evening Sharif had been detained. The coup was largely bloodless.

The coup of 1999 should be understood in context. It was the fourth time the army had taken direct control of the state, following 1958, 1969, and 1977. It differed in that it overturned a government with a very large parliamentary majority and in that it occurred after a decade in which the army had generally exercised influence through the presidency and intelligence rather than through direct rule. It also occurred after the army's own policy had produced a conflict that damaged Pakistan's international reputation. The military's justification emphasized the prime minister's attempt to dismiss the army chief illegally, the state of the economy, and the condition of institutions. These are the arguments that the next division examines in light of the regime that followed.

Historical Debate

Who Decided on Kargil, and What Did the Prime Minister Know?

The question: Was the Kargil operation an initiative of the army leadership that the elected government neither controlled nor fully understood, or a joint decision in which Prime Minister Sharif, whatever he later said, shared responsibility?

Interpretation A — A military operation outside civilian control: Sharif and his supporters, and some analysts, argue that the plan was developed by a small group of generals, that the prime minister received only limited and misleading briefings, and that the operation contradicted the diplomacy begun at Lahore. On this reading, the episode demonstrates the army's autonomy in security policy and its capacity to undercut elected leaders.
Interpretation B — A shared decision later disowned: Musharraf and other officers, and some civilian participants, argue that the prime minister was briefed, understood the objectives, and approved or acquiesced, and then disowned the operation when it met international resistance. On this reading, the episode shows how civilian leaders, too, sought to benefit from military initiatives while avoiding accountability.

Evidence: The accounts are memoirs and testimony produced after the coup by participants with strong interests in the outcome, together with limited contemporary documentation. Briefing records, meeting minutes, and internal military assessments have not been made generally available. The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, because a prime minister may have received a partial briefing and still have borne political responsibility.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the 1999 coup is judged, whether it is seen primarily as a defensive reaction to an attempted dismissal or as the culmination of a conflict over control of security policy, and what lessons are drawn about civilian oversight.

Student task: List the types of evidence that would help resolve the question, and explain why memoirs written after the coup must be read with particular caution.

Section 101.5

Pervez Musharraf and the Military Coup of 1999

General Pervez Musharraf did not at first declare martial law. On 14 October 1999 he proclaimed a state of emergency, suspended the Constitution and assemblies, and assumed the title of Chief Executive, an office whose novelty signaled an effort to avoid the vocabulary of earlier military regimes. A Provisional Constitution Order (PCO) held that Pakistan would be governed, as far as practicable, in accordance with the suspended Constitution, subject to the orders of the Chief Executive, and that the courts could not question the proclamation. Musharraf's first broadcast promised to revive the economy, restore national confidence, ensure accountability, and devolve power to the grass roots. His Seven-Point Agenda gave the regime a program that appealed to many Pakistanis weary of the preceding decade. The army, the principal institutional base of the regime, also placed serving officers in civilian bodies, the National Reconstruction Bureau, and the monitoring of administrative departments, extending a presence that had been indirect in the 1990s.

The judiciary's accommodation was central. In January 2000 the PCO required judges of the superior courts to take a fresh oath, and several, including Chief Justice Saeeduzzaman Siddiqui, declined and were removed. The remaining Supreme Court, in Zafar Ali Shah v. Pervez Musharraf (May 2000), validated the coup under the doctrine of necessity and authorized the regime to govern for three years from the coup, with the power to make constitutional amendments that did not alter the basic features of the Constitution, and with the requirement that elections be held by October 2002. The ruling followed precedent set in 1955 and 1958 (Chapters 90 and 95), which had enabled earlier military rulers.

Historical Vocabulary
The Doctrine of Necessity

The doctrine of necessity (nazariya-e-zarurat) is a principle in Pakistani constitutional law according to which acts that would otherwise be unlawful may be validated by courts when the survival of the state or the preservation of order requires them. Its use began with the Federal Court's decision in 1955, associated with Chief Justice Muhammad Munir, and it was applied to validate the dissolutions and military takeovers of 1958, 1977, and 1999. Its importance to this chapter is that it allowed each military regime to claim legal continuity with the Constitution. Misleading modern equivalents include the idea of emergency powers: the Constitution's own emergency provisions are written rules, while the doctrine of necessity operated as a judicial doctrine that supplied authority where none was written. In 2009 the Supreme Court declared that it would not validate future extra-constitutional actions, a decision examined in Section 101.9.

Musharraf consolidated authority in stages. Nawaz Sharif was tried on charges that included hijacking, arising from the denial of landing to Musharraf's aircraft, and was sentenced to life imprisonment in April 2000; in December 2000 he and his family were released and exiled to Saudi Arabia under an arrangement mediated in part by the Saudi government and by Lebanese politician Rafik Hariri. Musharraf became president in June 2001 while remaining army chief. A referendum in April 2002, widely criticized for irregularities, confirmed him as president for five years. The Legal Framework Order (LFO) of August 2002 inserted into the Constitution provisions for a National Security Council, restored presidential power to dismiss the government and dissolve the assembly, and expanded the president's authority to appoint the service chiefs, among other changes. The Court had not clearly authorized changes of this magnitude, and the opposition rejected them.

The elections of October 2002 followed rules that excluded candidates without a university degree, a requirement that reduced many established politicians' eligibility, and the regime's handling of the electoral process gave advantage to a pro-government faction of the Muslim League, the PML-Q, formed from defectors, often called the "King's party." Neither Bhutto nor Sharif was permitted to return. The outcome gave the PML-Q a plurality, and a government led by Mir Zafarullah Khan Jamali took office, but a surprising feature was the performance of the Muttahida Majlis-e-Amal (MMA), an alliance of religious parties that won a large number of seats and formed the provincial government in the North-West Frontier Province, as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was then named, and shared power in Balochistan. The result reflected domestic factors, including the exclusion of mainstream parties' leaders, as well as anti-American sentiment after the invasion of Afghanistan.

The constitutional settlement came in December 2003 through the Seventeenth Amendment, adopted after negotiations between the government and the MMA. It incorporated most of the LFO's provisions, restored Article 58(2)(b) in modified form, and gave the president authority to dismiss the government subject to review by the Supreme Court. Musharraf pledged to relinquish his army post by the end of 2004 and then reneged, retaining both offices until November 2007. In 2004 Shaukat Aziz, a former banker, became prime minister, and his government completed the assembly's five-year term in 2007, the first time an assembly elected in Pakistan had done so, though the legislature's autonomy was limited. The regime's methods, which included the management of elections, the use of the army and the intelligence agencies in politics, and the creation of a pro-regime party, would later be described by scholars as a hybrid of military control and civilian forms.

Historical Vocabulary
"The Establishment" and the Hybrid Regime

In Pakistani political usage, the establishment refers informally to the military leadership, the intelligence agencies, and the senior civil and judicial officials who are believed to coordinate on matters of political importance. Hybrid regime is a scholarly term for a system in which elections and civilian institutions exist but unelected institutions retain decisive influence over policy or outcomes. Both terms describe a pattern of influence, not a formal institution, and neither identifies a single decision-maker; they should not be used as if one body acted with one will. They matter to this chapter because Pakistan's authority problem is hard to describe using only the categories "civilian rule" and "military rule." Before 2025, these arrangements were mainly informal; the Twenty-Seventh Amendment gave some of them constitutional form (Section 101.18).

Section 101.6

Governance, Economic Policy, and Society under Musharraf

Musharraf's regime is often remembered for economic growth, and the record deserves careful treatment. The early years were difficult: Pakistan began the period with heavy external debt, low reserves, and sanctions. After September 2001 the situation changed. The United States lifted sanctions, provided large transfers of assistance and military support, and encouraged debt rescheduling, and remittances rose sharply after the global tightening of formal transfer channels. Pakistani growth accelerated in the mid-2000s, reaching high rates in several fiscal years, with expansion in construction, consumer credit, telecommunications, textiles, and services; foreign exchange reserves rose, and the Karachi Stock Exchange boomed. Prime Minister Shaukat Aziz presented this as the result of liberalizing reforms, including privatization of banks and the telecom sector. Critics countered that the growth depended on external inflows tied to the war on terror, on consumption financed by credit, and on remittances, and that it neglected investment in power generation and in the narrow tax base. The energy shortages that emerged from 2007, and the inflation that accompanied rising global commodity prices, exposed these weaknesses.

The regime's flagship administrative reform was the Local Government Plan of 2001, which created elected district governments and replaced the colonial-era office of the deputy commissioner with a district coordination officer, with the stated aim of devolving power to the grass roots. The system brought many new local representatives, including a reserved share of seats for women, into politics. It also served political purposes by creating a base of local officeholders independent of the national parties and by bypassing provincial governments, which saw it as an encroachment. The provinces later reversed or restructured the system, and subsequent civilian governments were reluctant to hold local elections, a pattern that illustrates the difficulty of building local democracy under a central military regime.

The regime also transformed the media. Following the creation of the Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority in 2002, private television channels were licensed and multiplied, among them Geo, ARY, and others, and by the end of the decade Pakistan had dozens of news channels. The change broadened public debate and gave journalists a new role in politics, but the regime used licensing, pressure, and in some cases direct measures to control coverage that threatened it. Education policy was a further concern. Public spending remained low as a share of national income, and the school system was divided among elite private schools, government schools of uneven quality, low-fee private schools, and religious seminaries (madrasas), whose role in militancy was debated and which the regime attempted unevenly to regulate. Efforts to revise curricula and textbooks met resistance from religious parties and from administrative inertia.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Electricity, and the New Urban Middle Classes

For a family in a mid-sized Punjabi town in the mid-2000s, the visible changes were mobile phones, which spread rapidly after telecom liberalization and became tools for work, remittance, and family communication; televisions carrying private channels; motorcycles and loans for appliances; and, in cities, new housing schemes. The invisible costs included the failure to expand electricity generation, which produced scheduled blackouts (load-shedding) in the later 2000s, and inflation in food and fuel that fell hardest on households with fixed incomes. A rural household in southern Punjab or interior Sindh might see schooling for sons by private tutors or low-fee schools, while girls' enrollment lagged in many districts. Lady Health Workers, a program begun in the 1990s and expanded under Musharraf, brought basic maternal and child health services into villages, and offered paid work to women, though workers complained of low and delayed pay. Evidence for everyday experience in this period comes from household surveys, newspaper reporting, and oral testimony, and the regional variations between Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and Balochistan were large: the new prosperity was concentrated in urban Punjab and Karachi.

The regime's political contradictions came into the open in 2007. Musharraf's attempt to secure re-election as president by the outgoing assemblies, while retaining his army post, required him to resolve a looming legal challenge. On 9 March 2007 he suspended Chief Justice Iftikhar Muhammad Chaudhry, who had begun to take up politically sensitive cases, including those concerning forced disappearances and the privatization of the Pakistan Steel Mills, and he did so by sending a reference alleging misconduct. The decision provoked an unexpected movement: lawyers, organized through the Supreme Court Bar Association and the bar councils, took to the streets in black suits, supported by journalists, opposition parties, and many ordinary citizens. In July 2007 a thirteen-member bench of the Supreme Court reinstated Chaudhry. The lawyers' movement was significant because it was a mobilization organized around the principle of judicial independence and constitutional order rather than around a party or a leader, and because the private media amplified it. It also showed that the regime's capacity to control the judiciary, which had seemed secure in 2000, had weakened.

Musharraf's response was the declaration of an emergency on 3 November 2007, under which he suspended the Constitution a second time, dismissed the superior judges, including Chaudhry, and restricted the television channels. He did so as the Supreme Court was preparing to rule on the validity of his election as president while serving as army chief. A new Supreme Court validated his re-election, he resigned as army chief on 28 November 2007 in favor of General Ashfaq Parvez Kayani, took office as a civilian president, and lifted the emergency in December. The costs to his political position, however, were high. The 2007 emergency showed that a regime that had presented itself as a reformist alternative to corrupt politicians now depended on removing judges to survive, and it turned many of its supporters, including the lawyers, the press, and parts of the military leadership, against it.

Section 101.7

Pakistan, September 11, and the War on Terror

The attacks of 11 September 2001 in the United States compelled Pakistan to decide on its Afghan policy. Since 1994 Pakistan had supported the Taliban movement, which had taken Kabul in 1996 and controlled most of Afghanistan, and it was one of only three states to recognize the Taliban government. The policy rested on strategic reasoning, including the desire for a friendly government in Kabul that would provide strategic depth against India, and on relationships between the ISI and Afghan Pashtun factions that reached back to the 1980s (Chapter 95). Washington, under President George W. Bush, demanded that Pakistan end its support for the Taliban, provide intelligence and logistical cooperation, and allow overflight and use of its territory. Musharraf, after consultation with his corps commanders, accepted these demands, justifying the decision as necessary to protect Pakistan's strategic assets, including its nuclear program, to prevent India from becoming the principal American partner in the region, and to avoid war with a superpower.

The decision had far-reaching consequences. Pakistan's logistical cooperation, through the ports of Karachi and the road routes into Afghanistan, became an essential supply line for American and NATO forces in Afghanistan for the next decade. The United States lifted sanctions, designated Pakistan a major non-NATO ally in 2004, and provided large amounts of military and economic assistance, much of it through Coalition Support Funds reimbursing Pakistan's military operations. Pakistan arrested numerous al-Qaeda suspects, including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed in Rawalpindi in 2003, and handed them to the United States. In 2004 the army began operations in South Waziristan in the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) against al-Qaeda affiliates and Pakistani militants who had sheltered Arab and Central Asian fighters. American missile strikes by unmanned aircraft (drones), which began in Pakistan in 2004 and grew in number after 2008, were conducted with an ambiguous degree of Pakistani consent, and became a continuing source of public anger.

Pakistan's position was complicated by its relationship with Afghan insurgents. After the fall of the Taliban in late 2001, many of its leaders, with their families and fighters, moved into Pakistan, particularly Balochistan and the tribal areas, where they regrouped. American and Afghan officials alleged that elements of the Pakistani state continued to tolerate or assist the Afghan Taliban as a hedge against Indian influence in Afghanistan and the instability of a government in Kabul; Pakistani officials denied official support and pointed to their own casualties. Historians should record both the existence of allegations that were repeated by different governments and the limits of public evidence about institutional policy, and should avoid treating Pakistan as a single actor. The army, the ISI, the civilian government, religious parties, and tribal communities held distinct views.

Domestically, the war on terror shaped legitimacy in contradictory ways. The decision to cooperate was unpopular among many Pakistanis, particularly in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, where the Taliban had sympathizers and where the American presence was resented. Religious parties, organized in the MMA, used the issue effectively in 2002. At the same time, the cooperation brought external resources and international acceptance for a regime that would otherwise have been isolated. The relationship also gave Musharraf a source of support that mattered more than domestic consent. American officials, who regarded him as indispensable to counterterrorism, tended to downplay democratic concerns, and critics argued that this prolonged military rule. After 2008 the American relationship continued, with a civilian government but under the same strains, as Section 101.9 shows.

Section 101.8

Militant Insurgency, Domestic Conflict, and the Crisis of 2007–2008

Militancy in Pakistan was not a single phenomenon, and accurate analysis requires distinguishing among organizations with different origins, goals, and relationships to the state. Al-Qaeda was an international network led by Arabs, with Pakistan as one of several bases. The Afghan Taliban was a movement of Afghan Pashtuns whose aims concerned Afghanistan. The Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), established in December 2007 as an umbrella of local militant groups under Baitullah Mehsud, aimed to overthrow the Pakistani state's alliance with the United States and to impose its version of Islamic law on Pakistan. Sectarian organizations such as Lashkar-e-Jhangvi targeted Shia communities, and groups focused on Kashmir, such as Lashkar-e-Taiba, had a longer history of relations with the intelligence services. Baloch insurgents, a separate phenomenon, were nationalist rather than Islamist.

The year 2007 brought the threat into the cities. In July the government besieged the Lal Masjid (Red Mosque) complex in Islamabad, whose clerics and students had conducted a campaign of vigilantism against what they called immoral activities and had defied the government. After a week-long standoff, army commandos stormed the complex on 10 July 2007. The official figure for the dead was around one hundred, while the clerics' supporters claimed far more; the numbers remain disputed. The operation was followed by a wave of suicide attacks on security forces, and militants repudiated agreements previously made with the government in the tribal areas. By 2007–2008 militant groups controlled parts of Swat and the tribal districts, and attacks on military and civilian targets expanded throughout the country.

It was in this environment that Benazir Bhutto returned from exile on 18 October 2007, after a negotiation with Musharraf, facilitated in part by American and British officials, that produced the National Reconciliation Ordinance, which withdrew pending corruption cases against many politicians. Her procession in Karachi was struck by a double suicide bombing that killed well over one hundred people. She campaigned anyway, and on 27 December 2007, in Rawalpindi, after addressing an election rally, she was assassinated in a gun and bomb attack. A United Nations commission of inquiry, in a 2010 report, criticized the failure of the authorities to protect her and the subsequent handling of the investigation. The responsibility for the planning of the attack has been attributed by the government to the TTP and disputed in other accounts, and the legal proceedings did not resolve all questions. The assassination triggered riots in Sindh and postponed the elections to 18 February 2008.

The elections gave the PPP the largest number of seats in the National Assembly, with the PML-N second and the pro-Musharraf PML-Q a distant third, and the MMA's defeat in the Frontier province, where the secular Awami National Party won, reflected popular disenchantment with the religious parties' performance. Yousaf Raza Gilani of the PPP became prime minister at the head of a coalition that included the PML-N, and the new assembly moved against Musharraf. The coalition's agreement to restore the dismissed judges soon collapsed, but threatened with impeachment, and deprived of the support of the army leadership, now headed by Kayani, who had distanced the institution from politics, Musharraf resigned as president on 18 August 2008. The most prominent military ruler since Zia thus left office without a coup or a mass uprising, but after losing the political support of the judges, lawyers, press, parties, and, ultimately, the army that had brought him to power. Section 101.9

The Return to Civilian Government and the PPP Administration, 2008–2013

The government that took office in March 2008 under Prime Minister Yousaf Raza Gilani inherited a country facing militant violence, an energy shortage, rising food and fuel prices, and a balance-of-payments crisis that forced Pakistan to seek an IMF Stand-By Arrangement in November 2008. Its political foundation was a coalition between the PPP and the PML-N, which had pledged to restore the judges dismissed in November 2007. The coalition broke apart in 2008 over that question, and the PML-N moved into opposition, though it continued to govern in Punjab. In September 2008, Asif Ali Zardari, Benazir Bhutto's widower and her party's co-chairman, was elected president by the electoral college, so that the PPP held both the presidency and the premiership, with Zardari holding the party's real center of authority. Pakistan's leading institutions were then held by figures with differing agendas: Zardari, Gilani, the army chief General Kayani, and the reinstated judiciary after March 2009, when a "long march" led by lawyers and the PML-N compelled the government to restore Iftikhar Chaudhry as chief justice.

The relationship between the government and the judiciary soon became the main arena of conflict. In December 2009 the Supreme Court declared the National Reconciliation Ordinance unconstitutional, reopening corruption cases against Zardari and others, and in the following years it pressed the government to write to Swiss authorities to revive a money-laundering case against the president. The government resisted on the ground that the president enjoyed immunity. In June 2012 the Court convicted Gilani of contempt for failing to act and disqualified him from office, and Raja Pervez Ashraf became prime minister. Supporters of the Court's approach saw an assertion of the rule of law against a corrupt executive; critics described judicial activism, selective enforcement, and an intrusion into political questions. The Court also played a role in the "Memogate" affair of 2011, in which Zardari's ambassador to Washington was accused of seeking American help to restrain the army after the killing of Osama bin Laden. Whatever the merits of each case, the effect was to place the elected government under constant legal and political pressure.

The security relationship with the United States was strained by successive crises: the Raymond Davis affair in January 2011, the American operation that killed Osama bin Laden in Abbottabad on 2 May 2011, which exposed Pakistan to the charge of either complicity or incompetence, and the NATO attack on the Salala border post in November 2011, which killed twenty-four Pakistani soldiers and led Pakistan to close the supply routes into Afghanistan for about seven months. Parliament debated and approved revised guidelines for relations with the United States, an episode of unusual parliamentary involvement in foreign policy, although key decisions remained with the army. The economy grew slowly, hindered by electricity shortages, security costs, and inflation, and the devastating floods of 2010, which affected some twenty million people according to the United Nations, strained the government's capacity.

What the PPP government did achieve, and what the period meant historically, was a sequence of political transitions. The Eighteenth Amendment (Section 101.10) and the Seventh National Finance Commission Award transformed the constitutional and fiscal structure. The Supreme Court, in a 2009 judgment, indicated that it would not again validate extra-constitutional takeovers. The government completed its five-year term. Elections held on 11 May 2013, supervised by a caretaker administration and an Election Commission that had acquired greater independence under the Eighteenth Amendment's new appointment procedure, brought the PML-N to power. It was the first time in Pakistan's history that one elected civilian government completed its term and handed power to another through an election. The historian should note both aspects: the transition was an achievement, and the government had been limited by the courts, the army, and its own record. No prime minister, in any government, had completed a full five-year term, and Gilani's disqualification had ended a premiership as dissolution and dismissal had earlier ones.

Section 101.10

The Eighteenth Amendment, Federalism, and Constitutional Change

The Eighteenth Amendment, passed by the National Assembly in April 2010 and signed on 19 April, was the most extensive revision of the 1973 Constitution since its adoption. It followed work by a parliamentary committee of representatives of all major parties, chaired by the PPP senator Raza Rabbani, which met in some seventy-eight sittings over a number of months and operated by consensus. The result, which passed without opposition, deserves attention as an example of cross-party constitution-making in a state that had been used to constitutional change by decree.

The amendment changed the structure of power in several ways. It removed the legacy of the Zia and Musharraf periods by repealing Article 58(2)(b) and thereby the president's power to dismiss the government and dissolve the assembly, and it removed the restrictions that prevented a person from serving a third time as prime minister, a change that benefited Nawaz Sharif. It altered the method of judicial appointments by creating a Judicial Commission and a parliamentary committee to confirm nominations, a reform intended to end the practice by which the Supreme Court controlled appointments but which later produced tension between the judiciary and parliament and was amended again by the Nineteenth Amendment in 2010. It transferred the appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner to a process involving the government and opposition. It renamed the North-West Frontier Province as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, answering a long-standing demand of its Pashtun majority, and it added to the Constitution a right to education for children aged five to sixteen (Article 25A).

Its largest effect on federalism was the abolition of the Concurrent Legislative List, which had given the federal and provincial governments shared authority over a range of subjects. Responsibilities for education, health, population welfare, the environment, and others passed to the provinces. The change reflected the demand of the smaller provinces, especially Balochistan and Sindh, for greater autonomy and was reinforced by the Seventh National Finance Commission (NFC) Award of December 2009, which raised the provinces' share of the divisible pool of federal taxes to 57.5 percent and introduced a formula that gave weight, in addition to population, to poverty, revenue generation, and inverse population density. The NFC Award was a fiscal measure rather than part of the amendment, but the two worked together to shift authority.

Historical Vocabulary
The Divisible Pool and the NFC Award

The divisible pool is the portion of federal tax revenue, including income and sales taxes, that is shared among the federal government and the provinces under a formula periodically agreed by a National Finance Commission. The NFC Award is the agreement that fixes the formula. Before 2009, population was the principal criterion, which favored Punjab, the most populous province, and the Seventh Award added further criteria. It matters to this chapter because the allocation of revenue became a central issue of federal politics: the provinces received larger transfers while the federal government retained heavy responsibilities, including debt service and defense, and a debate continues on whether provinces should raise more of their own taxes. In modern usage "federalism" can imply a stable division of powers, but in Pakistan the real distribution has depended on fiscal flows, security institutions, and political bargaining as much as on constitutional text.

Section 101.11

Militancy, Counterinsurgency, and the Human Cost of Internal Conflict

From 2007 the conflict with the TTP and allied groups became the most severe internal security crisis in Pakistan's history. In Swat, a former tourist valley in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, a local movement led by Maulana Fazlullah combined a demand for Islamic justice, grievances against a slow court system, and the intimidation of opponents. Militants beheaded or killed police and local leaders, attacked girls' schools, and in 2009 the provincial government of the Awami National Party agreed to a regulation introducing Islamic courts in the Malakand division. After the agreement collapsed and the militants moved into neighboring Buner, the army launched an operation in April–May 2009. It displaced an estimated two million or more people at its height, one of the largest short-term displacements in the world at that time, before the army regained control of Swat. Fighting then spread to South Waziristan in October 2009, and later to Bajaur, Orakzai, and North Waziristan, where the army launched Operation Zarb-e-Azb in June 2014.

The fighting in the tribal areas produced displacement, destruction of property, and civilian deaths from militants, army operations, and American drone strikes, whose human toll is disputed because of limited access and competing counts. Rights organizations documented abuses by militants, and also enforced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, and restrictions on movement associated with security operations. Residents of the tribal areas, who had long lacked ordinary courts and representation, were thus affected by militancy, military rule, and a legal framework, the Frontier Crimes Regulation, that had been inherited from the colonial period. The Pashtun Tahafuz Movement, founded in 2018 to protest enforced disappearances and landmines, grew from these experiences, and its demands remained controversial in the 2020s.

The most shocking attack was on 16 December 2014, when TTP gunmen entered the Army Public School in Peshawar and killed about one hundred and fifty people, the great majority of them schoolchildren. The attack produced a rare consensus in Pakistani politics. Within days, the prime minister, political parties, and army leaders agreed on a twenty-point National Action Plan (NAP), which included the lifting of the moratorium on the death penalty in terrorism cases, the establishment of military courts, action against hate speech and sectarian organizations, the regulation of madrasas, and efforts to cut terrorist financing. The Twenty-First Amendment of January 2015 and an amendment to the Army Act established military courts to try civilians accused of terrorism for a period of two years, a measure the Supreme Court upheld in 2015 by a divided bench, while some judges expressed reservations.

The conditions in Balochistan, Pakistan's largest province by area and smallest by population, differed. Nationalist insurgencies had arisen in the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s; the latest began after 2004 and intensified after the killing of the Baloch leader Nawab Akbar Bugti in a military operation in August 2006. Baloch nationalists demanded control of the province's natural resources, including the Sui gas fields and the Reko Diq mineral deposits, a larger share of revenue, an end to military operations, and the return of those who had disappeared. The state responded with military and paramilitary operations and with development projects, including the Gwadar port. Violence also took the form of attacks on Punjabi settlers, on Shia Hazara communities in Quetta by sectarian militants, and on security personnel by insurgent groups. Karachi saw a different cycle of violence, and a paramilitary Rangers-led operation from 2013 sharply reduced the killings associated with party militias, extortion, and sectarian groups, while also being criticized for extrajudicial killings and for the way it targeted the MQM.

Reported deaths from terrorism in Pakistan peaked around 2013–2014 and fell thereafter, and operations such as Zarb-e-Azb and the later Radd-ul-Fasaad (2017) were credited with reducing attacks. The reduction was uneven and, as Section 101.19 shows, proved reversible. The costs included tens of thousands of civilian and security-force deaths over the period, estimates of which vary by source and definition, and displacement, the destruction of local economies, and trauma. Historians should state such figures as ranges, and should note that the human toll was borne disproportionately by Pashtun, Hazara, Baloch, and religious-minority communities, as well as by police and soldiers.

Section 101.12

Nawaz Sharif's Third Government, Development, and the 2018 Political Transition

The elections of May 2013 returned Nawaz Sharif to office for a third time, with a majority in the National Assembly based on Punjab, and with the PPP reduced to its core in rural Sindh. The election marked another change: the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) of Imran Khan, the former cricket captain whose party had struggled for years, won a large vote and formed the provincial government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The campaign was held under the shadow of militant attacks, which targeted secular parties such as the PPP and the ANP, and turnout, at about 55 percent, was among the highest in decades. Sharif's government, with the support of the army chief of the time, set out to fix the energy shortage, expand infrastructure, and improve the economy, and it also attempted to bring Musharraf to trial for treason for the 2007 emergency, a move that angered the military. Musharraf was allowed to leave the country for medical treatment in 2016 and later was sentenced in absentia in 2019, a verdict that a Lahore High Court bench set aside.

The government's chief project was the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), announced during President Xi Jinping's visit in April 2015 with an initial headline figure of about forty-six billion dollars, a figure that later rose in official and press statements and that included commitments to power plants, roads, railways, and the development of Gwadar port. CPEC illustrated both the potential and the problems of large external-financed development: power plants reduced load-shedding substantially by the later 2010s, and some road projects improved connectivity, but the repayment obligations, the opaque terms of some agreements, the distribution of benefits among provinces, and the concerns of Balochistan's residents over Gwadar became subjects of controversy. The economy grew at higher rates in the late 2010s than in the early 2010s, helped by lower oil prices, but a widening current account deficit and import dependence left it exposed, and the government turned to the IMF's Extended Fund Facility in 2013, completed in 2016.

The political challenge to Sharif's government came from three sources. In August 2014 Imran Khan and the cleric Tahir-ul-Qadri led marches to Islamabad and held a sit-in lasting 126 days, alleging that the 2013 election had been rigged and demanding the prime minister's resignation. The protests, whose course, according to many analysts, involved at least some tolerance or encouragement by sections of the military establishment, weakened the government and made it dependent on the army's goodwill, although a parliamentary commission and a judicial commission found no evidence of systematic rigging. Second, relations with the military deteriorated over policy toward India, over the treatment of Musharraf, and in the "Dawn Leaks" affair of 2016, in which a newspaper reported discussions at a security meeting about the military's relations with militant groups.

The third source was the Panama Papers. In April 2016, the leaked documents of a Panamanian law firm named the children of Nawaz Sharif as owners of offshore companies connected to London property. After a petition brought by the PTI and others, the Supreme Court in April 2017 divided on the question, ordered a joint investigation team that included representatives of military intelligence agencies, and on 28 July 2017 disqualified Sharif under Article 62(1)(f), which requires a member of parliament to be "honest" (sadiq) and "righteous" (ameen), for failing to disclose an unwithdrawn salary receivable from a company owned by his son. The Court did not convict him of the corruption alleged in the Panama documents but referred them to an accountability court, which in July 2018 sentenced him to ten years in prison in the Avenfield properties case. Shahid Khaqan Abbasi served as prime minister until the assembly's term ended in May 2018. Sharif's supporters, who include many independent observers, considered the process politically driven and noted that the same standards had not been applied to other politicians. Others argued that the Court had enforced the law against a powerful figure. The provisions of Article 62 and 63, inserted in the Zia period, thus had once again become instruments in political conflict.

The elections of 25 July 2018 took place amid allegations of pre-poll manipulation, including pressure on candidates and on the media, and complaints of irregularities in the counting of results, which were delayed when the Election Commission's reporting system failed. The PTI won the largest number of National Assembly seats, about 116 of the roughly 270 directly contested, the PML-N about 64, and the PPP about 43, with PTI forming a government with smaller parties and independents. The Election Observation Mission of the European Union reported that the contest had been competitive but that the playing field had not been level, and the Pakistani military institution rejected claims of interference. The outcome did not demonstrate fraud on a scale that determined the result, which remains debated, but it confirmed that the election had occurred in a context of unequal conditions. Imran Khan took office on 18 August 2018, promising a "new Pakistan." Division IV examines what followed.

Section 101.13

Imran Khan and the Rise of Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf

Imran Khan entered politics with a reputation that no other Pakistani politician of his generation possessed. Born in 1952 in Lahore and educated at Oxford, he captained Pakistan's cricket team to victory in the 1992 World Cup, and then used his fame to found the Shaukat Khanum Memorial Cancer Hospital in Lahore, which opened in 1994 and was funded largely by public donations. In 1996 he founded the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (Movement for Justice), a party that for its first fifteen years won little: in the 2002 election he won only his own seat. Its rise came after 2011, when large rallies in Lahore and Karachi attracted young, urban, educated Pakistanis, many of them first-time voters, and drew established politicians from other parties. Its message combined a critique of the two dynastic parties, the PPP and the PML-N, as corrupt; a promise of accountability; opposition to the American war on terror and to drone strikes; a vision of a welfare state invoking the early Islamic polity of Medina; and the appeal of a leader who was not tainted by past office.

The PTI's social base deserves analysis rather than slogans. It was strongest among urban and suburban middle-class voters, among younger voters, among overseas Pakistanis, and in parts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where it won provincial office in 2013 and was re-elected in 2018, a rare case of a ruling party in Pakistan winning a second term. Observers differed on how far its rise should be explained by genuine mobilization of a new political constituency, by the changing media environment, in which social media played a growing role, or by assistance from the military establishment, which some commentators and politicians alleged. The evidence is not equally strong on all these claims. Imran Khan's popularity in 2018 was real and was demonstrated in elections of his own party's performance, but so were complaints, which the EU observers noted, about unequal conditions facing his opponents (Section 101.12). The two propositions can both be true.

The government that Khan formed in August 2018 depended on a narrow coalition with the Pakistan Muslim League (Q), the Muttahida Qaumi Movement-Pakistan, the Balochistan Awami Party, and others, and in Punjab, the largest province, on a chief minister, Usman Buzdar, who had little political experience. Its policy program included an anti-corruption campaign directed largely at opposition leaders, the establishment of the Ehsaas social-protection program, a health insurance scheme (the Sehat Sahulat card), a tree-planting initiative in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and low-cost housing promises that produced limited results. The opposition parties, the PML-N and the PPP, were pursued by the National Accountability Bureau, and several leaders, including Nawaz Sharif's daughter Maryam Nawaz, Shehbaz Sharif, and Asif Ali Zardari, were arrested or prosecuted at various points, while Nawaz Sharif left for medical treatment in London in 2019 and stayed. The opposition formed the Pakistan Democratic Movement in September 2020 to challenge the government, though internal differences, particularly between the PPP and the PML-N, limited its effectiveness.

The relationship between the government and the military establishment was initially described by both as being on "one page." The army chief, General Qamar Javed Bajwa, was granted a three-year extension in 2019 by the government, after a Supreme Court judgment that scrutinized the notification, and parliament passed legislation in January 2020 allowing such extensions with support from the PML-N and PPP, who voted for it. The military was visibly involved in economic decision-making, the management of the pandemic, and foreign policy. This cooperation did not prevent conflict. In October 2021, a dispute over the replacement of the head of the ISI, Lieutenant General Faiz Hameed, whom Khan wished to retain at a time when the army leadership had decided to transfer him, produced the first open sign of a rift. By the end of 2021 the relationship had changed, with consequences explained in Section 101.15.

The external environment complicated the government's task. Pakistan remained on the grey list of the Financial Action Task Force from June 2018 until October 2022 for deficiencies in controls against terrorist financing. The American withdrawal from Afghanistan and the return of the Taliban to power in August 2021 were welcomed by many in Pakistan's security establishment but also created risks, including the revival of the TTP, which had been weakened after 2014 but found sanctuary and encouragement across the border. Relations with the United States were cool; those with China remained close; and Khan's visit to Moscow on the day Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022 displeased Western governments. For the historian, the Khan government is evidence of the strengths and limits of an outsider's electoral mandate in a system where governing required the cooperation of institutions not under the prime minister's control.

Section 101.14

Economic Pressure, the Pandemic, and Everyday Life

Khan's government began with an external-payments crisis. Soon after taking office it sought assistance from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and China, and in July 2019 reached agreement with the IMF on a 39-month Extended Fund Facility of about six billion dollars. The rupee, which had been allowed to depreciate sharply in 2018–2019, lost a large part of its value against the dollar, and inflation rose to double digits, reaching high levels in food prices. The cost of living became a chief source of the government's unpopularity. At the same time the government sought to reduce the current account deficit by curbing imports, and the economy slowed markedly.

The COVID-19 pandemic reached Pakistan in February 2020. The government resisted the complete shutdown adopted in neighboring countries, arguing that a large part of the population depended on daily wages, and adopted a policy of selective, or "smart," lockdowns, together with an emergency cash-transfer program that reached roughly twelve million families under the Ehsaas program. The economy contracted slightly in the fiscal year to June 2020, the first contraction in many years, and then recovered strongly, helped by remittances, which reached record levels, and by an expansion of exports and construction. Official figures for deaths are widely thought to have understated the toll, as testing was limited, and it is difficult to give a precise figure. The vaccination campaign was supported by donations from China, COVAX, and others, and by a national program that registered large numbers of adults by late 2021. The effects on education were substantial, with schools closed for extended periods and limited access to remote learning in rural areas.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work, Gender, and Schooling during the 2019–2022 Price Shocks

For a household in Karachi's informal settlements, the combination of inflation, irregular daily work, and the pandemic meant reducing meals, deferring medical care, and relying on remittances from relatives abroad or in the Gulf, on zakat and informal credit, and on the cash transfers where families were registered. In rural Punjab, wheat and sugar prices, set partly by government policy and partly by smuggling and hoarding, shaped household budgets, while landowners with irrigated holdings benefited from higher crop prices. Women's participation in the formal labor force, measured in surveys at a quarter or less of women of working age, understated their agricultural, home-based, and domestic work. Girls were less likely than boys to be enrolled in school in many rural districts, especially in Balochistan and parts of Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and estimates of children out of school have exceeded twenty-five million in some official and international reports. These conditions varied widely by province, class, and the local presence of schools, and national averages hide them. The political effect of economic hardship was cumulative. The government's accomplishments, including the expansion of social protection, record remittances, and a growth rebound in fiscal year 2021, were overshadowed by high inflation and a widening current account deficit, and the IMF program was repeatedly interrupted when the government's policies diverged from agreed conditions. In early 2022, shortly before its removal, the government announced fuel and electricity subsidies that departed from the IMF's requirements, which left the successor government with a difficult fiscal situation. The historian should avoid assigning the crisis solely to one government, because the underlying structure, a low rate of tax collection, a large energy sector with accumulated debt, import dependence, and a pattern of borrowing to sustain consumption, was the product of decades of policy under both civilian and military governments. Section 101.15

The 2022 No-Confidence Vote and the Breakdown of Political Cooperation

In March 2022 the opposition parties, now united after the PPP and the PML-N had overcome their differences, submitted a motion of no confidence in the National Assembly against Prime Minister Khan under Article 95 of the Constitution. They counted on the defection of members of the PTI and of the government's coalition partners, including the MQM-P and the Balochistan Awami Party, and on a perceived loss of the army's support. Khan responded with a speech in which he waved a document, which he described as a diplomatic cable (the "cipher") from the Pakistani ambassador in Washington, to argue that the United States had conspired to remove him because of his foreign policy and his Moscow visit. American officials denied the allegation, and the content of the cable, as reported later, recorded a conversation in which an American official expressed displeasure at Pakistan's stance on Ukraine and indicated implications for relations if Khan remained in office. Whether this amounted to a plot is the point on which Khan's supporters and critics differed, and a historian can say that the document recorded diplomatic pressure but that the evidence made public did not demonstrate a conspiracy to remove a prime minister.

On 3 April 2022, the day the National Assembly was to vote, Deputy Speaker Qasim Khan Suri ruled that the motion was invalid under Article 5 of the Constitution, which requires loyalty to the state, on the ground that it was part of a foreign conspiracy. Khan then advised President Arif Alvi to dissolve the assembly, which was done, and called for new elections. The opposition petitioned the Supreme Court, and on 7 April a five-member bench headed by Chief Justice Umar Ata Bandial held unanimously that the deputy speaker's ruling, the dissolution, and the president's order were unconstitutional, and restored the assembly. After a delay in the session, which continued past midnight on 9–10 April, the vote was held and Khan lost, with 174 members in the 342-seat house voting for the motion. He became the first prime minister in Pakistan's history to be removed by a vote of no confidence. Shehbaz Sharif was elected prime minister on 11 April at the head of a coalition of eleven parties.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Governing Arrangement of 2018 Break Down in 2022?

Long-term causes

Structural dependence of elected governments on military support; a weak and fragmented coalition in the assembly; economic fragility; the polarization of party politics around accountability.

Short-term causes

Inflation and fiscal strain; the 2021 dispute over the ISI appointment; the loss of coalition partners; opposition unity; the foreign-policy controversy over Russia and the United States.

Immediate effects

The first removal of a prime minister by no-confidence vote; PTI's move into mass opposition; a coalition government under Shehbaz Sharif.

Long-term consequences

A confrontation between the PTI and the military leadership; legal cases against Khan; wider conflict over the roles of courts and institutions.

Historical complexity: The removal was lawful in form and followed a Supreme Court ruling, yet it occurred amid perceptions, disputed by the military, that the institution had withdrawn its support. No single cause explains it, and each side assigns weight differently.

Khan's removal began a more confrontational period. He held large rallies, resigned his party's seats in the assembly, and attacked the army leadership, including General Bajwa, whom he accused of having facilitated his removal. On 3 November 2022 he was shot and wounded in the leg during a rally at Wazirabad in Punjab; the attacker was arrested, and Khan accused senior officials of involvement, an allegation they denied. In late November 2022 General Bajwa retired and was succeeded by Lieutenant General Asim Munir, a former ISI chief whom Khan had removed from that post in 2019. The incoming chief was regarded as unsympathetic to Khan, and the relationship deteriorated further.

The government's economic position meanwhile worsened. In the summer of 2022 monsoon rains and glacier melt caused floods that submerged about a third of the country, killed roughly 1,700 people, affected some thirty-three million, and caused estimated damage and losses of roughly thirty billion dollars, according to assessments by the government and the World Bank, with Sindh and Balochistan hardest hit. Chapter 109 treats the environmental history of South Asia's floods. For this chapter the relevant fact is the combination of the disaster with a debt crisis: by early 2023 foreign-exchange reserves had fallen to a level covering only a few weeks of imports, the rupee was falling, and inflation reached nearly 38 percent in May 2023, a record in the available series. Pakistan avoided default through a nine-month, three-billion-dollar IMF Stand-By Arrangement approved in July 2023, supported by Saudi and UAE deposits and Chinese rollovers. The measures that accompanied it, higher energy prices and taxes, strained households. By the beginning of 2023 politics and economics were intertwined: elections were due, but an administration with a thin mandate had to impose austerity.

Section 101.16

Political Confrontation, Imran Khan's Imprisonment, and the 2024 Election

In January 2023 Khan dissolved the PTI-governed assemblies of Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in an effort to force early national elections. The Supreme Court ordered elections in Punjab, but the federal government and the election authorities did not hold them on the court's schedule, citing security and financial constraints, and the dispute produced a confrontation between the court, the government, and parliament. The events of 9 May 2023 changed the political landscape. On that day paramilitary Rangers arrested Khan at the Islamabad High Court in the Al-Qadir Trust case, a corruption case alleging that he had benefited from a property settlement involving a real-estate developer and money returned to Pakistan by the British National Crime Agency. Supporters rioted in cities across the country, and demonstrators attacked military installations, including the Lahore residence of the corps commander and the army's general headquarters in Rawalpindi. Deaths occurred in the unrest, with the numbers contested by the PTI and the government. The Supreme Court declared the arrest unlawful on 11 May and Khan was released, but the government and army moved to treat the attacks as an assault on the state.

Thousands of PTI supporters were arrested, and many leaders left the party or abandoned politics under pressure, some forming a breakaway party, the Istehkam-e-Pakistan Party. The army declared that those responsible would be tried under the Army Act, and the question whether civilians could be tried in military courts reached the Supreme Court, which in October 2023 held it unconstitutional, a ruling suspended on appeal; in 2025 a larger bench allowed trials of civilians in military courts for offenses connected to the May 9 attacks to proceed. In August 2023 a trial court convicted Khan in the Toshakhana case, involving the sale of state gifts, and sentenced him to three years, and he was arrested again; the conviction was later suspended but he remained in custody in other cases. In January 2024 he was sentenced to ten years in the "cipher" case, a verdict later set aside, and then to additional terms in other proceedings. He remains in prison as this chapter's research date, serving a fourteen-year sentence in the Al-Qadir Trust case imposed in January 2025 and facing further proceedings. His supporters regard the cases as politically motivated, and the government maintains that they are matters for the courts.

The conditions of the 8 February 2024 general election reflected this confrontation. A caretaker government under Anwaar-ul-Haq Kakar supervised the vote. In January 2024 the Supreme Court upheld an Election Commission decision that deprived the PTI of its "cricket bat" symbol on the ground that it had not held valid intra-party elections, which meant that PTI candidates had to stand as independents with individual symbols, a serious handicap in a country where many voters are unfamiliar with candidates' names. Mobile phone and internet services were suspended on election day, citing security concerns, after militant attacks in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa the day before. The vote count was slow, and results began to be announced overnight with long delays, and the Election Commission later explained the delays as caused by the suspension of communication services.

Historical Debate

How Legitimate Was the 2024 Election, and What Kind of Political Order Does It Reveal?

The question: Did the election of February 2024 produce a government with democratic legitimacy, and should Pakistan's system be characterized as a "hybrid regime" in which elected governments share power with military institutions?

Interpretation A — A flawed but valid electoral transfer: Government spokespeople, and some analysts, emphasize that the election was held on schedule, that the PTI-backed candidates won many seats and formed a large opposition, that courts and tribunals heard petitions, and that the coalition commanded a parliamentary majority. On this view, the election shows a competitive if imperfect system.
Interpretation B — A managed contest that distorted the popular mandate: The PTI, many domestic commentators, and a number of international observers emphasize the removal of the party's symbol, the arrests, the communications shutdown, the delayed results, and allegations of altered tallies. On this view, the outcome was shaped by institutions that did not accept the party's popularity, and the post-election government lacked a full mandate.

Evidence: The two interpretations rely on the same documented restrictions and the same official numbers, but disagree about whether the restrictions were decisive and whether tampering occurred. The strongest evidence for the second interpretation concerns the conditions before and during the vote; the evidence for specific alterations remains the subject of unresolved legal petitions.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how the 26th and 27th Amendments of 2024 and 2025 (Section 101.18) are assessed, because those measures were adopted by a parliament whose composition and reserved-seat allocation were themselves contested.

Student task: Identify which claims in each interpretation are established fact, which are allegations, and which are judgments of weight.

Section 101.17

Coalition Government and Opposition Politics, 2024–2026

The government that took office in March 2024 rested on an arrangement unusual even by Pakistani standards. Shehbaz Sharif, the younger brother of Nawaz Sharif, led a coalition in which the PML-N, with the largest party share among the government's partners, relied on the PPP, which voted for Sharif as prime minister and supplied the president, Asif Ali Zardari, but initially declined to take federal cabinet posts, preferring to hold office as a supporter whose votes were necessary. The MQM-P and smaller parties made up the remaining majority. In Punjab, Maryam Nawaz, Nawaz Sharif's daughter, became the first woman to serve as chief minister of a Pakistani province. Zardari's return to the presidency, with a more powerful role as a broker among parties and with the military leadership, extended the pattern, seen in 2008–2013, of a PPP leader who combined formal office with party management. The PPP's chairman, Bilawal Bhutto Zardari, ran a party that was in government at the federal level only conditionally, and which governed Sindh with its own majority. The terms on which the PPP supported the government, including its demands over water, development funds, and appointments, were negotiated repeatedly.

The opposition was dominated by the PTI, whose parliamentary strength was reduced by disputes over its status, and whose leadership was divided between those who favored negotiation and those who favored confrontation. A march on Islamabad called by the party's leadership in late November 2024 ended in a government crackdown at D-Chowk, and the casualties were disputed: the party claimed many deaths, while the government reported far fewer. The government and the PTI opened talks in December 2024, which collapsed in January 2025 when the PTI withdrew, saying that the government had not met its demands for a judicial commission to investigate the events of 9 May 2023 and November 2024 and for the release of detainees. Khan's conviction in the £190 million case, with a fourteen-year sentence, was announced in the same month. In the following months the PTI's capacity for street politics was limited by legal action against its leaders, including long prison sentences imposed in 2025 on some of its figures for their part in the May 2023 violence, and by internal divisions, though it continued to command wide public support in surveys and in by-elections. On 5 August 2026, the third anniversary of Khan's arrest in the Toshakhana case, thousands of supporters demonstrated in cities across the country, and police detained participants. The government's position was that Khan faced ordinary judicial processes; the party's, that his imprisonment, including reported restrictions on family visits and concerns about his health raised in early 2026, was arbitrary.

The federal–provincial confrontation entered a more serious phase on 11 October 2026, when President Asif Ali Zardari, acting on federal advice, proclaimed emergency and governor's rule in PTI-governed Khyber Pakhtunkhwa under constitutional Articles 232 and 234. The federal administration cited deteriorating security, militancy along the Afghan frontier, and what it described as a provincial failure to cooperate with counterterrorism operations; it authorized deployment of army personnel in aid of civil power. The intervention suspended the elected provincial administration, while Chief Minister Sohail Afridi and other PTI figures were detained. PTI denounced the step as an attack on its mandate and on provincial self-government. Parliament endorsed the proclamations on 15 October amid opposition protests, but the controversy continued: the PTI challenged the measures in court, and the governor defended them in November as exceptional responses to violence. The dispute exposed the limits of the Eighteenth Amendment's promise of provincial autonomy when the federal government invoked constitutional emergency powers. The military's profile rose during these years. In April 2025 an attack on tourists at Pahalgam in Indian-administered Kashmir was followed by India's suspension of the Indus Waters Treaty, and in May 2025 by several days of aerial and missile exchanges between India and Pakistan, ended by a ceasefire on 10 May. For Pakistan's domestic politics, its importance was that the army chief, General Asim Munir, was promoted to Field Marshal, and that the episode was presented by the government and army as a victory, strengthening the military's public standing, although the claims of each side regarding the outcome of air engagements were disputed by the other. Pakistan also strengthened ties with Saudi Arabia through a mutual defense agreement announced in September 2025 and improved its relations with the United States, whose president received Munir in Washington, even as it relied on China as its principal military and economic partner. Section 101.18

Constitutional Transformation, Judicial Authority, and Military Influence

The coalition government turned to constitutional amendment in 2024 and 2025, and the two measures that it passed, the Twenty-Sixth and Twenty-Seventh Amendments, rank among the most consequential changes to the Constitution since the Eighteenth. The question for a historian is how to place them: as institutional reform, as consolidation of power, or as both. The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010, adopted by consensus, reduced presidential power and increased provincial autonomy. The later amendments were passed by large majorities held by the government and its allies, but without the consensus of the main opposition party, and they were concerned primarily with the judiciary and the military.

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment, passed in October 2024 and signed on 21 October, changed the way the senior judiciary is constituted. It provided that the chief justice would be chosen by a special parliamentary committee from among the three most senior judges of the Supreme Court, rather than automatically by seniority; it altered the composition of the Judicial Commission of Pakistan, which nominates judges, to increase the role of parliament; it established constitutional benches in the Supreme Court and the high courts to hear constitutional cases; and it limited the courts' power to take up matters on their own initiative. The government said the reforms would end judicial overreach and reduce case backlogs. Critics, among them the PTI and many lawyers' groups, argued that the amendment, adopted rapidly and with disputed counting of votes, gave the government influence over appointments and the assignment of important cases, and that it came shortly after the Supreme Court's reserved-seats ruling had favored the PTI. Yahya Afridi, the third most senior judge, became chief justice in October 2024, and the amendment also contained provisions on environmental rights and an intention to eliminate interest (riba) from the financial system by 2028.

The Twenty-Seventh Amendment was signed into law by President Zardari on 13 November 2025, following approval by both houses; it passed the National Assembly by 234 votes to four and was adopted in the Senate amid a boycott by opposition members. It rewrote Article 243, abolished the office of Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee, and created the new office of Chief of Defence Forces, to be held by the Chief of the Army Staff, giving the army chief constitutional authority over the army, navy, and air force; it provided that the prime minister would appoint the commander of the National Strategic Command, responsible for nuclear forces, from within the army on the recommendation of the Chief of Defence Forces; and it made the titles of Field Marshal and the equivalent ranks of the other services held for life, with immunity from prosecution for holders of the rank. It established a Federal Constitutional Court, whose decisions bind all other courts including the Supreme Court, with the Supreme Court retaining civil and criminal appellate jurisdiction; it created a mechanism for the transfer of high-court judges; and it granted the president lifetime immunity from criminal proceedings, with exceptions. Justice Aminuddin Khan was sworn in as the first chief justice of the new court. Senior judges registered their opposition, and reporting indicated that some resigned in protest.

Section 101.19

Economic Stabilization, Social Strains, and Pakistan in 2026

The economic record after 2023 shows a stabilization that was real and fragile. Following the Stand-By Arrangement of 2023, the government negotiated a new 37-month Extended Fund Facility of about seven billion dollars, approved by the IMF Executive Board in September 2024, and in 2025 a Resilience and Sustainability Facility of about 1.4 billion dollars for climate-related reform. Inflation, which had reached nearly 38 percent in May 2023, fell to low single digits in 2025, the policy interest rate was reduced from its peak, foreign-exchange reserves rose from their lowest levels, and the current account moved closer to balance, supported by remittances that reached a record of roughly 38 billion dollars in the fiscal year 2024–2025. The government raised taxes, including on salaried workers and on sectors that had been lightly taxed, and reduced some energy subsidies. The agreements also required restructuring of power-sector debt and measures on state-owned enterprises, with mixed progress.

The strains remained. Growth was modest by the standards of a country with a young and growing population; public debt service consumed a very large share of federal revenue; the tax base remained narrow; and the energy sector, with high tariffs and heavy circular debt, imposed burdens on industry and households. An energy-price shock in early 2026, associated with war involving Iran, led to sharp fuel-price increases in Pakistan. On 7 October 2026 the IMF announced that its staff and the Pakistani authorities had reached a staff-level agreement on the fourth review of the Extended Fund Facility and the third review of the Resilience and Sustainability Facility, which, if approved by the Executive Board, would make about 1.2 billion dollars available. The distinction between a staff-level agreement and Board approval matters: a staff-level agreement is an understanding between IMF staff and the authorities, and as of this chapter's research date it had not been confirmed as a completed review. The IMF also reported continuing risks from geopolitical tensions, energy prices, and trade conditions, and press reports in October noted that it urged the phasing out of fuel subsidies, exchange-rate flexibility, and support targeted at vulnerable households; a projection of inflation near ten percent for fiscal year 2026–2027 was also reported, and should be treated as a forecast, not a result.

By the historical cutoff, the inflation picture had changed markedly from the unusually low readings of 2025: Pakistan's official year-on-year consumer-price inflation was 10.3 percent in September 2026 and 9.6 percent in October. The State Bank of Pakistan raised its policy interest rate by 50 basis points to 12 percent effective 27 October 2026, citing inflationary and macroeconomic risks. Section 101.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The historical question that organizes this chapter is why repeated elections, constitutional reforms, and changes of government failed to settle the distribution of political authority in Pakistan, even as Pakistani society and political participation changed substantially. The answer is not that democracy failed or that the military simply dominated. It is that four processes unfolded together and each altered the terms on which the others operated.

The first is the recurring confrontation between elected governments and the military establishment. In the 1990s it took the form of dismissals by presidents who acted with the army's weight behind them, and ended in the coup of 1999. Under Musharraf the army ruled directly, but used legal forms, an election program, and a pro-regime party; its authority eroded when it fell into conflict with the judiciary and public opinion in 2007. The restoration of civilian government in 2008 brought no coup but constant pressure through courts, intelligence agencies, and the control of security policy. After 2018 the military was the arbiter of a hybrid arrangement with Imran Khan, and after 2022 of a confrontation with him. The Twenty-Seventh Amendment of 2025 differs from earlier episodes in that it wrote military authority into the text of the Constitution. What did not occur was a direct coup in 2022 or 2024, which suggests that the military's methods changed: influence through legal, judicial, and electoral channels replaced open seizure of power.

The second process is the changing character of militancy and security governance. Pakistan moved from a state that had used militant groups as instruments in Afghanistan and Kashmir to one that suffered more from militant violence than any other in the region, and the war on terror brought both resources and legitimacy for the army and domestic conflict. After the Peshawar massacre of 2014 the National Action Plan seemed to produce national consensus, but its implementation emphasized military means. The return of the Taliban to Kabul in 2021 and the revival of the TTP, together with the Baloch insurgency, left the western borderlands as a continuing source of violence, while communities in these regions asked about their citizenship rights. The security institutions that fought militancy also gained political weight from doing so.

The third is the expansion and limitation of electoral politics. Pakistanis voted in nine party-based national elections between 1988 and 2024, parties alternated in office, the media expanded, and a new political force, the PTI, mobilized millions of voters who had previously been outside the party system. The Eighteenth Amendment shifted authority to provinces. At the same time, every prime minister has been removed or has left before a full term, elections have been challenged on grounds of unequal conditions, popular leaders have been disqualified or imprisoned, and courts have both defended parliament, as in 2022, and enabled removals of elected leaders. The constant element across Bhutto, Sharif, and Khan is that the accountability process and the judiciary were used by rivals and institutions alike, which weakened both.

The June 2025 reserved-seats reversal and October 2026 intervention in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa tested the reach of electoral representation and provincial autonomy. The March 2026 Kabul hospital strike also revealed the civilian costs of the unresolved frontier conflict. Constitutional legality, political legitimacy, and humanitarian accountability remained contested rather than settled.

The fourth is the recurrence of economic stabilization efforts. Pakistan has sought IMF assistance repeatedly, and each cycle followed a pattern of external borrowing, consumption-led growth, and crisis, with taxation, energy, and exports constrained by structural limits. The 2022–2023 crisis showed how economic and political crises reinforce each other: political confrontation delayed agreements with creditors, and austerity affected the legitimacy of governments. The stabilization of 2024–2026 was a genuine achievement of macroeconomic management, but its social benefits and durability remain to be seen, and the IMF's own provisional language of October 2026 illustrates that the history of this period is not complete.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Authority and Participation in Pakistan, 1990–2026

What Changed

  • The dismissal power was abolished; coups were replaced by legal and electoral mechanisms of influence
  • Provinces gained legislative and fiscal authority after 2010; the tribal areas were merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018
  • Private media, mobile telephony, and social media transformed political communication; the population grew from about 132 million (1998) to about 241 million (2023)
  • Militancy shifted from regional proxy conflicts to a domestic insurgency and renewed conflict with Afghanistan
  • Military authority was written into the Constitution in 2025

What Remained Continuous

  • The military's control of security and foreign policy toward India and Afghanistan
  • Use of accountability and courts in party conflict; incomplete terms for prime ministers
  • Dependence on external financing and IMF programs; a narrow tax base
  • Regional inequality among provinces and between urban and rural areas

Who experienced change most directly? Urban and middle-class voters, media workers, and residents of the northwestern and Baloch districts experienced the greatest change; who experienced greater continuity? Rural households dependent on agriculture and informal labor, whose conditions depended on prices and local power. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that institutional change and social change moved at different speeds.

The chapter's controlling interpretation is that Pakistan's history since 1990 has been neither an uninterrupted failure of democracy nor a march toward civilian consolidation. There have been genuine reforms, notable electoral transitions, a free if pressured press, and social change; and there have been persistent military influence, political exclusion, regional inequality, and economic vulnerability. The reason elections did not settle authority is that those who contested them did not agree on who should decide: voters, courts, the army, the president, or the provinces, and each institution acquired reasons to defend its own claim. Each reform responded to the previous round of conflict, and each was in turn used by those it empowered. The reader should resist the simple assumption that the outcome was inevitable, because alternatives existed at each stage: 1993, 1997, 2008, 2013, and 2022 each opened possibilities that were not taken.

Chapter 102 turns to Bangladesh, which was part of Pakistan until 1971 (Chapter 92) and which has followed its own trajectory since. Bangladesh also experienced military rule, a restoration of electoral democracy, intense rivalry between two dominant parties, and a political crisis, but in different institutional, economic, and social conditions: a parliamentary system that concentrated power in the prime minister, a dynamic garment export sector, and an uprising in 2024 whose causes lay in Bangladeshi circumstances. The two countries' histories should be compared without being treated as identical, and the comparison begins from the observation that shared inheritances produced different outcomes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Pakistan's history from the return of competitive politics after 1988 to the research cutoff of 19 November 2026, with attention to the relationship among elections, the military, constitutional authority, the economy, and social life. Its controlling claim is that Pakistan's history since 1990 has been neither an uninterrupted failure of democracy nor a march toward civilian consolidation.

In the 1990s Benazir Bhutto and Nawaz Sharif alternated in office, and the Eighth Amendment's Article 58(2)(b) allowed presidents to dismiss them. Three governments were dismissed, a Supreme Court restoration in 1993 was followed by a negotiated double resignation, and Karachi's violence showed the weakness of governance. The Thirteenth Amendment of 1997 removed the dissolution power, but Sharif then concentrated authority by overcoming the president and the chief justice.

The nuclear tests of May 1998 responded to India's tests, enjoyed wide popular support, and brought sanctions and financial emergency. The Lahore Declaration of February 1999 showed civilian diplomacy at its most ambitious, but the army's Kargil operation contradicted it, led to withdrawal under American pressure, and produced mutual blame between Sharif and Musharraf. Sharif's attempt to dismiss Musharraf on 12 October 1999 was answered by a coup.

Musharraf's regime was legitimated by the Supreme Court under the doctrine of necessity, extended by a referendum, the Legal Framework Order, and the Seventeenth Amendment, and sustained by a pro-regime party and by the American partnership after September 2001. Growth, devolution, and media liberalization were genuine, but depended on external conditions and on military control. The regime's contradictions emerged in 2007 with the suspension of the chief justice, the lawyers' movement, the emergency, the Red Mosque operation, and the assassination of Benazir Bhutto.

The civilian restoration of 2008 produced a government that completed its term, the Eighteenth Amendment of 2010, which removed presidential dismissal powers and devolved authority, and the first transfer between elected governments in 2013. The period was also marked by confrontations with the courts, the bin Laden raid, and the Salala crisis. Militancy in Swat and the tribal areas, the Army Public School massacre, and the National Action Plan showed the human cost and the growing role of the army in internal security, while Balochistan and Karachi followed different trajectories.

Nawaz Sharif's third government pursued energy and infrastructure projects, including CPEC, but faced the 2014 sit-in, military tension, and the Panama Papers case, which led to his disqualification in 2017. The 2018 election brought the PTI to power amid complaints of unequal conditions. Imran Khan's government faced economic stress, the pandemic, a deteriorating relationship with the military, and, in April 2022, removal by a no-confidence vote after the Supreme Court restored the assembly.

The confrontation that followed, including the events of 9 May 2023, military trials of civilians, Khan's imprisonment, the loss of the PTI's symbol, and the election of February 2024 with its disputed counts, tested the legitimacy of the system. The coalition government of Shehbaz Sharif and Asif Ali Zardari adopted the Twenty-Sixth and Twenty-Seventh Amendments, which reshaped the judiciary and gave constitutional form to the army chief's command of all services.

Economic stabilization under IMF programs reduced inflation and raised reserves, but left a narrow tax base, heavy debt service, energy-sector problems, and a young population needing jobs; the IMF's staff-level agreement of 7 October 2026 awaited Executive Board approval. Security conditions worsened on the Afghan border and in Balochistan, with open conflict with the Afghan Taliban government in 2026.

The synthesis argued that four transformations account for the persistence of the authority question: repeated civil-military confrontation, changing militancy and security governance, the expansion and limits of electoral politics, and recurring economic stabilization. The chapter ends by linking Pakistan to the separate Bangladeshi trajectory in Chapter 102.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a large and young society whose political institutions have changed repeatedly without settling who decides. Pakistan's experience is often compared with India's, and the comparison is informative, but it is only the beginning. Pakistan's story includes a decisive reform in 2010, a lawyers' movement that revived an independent judiciary, a record of resilience under militant violence, and elections that mobilized millions.

The chapter also reveals how the past shaped the present. The doctrine of necessity, the dismissal power, the habit of using accountability against rivals, the army's control of security policy, and the structure of the economy all had earlier roots, in the periods covered by Chapters 90 and 95, and they recurred with new forms. The Twenty-Seventh Amendment, in writing military authority into the Constitution, continued an old argument by new means.

In the six-volume series, the chapter stands alongside Chapter 100's account of India and before Chapter 102's account of Bangladesh. Together they show that South Asia's countries, which shared a colonial inheritance, followed different paths, and that the history of the region cannot be told as a single national story. Students should carry forward the habit of separating documented fact from allegation and of asking who held authority, how it was exercised, and who bore its consequences.
