The Iranian Revolution, 1977–1979

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between the spring of 1977 and the afternoon of 11 February 1979, a monarchy that possessed one of the largest armies in the Middle East, an extensive security apparatus, and an oil income that had astonished the world lost its capacity to govern. It did not fall to a foreign army or a single coup. It was undone by a movement whose members agreed on little except that the existing order could no longer continue.

In October 1977, for ten evenings, audiences gathered at the German-Iranian Cultural Association on Pahlavi Avenue in Tehran to hear poets and essayists read. The event was co-organized with the Goethe-Institut, whose own premises were too small for the audiences. Such events were not unusual in themselves. What made them remarkable was the size of the crowds, the fact that the sponsoring body, the Writers' Association of Iran, was making an open case for freedom of expression, and the manner in which verse and lecture became a language of political criticism that the censors could not easily suppress. Fifteen months later, on 11 February 1979, the commanders of the Iranian armed forces met and declared that the military would remain neutral in the political struggle. Within hours, the last government appointed by Mohammad Reza Shah had lost the one institution on which its survival depended. This chapter explains how Iran travelled from the first of those moments to the second.

The chapter's central problem is one of combination. By 1977 Iran contained many kinds of discontent: bazaar merchants who resented state intervention in commerce, clerics who resented the monarchy's religious policies, constitutional nationalists who regarded the Shah's authority as a violation of the 1906 constitution, students and intellectuals frustrated by censorship, leftist organizations committed to armed or revolutionary struggle, migrants in overcrowded urban districts, industrial and white-collar workers with wage and workplace grievances, and women who participated in political life from very different assumptions. These groups did not form a single movement in 1977. The historical question is how, over some twenty months, they came to act in a loosely coordinated way against a common target, and why the state found no effective means of dividing them, accommodating them, or defeating them.

Three processes, examined throughout this chapter, answer that question. The first is the expansion of opposition: the movement from limited letters, poetry readings, and professional petitions to commemorations, street demonstrations, and finally a nationwide mobilization of religious networks, markets, universities, and workplaces. The second is the breakdown of governing capacity: strikes that interrupted administration, finance, communications, and the petroleum industry; successive governments that oscillated between concession and coercion; and finally a military whose senior leadership concluded it could not or would not rescue the monarchy. The third is the emergence of revolutionary sovereignty: the growing belief among millions of Iranians that legitimate authority no longer resided in the Shah or in governments appointed by him, though without agreement on what should replace them.

Writing the history of a revolution requires unusual care with evidence. Many of the best-known claims about 1978 were shaped by the circumstances in which they were made. Opposition leaders circulated casualty figures that served mobilizing purposes. Government censorship and press strikes limited what newspapers could report. Foreign diplomats and journalists saw particular parts of the country and relied on particular informants. After February 1979, the victorious movement had its own reasons to simplify the story, and exiled royalists had theirs. Historians therefore work with several kinds of evidence at once: contemporary newspapers, declassified diplomatic cables, oral and written memoirs, the records of organizations, labor histories reconstructed from workers' own testimony, and studies based on post-revolutionary records of the dead. Throughout the chapter, what contemporaries believed is distinguished from what later research has established.

Two cautions govern the whole narrative. First, the revolution should not be read backward from its outcome. In early 1977 few observers, inside or outside Iran, expected the monarchy to fall within two years, and many prominent critics sought constitutional reform rather than the end of the dynasty. Second, the coalition that overthrew the monarchy must be distinguished from the political system established afterward. People who marched together in December 1978 held different visions of religion, law, property, gender, nationhood, and the state. The institutions of the Islamic Republic, examined from Chapter 92 onward, emerged from a subsequent struggle among revolutionary forces; they were not simply the program of the whole movement that brought down the Shah.

This chapter concludes Volume VII and Part XV. It builds on the economic and social conditions examined in Chapter 90 and the networks of repression, clerical organization, leftist insurgency, and intellectual dissent examined in Chapter 89, and it assumes the history of royal authority traced from Chapter 81 through the 1953 coup of Chapter 87 and the White Revolution of Chapter 88. It ends on 11 February 1979. The detailed institutional history of the transfer of power, the provisional government, and the revolutionary committees belongs to Chapter 92, and the construction of the constitutional order to Chapter 93.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why economic growth and administrative modernization did not by themselves secure political legitimacy for the Pahlavi monarchy.
  2. Trace the revival of public dissent in 1977, including the roles of constitutional nationalists, writers, lawyers, students, and religious networks.
  3. Reconstruct the sequence of demonstrations, killings, and commemorations from Qom in January 1978 to the Muharram marches of December 1978.
  4. Evaluate competing casualty claims for Qom, Tabriz, Cinema Rex, and Black Friday, distinguishing contemporary assertions from later research.
  5. Analyze how strikes, especially in the public sector and the oil industry, undermined the state's capacity to govern and changed the character of the movement.
  6. Assess the successive efforts of the Shah and his governments to contain the crisis through concession, accommodation, and military rule.
  7. Distinguish the participation and aims of religious leaders, constitutional nationalists, leftists, workers, bazaaris, students, and women within the revolutionary coalition.
  8. Interpret the roles of Ayatollah Khomeini's exile leadership and international communications without reducing the revolution to a single leader.
  9. Evaluate the evidence for and against claims that foreign governments directed the revolution or merely responded to it.
  10. Distinguish the Shah's departure of 16 January 1979 from the collapse of the monarchy on 11 February 1979 and explain the military's decisive role.
  11. Compare scholarly explanations of the revolution's success and identify what each emphasizes and omits.
  12. Connect the revolution to earlier constitutional, nationalist, and oil-related struggles and to the problems confronting the post-monarchical state.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a movement that began with letters, poetry readings, and funeral commemorations become capable of paralyzing the Iranian state?
  2. Why did a government with substantial military and financial resources fail to find an effective response to the protests of 1978?
  3. In what ways did religious networks, bazaars, universities, and workplaces each contribute distinct resources to the opposition?
  4. How should historians weigh organized strategy against spontaneous popular anger when explaining the escalation of 1978?
  5. Why do casualty figures for key events of 1978 differ so widely, and what do those differences reveal about evidence and politics?
  6. To what extent did workers act as an independent force, and to what extent were they enlisted into a movement led by others?
  7. What did participants mean when they demanded the end of the monarchy, and how did their expectations of the future differ?
  8. Did foreign policy shape the timing and outcome of the crisis, and what limits does the evidence place on that claim?
  9. Why did the monarchy collapse in February 1979 rather than earlier or later, and what role did contingency play?

Historical Context

The Pahlavi state that confronted the crisis of 1977 was the product of five decades of centralization. Reza Shah had built a standing army, a modern bureaucracy, and a secular legal and educational system after 1925 (Chapters 81–83). His son, Mohammad Reza Shah, survived the political openings of the 1940s and early 1950s, the crisis over oil nationalization under Mohammad Mossadegh (Chapter 86), and the coup of August 1953 (Chapter 87), and in the following decades consolidated a monarchy in which the Shah personally directed foreign policy, security, and major economic decisions. The White Revolution of the 1960s (Chapter 88) redistributed some land, expanded literacy, and extended the vote to women, while the 1963 suppression of religious and political protest removed organized opposition from public life. In March 1975 the Shah proclaimed a single legal party, Rastakhiz, an act that formally ended even the limited competition that had previously existed inside the Majles.

The oil boom that followed the 1973 price increases (Chapter 90) enlarged both the state's ambitions and its vulnerabilities. Public spending rose sharply, new industries and infrastructure projects multiplied, universities and urban populations expanded, and migration from rural areas transformed the major cities. At the same time, inflation, housing shortages, uneven distribution of benefits, dependence on imported goods and foreign technicians, and a government campaign against so-called profiteering in the bazaar generated resentment. The security organization SAVAK, and associated police and military courts, repressed leftist guerrillas, Islamist militants, and many nonviolent critics (Chapter 89). Opposition therefore existed in 1976, but it was fragmented, often clandestine, and in the view of most contemporary observers unable to threaten the regime's survival.

Several institutions and traditions proved important once political space reopened. The Shiʿi religious establishment, though subjected to state control over endowments and religious education, retained mosques, seminaries, preachers, charitable networks, and a calendar of commemorations through which large groups could assemble. The bazaar, a commercial and social network linking merchants, artisans, financiers, and clerics, retained both economic leverage and organizational habits. Universities, now much larger, produced a politically aware student body and a generation of educated professionals. The memory of the 1906 Constitutional Revolution (Chapters 78–79) and of Mossadegh's nationalist movement supplied vocabularies of constitutional government and national independence that neither the monarchy nor the clergy could fully claim for themselves. Finally, Iran was integrated into international communications and diplomacy in ways that made events inside the country quickly visible to the outside world.

Finally, the international environment changed. In the United States, the administration of Jimmy Carter, inaugurated in January 1977, publicly emphasized human rights, and international organizations such as Amnesty International had drawn attention to the treatment of political prisoners in Iran. These developments did not cause the revolution, but they altered the expectations of critics about how safe it was to speak, and they gave the government reasons to adjust its behavior. At the same time, Iran remained a central American and Western strategic partner in the Persian Gulf, a major purchaser of arms, and a leading oil exporter. The combination of strategic importance and public scrutiny shaped how foreign governments, and the Shah himself, interpreted the crisis as it unfolded.

Section 91.1

Iran on the Eve of Revolution: The Limits of the Pahlavi Monarchy

In 1976 and early 1977 Iran appeared, to most foreign observers and to the Shah himself, to be a country of rapid and successful transformation. The census of 1976 recorded a population of roughly thirty-four million, a large proportion of it now living in towns and cities. Tehran had grown into a metropolis of several million, with new highways, apartment blocks, universities, hospitals, and industrial suburbs. School enrollment had expanded dramatically since the early 1960s, literacy had risen, and a salaried middle class of teachers, engineers, civil servants, and technicians had become a significant social presence. Oil income, multiplied after 1973, financed large development programs and an extensive military procurement effort that Chapter 90 examines in detail. By the standards of many developing countries, these were substantial achievements.

Yet the same processes generated grievances. The speed of the boom produced bottlenecks in ports, housing, power supply, and construction labor. Inflation, particularly in rents and food, eroded the incomes of wage earners and the lower middle class. Rural migrants crowded into the southern and eastern districts of Tehran and other large cities, often living in informal housing with limited services. The government's anti-profiteering campaign of 1975–1976, which imposed price controls, fines, and in some cases banishments on merchants and shopkeepers, was widely resented in the bazaar and fed a perception that the state was blaming commercial classes for problems it had itself created. When oil revenues fell short of expectations and the government introduced an economic stabilization program after the appointment of Prime Minister Jamshid Amouzegar in August 1977, cuts in spending and a slowdown in construction contributed to unemployment among the migrant workers who had been drawn to building sites. These economic strains did not produce a revolution on their own, but they enlarged the pool of people with reasons to distrust the government.

Political conditions mattered at least as much. Since the proclamation of the Rastakhiz Party in 1975, the political system had been formally organized around a single party under the Shah's direction. Political parties independent of the monarchy had no legal existence, the press was subject to censorship, and security institutions monitored political, religious, and student activity. Political trials and military tribunals dealt with accused guerrillas and activists. Many Iranians who would not have identified with any opposition group nonetheless experienced the state as arbitrary, intrusive, and unaccountable. Concentrated decision-making also meant that policy failures were attributed directly to the monarch. It is worth noting too that the Shah had been diagnosed with a serious illness, a form of lymphatic cancer, that was known to only a handful of people at the time; historians disagree about how far his health and the secrecy surrounding it affected the decisiveness of his response in 1978.

It is necessary to distinguish several kinds of objection. Material grievances concerned wages, prices, housing, and employment. Constitutional objections held that the Shah had violated the 1906 constitution by ruling rather than reigning. Religious criticism targeted state control of religious institutions, policies viewed as promoting Western cultural influence, and the monarchy's moral legitimacy. Nationalist objections concerned the Shah's alleged dependence on the United States and other foreign powers, a theme with deep roots in the memory of 1953. Demands for civil liberties focused on censorship, political imprisonment, and the treatment of detainees. Individuals could hold several of these views simultaneously, but different groups gave them different weight, and the policies that might satisfy one set of critics might not satisfy another.

Why did evident development achievements not translate into durable legitimacy? Historians have offered several answers. One emphasizes that the regime's legitimacy rested largely on performance and on the personal authority of the monarch rather than on institutions through which citizens could participate in decisions, so that economic disappointment, or the perception of unfair distribution, was immediately political. Another stresses that rapid modernization created new, educated, and mobilized groups whose expectations of participation exceeded what the system offered. A third points to the symbolic and cultural dimension: many Iranians, including some who benefited materially from the boom, felt that official ceremonies, ostentatious celebrations such as the 1971 festivities at Persepolis, and policies such as the 1976 replacement of the Hijri solar calendar with an imperial calendar expressed a monarchy detached from their own religious and national identities. None of these explanations is sufficient alone.

The section must close with a caution against hindsight. In early 1977, revolutionary overthrow was not an expected outcome. The monarchy retained control of the army, the security services, and the oil income. The opposition was divided and had been defeated repeatedly. The first public criticisms of 1977 asked the Shah to observe the constitution and respect rights, not to abdicate. The revolution's later radicalization was a result of the interaction of events, decisions, and responses examined in the rest of this chapter, not a foreordained consequence of the conditions described here.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Legitimacy, Revolutionary Situation, and Constitutional Opposition

Definition: Political legitimacy is the widely shared belief that a government or ruler has the right to exercise authority, as distinct from the mere capacity to compel obedience. A revolutionary situation is a condition in which significant numbers of people reject the authority of the existing state and some organized body is able to advance a rival claim to authority. Constitutional opposition refers to critics who seek to hold the state to constitutional or legal limits rather than to destroy it.

Historical context: In 1977 the constitutional opposition of the National Front and similar groups argued that the monarchy should conform to the 1906 constitution. By late 1978 many Iranians who had begun with such arguments considered that the Shah himself could no longer remain in power; the demand moved from reforming the system to replacing it.

Why it matters: These terms help explain why concessions that might have satisfied critics in 1977 failed in late 1978. A revolutionary situation does not exist merely because there is discontent; it emerges when legitimacy has eroded and an alternative source of authority exists.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that legitimacy is lost because a regime is repressive or unpopular at a given moment, or that revolutionary situations always produce revolutions. Whether a revolutionary situation becomes a successful revolution depends on the resources and decisions of both the state and its opponents.

Section 91.2

The Revival of Public Criticism: Writers, Lawyers, Students, and Human Rights

During 1977 the government allowed, or at least failed to prevent, a partial widening of public expression. The reasons were varied and are debated. International attention to Iranian prisoners, the new emphasis on human rights in American policy, visits by representatives of international organizations, and the government's wish to improve its image abroad all played some part. Domestic officials may also have believed that a limited release of pressure would be safe, or have been divided over how to respond. Whatever the motives, critics detected a change in the risk of speaking. Individuals who had previously confined their views to private conversation began to put them in writing and to sign their names.

The first manifestation was a series of open letters and petitions. Retired politicians, professionals, and activists addressed letters to the Shah and to government officials, calling for respect for legal rights and constitutional procedure. Associations of lawyers and jurists raised questions about military tribunals, the conduct of trials, and the independence of the judiciary. Members of the Writers' Association of Iran, an organization whose history of tension with the censors stretched back more than a decade, issued statements demanding freedom of the press and the right to organize. Students at universities in Tehran and elsewhere resumed political discussion and, at times, organized protests, often drawing on the broader climate of criticism to challenge campus restrictions. The cumulative effect was not a mass movement but a visible change in what could be said publicly.

The most dramatic expression of this change was the Ten Nights of Poetry and Lectures, held in Tehran from 10 to 19 October 1977. The Writers' Association of Iran collaborated with the Goethe-Institut, but the gatherings themselves took place in the spacious garden of the German-Iranian Cultural Association on Pahlavi Avenue; the Goethe-Institut's own facilities were too small for the audiences. This location is documented in contemporary institutional records and subsequent scholarship. Poets and writers of different generations and political tendencies read verse and presented talks; the audience was larger than the hall could hold, and attendance reportedly swelled over the course of the series, with crowds spilling into the grounds. The programs ostensibly concerned literature, but their content, and the response of the audience, often concerned freedom, censorship, and political repression. Because the events were organized by a cultural association and hosted by a foreign institution, the authorities were uncertain how to treat them, and the participants exploited the ambiguity. Later gatherings of the same movement, including events on university campuses, were disrupted by police, and at least one episode involved violence, but the Ten Nights as a whole demonstrated that large audiences would assemble to hear political criticism expressed through literary forms.

The significance of this literary mobilization is easily exaggerated or underestimated. It did not itself bring thousands of workers or bazaar merchants into the streets, and most of its participants were intellectuals, students, and members of the educated urban public. But it established several precedents. It showed that open criticism could be undertaken without immediate suppression. It linked diverse writers, including secular leftists, nationalists, and others, in a public defense of expression. It exemplified a feature of Iranian political culture in which poetry, ritual, and commemoration had long served as vehicles for dissent when direct political organization was impossible. And it contributed to a shared sense, among educated Iranians, that 1977 had opened a different political season.

Evidence for these developments includes the open letters themselves, the published statements of the Writers' Association, contemporary press coverage constrained by censorship, memoirs by participants, and scholarly studies of the Ten Nights drawing on German diplomatic and church sources as well as Iranian testimony. Students should note that participants' recollections, written after the revolution, sometimes retrospectively emphasize the movement's inevitability or its alignment with later political outcomes. The contemporary documents show greater uncertainty and a stronger emphasis on constitutional and cultural claims than on revolutionary change.

The section's central point is chronological. Cultural, professional, and constitutional opposition had begun to reopen the public sphere months before the mass demonstrations of 1978. The later protest movement did not arise from nowhere; it entered a political space already partly cleared by writers, lawyers, students, and critics, many of whom would later find themselves in disagreement with the movement's more radical or religiously defined wings.

Section 91.3

The National Front and the Constitutional Challenge to the Shah

Among the groups that re-emerged in 1977 was the constitutional nationalist opposition associated with the National Front, the coalition that had supported Mohammad Mossadegh in the early 1950s (Chapter 86). After the 1953 coup and the subsequent repression, the National Front had been banned, its leaders imprisoned, exiled, or marginalized, and its organization reduced to scattered networks and memory. In 1977 several of its veterans resumed public activity. Karim Sanjabi, a longtime political leader who had served in Mossadegh's circle, Shapour Bakhtiar, a lawyer and former deputy minister of labor under Mossadegh, and Dariush Forouhar, who headed a nationalist party within the front's tradition, emerged as leading figures. The related Freedom Movement of Iran, led by Mehdi Bazargan, brought a religious-nationalist perspective: Bazargan, an engineer and former head of the National Iranian Oil Company's operations at Mossadegh's request, combined commitment to constitutional politics with Islamic conviction.

The National Front and the Freedom Movement should not be confused. The National Front was a broader coalition of secular and moderate nationalists with a history of parliamentary politics and an emphasis on national independence and constitutional government. The Freedom Movement, founded in the early 1960s, argued for a politics rooted in Islamic values together with liberal and nationalist aims, and its leaders, including Bazargan and Yadollah Sahabi, had close relationships with religious activists. The two organizations overlapped in membership and commitments, particularly the defense of the constitution, opposition to arbitrary rule, and the memory of Mossadegh, but they differed in religious emphasis, organizational style, and their eventual relationships with the clerical movement.

On 12 June 1977 Sanjabi, Bakhtiar, and Forouhar issued an open letter to the Shah. In general terms, the letter criticized the concentration of authority in the monarch's hands, asserted that the existing political order violated constitutional principles, and called for restoration of political and civil rights, an end to arbitrary imprisonment, and respect for law. The signers did not call for abolition of the monarchy. Their position, consistent with National Front tradition since the 1950s, was that the Shah should reign within the constitution and that political life should be governed by lawful, representative institutions. The letter was circulated beyond the immediate political class and attracted attention in the foreign press.

The letter is important for what it shows about the starting point of the revolutionary process. In mid-1977, significant opponents of the regime still imagined a reformed monarchy. They called on the Shah himself to alter course, and they appealed to a constitutional tradition that the monarchy officially claimed to uphold. The legacy of 1906, discussed in Chapters 78 and 79, and the myth of Mossadegh as a defender of national sovereignty shaped their language. Their strategy was to use the opening created by international attention and domestic discontent to press the Shah toward political liberalization. The strategy had limits. The National Front lacked a mass organization, relied on personal prestige and a few publications, and depended on the Shah's willingness to respond. When that willingness proved limited and the crisis deepened, the front's leaders found themselves caught between a monarch who would not concede enough and a movement that increasingly demanded more.

Students should also see why the letter matters to historiography. Accounts written after the revolution sometimes treat the opposition as having been consistently revolutionary from the start. The 1977 letter, together with other contemporary documents, demonstrates a more contingent process in which demands changed as the government's responses, the scale of mobilization, and the numbers killed changed the political calculus. This is the kind of evidence that allows historians to resist reading 1979 back into 1977.

Primary Source Window

The National Front Leaders' Open Letter to the Shah, 12 June 1977

Creators: Karim Sanjabi, Shapour Bakhtiar, and Dariush Forouhar · Date: 12 June 1977 · Type: Political open letter addressed to the monarch and circulated publicly

Context: The letter was written when international attention to political rights in Iran was high and the government was tolerating a limited expansion of public criticism. The signers were veteran nationalist politicians whose organization had been suppressed for more than two decades.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The signers addressed the Shah directly, argued that political life had departed from the constitution adopted after the Constitutional Revolution, criticized arbitrary and unaccountable government, and called for respect for legal rights, freedom of political expression and association, and the release of political prisoners. They framed these demands as remedies that would strengthen, not abolish, constitutional government. Students should consult the Persian letter dated 22 Khordad 1356 (12 June 1977), or a documented translation; a digitized image of the signed letter is catalogued at Wikisource's Persian-language document collection. This paraphrase does not reproduce its wording.

Analysis: The letter reveals a reformist strategy grounded in constitutional legitimacy. Its authors assumed that the Shah could, if he chose, change course and that the language of the constitution was a shared reference point. It also shows how the opposition justified itself: not as an enemy of the state, but as a defender of its lawful foundations.

Limitations: A single letter cannot establish how many Iranians shared its views or whether its signers would have accepted alternatives to a constitutional monarchy under different circumstances. It also reflects the perspective of a narrow political elite with few organizational resources and little connection to the religious networks and workplaces that later drove mass mobilization.

Perspective: The source represents secular and moderate nationalist politicians. It does not directly reflect the views of clerics, leftist organizations, workers, or the unorganized urban poor.

Section 91.4

Religious Networks, Exile Leadership, and the Expansion of Opposition

The third source of renewed opposition was the religious establishment and the networks surrounding it. Chapter 89 described how the state had sought to control religious institutions after 1963 while stopping short of dismantling them. Mosques remained gathering places, preachers continued to speak at weekly prayers and special occasions, seminaries in Qom, Mashhad, Isfahan, and other cities trained students who dispersed into provincial towns, and religious associations raised and distributed funds for charity and education. These institutions were not inherently oppositional. Many clerics avoided politics, some cooperated with the state, and senior scholars differed sharply about whether religious authority should engage in government. But the institutions provided a communication system, a calendar of gatherings, and a vocabulary of moral critique that could be mobilized in politically charged circumstances.

The bazaar was closely linked to this network. Bazaar merchants and artisans financed mosques and religious schools, attended the same rituals, and frequently shared family ties with clerics. The bazaar's resentment at the anti-profiteering campaign and at state policies perceived as favoring large modern enterprises and foreign firms gave it independent reasons for opposition, while its commercial networks provided communications, money, and the ability to close shops as a collective act. This relationship between preachers, seminary students, merchants, and charitable associations created a social infrastructure that the secular opposition lacked.

Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini occupied a distinctive place within this world. Expelled from Iran in 1964 after his denunciation of the capitulations law and of the government's policies, he had lived first in Turkey and then, from 1965, in the Shiʿi shrine city of Najaf in Iraq. From there he continued to teach and, in the early 1970s, articulated the doctrine of clerical governance, velayat-e faqih, in lectures later circulated as a book. His statements reached Iran through correspondence, printed declarations, cassette recordings, the movements of pilgrims and students, and networks of former students and sympathizers. Khomeini's views were known to a segment of the religious public, but many Iranians, including many clerics, had not read his lectures on government, and their support in 1977–1978 did not necessarily rest on endorsement of his full political theory.

The death of Khomeini's elder son, Mostafa Khomeini, in Najaf in October 1977 provided an occasion for gatherings in Iran. Many opponents of the regime suspected that the death, officially attributed to a heart condition, had been caused by SAVAK; no conclusive evidence has been established, and the claim is best described as a widely held suspicion. Memorial services were organized in Tehran, Qom, and other cities, and they were used to express admiration for Khomeini and criticism of the monarchy. The incident illustrates a pattern that recurred throughout 1978: deaths and mourning created legitimate occasions for religious assembly that the state found difficult to prohibit without appearing to attack religion itself.

It would be a mistake to portray the religious establishment as a unified supporter of Khomeini. Ayatollah Kazem Shariatmadari, one of the leading religious authorities in Qom and an Azerbaijani by origin with a large following in Tabriz, favored a more cautious approach and defended the 1906 constitution. Ayatollah Mahmud Taleghani, a Tehran cleric with a history of imprisonment and a reputation for sympathy toward activists of various tendencies, became a significant bridge between religious and other opposition groups. Some senior scholars maintained a quietist position; others cooperated with the state. The ways in which religious leaders interacted with Khomeini, with each other, and with secular activists were a major variable in the course of the revolution, and are examined in later sections.

As Division I closes, the sources of opposition were visible but distinct: constitutional nationalists who wrote letters, writers and lawyers who tested freedom of expression, and a religious-bazaar network linked to an exiled leader. They did not yet constitute a coordinated movement. What was missing was an event or process that would bring these elements into repeated, large-scale, public contact with each other and with the state's coercive power. That event came in January 1978.

Section 91.5

The January 1978 Qom Demonstrations and the Beginning of a Protest Cycle

On 7 January 1978 the Tehran daily Ettela'at published an article titled in translation as "Iran and Red and Black Colonization," attributed to a pseudonymous author. The article attacked Ayatollah Khomeini in harsh terms, questioning his patriotism and ancestry and insinuating that he served foreign interests and that his movement was allied with reactionary and radical forces opposed to the Shah's reforms. Reports and later scholarship indicate that the piece was published with the knowledge or at the instigation of officials at the Ministry of Information and perhaps at higher levels, as part of a campaign to discredit Khomeini. The precise chain of decision-making remains imperfectly documented. What is clear is that the article was widely read in religious circles as an insult to a revered senior cleric and, by extension, to the religious establishment.

The reaction came first in Qom, the principal seminary city. On 8 January seminary students and teachers closed classes and protested, and some bazaar shops shut in solidarity. Senior clerics, including Shariatmadari, expressed disapproval. On 9 January a larger demonstration moved through the city; security forces confronted the crowd, and shooting occurred. Some participants were killed. The government's account at the time acknowledged a small number of deaths, in the single digits, and blamed the violence on provocateurs and agitators. Opposition sources claimed that many dozens or more had died, and in later years some accounts cited figures in the hundreds. Subsequent scholarship has generally treated the higher claims with caution and the official numbers as an understatement, concluding that a modest but uncertain number, more than the government acknowledged and considerably fewer than the largest claims, were killed. The exact number cannot be settled from the surviving evidence, and the chapter's later practice of distinguishing "claimed" from "documented" casualties begins here.

The importance of Qom lay less in the number of the dead than in what their deaths came to mean. The victims, who included seminary students, became "martyrs" in the language of Shiʿi commemoration, and the event was framed as the state's attack on religion and on a beloved leader's honor. For many Iranians who had no direct involvement in politics, the shooting of unarmed religious students violated a basic norm. For opposition activists, the episode offered a demonstration of the regime's brutality and a focus for organizing. For the government, it was the first of several moments when force produced political consequences opposite to those intended.

The government's reaction reveals a persistent difficulty. The leadership did not anticipate that publishing an attack on a senior cleric during a period of expanding criticism would provoke a significant response, and it misjudged the way seminary protest could connect to the bazaar and to wider urban publics. Once the demonstrations began, the authorities used a combination of police and military force and public denunciations of the protesters as agents of "black reaction" and "red" subversion, categories drawn from official political language that carried little resonance for those who saw themselves as defending religion. The state had few instruments for engaging religious leaders politically, having spent years reducing the autonomy of religious institutions while insisting on its own religious legitimacy.

Evidence for these events is uneven. The article itself survives and is widely cited. Press reports from the period were shaped by censorship. Foreign correspondents and diplomats offered partial accounts. Memoirs by clerics and activists, and later accounts compiled by revolutionary institutions, emphasized the martyrs and the regime's culpability. Historians therefore treat the Qom events as established in broad outline but uncertain in detail, particularly with respect to casualty numbers and the degree of planning by officials.

Section 91.6

Tabriz and the Forty-Day Mourning Cycle

In Shiʿi practice, the dead are commemorated on the third, seventh, and fortieth days after death, with the fortieth, called chehelom, marking a major occasion of mourning. The deaths in Qom on 9 January produced a fortieth-day observance on or around 18 February. Religious leaders called for memorial gatherings, and in Tabriz a large demonstration took place. The protest began as a commemoration but turned violent. Crowds attacked and damaged banks, cinemas, hotels, offices of the ruling party, and other symbols associated with the state, wealth, and Western-style leisure. Security forces responded with gunfire, and the army was brought in. Deaths occurred, with estimates varying widely, from the official figures in the low double digits to opposition claims reaching into the hundreds. As with Qom, exact counts remain uncertain.

Tabriz was significant for several reasons. Azerbaijan was an economically and politically important region with strong links to the national bazaar, a history of regional political movements, and the base of Ayatollah Shariatmadari's following. The city's largely Azerbaijani Turkic-speaking population was not outside the national movement; Azerbaijanis took part in Iranian religious, nationalist, and revolutionary politics throughout the period. And the fact that unrest of this scale could arise in a major provincial city only forty days after Qom suggested that Qom had not been an isolated incident.

Subsequent cycles followed. About forty days after Tabriz, on or about 29–30 March, demonstrations took place in Yazd and in other cities. A new set of deaths followed. In May and June, further commemorations and confrontations occurred, including in Qom and other cities, as well as sporadic bazaar closures and student protests in Tehran and elsewhere. Each cycle involved local organizers, clerics who announced commemorations, merchants who closed shops, students who circulated leaflets, and security forces who sought to prevent or contain gatherings. When shooting occurred, new deaths created the occasion for another forty-day gathering.

The commemorative cycle had real power. It supplied a recurring, religiously legitimate occasion for assembly; it linked distant cities through a shared calendar; it turned individual deaths into collective narratives of injustice; and it placed the government in a dilemma, since prohibiting mourning ceremonies could be portrayed as an assault on religion. These features made it a potent mechanism for prolonging protest after the initial shock of an event.

But the mechanism should not be overstated. A cycle of forty-day observances did not automatically produce mass demonstrations; the cycle's later iterations were not uniform in size or consequence; and the movement did not simply move from one city to another in a perfectly timed sequence. Local conditions mattered: the readiness of clerics to call gatherings, the willingness of the bazaar to close, the presence of student networks, the decisions of local police and military commanders, and economic circumstances. Government decisions also mattered, as when authorities chose to ban a gathering, to allow it, or to react with force. Other events, such as arrests, local scandals, and shifts in national political tactics, also produced protests outside the forty-day sequence. The cycle was therefore a structure that organizers could use, not a clockwork that determined events.

Between the cycles, quieter organizing took place. Preachers delivered sermons that connected local grievances to national themes. Leaflets and cassette recordings circulated. Religious students from Qom and other seminaries traveled to provincial towns. Lawyers and professionals continued to petition. The sources for this work are fragmentary, but they suggest that each public protest rested on a less visible effort to prepare the next.

The distribution of commemorations also reveals the importance of provincial autonomy within the uprising. Tabriz had political traditions of its own, including the constitutional movement and the activism of religious scholars and urban merchants. Local participants did not need to receive an identical instruction from Tehran or Najaf before deciding to close shops, gather for mourning, or challenge the authorities. Networks carried news and supplied a shared symbolic language, but local relationships determined whether a commemoration became a large protest. That difference helps explain why some towns saw major confrontations at one moment while neighboring places remained comparatively quiet, and why the official reliance on security forces sometimes widened rather than contained mobilization.

Section 91.7

The Expansion of Demonstrations and the Changing Demands of Protesters

During the spring and summer of 1978, localized protest became a more extensive movement. Several developments contributed. The protests drew in additional constituencies: university and high-school students, bazaar merchants and artisans, urban neighborhoods with strong mosque associations, and, increasingly, professionals and employees. The movement acquired a more extensive set of local organizers, including preachers, neighborhood committees, and student groups. Opposition leaders in different cities began to communicate with one another and, to a limited extent, to coordinate. Religious figures, including Khomeini through audio messages and letters, encouraged continued protest, and other leaders such as Shariatmadari and Taleghani adopted positions that supported mobilization while differing about aims.

The demands of protesters changed over time. Early demonstrations in Qom and Tabriz expressed outrage at specific insults and killings and called for punishment of those responsible, release of detainees, and respect for religious institutions. Gradually, protesters added broader demands: freedom of the press and political expression, the release of political prisoners, the lifting of restrictions on religious activity, an end to censorship, and the dismissal of officials associated with repression. Some slogans began to target the monarchy itself, and by the late summer demands for the Shah's removal were more frequently heard. This transformation was uneven. Some groups kept to constitutional or reformist demands for months, and many participants held several demands simultaneously. But the trend was toward the delegitimation of the Shah personally, a trend reinforced by each killing and by the failure of the government to satisfy earlier demands.

The government's responses oscillated. It arrested activists, banned gatherings, and used police and soldiers to disperse crowds. It also made concessions: relaxing some press restrictions, releasing certain prisoners, and replacing or reshuffling officials. In August the Shah replaced Prime Minister Amouzegar with Jafar Sharif-Emami, a figure with links to the religious and political establishment, in an effort to conciliate religious leaders and moderate critics. The government also sought, through intermediaries, to open dialogue with moderate opposition figures. These measures were often interpreted by opponents as signs of weakness, while the continuing use of force sustained the movement's sense of grievance. The Shah, uncertain, ill, and reportedly unwilling to order a large-scale massacre, struggled to choose a consistent course; historians continue to debate how far this indecision was personal, institutional, or a reflection of divided advice from civilian and military aides and foreign governments.

A critical distinction is between popular anger and organized strategy. Hundreds of thousands of Iranians who took part in demonstrations by autumn were motivated by diverse grievances: religious sentiment, economic hardship, family losses, outrage at repression, hostility to foreign influence, or belief in a more just political order. There was no single command structure directing the marches. Religious leaders, bazaar associations, student groups, and political organizations each played some role in calling, shaping, and marshaling them, but their coordination was partial and their aims differed. Khomeini's messages offered a strategic line, the refusal of compromise with the Shah, that many protesters adopted, but the line coexisted with other strategies, including National Front negotiation and leftist organization. The movement was thus both widely popular and imperfectly organized.

Students should observe how these features interacted. The government's failure to satisfy modest demands in 1977–early 1978 helped push protesters toward more radical goals. Meanwhile, the more radical the demands became, the narrower the room for compromise on both sides. A regime that regarded abdication as unacceptable and a movement that increasingly saw anything short of it as insufficient created a structural impasse. This did not make the monarchy's collapse inevitable, but it removed the middle ground that moderate reformers had hoped to occupy.

Participants' choices also changed as the perceived risks and opportunities shifted. A merchant closing a shop for a mourning procession could register a political position without joining a permanent organization; a student could distribute a leaflet without commanding a large following; a cleric could speak in religious language that listeners translated into different constitutional or revolutionary demands. These partial acts accumulated. Yet coordination was fragile: local leaders debated the timing of marches, residents disagreed over whether commerce should stop, and families weighed the danger of arrest or shooting. The appearance of a seamless national movement is therefore partly a product of viewing 1978 from the perspective of February 1979.

Historical Debate

Did the Forty-Day Mourning Cycle Drive the Revolution, or Did It Give Form to Broader Discontent?

The question: Many narratives present the sequence of forty-day commemorations as the engine that propelled the revolution. Others argue that the cycle was a convenient organizing form for a movement whose strength came from deeper social and political sources. The question concerns causation: what role did the commemorative calendar play relative to other factors?

Interpretation A — The cycle as engine: Each killing produced a mourning ceremony, which produced new protests and killings, creating a self-reinforcing sequence that expanded the movement geographically and socially. The religious calendar supplied legitimacy and coordination that secular opposition lacked.
Interpretation B — The cycle as organizational form: The cycle was effective because discontent was already widespread and because networks of clerics, bazaaris, and students could use mourning as a vehicle. The cycle did not explain why the government failed to contain protests, why workers later struck, or why the military fractured; those required additional causes.

Evidence: Both views rely on the pattern of dates and places and on testimony from organizers, but they weigh differently the evidence of protests that did not follow forty-day timing, the resources of local organizations, and the decisions of authorities.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and scale. It is less about whether commemorations mattered than about whether they were necessary and sufficient.

Why it matters: If the cycle was the engine, explanations of the revolution focus on religious networks and symbolism. If it was a form, explanations must give greater weight to state behavior, social grievances, and labor and political dynamics.

Section 91.8

The Cinema Rex Fire and the Crisis of Confidence in August 1978

On 19 August 1978 a fire broke out at the Cinema Rex in the oil city of Abadan during a film screening. The doors were reportedly blocked or locked, the building filled with fire and smoke, and a large number of people died. Reported death tolls vary: figures in the range of several hundred, with commonly cited numbers around four hundred and some estimates higher, appear in the sources. The disaster was among the deadliest incidents of the period and took place at a time when the movement's grievances against the state were already acute.

Public belief about responsibility formed rapidly. Opposition leaders and many ordinary Iranians attributed the fire to SAVAK or to the government, which they accused of seeking to discredit religious opponents or terrorize the population. The government, by contrast, blamed religious extremists. In the charged atmosphere of August, the first account was widely believed and became a powerful symbol of the regime's perceived criminality. Funerals and protests followed, and the fire contributed to the sense that the state had lost its moral standing.

Later evidence complicated the picture. After the revolution, investigations and trials established that the fire had been set by arsonists, and one of the principal perpetrators was convicted and executed. These findings are widely, though not universally, accepted, and they show that the early opposition narrative that the state had set the fire was not supported by later judicial and investigative findings. Questions persist, however, about the full organization behind the act, the participation of others, and the political context of the investigations conducted under the Islamic Republic, which had its own interests in the outcome. Historians therefore distinguish three things: what Iranians believed in August 1978, what later investigations concluded about the perpetrators, and what remains uncertain about wider responsibility.

The Cinema Rex case illustrates how rumor and belief can shape political outcomes. Whatever the truth about the arson, the immediate effect of the widespread belief in state responsibility was to increase anger and to harden opposition. This does not make the belief a deliberate fabrication; given the state's record of repression, many Iranians found official responsibility plausible. But it shows why historians cannot rely on contemporary conviction as evidence of fact, and why they must also avoid presuming that later investigations are free of political influence.

Politically, August was a turning point. Facing continued unrest, the Shah dismissed Prime Minister Amouzegar and, on 27 August, appointed Jafar Sharif-Emami, who headed what he called a government of national reconciliation. The new government moved quickly to conciliate religious and political critics: it abolished the imperial calendar and restored the Hijri solar calendar, closed gambling casinos, dissolved the Rastakhiz Party, relaxed press restrictions, and released some prisoners. These measures addressed some religious grievances and widened political freedom, but they did not satisfy the movement. For many opponents, concessions confirmed that pressure worked and that the government was vulnerable; for others, including supporters of the monarchy, they signalled dangerous weakness. At the same time, the government's tolerance of protests during Ramadan allowed large gatherings, including marches at the end of the month in early September.

By the end of August, Iran faced a crisis of confidence. The government had tried force and conciliation and had not restored order. The opposition had grown in numbers and in the range of its demands. Different groups in the establishment, including military leaders, were beginning to question the Shah's ability to manage the crisis. Events in early September, beginning with the large marches that accompanied the end of Ramadan and culminating in the declaration of martial law, would move the confrontation to a new level.

The later legal proceedings surrounding Cinema Rex are themselves part of the history of evidence. In 1980 a revolutionary tribunal tried Hossein Takbalizadeh, who acknowledged a role in the arson, together with other defendants. The court proceedings and subsequent investigations altered the plausible account of the immediate perpetrators, but they did not resolve every allegation concerning who encouraged them, what the various authorities knew, or why rescue failed so catastrophically. Historians should neither repeat the widespread 1978 accusation against SAVAK as an established finding nor infer that identifying an arsonist settles responsibility at every organizational level. The trial was conducted after the overthrow of the monarchy, when the new authorities had political reasons to shape the public narrative. The distinction among evidence for ignition, evidence for prior planning, and evidence for official conduct is essential. For relatives of the dead, uncertainty over responsibility was a continuing injury; for the wider movement, contemporary accusations had already influenced political decisions before any post-revolutionary inquiry could begin.

Abadan was not an incidental setting. Its refinery, migrant workforce, and experience of oil-led urban development made it a city in which anxieties about politics and everyday security were widely shared. The disaster therefore connected private grief with a national conflict at a moment when confidence in official explanations had already weakened.

Section 91.9

Black Friday: Martial Law and the Demonstrations of 8 September 1978

In early September the movement displayed its scale. Marches marking the end of Ramadan, around 4 September, drew very large crowds in Tehran and other cities, and a further demonstration took place on 7 September. Observers, including foreign diplomats and journalists, reported crowds in the hundreds of thousands in the capital, though the figures were estimates and varied with the source. The processions were largely orderly and were marked by religious slogans and, increasingly, by calls for the end of the monarchy. The government, which had permitted some gatherings as part of its conciliatory policy, now faced evidence that concessions had not diminished the movement.

Early on the morning of 8 September, the government announced martial law in Tehran and a number of other cities, to take effect that day, with a curfew and a ban on public gatherings. General Gholam Ali Oveisi was named military governor of Tehran. Many people who gathered in Jaleh Square and nearby areas of eastern Tehran that morning reportedly had not heard of the announcement, or did not credit it. Soldiers confronted the crowd. Accounts differ on how the confrontation developed, including whether warnings were given, whether some demonstrators or others fired first, and what orders were issued, but it is established that troops opened fire and that people were killed in Jaleh Square and in other parts of Tehran that day. Helicopters and tanks were also reported in the city. The day became known as Black Friday (Jom'eh-ye Siyah).

Casualty figures for the day are among the most contested of the revolution. Opposition leaders, including Khomeini, asserted that thousands had been killed, with some claims reaching the tens of thousands or higher; the government acknowledged a much smaller number. After the revolution, researchers using records of the dead, including those compiled by post-revolutionary institutions, concluded that the number killed on that day was far lower than the opposition claims, on the order of dozens in Jaleh Square and somewhat under or around a hundred in Tehran as a whole, depending on how the geographic and chronological boundaries are defined. These later studies have limits: they depend on records of those identified and registered as martyrs, and they may undercount. But the contrast between early claims in the thousands and later documented counts in the dozens is a central example of the need to treat casualty numbers as evidence to be evaluated. Whatever the exact number, the killing of unarmed or largely unarmed demonstrators by troops was widely regarded as a decisive moral and political event.

The political consequences were large. For many Iranians, Black Friday demonstrated that the Shah's government would use lethal force on a significant scale. The exaggerated early claims of mass slaughter were widely believed, and they intensified outrage even though they exceeded what later research supports. The killings undermined the position of moderates who argued for negotiation and strengthened those who held that compromise with the Shah was impossible. They also complicated the government's efforts at conciliation, because martial law and conciliation pulled in opposite directions. Foreign governments, including the United States, which publicly expressed support for the Shah, were left to assess whether his regime could stabilize. A telephone call from President Carter to the Shah after the shooting, widely reported, signalled continued American support, though some Iranian critics interpreted such support as complicity.

Black Friday should not be treated as the single cause of the revolution. The movement had been growing for months, and the killings were one of several events that raised the stakes. Nor did martial law immediately end protest. In the following weeks, demonstrations continued in various forms, and the movement shifted increasingly to strikes, which were harder to suppress by military force. The government's decision to impose martial law, while seeking to restore order, also tied the armed forces more directly to the political crisis, a development that would later matter for the military's own cohesion.

The difference between the death toll at Jaleh Square and that across Tehran on the same day is particularly important for historical method. Some later reconstructions, drawing on burial and other records, arrive at approximately sixty deaths at the square and a somewhat higher citywide figure. These are not measures of every casualty during the entire period of martial law, and the surviving records have limitations. Contemporary rumors of thousands dead nevertheless had a powerful political life even where they could not be sustained as demographic counts. Families, mourners, journalists, and opposition organizers acted upon the information they heard; what people believed in September is itself part of the revolution's history, but it must not be mistaken for a verified final tally.

Section 91.10

General Strikes, Public Employees, and the Disruption of Everyday Life

From September 1978 onward, strikes spread across Iran. Their growth transformed the character of the confrontation. Street demonstrations could be banned, dispersed, or contained by military force. Strikes, by contrast, removed the labor on which the state, the economy, and daily life depended. A government can arrest demonstrators; it is much harder to compel millions of employees to work, or to run a modern economy with its administrative and technical personnel absent.

The strikes drew in a wide array of workers. Employees of government ministries, banks, the postal and telecommunications services, customs, the national airline, and the state radio and television organization stopped work or reduced it in repeated waves from autumn. Teachers and some university staff participated, as did journalists and press workers, whose strikes interrupted the publication of newspapers in the capital for a period. Industrial workers in factories, including steel, automobile, and other manufacturing plants, struck over wages and conditions, while employees of the national oil company, discussed in the next section, struck on a scale that affected the national economy. Bazaar closures, which were not strikes by employees but collective decisions by merchants and artisans, further disrupted commerce.

The relationship between economic grievances and political demands was complex. Many strikes began with demands about pay, bonuses, working conditions, promotion, housing, or the dismissal of unpopular managers. Workers in the expanding public sector had experienced inflation and the pressures of the 1977 stabilization measures. The government, anxious to preserve order, often made concessions, including wage increases, but these did not end the strikes. As the political crisis deepened, workers added demands for the release of political prisoners, the end of martial law, and the removal of SAVAK, and later for the departure of the Shah. Some strikes were primarily political from the outset; others combined demands in ways that varied from workplace to workplace. Historians therefore warn against treating the strikes as either purely economic or merely a political tool of the religious movement. Strikers had their own organizations, grievances, and decisions, and they operated within a broader political climate in which participation in the movement carried moral weight.

The effects on administration and finance were significant. Government offices operated at reduced capacity, delays affected payments and services, banks experienced disruptions, and the state's ability to collect revenue and distribute funds was impaired. Transport and communications were affected, and shortages of fuel, notably kerosene used for heating and cooking, developed in the winter, forcing long queues. Military rule did not solve these problems; soldiers could be ordered to guard facilities or deliver fuel, but they could not substitute for the specialized skills of striking employees at scale. By late autumn the government faced the prospect that it could no longer deliver the services and revenue on which its authority rested.

The strikes also brought a distinctive relationship to the question of legitimacy. By refusing to work, employees withdrew practical cooperation from a regime they no longer regarded as legitimate. In many workplaces, strike committees or informal councils formed to organize, to decide when to return to work, and in some cases to distribute necessities or maintain essential functions. These forms of self-organization, whatever their limits, indicated that parts of society were developing their own institutions in parallel with the state, a development associated with the idea of dual power discussed below.

Strike coordination did not arise from a single central command. Teachers met within occupational networks; bank and ministry employees operated within different administrative hierarchies; some industrial workers depended on committee decisions at individual workplaces. Participation could be intermittent, and workers sometimes returned for essential tasks before resuming a stoppage. These distinctions mattered because a government could negotiate with one profession or dismiss a group of employees yet find that unrest had appeared in another branch of administration. A general strike was as much a cumulative political condition as a formally declared national event.

Work stoppages also affected the pace at which news moved. When newspapers ceased publication, people relied more heavily on leaflets, personal networks, foreign broadcasts, and rumor. Disruption of banks and transport placed pressures on families with savings or daily wages. These secondary consequences help explain why a strike could mobilize people who did not themselves belong to an opposition association.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Striking Worker, a Bazaar Household, and a Family Living under Martial Law

A striking worker. A technician in a state enterprise has watched prices rise faster than his pay since 1975 and has seen colleagues laid off after the 1977 spending cuts. When a strike begins at his workplace, the first demands concern wages and the dismissal of a hated manager. He weighs the loss of income against the pressure of colleagues and the risk of punishment under martial law. As weeks pass, strike meetings add demands about political prisoners, and he finds himself part of a collective decision whose political meaning exceeds his original grievance. Labor histories suggest that workers often experienced strikes as a mix of economic necessity, solidarity, and a moral sense of participating in a national cause.

A bazaar household. A cloth merchant's family has long contributed to a neighborhood mosque and relied on credit networks within the bazaar. The anti-profiteering campaign of 1975–1976 left them resentful. When the bazaar closes in protest, income stops, but merchants' associations and mosque funds help families through; the household's sons and daughters take part in marches. The family is not uniform in outlook: one relative is a government clerk worried about losing his post, another sympathizes with the National Front. Closing the shop is both a political act and a financial risk.

A family living under martial law. In a Tehran apartment, parents try to maintain routines as curfew, fuel shortages, interrupted schooling, and the sound of gunfire shape daily life. They queue for kerosene, worry about relatives in other cities, and decide whether their children should attend demonstrations. They listen to foreign radio broadcasts, including the Persian service of the BBC, and share rumors, some of them false, about events across the city. Fear of violence coexists with fear that the disorder will worsen.

Because most surviving sources record the voices of leaders, journalists, and organizers, historians reconstruct such everyday experience from oral histories, memoirs, labor studies, and contemporary reporting, and they should read these sources with attention to what they omit, including the experiences of those who did not participate in the movement.

Section 91.11

Oil Workers, Petroleum Strikes, and the Economic Power of Revolution

No strike mattered more than those in the petroleum industry. Oil provided the overwhelming majority of Iran's export earnings and a large share of government revenue; it fueled the economy and, in the form of refined products, heated and cooked for much of the population. An interruption of production and export therefore struck at the financial foundation of the state and at ordinary households' fuel supply. The oil industry's workforce, concentrated in Khuzestan and in refineries around the country, was among the best organized and most strategically placed in the country.

Research by the historian Peyman Jafari and others has shown that oil workers' participation drew on their own grievances and organizations. During the 1970s oil workers had experienced differences between permanent and contract employees, disputes over wages and benefits, the stratification of professional and manual workers, poor housing in some oil towns, and tensions with managers and with the security apparatus that monitored industrial labor. Clandestine and semi-clandestine networks, some with leftist and some with religious orientation, existed in the workplaces. When political turmoil spread in 1978, workers had networks, habits of discussion, and demands of their own. The strikes were not simply a reaction to instructions from outside; they were also a continuation of workplace conflicts in a new political context.

Strikes in the oil sector began in September, with refinery workers in Tehran and other sites taking action after Black Friday, and spread through October. Strike committees formed, and demands combined wage and benefit issues with calls for the release of prisoners, the end of martial law, and, increasingly, political change. By late October, strikes in the Khuzestan oilfields and at Abadan and other installations were reducing output. Production of crude oil, which had been several million barrels per day in the preceding months, fell sharply, and exports were seriously reduced and, for a period in December, largely halted. Figures vary by source and by definition, but the scale of the decline, from around five or six million barrels per day to a small fraction of that, is well attested in secondary accounts. The government reacted with a mixture of concessions and coercion: it increased pay, sent troops to oil installations after the November military government took office, arrested some strike leaders, and ordered a partial return to work. Some workers resumed under pressure, but strikes resumed or continued in intermittent form.

It is important to distinguish the motives and organization of oil workers from the directives of religious leaders. Khomeini and others called on workers to strike and to deprive the regime of oil revenue, and these appeals carried weight. But strike committees decided for themselves when to stop and start, how to maintain domestic fuel supply, and what to demand. Some workers who were not religious activists participated; some members of the workforce supported different political tendencies, and many acted for mixed reasons. The oil workers' strike thus shows a labor movement that was integrated into the revolutionary coalition without being reducible to it.

The effects on government finances and confidence were serious. Oil revenue constituted the state's main source of foreign exchange and a primary basis for budgeting. A collapse in production and export reduced the funds available to pay public employees, sustain imports, and finance the security forces. It also signalled to businesses, foreign governments, and ordinary people that the state could not reliably maintain even its most important industry. The strikes therefore combined economic, political, and symbolic effects. They demonstrated that the revolutionary movement could inflict serious damage on the state, and they convinced many observers, inside and outside Iran, that the Shah's government had lost control of a basic function.

Oil workers' demands illustrate the transition from a workplace dispute to a challenge over sovereignty. Early complaints encompassed housing allowances, technical pay, discrimination between staff grades, and conditions in difficult climates. As the movement widened, some strike committees called for an end to martial law, the release of prisoners, the abolition of SAVAK, and the departure of foreign employees. The demands were not all identical, and technical staff, production workers, and clerical employees did not invariably agree. Their capacity to slow exports rested on specialized knowledge of installations as well as on collective action. Scholars such as Peyman Jafari argue that the eventual connection between labor organization and revolutionary leadership was negotiated and contingent, not simply imposed from above.

In the final weeks of 1978, oil strikes also created the practical problem of supplying fuel to Iranian households without restoring the state's export income. As domestic shortages worsened, different groups discussed or organized limited production for internal use. By late December Khomeini and advisers were communicating with workers and technical specialists about how to restart essential deliveries while withholding oil exports; an Oil Strikes Coordinating Committee developed in this setting. Its emergence did not erase the local committees that had organized earlier stoppages. Rather, it shows that revolutionary authority was being negotiated around a critical economic system even before the monarchy fell. Production for domestic consumption, export restrictions, refinery operations, and distribution to cities involved different decisions and personnel. The episode exemplifies a transition from obstructing the existing government toward exercising selected administrative functions, while also demonstrating how vulnerable the urban population had become to interrupted fuel supplies.

Section 91.12

Government Responses: Concessions, Repression, and the Military Government

Between August and December 1978 the Shah and his governments attempted a series of strategies. None stabilized the situation, and the reasons illuminate why the monarchy ultimately failed.

The first was conciliation under Sharif-Emami, discussed above. The government sought to appeal to religious sentiment and to moderate critics through symbolic and legal changes, and it allowed a more open press and more space for demonstrations. Its difficulty was that the concessions arrived while the movement's demands were expanding and after the killings of the spring and summer. In the autumn the government also announced investigations into corruption and began to arrest some former officials, a measure intended to demonstrate accountability. The former prime minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda, who had served from 1965 to 1977, was arrested on 8 November 1978; former SAVAK director Nematollah Nassiri was detained during the preceding roundup. The arrests were intended to demonstrate accountability, but also signaled the erosion of the circle around the Shah.

The second strategy was the greater use of military authority. Martial law had been declared on 8 September, and in the following weeks the military assumed responsibility for order in several cities. As strikes spread, the Shah and his advisers weighed more forceful measures. On 4 and 5 November, anti-government riots in Tehran, including attacks on banks, cinemas, and the British embassy, burned parts of the city's commercial districts. Sharif-Emami resigned, and on 6 November the Shah appointed General Gholam Reza Azhari, the chief of the Supreme Commander's Staff, as prime minister at the head of a government that included military officers. The same day, the Shah delivered a nationally broadcast address in which he acknowledged the scale of the popular movement, admitted past errors, and promised to correct them and to establish a freer political order. The speech combined a military cabinet with a conciliatory promise and was widely read as an admission of crisis.

The Azhari government attempted to restore order by force, ordering troops into oil facilities, arresting strike leaders and political figures, and reinforcing press censorship. These measures had a limited and temporary effect. Some strikes were interrupted, but others persisted or resumed; demonstrations continued; and the military government struggled to enforce curfews and to maintain production. The government also faced the difficulty that soldiers could not replace skilled civilians, and that repeated use of troops against civilians risked straining discipline and morale within the armed forces. Reports of desertions, soldiers' sympathy for demonstrators, and incidents in which conscripts fraternized with crowds appeared with increasing frequency, though their extent remains debated and should not be exaggerated before the final weeks.

The reasons for the failure of these strategies can be analyzed under several headings. First, the strategies were not coordinated into a coherent whole; concession and repression were applied alternately, and each undermined the effect of the other. Second, the Shah's uncertainty and reportedly poor health, along with conflicting advice from civilian and military officials and foreign governments, made decisive action less likely. Third, the strikes could not be broken by the instruments available. Fourth, protesters' expectations were rising: each concession was seen as evidence of weakness, while each act of repression generated new anger. Fifth, the security forces' effectiveness depended on their cohesion, which was increasingly tested by the political crisis. These factors interacted, and none alone explains the outcome.

The detention of senior figures associated with the old government also showed the ambiguities of attempted conciliation. Former prime minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda was arrested on 8 November 1978, while former SAVAK chief Nematollah Nassiri was among the officials detained earlier. Such arrests were intended in part to signal accountability and break the public association between government and misconduct. Yet a change in the treatment of former officials did not establish independent courts or persuade protesters that authority had shifted away from the palace. On the contrary, the measures exposed the government's difficulty in separating the monarch from officials who had served him for years.

Historical Vocabulary
Martial Law, General Strike, and Dual Power

Definition: Martial law is the temporary imposition of military authority over civilian government and public order, typically with curfews and restrictions on assembly. A general strike is a work stoppage involving large numbers of workers across many industries and sectors, intended to exert political or economic pressure. Dual power describes a situation in which a rival set of institutions claims or exercises authority alongside the existing state.

Historical context: The Iranian government declared martial law on 8 September 1978 and later formed a military cabinet. Strikes spread across public administration, industry, and oil, and strike committees and other local bodies assumed organizing functions. The idea of dual power, drawn from revolutionary theory, has been applied by some scholars to the period when the opposition's institutions and leadership began to rival those of the state.

Why it matters: These terms identify the mechanisms by which the state's capacity to rule was undermined. Martial law shows the state turning to coercion, a general strike shows the movement turning to economic non-cooperation, and dual power points to the emergence of an alternative authority.

Potential misunderstanding: "Dual power" is an analytical concept, not a term used by all participants, and scholars disagree about how fully it describes Iran in 1978–1979. A general strike does not mean that every worker stopped working at all times.

Cause and Effect

How Protest, Repression, and Strikes Undermined the Monarchy's Capacity to Govern

Long-term causes

Concentrated personal authority, limited political participation, economic strains of the oil-boom economy, and weakened autonomous institutions that could mediate between state and society.

Short-term causes

The partial opening of 1977, the Qom killings, the forty-day commemorative cycle, the misjudged concessions and coercion of 1978, and the spread of strikes after Black Friday.

Immediate trigger and context

Martial law of 8 September, the oil strikes of October, the November riots, and the formation of a military government.

Transformation

The state moved from facing protest to losing its ability to administer, collect revenue, produce oil, and rely on every part of its coercive apparatus.

Immediate effects

Fuel shortages, interrupted administration, falling oil income, rising political confidence among opponents, and doubts among elites and foreign governments.

Long-term consequences

A situation in which neither concession nor repression restored authority, preparing the ground for Muharram mobilization, the Shah's departure, and military fragmentation.

Historical complexity: The monarchy's loss of capacity cannot be reduced to any single factor. It resulted from the interaction of mass mobilization, economic non-cooperation, political misjudgment, and institutional strain.

Section 91.13

Khomeini in France and the Internationalization of Revolutionary Leadership

In the autumn of 1978 Khomeini's location changed in a way that magnified his influence. Iraq's government, which had hosted him since 1965, had been under pressure from Iran to limit his activities, and relations between the two states, which had agreed on a border settlement in 1975, favored restraint. In early October 1978 Khomeini left Iraq. He reportedly sought to enter Kuwait, was refused, and then traveled to France, arriving in Paris on 6 October. He soon settled in a house in the village of Neauphle-le-Château, west of Paris, provided by Iranian exiles. The French government, which had its own interests in not antagonizing either the Shah or the growing movement, allowed his stay while maintaining that he should not engage in activities that would harm relations with Iran, a condition that proved difficult to enforce.

The move to France had effects that neither the Shah's government nor Khomeini's own circle could fully have predicted. In Najaf Khomeini had been accessible mainly to Iranian pilgrims, students, and couriers. In France he was accessible to international journalists, television crews, and visitors from around the world. A small circle of Iranian advisers and activists, including Ebrahim Yazdi, Abolhassan Banisadr, and Sadeq Qotbzadeh, who had lived in Western countries and understood media and political organization, helped to arrange interviews, translate statements, and manage contacts. Telephone links allowed Khomeini to communicate quickly with supporters in Iran; messages and recordings were then copied and distributed through religious networks. International radio services, including the Persian broadcasts of the BBC, relayed his statements and reports of the movement to listeners inside Iran, who often trusted them more than state-controlled media.

Exile therefore expanded rather than diminished his visibility. Because the Iranian state controlled domestic broadcasting and the press, communication technologies that crossed borders, such as telephones, cassette tapes, and foreign radio, became tools of opposition. The Shah's government could not easily silence a leader outside its territory, and attempts to discredit him through the domestic press had backfired as the January episode had shown. At the same time, the image Khomeini presented in interviews was calibrated for different audiences. To foreign journalists and to Iranian secular liberals, he spoke of freedom, independence, and the people's right to determine their government; to religious followers, he spoke the language of Islamic justice and resistance to tyranny.

Interpreting Khomeini's public statements in late 1978 is among the most contested tasks in the historiography of the revolution. He insisted that the Shah must go and that no compromise with the monarchy was acceptable. He spoke of an Islamic government and of an Islamic republic as the desired outcome, but his statements about the specific institutions of that government, the role of clerics in it, and the place of different political and religious groups were general and capable of varied interpretations. He said, in general terms, that clerics would not themselves hold governmental office and would serve as guides, a position that some observers and allies took as a commitment and that others, then and later, regarded as inconsistent with his earlier writings on clerical governance and with what happened after February 1979. Historians should therefore distinguish three things: what Khomeini said publicly in 1978, how contemporaries understood those statements, and what institutions were actually established in 1979–1980, the subject of Chapters 92 and 93.

Khomeini also dealt with secular opposition figures. In early November, National Front leader Karim Sanjabi traveled to Paris, met with him, and agreed to a joint declaration that rejected the legitimacy of the monarchy and called for a government based on Islamic principles and democratic rights. The statement caused controversy within the National Front and contributed to its fragmentation, a theme taken up in the next section. Such agreements illustrate how exile leadership could serve as a point of convergence for groups with different aims, but they also masked disagreements that had not been resolved.

The communications environment was more complex than the familiar image of a sermon passed from one cassette recorder to another. Recordings were important, but so were telephone calls, typed announcements, printed leaflets, newspapers, visiting intermediaries, and personal contacts between France, Qom, Tehran, and provincial cities. A message could travel through different media before reaching listeners: instructions spoken in France might be conveyed by telephone, transcribed or recorded locally, reproduced, and then read in mosques or neighborhoods. This distributed process extended the movement's reach, but it also meant that transmission depended on people who selected, translated, and sometimes interpreted the material. Communications therefore aided coordination without creating a perfectly centralized command chain.

Section 91.14

Religious and Secular Coalitions: Leadership, Ideology, and Political Uncertainty

By late 1978 the main components of the opposition had come to share a common immediate objective, the end of the Shah's rule. How this convergence occurred is central to the revolution's history, and so is what it concealed. The coalition was not a formal alliance with an agreed program. It was a convergence of organizations, networks, and individuals responding to the same events, and its members often understood the movement in different terms.

Khomeini represented the most uncompromising position: refusal of any negotiation with the Shah and a call for the monarchy's replacement by an Islamic government. His message resonated with a broad religious public and with many who, regardless of their views on clerical government, saw intransigence as the only response to the regime's violence. Mehdi Bazargan and the Freedom Movement sought a government combining Islamic and democratic principles and tended to see Khomeini as the movement's leader while hoping to guide the transition. Karim Sanjabi and many National Front members moved from reform of the monarchy to its rejection, a shift culminating in the Paris declaration. Dariush Forouhar and others in the nationalist current pursued their own political agendas. Shapour Bakhtiar, by contrast, continued to believe that a constitutional monarchy with a restored parliamentary system could be preserved, a view that would lead him to accept the premiership in December and to be expelled from the National Front.

Within the clergy, Ayatollah Mahmud Taleghani acted as a conciliator among religious and political tendencies and was admired by some leftists, while Ayatollah Kazem Shariatmadari emphasized the 1906 constitution and expressed reservations about clerical rule and about certain forms of revolutionary violence. Neither of them was a mere appendage of Khomeini, and their positions illustrate that the religious current was plural. Younger clerics and lay Islamists, some of them associated with the ideas of the intellectual Ali Shariati, who had died in 1977, and with the Mojahedin organization, held views that differed from those of the traditional clerical establishment and from one another.

On the left, organizations including the Fedayeen-e Khalq and Mojahedin-e Khalq, which had waged armed struggle in the 1970s, and various Marxist and student groups sought to expand their influence in universities, factories, and urban neighborhoods. Their ideological commitments, socialist revolution for some, a militant revolutionary Islam for others, differed from those of both liberal nationalists and the conservative clergy. They joined demonstrations, contributed organizers to strikes and student movements, and generally accepted that the movement was led by Khomeini in the short run while anticipating a later struggle over the revolution's direction. The pro-Soviet Tudeh Party, long banned and operating largely in exile, played a smaller organizational role in this period than its historical prestige might suggest.

Women participated in the movement in large numbers and in varied roles. They took part in marches, often in groups, and many wore the chador as a sign of religious identification and, for some, of solidarity and protection. They organized in mosques and neighborhood networks, supported strikers, cared for the wounded, and served as couriers and organizers. But the meaning of their participation differed. Some women supported an explicitly Islamic political order and saw in the movement a defense of religious identity against state-promoted Westernization. Others, including professionals, students, and leftist and liberal activists, sought democratic participation, an end to repression, labor and women's rights, or national independence, and some wore the chador in demonstrations without endorsing a future compulsory dress code. Women's expectations of the revolution were therefore not uniform. After February 1979, the divergence became visible, a subject taken up in later chapters.

The central historical problem is how such a diverse coalition could act together without resolving the question of what would follow. Several factors made this possible. The shared enemy and the shared experience of repression provided strong unity. Khomeini's role as a symbol of uncompromising opposition allowed groups with different aims to rally behind a leader without agreeing on a program, and his general statements about freedom and independence allowed them to project their hopes onto the movement. Most participants gave priority to ending the monarchy and postponed constitutional questions. Some leaders believed that they would be able to shape the new order through their organizational skills or their popular support. And the speed of events in the autumn left little time for debate on programs. The result was a coalition with a clear negative aim and an undefined positive one. After 11 February, that ambiguity became the central political problem.

Women were visible in this coalition as demonstrators, organizers of neighborhood aid, students, professionals, and participants in religious and secular associations. Their clothing, political affiliations, and expectations varied. For some, public expression of Islamic solidarity was inseparable from political opposition; for others, earlier gains in education and employment strengthened demands for civic equality and constitutional rights. The convergence of these women in marches against the Shah cannot by itself establish agreement about future family law or the role of religious authority. Recognizing that uncertainty is necessary to understand both the strength and the unresolved disagreements of the coalition.

Section 91.15

Muharram, Mass Demonstrations, and the Revolutionary Movement in December 1978

The month of Muharram, which began in early December 1978, is the first month of the Islamic calendar and holds special significance for Shiʿi Muslims, who commemorate the martyrdom of Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet, at Karbala in 680. The first ten days culminate in the observances of Tasu'a (the ninth) and Ashura (the tenth). Ritual processions, mourning gatherings, and sermons recounting the story of Husayn's stand against an unjust ruler are central to Shiʿi religious life. In the Iranian context of 1978, this symbolism of sacrifice, injustice, and resistance to tyranny lent itself readily to political interpretation, though it is important to note that Muharram observances have a long history independent of any political movement and that interpretations of Husayn's example varied.

The government, concerned about the potential for confrontation, had imposed restrictions on processions, and the military government faced a dilemma: to ban the most important religious observances of the year would risk further confrontation, while to allow them risked providing an occasion for the largest demonstrations yet. Negotiations took place among military authorities, religious leaders, and National Front figures, and the outcome was that marches were permitted in some form with agreed routes and with the organizers committing to maintain order. On 10 and 11 December, the days of Tasu'a and Ashura, enormous demonstrations took place in Tehran and in cities across the country, including Tabriz, Mashhad, Isfahan, Shiraz, Qom, and many smaller towns.

In Tehran, marchers moved through the city toward the Shahyad (later Azadi) Square. Organization was notable: marshals, often drawn from mosque associations, student groups, and bazaar associations, regulated the flow, and religious figures delivered speeches. Entire families, including women and children, joined. The marches were, on the whole, peaceful and disciplined. A resolution was presented to the crowd and received acclamation that, in general terms, rejected the Shah's rule, called for the end of the monarchy, and endorsed Khomeini's leadership and the establishment of an Islamic government. The content was widely reported, and it was a clear indication of how far the movement's demands had advanced since the summer. Students should note that the resolution expressed the consensus of the organizers and the assembled crowd as presented, which does not necessarily mean that every participant endorsed each item.

Estimates of the size of the Tehran demonstrations varied widely, from several hundred thousand to over a million or more, with opposition leaders claiming larger numbers, and some claims reaching two million. No reliable counts exist. Crowd estimates in revolutionary conditions are shaped by political aims, by the difficulty of counting moving crowds, and by the lack of independent measurement. This chapter therefore describes the demonstrations as among the largest in modern Iranian history, with a range of estimates, rather than adopting any single figure.

The political importance of these marches was considerable. They demonstrated that the movement could mobilize very large numbers across the country in a disciplined manner, and that it had a network capable of organizing marches, security, and communication despite military rule. They also displayed the unity of different elements, with religious and secular participants and different generations and classes present, under common slogans. For the Shah and his advisers, as well as for foreign observers, the marches reduced the plausibility of a political solution in which the Shah remained in effective control. Even if some reformist politicians still hoped to preserve a constitutional monarchy, the scale and content of the demonstrations indicated that any government appointed by the Shah would lack popular legitimacy. The marches did not by themselves topple the regime, which retained armed forces and institutions, but they transformed the political calculation of both opponents and supporters.

Section 91.16

The United States, Foreign Governments, and the Search for a Political Settlement

The Iranian crisis unfolded in an international context in which several governments had major interests. The United States regarded Iran as a key ally in the Persian Gulf, a counterweight to Soviet influence, a major purchaser of American military equipment, and a leading oil supplier. The administration of President Jimmy Carter, which came into office with a stated emphasis on human rights, nonetheless maintained close relations with the Shah, who had visited Washington in November 1977, and publicly supported him. Britain, the Soviet Union, France, West Germany, and regional states each also followed events closely.

American assessments in 1978 were neither uniform nor clairvoyant. In the early months, many officials considered the Shah's position secure, and intelligence assessments in the summer were famously cautious about whether Iran was in a pre-revolutionary condition. Diplomats at the embassy in Tehran, military officers and advisers, intelligence analysts, and senior policymakers in Washington held different views and at times reached different conclusions. National Security Adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski generally favored firm support for the Shah and the use of military authority; elements of the State Department and some in the embassy were more inclined to press for political accommodation with moderate opposition leaders; President Carter himself wavered between expressions of support and reluctance to endorse repression. The administration was also constrained by the difficulty of obtaining reliable information about a society in which it had limited contact with opposition groups, particularly the religious networks.

On 9 November 1978, Ambassador William Sullivan sent a confidential cable to Washington titled "Thinking the Unthinkable." Its significance lay in suggesting that the United States should begin to consider the possibility that the Shah might not remain in power, and that Washington should consider alternative political arrangements and contingency planning. The cable did not predict the precise course of events, and it was one input among others. But it marked a shift in the embassy's reporting and became a reference point in later debates about American policy. In subsequent weeks, the administration sent additional emissaries and advisers, and in December former Under Secretary of State George Ball was asked to review policy; his assessment, which suggested that the Shah's prospects were poor and that the United States should support a transition, was not fully adopted. In early January 1979, leaders of the United States, Britain, France, and West Germany met on the Caribbean island of Guadeloupe, where the crisis in Iran was among the topics discussed. Also in January, the American general Robert Huyser traveled to Tehran with the purpose of encouraging Iranian military leaders to remain cohesive and to prevent a coup or disintegration; scholars and participants differ in their assessments of what he did and with what effect.

The relationship between these actions and the revolution's outcome has been interpreted in contradictory ways. Some Iranian and foreign commentators, including royalists who felt abandoned and some revolutionaries who attributed events to foreign manipulation, have argued that the United States or Britain either engineered the Shah's fall or deliberately abandoned him. The documentary record does not support a thesis that foreign governments controlled the revolution. It shows instead uncertainty, disagreement, and shifting assessment within governments, together with efforts to support the Shah, to persuade him to adopt a particular policy, to establish contacts with opposition figures, and to prepare for different scenarios. At the same time, it would be inaccurate to describe foreign powers as irrelevant: their advice, arms, expressions of support or doubt, and decisions about contact with the opposition affected the calculations of Iranian actors. The most defensible conclusion is that foreign policies mattered as one set of influences within a process driven principally by Iranian social and political forces.

Foreign reporting must also be read as evidence of uncertainty rather than merely as a catalogue of mistakes. Diplomats spoke with palace officials, opposition contacts, military officers, and other intermediaries, but access was uneven and informants pursued their own interests. A confident intelligence forecast could be undermined in weeks as strikes or funerals changed political expectations. Conversely, contingency planning for the Shah's departure did not mean a government had adopted a program to remove him. This distinction is particularly useful when studying Sullivan's November cable: he proposed examining an outcome previously regarded as implausible and warned explicitly that the scenario was not a prediction.

Section 91.17

Shapour Bakhtiar, the Last Royal Government, and the Shah's Departure

By the end of December 1978, the monarchy was searching for a government that could negotiate with its opponents without openly surrendering royal authority. General Gholam Reza Azhari's military cabinet had failed to restart ordinary administration or halt the strikes. Public order still depended on soldiers, but the scale of demonstrations made a lasting military solution appear increasingly hazardous. The Shah's advisers explored several civilian possibilities before turning to Shapour Bakhtiar, a veteran of the National Front and a defender of the constitutional tradition of 1906. Bakhtiar's decision to accept the premiership was historically decisive because he proposed an alternative neither to the monarchy itself nor to the revolution's demand for complete replacement: he would attempt to make the Shah a constitutional monarch whose government answered to parliament and law.

Bakhtiar was not merely a convenient palace official. He had participated in Mossadegh-era nationalism, opposed arbitrary royal rule, and endured periods of political marginalization. These credentials gave him a language that a serving general or court minister could not plausibly use. He argued that the country needed civil rights, independent institutions, constitutional procedures, and an end to the dictatorship, but that replacing monarchy by an undefined revolutionary regime risked trading one arbitrary authority for another. His position exposed a fundamental choice that earlier sections of this chapter have traced: in 1977 the National Front had appealed to the Shah to respect the constitution; by January 1979 much of the movement no longer considered that an acceptable response. Bakhtiar accepted a constitutional remedy at the moment when its possible social constituency was shrinking.

His former colleagues were unwilling to treat the new cabinet as the achievement of their campaign. Karim Sanjabi and other National Front leaders repudiated him, and his political isolation deepened when Khomeini rejected any cabinet installed by the Shah. Bakhtiar maintained that a constitutional government, not a revolutionary leader's proclamation, was the legal route out of the crisis. But the monarchy's continuing formal authority over his appointment weakened his claim to independence: protesters could point to the source of his office as proof that the old system survived. The same paradox afflicted his proposed reforms. Lifting restrictions, releasing prisoners, promising the abolition of SAVAK, and ending censorship were significant departures from previous policy, but their implementation required administrators, police, legislators, and military commanders whose loyalties and capacities were uncertain.

In early January Bakhtiar assembled his cabinet and sought a vote of parliamentary confidence. The Shah agreed to leave Iran, while a Regency Council was established on 13 January to provide a constitutional mechanism through which royal authority might formally continue during his absence. The Council's creation was legally consequential: it represented an attempt to separate the existence of the monarchy from the physical presence of Mohammad Reza Shah. Yet an institution can be correctly constituted under an existing legal order and still lack broad acceptance. Opponents rejected the arrangement as an extension of royal rule, and senior figures associated with it faced pressure to resign or recognize revolutionary authority. The council had little practical capacity to command streets, workplaces, or mass political loyalty.

On 16 January 1979, the Shah and Empress Farah left Iran. The departure was a televised public event. Crowds celebrated in Tehran and other cities, while supporters of the monarchy and sections of the armed forces experienced it as a profound rupture and feared what might follow. The Shah presented the trip as a temporary absence for rest and did not formally abdicate. Students must therefore distinguish three dates: 16 January, when the ruler departed; 1 February, when Khomeini returned; and 11 February, when the last government resting on royal authority collapsed. To call the first event the formal abolition of the monarchy would obscure the interval in which Bakhtiar's cabinet, the Regency Council, the military high command, and the revolutionary leadership made competing claims.

The departure also transformed the psychology of the crisis. The monarchy had revolved around the Shah's person for decades, making his physical absence unusually consequential. Some officials anticipated that a constitutional cabinet might regain confidence once the Shah was gone; others feared that no civilian government could replace the personal authority on which military and bureaucratic discipline had depended. Strike committees and neighborhood organizers often interpreted the departure as an admission of defeat, but continued work stoppages and mobilization because their central demand—an end to the royal order—had not yet been achieved. The government possessed ministries, laws, a budget, and armed forces, while the revolution possessed the capacity to mobilize crowds, sustain strikes, and claim legitimacy through religious, political, and occupational networks. These resources were different, and neither side's final victory was settled on 16 January.

Foreign relations increased the uncertainty. United States officials had supported the Shah while also considering how to preserve the Iranian military and protect American interests if he departed. General Robert Huyser was in Tehran to consult senior military commanders and encourage cohesion; his presence has subsequently generated conflicting accounts of American purposes and influence. The surviving documentary record does not reduce Bakhtiar to a foreign appointee or establish a foreign plan directing the revolution. His immediate challenge was domestic: reconciling formal constitutional authority with a population in which large organized constituencies no longer recognized the monarchy as legitimate. He retained personal courage and a coherent reformist argument, but the late timing, rejection by former nationalist allies, and demands of the revolutionary movement left him with little effective political base. The struggle over who could govern the country entered a new phase.

Section 91.18

Khomeini's Return and the Emergence of Competing Claims to Sovereignty

Khomeini's return on 1 February 1979 brought a leader who had directed much of the revolution from exile into the center of a still-unresolved crisis. The flight from France landed at Tehran's Mehrabad airport after questions about its safety, arrangements for the journey, and the government's possible response. Vast crowds lined the routes used by his entourage; contemporaneous estimates differ so widely that a single precise count would convey false certainty. The symbolism, however, was unmistakable. A cleric expelled by the monarchy in 1964 had returned openly to a capital in which the Shah was absent and the last royal prime minister was trying to demonstrate that constitutional government could survive without royal dictatorship. The public reception indicated a remarkable capacity to mobilize support but did not constitute a nationwide referendum on every element of a future constitution.

Khomeini visited the Behesht-e Zahra cemetery, where many families associated the revolution with relatives killed during the protests. The venue placed remembrance, martyrdom, and political authority in a single public setting. In his address he rejected the legitimacy of the government appointed under the monarchy and asserted that a government deriving authority from the revolutionary movement should replace it. This was a direct challenge to Bakhtiar's contention that legal continuity through the old constitution furnished the only legitimate basis for government. Students should separate the two rival conceptions: Bakhtiar drew on the existing constitutional order and its procedures; Khomeini claimed that the sovereign people, represented by the revolutionary movement, had withdrawn legitimacy from that order and empowered new leadership. The distinction concerned not simply individuals but where lawful authority was believed to originate.

The crowds were diverse. Religious associations, bazaar organizations, students, workers, professionals, members of leftist groups, and people who had never belonged to a political association all attended or participated in the wider celebrations. For some Khomeini represented religious authenticity and moral opposition to dictatorship; others valued his uncompromising demand that the Shah depart; still others expected constitutional liberty, social redistribution, or national independence. Some opponents of the monarchy remained skeptical of his political program and of the possibility that religious leadership might restrict future pluralism. The same crowd could express an unambiguous demand against monarchy while concealing different answers to the question of what came next. The historian must distinguish an individual's presence at a demonstration from an endorsement of a detailed institutional plan.

Within the revolutionary movement, senior figures had already been considering how to move from opposition to an administration. A Revolutionary Council was organized during January 1979; its composition and internal deliberations remained relatively obscure to most people at the time. Mehdi Bazargan, an engineer, veteran nationalist, and leader of the Freedom Movement, was selected to head a provisional government. Khomeini's appointment was publicly announced on 5 February, although the decision was prepared earlier. Bazargan's reputation for integrity and constitutional reform suggested to some observers that the new administration might accommodate a range of Islamic and nationalist positions. It did not, by itself, resolve the distribution of power among the council, Bazargan's proposed ministers, clerics, protest committees, workers' organizations, and armed supporters of the revolution.

Bakhtiar refused to concede that a rival government could be legally established while his own cabinet held the country's ministries and claimed the confidence of parliament. The confrontation therefore created an emerging situation of competing sovereignty, often described analytically as dual power. But the phrase should not imply that two equally organized states already existed. Bakhtiar controlled formal offices and could issue orders within the existing administrative system, whereas the revolutionary leadership relied on mass mobilization, moral authority, informal committees, and the increasingly uncertain obedience of civil servants and soldiers. Some ministries continued working, others were disrupted, and local officials faced choices about whose instructions to follow. Ordinary residents encountered overlapping claims in practical matters: whether to respect a curfew, whether schools or factories should open, and whom police or soldiers would obey when crowds entered the streets.

Nor were the revolutionary side's institutions uniform. Clerical intermediaries, the emerging Revolutionary Council, neighborhood committees, political parties, and strike committees shared anti-monarchical objectives but not always decision-making procedures. Bazargan's preference for regular ministries and predictable legal administration differed from the habits of local groups whose authority came from participation in the struggle. The question of who could control weapons or arrest former officials was especially urgent, although its full consequences belong to Chapter 92. Different social constituencies also looked forward to different policies on women's rights, employment, property, the press, and religious law. The battle for legitimate authority preceded the creation of a workable system for exercising it.

The early-February standoff illustrates why revolutionary victory was not simply an announcement made by a charismatic leader. Khomeini could issue a command and millions might heed it, but the monarchy's remaining military and administrative structures did not thereby disappear. Bakhtiar could invoke the constitution, but declarations did not guarantee loyalty from soldiers, clerks, or the public. Each side needed institutions capable of translating claims into action. The next turning point came when the armed forces, long the ultimate enforcement mechanism of the monarchy, became divided not only about political preference but about orders and weapons. By the second week of February, the contest over legitimacy was moving into an immediate test of force.

Section 91.19

The Military Crisis and the Collapse of the Monarchy, 9–11 February 1979

The armed forces were central to the Shah's state, but they were not politically homogeneous. Senior commanders had gained rank within a royal system that made loyalty to the monarch a professional requirement. Their subordinates included conscripts, young officers, skilled technicians, and air-force personnel whose social experiences and political views could differ substantially. During the months of demonstrations the military had been used to enforce martial law while facing the visible disapproval of large crowds. Desertions, refusals to fire, rumors of disaffection, and negotiations over how to use force eroded cohesion. Yet the army had not collectively defected when the Shah departed, and Bakhtiar continued to rely on at least the possibility of military obedience. Explaining the collapse therefore requires attention to the sequence in which particular units lost discipline and how their actions affected wider command decisions.

On 8 February, groups of air-force technicians and other personnel commonly known as homafaran publicly demonstrated support for Khomeini. Their act was important because they belonged to a technologically advanced branch of the armed forces closely associated with royal modernization and American-supplied equipment. The ceremony did not mean the whole air force had chosen the revolution, but it exposed a visible break between elements of the service and the government's senior command. Images and reports of the event circulated rapidly. Officials who still believed the armed forces would act as a unified bulwark confronted a problem of practical organization: how could commanders rely on personnel whose loyalty had become a public question?

Fighting broke out around the Doshan Tappeh air base in eastern Tehran on the evening of 9 February, involving air-force personnel and loyalist troops, including elements of the Imperial Guard. Accounts differ on the immediate provocation and the identity of everyone who joined the clashes. The conflict quickly became more than an internal military dispute as civilians gathered near the base and, in some cases, fought alongside rebellious personnel. By 10 February armed confrontations had spread. Members and sympathizers of leftist guerrilla organizations, including the Fedai and Mojahedin, were among those who helped seize or distribute weapons, while neighborhood participants and other armed civilians acted through less formal networks. Their participation makes it inaccurate to describe the fighting as the work of a single religious organization, even though Khomeini's leadership supplied the wider movement's most prominent political symbol.

Weapons transformed the situation. A state can withstand demonstrations so long as it retains an effective monopoly on organized force; a state whose depots, police stations, and barracks are contested must confront a different problem. Crowds and armed groups began taking positions around military and police installations. Some soldiers returned to barracks; others surrendered weapons, abandoned posts, or joined the opposition. Several senior officers continued to favor resistance, but the chain of command was increasingly unreliable. Reports from individual streets, garrisons, and military units varied, and contemporary journalists often could not see beyond immediate confrontations. Historians should therefore resist describing a perfectly coordinated nationwide uprising when much of what occurred was improvised amid fear, excitement, and uncertainty.

Bakhtiar's government tried to impose curfew restrictions as fighting spread. Khomeini urged supporters not to comply with the orders, and revolutionary organizers mobilized residents in defiance. These competing instructions tested which authority people believed would endure. For a household near a base, remaining indoors might mean physical safety, but for a young activist defying curfew could represent participation in the final defeat of the monarchy. Soldiers confronted choices with serious personal consequences, including whether to obey commanders, disarm, protect civilians, or risk a deadly confrontation with neighbors. The experience of the last weekend of the monarchy was thus shaped by small decisions within institutions that the Shah had built to be disciplined and centralized.

On 11 February 1979, the Supreme Military Council declared that the armed forces would remain neutral in the political conflict and called for troops to return to their bases. General Abbas Qarabaghi, the chief of staff, was associated with the decision. Neutrality was not a declaration by every unit that it had embraced Khomeini's politics, and it did not end every armed clash instantly. Its decisive consequence was institutional: the last royal cabinet could no longer count on coordinated military support to enforce its authority. As armories, police stations, military buildings, and broadcasting facilities passed into revolutionary hands, Bakhtiar's government ceased to function. The collapse was reported through radio and other public announcements as well as through events in the streets. The date became known in the Iranian solar calendar as 22 Bahman 1357.

It is useful to distinguish three related outcomes. First, the dynasty's governing authority ended; the Shah remained abroad and never returned to exercise it. Second, the revolutionary movement gained control of many of the institutions and weapons needed to govern, but control was initially dispersed among groups rather than consolidated in a single administrative chain. Third, the formal problem of political legitimacy was not yet settled by constitutional procedures: different people supported the overthrow for different reasons, and the task of creating an agreed political order had barely begun. To say that the monarchy fell on 11 February is therefore precise; to say that a completed, uncontested new state took its place that afternoon would be misleading. Chapter 92 will examine that distinction in detail.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Constitutional Monarchy to Revolutionary Sovereignty, 1906–1979

1905–1906: The Constitutional Revolution demanded limits on arbitrary royal power and established the Majles as an institution that could claim lawful representative authority. A constitution was written, but its meaning and enforcement remained contested.

1925–1941: Reza Shah enlarged central state capacity, disciplined military forces, and strengthened the bureaucratic authority of the crown, while diminishing the effective independence of parliament and political associations.

1941–1953: A comparatively open political arena allowed competing parliamentary and nationalist projects, culminating in the confrontation over oil and the coup of August 1953.

1953–1977: Mohammad Reza Shah consolidated personal authority while encouraging rapid social and economic modernization. The formal constitution survived, but effective accountability and opposition organization were sharply limited.

1977–11 February 1979: Constitutional petitions gave way to mass demonstrations, strikes, and competing assertions of sovereignty. The armed forces' neutrality ended the last royal cabinet's capacity to rule without establishing agreement about its replacement.

Continuity: Across these changes, Iranian political actors repeatedly appealed to the nation, law, religious authority, and public participation to justify or challenge power. Change: The central question moved from limiting royal authority within a constitutional monarchy to identifying a new foundation for government after monarchy.

Section 91.20

Historical Synthesis: Why the Iranian Revolution Succeeded

The Iranian Revolution succeeded because several processes that had developed separately converged at a particular historical moment. It was neither the automatic outcome of oil-led modernization nor simply a religious uprising, nor was it brought about by a foreign decision to withdraw support from the Shah. Economic discontent, blocked political participation, organized religious and secular networks, labor strikes, changing expectations, shifting state responses, and military fragmentation all mattered. None, considered alone, explains why the government fell on 11 February 1979 rather than surviving with reforms or confronting an earlier breakdown. The revolution must be studied as a sequence in which the actions of participants changed what other participants believed possible.

One long-term condition was the concentration of governmental power around the Shah. From the post-1953 reconstruction of royal authority to the one-party system of 1975, the monarchy had reduced the independence of the press, parliament, parties, and professional associations. This made organized opposition difficult and gave the state short-term advantages in monitoring and containing political challenges. But it also eliminated institutions through which criticism might be expressed, decisions corrected, and political responsibility transferred without threatening the entire system. By 1978 complaints about a ministry or a pricing campaign frequently became complaints about the Shah personally. A constitutional cabinet proposed late in the crisis therefore carried the burden of defending a monarch who had long insisted on directing governmental choices himself.

Economic conditions heightened that vulnerability without determining the outcome. Iran's 1960s and 1970s development brought real gains in industrial production, schools, urban employment, and household consumption. Those achievements were not erased by inflation or inequality. Yet the oil windfall after 1973 also encouraged ambitious expenditure schedules, congested ports, speculative housing markets, and policies that angered merchants. Stabilization and reduced construction spending in 1977 affected some wage earners and migrants whose expectations had risen during the boom. The causal point is not that prosperity must produce revolt or that poverty automatically produces revolution: rapidly changing opportunity, perceived unfairness, and insecure expectations can alter political judgments, especially when people cannot influence policy through representative institutions.

The organization of opposition was equally consequential. Mosques, seminaries, religious commemorations, neighborhood associations, bazaar networks, universities, professional groups, and workplaces supplied ways for people to gather, circulate information, fund action, and maintain solidarity. These institutions had different capacities. A mosque could host a commemoration, a bazaar association could close commercial activity, a university could distribute protest literature, and an oil facility could interrupt exports. As protest spread, no central organization needed to command every participant for the government's problems to multiply. Public gatherings made opposition visible, and visibility altered calculations about risk: a merchant or student who previously expected isolation could now see that others were acting too. This process depended on local initiative and changing expectations as much as on national leadership.

Religious symbolism provided a broadly recognizable language in which suffering and resistance could be interpreted. Qom, Tabriz, and subsequent commemorations brought the deaths of demonstrators into public rituals through which local grievances acquired national significance. Khomeini's uncompromising opposition to monarchy and his access to communication networks helped unify the demand that the Shah leave. Yet it is inaccurate to treat his prominence as proof that every participant endorsed government by Islamic jurists, a detailed religious constitution, or an agreed social program. Nationalists, Marxists, religious reformers, workers, and many women took part with different expectations. The capacity to unite around what should end was greater than the capacity to agree about what should begin.

State responses helped determine how those expectations evolved. In 1977 some leading critics appealed for constitutional reform. Later governments tried varying combinations of arrest, military policing, conciliatory announcements, legal concessions, and new personnel. The shootings of 1978 damaged the credibility of promises made by a government still willing to use deadly force, while the recurring demonstrations showed that repression had not restored durable compliance. The Shah's alternating willingness to negotiate and authorize coercion reflected genuine strategic dilemmas, not merely a uniform policy of violence. Nevertheless, an approach that changed repeatedly without creating a trusted channel for settlement encouraged protesters to discount promises and wait for fuller victory.

Labor strikes altered the balance between opposition and state. Religious demonstrations had already proved that large crowds would mobilize, but work stoppages made the crisis material to public administration and government finance. Oil workers' actions were especially important because petroleum exports supplied the state with revenue and international influence. Workers had occupational grievances and their own organizational processes; the eventual alignment of strikes with wider political demands should not erase those origins. As public employees, bank staff, teachers, journalists, and industrial workers joined different stoppages, ministers could issue instructions without being confident that they would be implemented. The monarchy was losing not only public admiration but the ordinary cooperation on which government depends.

The final political mechanism was military uncertainty. A large army with modern equipment could theoretically suppress an uprising, but only if orders could be issued, transmitted, and carried out by cohesive units. By January 1979 the Shah's departure had weakened the personal anchor of military loyalty. In February air-force technicians openly endorsed Khomeini, clashes drew in loyalists, rebellious service members, and civilians, and weapons spread beyond regular command. The Supreme Military Council's decision on 11 February to withdraw troops from the political confrontation was therefore decisive. It removed the final organized support on which Bakhtiar's authority rested. It was not identical to a unanimous ideological conversion of the armed forces, nor does it imply that the military had been powerless throughout the preceding months.

International policy shaped the setting but did not supply a complete explanation. The United States had strong interests in preserving a pro-Western Iranian state, officials differed about the Shah's prospects, and diplomatic cables show contingency planning as the crisis deepened. Such evidence does not prove that foreign governments commanded the movement or selected the revolution's outcome. The social organizations that mobilized demonstrators, the strike committees that halted production, and the soldiers who decided how to respond were Iranian actors whose choices cannot be inferred from foreign correspondence. Equally, excluding foreign interests altogether would distort the importance of arms relationships, international human-rights attention, and diplomatic discussions during the final months.

The revolution's success must finally be understood as contingent. Alternatives existed at particular moments: earlier liberalization, a different government response to the January and September confrontations, more effective negotiations, or a different level of military cohesion might have changed the sequence. Historians need not claim that any one alternative was likely to succeed to recognize that the outcome was not predetermined. During 1978, people revised expectations of what could happen, and those expectations affected their decisions to strike, march, negotiate, disobey, or remain loyal. By February 1979 a mass opposition coalition and a collapsing administrative and military chain came together. That combination, rather than any single cause, explains the fall of a monarchy that had seemed secure only two years earlier.

On 11 February 1979 the Pahlavi government ceased to rule. For many Iranians the event represented victory over authoritarianism, censorship, political imprisonment, and perceived foreign dependence; for others it brought anxiety about the future of constitutional liberties, property, religion, and social rights. The revolutionaries had overthrown an existing political order without agreeing on a settled replacement. That unresolved problem is not a minor qualification to the story but its necessary conclusion. Volume VIII begins with the struggle to determine which institutions would govern, whose conception of sovereignty would prevail, and how a broad revolutionary movement would become a state.

Chapter Summary

The Iranian Revolution emerged from a monarchy that had pursued major economic and social changes while sharply restricting independent political participation. Growth in education, industry, infrastructure, and urban employment was real, but rapid spending, inflation, unequal opportunity, and arbitrary government contributed to discontent. The existence of grievances in 1977 did not make revolution inevitable.

The earliest visible reopening of dissent involved constitutional nationalists, lawyers, writers, students, and professional organizations. The June 1977 National Front letter sought to revive constitutional government; the October Ten Nights of Poetry demonstrated that cultural activity could sustain large public gatherings and political discussion.

Clerical, bazaar, and religious networks also supplied institutions for public mobilization. The death of Mostafa Khomeini led to commemorations that extended the reach of religious political speech. Yet Shiʿi leaders and lay participants did not uniformly favor the same political order.

The January 1978 Qom protests, the February events at Tabriz, and subsequent demonstrations helped build a cycle of mourning and resistance. Local organizers, communication networks, repression, and changing political expectations all mattered; the forty-day pattern was neither automatic nor universal.

The Cinema Rex fire at Abadan intensified public mistrust, in part because competing accusations circulated amid limited reliable information. In September the Jaleh Square shootings under martial law became a major political turning point. Historians must distinguish contemporary mass casualty claims from later reconstructions with narrower evidentiary boundaries.

During autumn 1978, work stoppages changed the character of the uprising. Teachers, public employees, bank staff, journalists, industrial workers, and others challenged routine administration. Oil-industry strikes were particularly consequential because petroleum revenue financed the state; workers' grievances and organizations also retained their own histories.

The monarchy alternated coercion and accommodation. Sharif-Emami sought conciliation, Azhari directed a military government, and the Shah promised reform, but neither force nor concession restored trusted political authority. Arrests of former officials also failed to persuade much of the public that royal power had fundamentally changed.

Khomeini's residence in France gave his pronouncements international visibility and a channel to networks inside Iran. Revolutionary opposition converged on the removal of the Shah without agreement among religious, nationalist, socialist, professional, and labor participants concerning the institutions that should follow. Women participated in these movements with diverse political and social expectations.

In January 1979 Bakhtiar attempted a constitutional alternative. The Shah's departure on 16 January weakened the personalized monarchy but was not an abdication. Khomeini's return on 1 February and Bazargan's designation as head of a provisional government produced competing claims of authority against the existing cabinet.

The military crisis of 9–11 February transformed a legitimacy struggle into the final breakdown of royal government. Air-force technicians, armed civilians, and other groups challenged loyalist formations, and the Supreme Military Council decided to withdraw from the political confrontation on 11 February. Bakhtiar's government then collapsed.

The revolution thus succeeded through interacting processes: political exclusion, mass organization, popular expectations, strikes, leadership, state miscalculation, and military fragmentation. It ended the Pahlavi monarchy but did not resolve the debate over constitutionalism, religious authority, civil liberties, or economic and social policy.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 91 completes the history of the Pahlavi state not with a predetermined catastrophe, but with a problem of political authority that evolved through decisions. The monarchy had built considerable material power, yet its control of armed forces, finances, and ministries could not assure the consent or cooperation needed to govern. A succession of petitions, protests, mourning ceremonies, strikes, and military confrontations altered what people thought possible. The events of 11 February were therefore the culmination of an historical process, not a magic consequence of a single speech, a single oil statistic, or one foreign government's decision.

The chapter also establishes a crucial limit to the meaning of revolutionary victory. The coalition's unity was strongest in opposition to the Shah and weaker in its ideas about constitutional liberty, religious authority, property, labor, women's rights, and representation. That disagreement is part of the revolution itself, not merely a later footnote. The fall of a regime and the construction of a new state are related but distinct historical tasks.

The next volume begins where this chapter ends: with a victorious revolution facing the challenge of converting mass mobilization into laws, offices, institutions, and authoritative claims to govern. Historians must ask not only who gained power after February 1979, but how the alternatives debated during the revolution were narrowed, transformed, or defeated.
