Chapter Opening

Rebuilding a refinery is an engineering problem; rebuilding a political economy is a problem of power. Between 1989 and 1997 the Islamic Republic undertook both, restoring much of its war-damaged material base while leaving the revolutionary distribution of political and religious authority largely intact, and in doing so it created new prosperity, new grievances, and new questions that its institutions could not easily answer.

When the ceasefire with Iraq took effect in August 1988, Iran was a country of roughly fifty million people whose government had spent eight years organizing nearly every part of public life around war. Ports, refineries, bridges, and housing in the western and southwestern provinces had been damaged or destroyed. Consumer goods had been rationed through coupons. Prices, credit, and foreign exchange had been managed through layers of administrative control. Hundreds of thousands of families carried the memory of dead, disabled, or imprisoned relatives. The state that emerged from this experience was confident in its survival and uncertain about its future, and the most urgent question it faced was not ideological but practical: how would a revolutionary republic, whose founder died in June 1989, turn emergency mobilization into lasting development?

The answer associated with the presidency of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, who took office in August 1989 and served two terms until August 1997, was sazandegi, usually translated as "reconstruction" or "construction." The word named a program of public investment, imported technology, expanded credit, and selective market opening, but it also named a style of government that valued administrative competence and measurable results. Its supporters argued that revolutionary legitimacy could not rest indefinitely on sacrifice; it required roads, electricity, factories, and rising living standards. Its critics, who came from several directions, argued that reconstruction favored contractors and merchants over the poor, imported consumer culture the revolution had promised to resist, or threatened the political leverage of institutions that had grown powerful during the war.

This chapter treats reconstruction as more than a list of projects. It distinguishes the restoration of destroyed assets from the expansion of productive capacity, and both of these from the transformation of how an economy is owned, financed, and governed. The Islamic Republic did not move along a straight line from a command economy to a market economy. Wartime controls were loosened, partially restored, and loosened again; state enterprises were sold into hands that were not always private in any competitive sense; and the same institutions that dispensed licenses, credit, and foreign exchange also shaped who could succeed. Understanding these distinctions is necessary for any honest assessment of what the period achieved.

The chapter also follows reconstruction outside the offices of the planners. Veterans and the bereaved had to find places in a civilian society that honored their sacrifice more easily than it employed them. Villages gained roads, electricity, and clinics, while some peripheral regions continued to lag. A national family-planning program, launched after a decade of pronatalist rhetoric, accompanied one of the fastest declines in fertility recorded anywhere in the twentieth century. Tehran's municipality built parks, highways, and cultural centers, and in several cities residents protested over housing, prices, and municipal policy. Workers, entrepreneurs, professionals, and low-income households experienced the same policies very differently.

Foreign policy was part of the same project. A government that needed imported machinery, foreign technical assistance, and credit had reason to reduce confrontation with its neighbors and to widen its commercial partnerships. Iran repaired relations with Saudi Arabia, stayed out of the 1990–1991 Gulf War, and looked to new opportunities after the Soviet collapse in Central Asia and the Caucasus. Yet normalization was limited. Disputes with the United States hardened into sanctions, European "critical dialogue" collapsed after a German court's 1997 verdict, and the government's own security and ideological commitments continued to generate controversy abroad.

Historians draw on several kinds of evidence for this period: Iranian statistical publications and census data, the national development plans and budget laws, parliamentary and constitutional records, contemporary reporting by the International Monetary Fund and other international bodies, press and periodical sources, memoirs and interviews, and legal texts such as American executive orders and the Iran and Libya Sanctions Act of 1996. Each has limits. Official Iranian statistics were produced by institutions with policy stakes; international assessments often relied on government data; press freedom was restricted, though not uniform; and many memoirs were written after the political fortunes of their authors changed. The chapter distinguishes reported observations, rounded estimates and interpretations, with sources identified in the documentation section.

The chapter's argument can be stated briefly. Rafsanjani's presidency was an attempt to modernize the Islamic Republic economically without fundamentally liberalizing it politically. It restored productive capacity, expanded infrastructure, and widened opportunity for many Iranians, but financial instability, uneven distribution, institutional resistance, and political restriction exposed the limits of that strategy. These contradictions contributed to the conditions in which Mohammad Khatami won the presidency in May 1997, an event analyzed in Chapter 100, though the chapter takes care not to treat that outcome as inevitable. It follows Chapter 98, which examined the end of the war, the 1989 constitutional revisions, and the selection of Ali Khamenei as Supreme Leader.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the economic, physical, and social conditions Iran inherited from the Iran–Iraq War in 1989.
  2. Explain how Rafsanjani assembled a governing coalition and how the 1989 constitutional changes shaped the presidency.
  3. Analyze the objectives, instruments, and outcomes of the First and Second Five-Year Development Plans.
  4. Distinguish reconstruction of damaged assets from expansion of productive capacity, and economic liberalization from political liberalization.
  5. Evaluate the role of petroleum revenues, foreign borrowing, and imports in financing reconstruction.
  6. Differentiate state, private, cooperative, and parastatal ownership, and explain why privatization did not automatically create competitive markets.
  7. Trace the sequence from rapid recovery to payment arrears, exchange-rate instability, inflation, and renewed controls.
  8. Assess the uneven social consequences of reconstruction for veterans, rural communities, regions, workers, entrepreneurs, and low-income households.
  9. Interpret changes in fertility, education, urbanization, and literacy as the product of several interacting causes.
  10. Compare Iran's relations with the Gulf states, Russia, Central Asia, the Caucasus, Europe, and the United States, and explain why engagement differed from normalization.
  11. Examine the distribution of authority among the presidency, the Supreme Leader, and other institutions, and the competition among political factions.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the Rafsanjani presidency using economic, electoral, and documentary evidence.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  9. Why did economic change not produce a simple demand for political change, and how should historians treat correlation between social change and reformist politics?
  10. What evidence would allow historians to judge the Rafsanjani presidency as a developmental success, an incomplete project, or something else?

Historical Context

The reconstruction era grew out of the revolutionary decade covered in Chapters 92 through 98. The 1979 Revolution had replaced a monarchy that had pursued oil-financed industrialization and had produced a state that combined elected institutions with clerical oversight under the doctrine of velayat-e faqih (Chapter 93). The new republic nationalized banks, insurance companies, and many large industrial firms in 1979–1980, expropriated assets associated with the former regime, and placed many of them under revolutionary foundations, bonyads, that answered to the leadership rather than to ordinary ministries. The Constitution recognized state, cooperative, and private sectors, but in practice the state and quasi-public institutions occupied the commanding heights.

War then reorganized the economy again. Iraq's invasion in September 1980 and the long conflict that followed (Chapters 96–97) diverted resources to the front, interrupted oil exports and industrial supply, drew labor into the army and the volunteer Basij, and displaced populations from border cities. The government responded with rationing, price controls, import licensing, and administered exchange rates. These measures protected basic consumption and legitimized sacrifice, but they also created shortages, parallel markets, and privileged access for those who could obtain licenses or subsidized foreign exchange. By 1988 attacks on oil installations and shipping, falling oil prices in the mid-1980s, and the costs of the war had left the state short of revenue and investment.

The ceasefire of August 1988, the death of Ayatollah Khomeini on June 3, 1989, the selection of Ali Khamenei as Supreme Leader, and the constitutional revisions approved by referendum in July 1989 are treated in Chapter 98. For present purposes the most important changes were these: the office of prime minister was abolished and executive authority was concentrated in the president; the Expediency Council was given a constitutional basis; and the leadership qualifications were adjusted. The presidency Rafsanjani assumed was therefore institutionally stronger than its predecessor, yet it operated beneath a Supreme Leader who held ultimate authority over the armed forces, the judiciary, broadcasting, and the setting of general policy.

Rafsanjani also governed in a changing world. The Cold War was ending. The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, opening new states on Iran's northern frontier. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and the American-led response of 1991 transformed the Gulf. Oil markets were unstable, with price spikes followed by years of relatively low prices. Many developing countries were experimenting with structural adjustment, privatization, and trade liberalization under pressure from international institutions and the example of rapid growth in East Asia. Iranian technocrats were aware of these experiences, though Iran did not adopt a formal adjustment program with the IMF, and the government was sensitive to charges that it was importing a foreign economic model.

Finally, the period must be read against Iran's long demographic and social trajectory. Population growth had accelerated in the 1970s and 1980s; urbanization had been underway since the mid-twentieth century; and the literate and educated share of the population, including women, had been rising. Revolutionary policies expanded rural services and schooling, and wartime mobilization reinforced a language of national sacrifice and social justice. Reconstruction policy interacted with these long processes. Much of what happened in the 1990s, from falling birth rates to rising demands on universities and labor markets, cannot be attributed to the presidency alone.

A note on dates. The Iranian fiscal year begins on 1 Farvardin, usually March 20 or 21. The First Five-Year Plan is therefore described as covering 1989/90 to 1993/94, and international reports often refer to fiscal years such as 1994/95. This textbook uses that convention for economic data and Gregorian dates for political events. Readers should not treat a fiscal year as identical to a calendar year.

The Reconstruction Presidency

From revolutionary consolidation to developmental government: the postwar inheritance, the political leadership of reconstruction, and the first national plan.

Section 99.1

Iran at the Beginning of the Reconstruction Era

Any account of reconstruction must begin by asking what, exactly, had to be reconstructed. The Iran–Iraq War had damaged Iran in uneven ways. The most visible destruction lay in the southwest and the west. Khorramshahr, occupied in 1980 and recaptured in 1982, and Abadan, besieged early in the war, had suffered heavy damage; so had towns in Kurdistan, Kermanshah, Ilam, and other border districts that were shelled, bombed, or fought over. Oil installations, including refining and export facilities in Khuzestan and at offshore sites in the Gulf, had been struck repeatedly, particularly during the "tanker war" and the exchanges of missile and air attacks on cities in the late 1980s. Industrial plants, power lines, roads, bridges, housing, and schools in affected areas required repair or replacement.

Estimates of the monetary cost of the war vary enormously. Iranian official claims, which served diplomatic purposes as well as accounting ones, have ranged from hundreds of billions to more than a trillion dollars depending on what is counted: direct physical damage, lost output, lost oil revenue, military expenditure, or damage claimed from Iraq. Independent estimates are lower and rest on contested assumptions. A careful reader should therefore treat any single figure as an advocacy number or a modeling result rather than as a measured fact, and should distinguish damage to physical assets from the larger and less measurable loss of potential output during eight years in which investment and trade were distorted.

Beyond physical damage, the economy was strained by the way it had been managed. During the war the government had used rationing coupons, administered prices for essential goods, import and export licensing, and an official exchange rate far stronger than the rate at which dollars were traded on the open market. Consumers received staples at controlled prices, and holders of licenses obtained foreign exchange at the official rate; but supplies were limited, queues and informal markets persisted, and incentives to produce or invest in many sectors were weak. Public finances relied heavily on oil revenue, which had fallen with the price collapse of 1986, and on money creation and borrowing from domestic banks. Inflation, though restrained in official indices by price controls, eroded real incomes, and the black market became an important reference for ordinary Iranians.

Population pressure compounded these constraints. The population had grown rapidly through the 1980s, and the 1986 census, conducted during the war, counted roughly 49 million people. A young population, much of it born during the high-fertility years after the revolution, would soon enter schools, universities, and the labor market. Displaced families from war zones and rural migrants to cities increased demand for housing and public services. Unemployment, though difficult to measure in a mobilized economy, was a growing concern as soldiers and volunteers returned and public employment could not absorb all of them.

The expectations of the revolution also shaped the inheritance. The revolutionary coalition had promised social justice, protection of the "oppressed" (mostazafin), and independence from foreign economic dominance. The war had demanded sacrifice in the name of those promises. Many Iranians, particularly veterans and families in poorer districts, expected that peace would bring redistribution, housing, jobs, and recognition. At the same time a growing urban and commercial constituency, including many who had supported the revolution, expected relief from shortages and controls. Reconstruction would have to satisfy both groups, and it could not do so fully.

The constitutional environment of 1989 gave the new government some tools and imposed some limits. The abolition of the prime ministership concentrated executive responsibility in the president, who could now be held accountable, and could hold himself accountable, for economic performance. But the Constitution still reserved major decisions about war and peace, the armed forces, and the judiciary to the Supreme Leader, and Article 44's three-sector framework, together with Articles on foreign concessions, constrained how far privatization and foreign investment could go. The Guardian Council could review parliamentary legislation for conformity with Islamic law and the Constitution, which gave conservative jurists leverage over economic as well as social policy.

The distinction between reconstruction and development deserves emphasis at the outset. Reconstruction in the strict sense meant restoring what had existed: repairing refineries, rebuilding Khorramshahr, restoring power lines. Development meant expanding capacity beyond prewar levels and altering the structure of the economy: new industries, a diversified export base, higher productivity. Iranian leaders used the word sazandegi to cover both, and the ambiguity was politically useful. It allowed the government to present modernization as fulfillment of revolutionary duty, but it also meant that the success of the program could be measured against two very different standards, which are examined in the closing section of this chapter.

The June 1990 earthquake in the Manjil–Rudbar region of Gilan and Zanjan provinces, which killed tens of thousands of people (estimates commonly range from roughly 35,000 to 50,000), tested the new administration's capacity almost immediately. The disaster required relief, resettlement, and reconstruction of towns and villages, and it drew international assistance despite Iran's isolation. It also illustrated both the capacities of revolutionary institutions to mobilize volunteers and materials and the difficulties of coordinating relief across overlapping agencies.

Historical Evidence Study

Measuring the Economic Inheritance of War

The problem. Historians who wish to describe the economy of 1988–1989 must rely on indicators that were produced under wartime controls. Official consumer price indices covered controlled and uncontrolled goods in different proportions; the official exchange rate bore little relation to the market rate; and national accounts for the war years were prepared with limited coverage of informal activity.

Kinds of evidence. (1) Central Bank and Plan and Budget Organization statistics on output, prices, and trade. (2) The 1986 census and subsequent surveys on population, employment, and housing. (3) Reports of the International Monetary Fund and other international bodies, which drew on government data. (4) Iranian government damage assessments prepared for diplomatic claims. (5) Press accounts, memoirs, and later interviews.

Cautions. Damage estimates prepared for claims against Iraq have an obvious interest in totals. Indicators that mix controlled and market prices can understate inflation. Data on the informal sector are weak. Students should ask which institution produced a figure, for what purpose, and with what coverage.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might an official damage estimate differ from an independent economic estimate of the same war?
  2. How could administered prices and multiple exchange rates make wartime statistics misleading?
  3. What kinds of non-statistical evidence could help a historian understand shortages and rationing?
Section 99.2

Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani and the Politics of Reconstruction

Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani was born in 1934 into a landowning and pistachio-growing family near Rafsanjan in Kerman province, studied religious sciences in Qom, and became a student and political associate of Ruhollah Khomeini. He was imprisoned several times under the monarchy, helped organize clerical networks that supported Khomeini, and after 1979 became one of the most influential figures of the new regime: a member of the Revolutionary Council, a founder of the Islamic Republican Party, and, from 1980 to 1989, Speaker of the Majles. He survived an assassination attempt in 1979, and he combined clerical standing with a reputation as an organizer and a negotiator who understood how institutions, money, and personal networks operated.

His wartime role was central. As Speaker he was one of the most visible voices explaining the war to the public, and in 1988 Khomeini appointed him acting commander-in-chief of the armed forces. In the closing months of the conflict he was among those who argued that continuing the war was no longer sustainable, and he played a significant part in the process by which Khomeini accepted United Nations Security Council Resolution 598 in July 1988 (Chapter 98). The decision was painful and politically dangerous, and it identified Rafsanjani with a pragmatic assessment of what the Republic could afford.

In the presidential election of July 1989, held on the same day as the referendum on the constitutional revisions, Rafsanjani won by a very large margin against a minor opponent, with a turnout of just over 54 percent of eligible voters according to the figures compiled by the Iran Data Portal. The election was not a competitive contest in the later sense: the field was narrow and the outcome was widely expected. But it gave him a clear mandate and strong institutional authority, and he moved quickly to build a cabinet that mixed experienced clerical politicians with engineers, economists, and administrators, many of whom had training abroad or experience in industry.

That recruitment of technocrats is one of the defining features of his administration. Rafsanjani preferred officials who could manage budgets, negotiate contracts, and run large organizations, and he valued administrative results over ideological display. He relied on first vice president Hassan Habibi, on economic officials such as Mohsen Noorbakhsh at the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Finance (1989–1993), then as vice president for economic affairs after the Majles rejected his renewed nomination in August 1993, and from September 1994 as governor of the Central Bank, and on the Plan and Budget Organization to prepare plans and negotiate priorities. Many of these officials argued that revolutionary goals required a stronger economy and that the experience of other developing countries offered lessons about how to build one. The result was a government that was less preoccupied with mass mobilization than its predecessor and more oriented toward project planning and credit. [Sources: Middle East Economic Survey; UPI.]

The relationship with the new Supreme Leader was both cooperative and unequal. Khamenei lacked Khomeini's charismatic authority and the religious credentials that had made Khomeini's rulings difficult to challenge, and in the early years he relied on allies in the clerical establishment, the security services, and the military. Rafsanjani had been influential in securing Khamenei's selection, and the two worked together on many matters, including the approval of the first plan, the handling of the economy, and the pursuit of reconstruction. Over time, however, Khamenei built his own institutional base, especially in the judiciary, the media, the foundations, the Revolutionary Guards, and the conservative clergy, and differences between the leader and the president emerged over cultural policy, foreign policy, and the pace of liberalization.

It would be misleading to describe Rafsanjani's political coalition as a party. It consisted of overlapping groups: technocrats and managers in government ministries; commercial and industrial entrepreneurs who saw opportunities in liberalization; clerics and administrators personally connected to him; sections of the Islamic left who supported state-led development though not necessarily market reform, at least initially; and those who valued the stability he promised. This coalition was held together by access to office, patronage, and a shared commitment to reconstruction, and it would later fracture over economic policy and over political questions. Chapter 99's later sections return to its institutional form, the Executives of Construction, founded in 1996.

Rafsanjani's personal reputation has remained contested. Supporters credit him with stabilizing the state after the war, guiding the transition after Khomeini, and expanding infrastructure and education. Critics, including some former allies, accuse him of favoring relatives and associates, tolerating corruption, and subordinating social justice to economic expediency. Many of the more specific allegations about wealth and patronage that circulated in the 1990s were made in a politicized environment and were difficult to verify, and historians should distinguish between documented findings, widely repeated claims, and polemical assertion. His family and associates did occupy prominent positions in business and politics, and public perception of this fact mattered politically whether or not particular allegations could be substantiated.

Historical Profile

Rafsanjani as Revolutionary Leader and Developmental Pragmatist

Revolutionary credentials. Close association with Khomeini, imprisonment under the monarchy, early leadership in the revolutionary institutions, and a long tenure as Speaker gave him standing that few contemporaries could match.

Pragmatic record. His role in the decision to end the war, his preference for experienced administrators, and his emphasis on production and foreign investment marked him as an advocate of practical solutions within the revolutionary framework.

Limits. He governed beneath a Supreme Leader who held ultimate authority; he faced a Majles and Guardian Council with powers to block or reshape policy; and his coalition was sustained by patronage that created reputational costs. A fair assessment should avoid both hagiography and caricature.

Questions for students:

  1. Which of Rafsanjani's earlier roles most prepared him for the presidency, and why?
  2. Why might a coalition built around reconstruction be harder to sustain than a coalition built around revolution or war?
  3. How should historians treat allegations about wealth and patronage that were widely circulated but difficult to verify?
Section 99.3

The First Five-Year Development Plan

Development planning was not new to Iran. The monarchy had issued a series of national development plans from 1948 onward, and the Plan Organization had been an influential institution under both Mohammad Reza Shah and the Islamic Republic, which retained it in the form of the Plan and Budget Organization. During the war, however, comprehensive planning had given way to emergency budgeting, and a first postwar plan had a political as well as an economic function. It would present reconstruction as a program with goals, priorities, and measures of success, and it would serve as a framework for negotiating among ministries, the Majles, and the Leader.

The First Five-Year Plan covered the Iranian years 1989/90 to 1993/94 and was approved after discussion in the Majles. It set ambitious targets for economic growth and investment, with high rates of capital formation intended to restore and expand productive capacity. The plan emphasized the repair of war damage, the expansion of energy, transport, and communications infrastructure, the development of industry and agriculture, the improvement of public services, and an increase in non-oil exports. It anticipated a significant role for imported machinery and technology and projected substantial foreign borrowing to finance them. [Sources: Iran Data Portal; Middle East Economic Survey.]

The plan also embodied a program of economic liberalization. It called for reduced administrative controls on prices, a more market-oriented approach to allocating foreign exchange, greater scope for private investment, the sale of some state-owned enterprises, the reduction of subsidies over time, and measures to attract foreign capital and technical expertise. These measures were not presented as a rejection of the revolution. They were framed within the Constitution's recognition of three sectors and within arguments that the government needed to use resources more efficiently. But they represented a sharp departure from the wartime reliance on rationing and administrative allocation, and they met resistance among those who benefited from controlled prices and licensed access.

Implementation diverged from the plan in important ways. In its early years the economy recovered rapidly, helped by the restoration of oil output, higher imports, the use of idle capacity, and a surge of investment and consumption that had been repressed during the war. The IMF's contemporary reporting records strong growth during the plan period, with average annual real GDP growth of around 7 percent in its 1995 assessment. Oil revenues, imports, and borrowing all rose. But the plan's financing assumptions proved optimistic. Foreign borrowing, much of it short-term trade credit, increased faster than export revenue, and by 1993–1994 the country was struggling to meet its obligations, as Section 99.8 explains. [Sources: IMF 1995.]

A second divergence concerned the scope of liberalization. Many price controls and subsidies remained, and attempts to reduce them were partial and politically contentious. Basic goods continued to receive subsidies, and the government retained significant control over credit through state-owned banks. Foreign exchange allocation changed more than once. The gap between planned and actual outcomes was not unusual for development plans in any country, but it was exacerbated in Iran's case by the fact that policy authority was dispersed: the government prepared the plan, but the Majles amended and approved the budget; the Guardian Council could object; the Leader's views mattered; and powerful institutions could delay or deflect policies that threatened them.

Students should take care not to read the plan as a simple instruction to build a market economy. It combined state-led investment, the extension of state and quasi-state economic activity in some sectors, and selective deregulation in others. The government remained the principal investor, the largest employer, and the owner of the banks, the oil sector, and large industrial holdings. The plan's liberalizing elements were therefore a modification of a state-dominated system rather than its replacement. That hybrid character is crucial for the interpretation of later events, because it meant that both the successes and the failures of the period belonged to the hybrid and not to a purely market or purely planned model.

The plan's legislative history also illustrates the structure of Iranian economic policy-making. The Plan and Budget Organization prepared targets and sectoral allocations; the cabinet approved a draft; the Majles debated and amended it; and the Guardian Council reviewed it. Along the way, disagreements surfaced about regional allocations, subsidies, the role of foreign capital, and the treatment of the foundations and cooperatives. These disagreements show that the plan was an arena of political bargaining as well as a technical document.

Section 99.4

Technocracy, Development, and Revolutionary Legitimacy

Reconstruction raised a question that every revolutionary regime eventually faces: what is the relationship between the ideals that justified the revolution and the administrative competence needed to govern? In Iran's case the question was sharpened by the nature of the regime. The Islamic Republic grounded its legitimacy in a combination of popular revolution, Islamic jurisprudence, and the leadership of the jurist, and it linked political authority to religious qualification. Reconstruction, by contrast, depended on engineers, economists, managers, and contractors whose authority rested on technical knowledge. The coexistence of these two forms of authority was never entirely comfortable.

Supporters of technocratic government offered several arguments. Islamic governance, they said, required competence as well as piety, and a state that could not supply electricity, housing, and jobs would lose public support. Economic efficiency was a way of serving the oppressed, because growth created resources for welfare. National sovereignty also depended on productive capacity; a country that could not feed itself, repair its refineries, or build its own infrastructure would remain dependent on others. These arguments allowed technocrats to present their work as an expression of revolutionary values rather than a departure from them.

Opponents and skeptics came from different positions. Some on the Islamic left, who had favored state control of foreign trade, redistribution, and an expansive role for public institutions during the 1980s, feared that liberalization would widen inequality and weaken protections for the poor. Some conservative clerics and bazaar merchants, who generally favored private property and trade, nevertheless distrusted foreign influence, cultural change, and the growth of a new managerial class not tied to the traditional networks of the mosque and bazaar. Revolutionary organizations and foundations worried about their autonomy. Meanwhile, some who benefited from controlled prices, licensing, or preferential access to foreign exchange feared losing privileges. These groups did not form a single bloc and their positions shifted with circumstances.

The result was a politics of cross-cutting alliances. A bazaar merchant might welcome trade liberalization while resenting competition from new import-export firms linked to the state. A leftist might support infrastructure investment in rural areas while opposing the sale of public enterprises. A cleric might approve of building programs and object to consumer imports. The language of "Islamic economics," "social justice," and "self-sufficiency" was available to every side, and the lack of a single authoritative Islamic economic doctrine meant that policy debate often took the form of competing appeals to the same revolutionary vocabulary. Students should avoid assuming that "conservative" and "liberal" labels map neatly onto economic positions in this period.

Technocracy also had institutional implications. A government that emphasizes technical expertise tends to value stable bureaucratic procedures, long-term planning, and negotiated contracts, and it may be less responsive to mass mobilization. In Iran this created friction with institutions whose legitimacy depended on revolutionary credentials and popular mobilization, such as the Basij, certain foundations, and revolutionary courts. It also meant that technocrats had to defend their policies in terms the clerical leadership would accept, which sometimes led to compromises about the pace and form of reform.

Finally, the technocratic project was shaped by its limited political foundation. Technocrats could design plans, but they depended on the president, the Leader, and the Majles for authority and on conservative institutions for approval. They were not elected officials accountable to organized constituencies, and they lacked a party. This limited their capacity to build durable support, and it left them vulnerable to attack when economic difficulties arose in the mid-1990s. Section 99.18 returns to this point in connection with the Executives of Construction.

Historical Debate

Did Economic Reconstruction Represent a Departure from Revolutionary Ideals?

The question: Was the shift toward market-oriented reconstruction a betrayal or abandonment of the revolution's commitments to social justice and independence, or a legitimate adaptation of those commitments to peacetime conditions?

Interpretation A — A departure: Critics, including many on the Islamic left and some later reformers and scholars, argue that the policies of the 1990s rested on assumptions about markets, imports, and private accumulation that were at odds with revolutionary promises. They point to rising inequality, the prominence of contractors and importers, and the diminished role of redistribution.
Interpretation B — An adaptation: Defenders argue that the revolution never established a single economic doctrine, that the Constitution explicitly recognized private and cooperative sectors, and that restoring production and employment was essential to protecting the poor. They emphasize that the state remained the largest economic actor and that welfare and subsidy institutions persisted.
Interpretation C — Continuity of state-centered patterns: Some scholars argue that the period shows continuity with earlier state-centered, oil-financed development, in which political access shaped economic opportunity, and that the labels "liberalization" and "revolutionary ideals" both obscure this continuity.

Evidence: Plan documents, budget data, subsidy and welfare expenditure, privatization records, patterns of credit allocation, and the statements of political actors. Disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology more than basic facts.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians understand the origins of later reformist and populist movements, which drew selectively on each of these critiques.

Student task: Choose one piece of evidence (for example, the treatment of subsidies or the role of the foundations) and explain which interpretation it best supports, and which it complicates.

Section 99.5

Rebuilding Infrastructure and National Productive Capacity

The most visible achievement of the reconstruction era was physical. Highways, power plants, dams, telephone exchanges, housing, factories, schools, and hospitals were built or restored in large numbers. Historians face a methodological challenge here: many projects were begun before 1989, continued through the 1990s, and completed after 1997, and attributing them to a single presidency can mislead. This section therefore distinguishes projects initiated, continued, and completed during Rafsanjani's presidency wherever the record allows.

Transportation. Road building expanded the national network, with new freeways and highways linking Tehran to Karaj, Qom, and other cities, and improved connections to the Caspian coast and the southern ports. The railway network also grew. The line connecting Bafq in central Iran to the Persian Gulf port of Bandar Abbas, long planned, was constructed in stages from 1982 onward and linked the plateau to the Gulf during the reconstruction years and gave the central plateau direct rail access to the Gulf. In May 1996 the Mashhad–Sarakhs–Tejen line connected Iran's rail system with Turkmenistan and, through it, with Central Asia, a link of obvious significance after the Soviet collapse. Port expansion at Bandar Abbas and other Gulf ports supported rising imports. In Tehran, work on the metro that had begun before the revolution continued, though the first operational lines opened only at the end of the decade. [Sources: UN ESCAP; UN ESCAP.]

Energy and water. Electricity generation capacity and the transmission and distribution network expanded substantially, reaching more rural households and supporting industrial demand. Hydroelectric and irrigation dams were planned and built on several rivers, including projects in Khuzestan and the Zagros, and the government invested in drinking-water supply and sanitation in cities and villages. Water policy during these years emphasized large engineering projects, a choice whose environmental and hydrological consequences became subjects of debate in later decades. The broad expansion should be distinguished from the commissioning of individual dams, which often spanned several administrations.

Communications. Telephone service was expanded, with more exchanges and lines, though demand continued to exceed supply and waiting lists remained long. Broadcasting expanded, and the government invested in satellite and microwave links. At the same time the government restricted access to foreign satellite television, a measure discussed in Section 99.19, which illustrates how infrastructure expansion coexisted with efforts to control content.

Housing and urban services. Reconstruction of war-damaged housing and towns, resettlement after the 1990 earthquake, and the expansion of urban residential construction absorbed substantial resources. The Housing Foundation and other institutions supported rural housing and low-cost building, while private developers expanded in major cities. Urban services, including roads, parks, and municipal facilities, grew in Tehran, Isfahan, Mashhad, and other cities.

Industry. Industrial plants damaged or underused during the war were restored, and new capacity was added in steel, petrochemicals, automobiles, cement, and consumer goods. The Mobarakeh Steel Complex near Isfahan, which began operations in the early 1990s, became one of the country's largest industrial enterprises. Petrochemical complexes in the Gulf region, including at Bandar Imam Khomeini, expanded production. Automobile manufacturing, concentrated in Tehran and its vicinity, expanded through assembly arrangements with foreign firms and domestic production under licensing, serving a growing urban market. Many industrial projects depended on imported machinery and components, which tied industrial expansion to access to foreign exchange.

Public services. The government increased the number of schools, universities, clinics, and hospitals, and continued the rural health network described in Section 99.11. Spending on education and health supported rising enrollment and improved indicators, though quality and access varied by region.

Assessing these investments requires attention to several questions. First, how much of the gain was restoration of prewar capacity and how much was genuine expansion? In some sectors, such as power and oil, much early growth was restoration. In others, such as telecommunications and higher education, expansion was substantial. Second, how efficiently were funds used? Large projects were often delayed or went over budget, and the quality of contracting and supervision varied. Third, who benefited? Roads and electricity broadened access and reduced isolation for many communities, while contracts and procurement created opportunities for firms with political and personal connections. Fourth, how did infrastructure affect the relationship between state and society? By delivering services directly, the government strengthened its claim to legitimacy, but unmet expectations created disappointment.

Section 99.6

Oil, Natural Gas, Industry, and International Investment

Petroleum was the financial center of reconstruction. Crude oil exports supplied the large majority of Iran's foreign-exchange earnings and a substantial portion of government revenue, and the capacity to import machinery, spare parts, raw materials, and consumer goods depended on them. The war had damaged export facilities and reduced capacity, and production in the late 1980s was well below the levels of the mid-1970s. Restoring output was therefore both an engineering priority and a financial necessity.

The National Iranian Oil Company repaired damaged installations, including refineries and offshore platforms, and worked to increase output toward the quota assigned to Iran within OPEC. Production recovered during the early 1990s to about 3.6 million barrels per day by 1996/97, which was well below the peak of the 1970s but sufficient to generate substantial revenue when prices permitted. Oil export revenues were on the order of tens of billions of dollars cumulatively over the period but varied considerably from year to year with prices. The 1990 Iraqi invasion of Kuwait and the resulting disruption of Gulf supplies temporarily raised prices and boosted Iranian revenue, but prices subsequently fell, and the mid-1990s were a period of relatively low and unstable prices. This volatility made planning difficult and contributed directly to the financing problems examined in Section 99.8. [Sources: IMF 1998.]

Natural gas received growing attention. Iran possesses very large gas reserves, much of it located in the South Pars field shared with Qatar under the Gulf, and gas offered possibilities for domestic consumption, petrochemical feedstock, reinjection to maintain oil production, and eventually export. Development required technology and capital that Iran lacked, which led to negotiations with foreign firms. Pipeline arrangements were also pursued. A natural-gas supply agreement with Turkey concluded in the mid-1990s became a flashpoint with the United States, which viewed such deals as inconsistent with its sanctions policy (see Section 99.16).

The terms on which foreign companies could participate were constrained by the Constitution, which establishes public control of major resources and restricts foreign concessions (Articles 44, 45 and 81), and by the political sensitivity of any arrangement resembling the prerevolutionary oil agreements. The solution that emerged was the buyback contract. Under a buyback, a foreign company finances and carries out development of a field or project for a fixed contractual period, is repaid from the production in a predetermined manner and with a limited rate of return, and then hands over operations to the National Iranian Oil Company. The company does not own the reserves and does not hold equity in the field in the sense of an ownership stake. Critics of the model, including foreign companies, argued that it limited returns and discouraged long-term investment; defenders argued that it respected constitutional limits and protected sovereignty. The first major contracts of this type were negotiated in the mid-1990s. An initial agreement with the American firm Conoco was announced in 1995 and then blocked when the United States prohibited American involvement in Iranian petroleum development; a similar project was subsequently taken up by the French company Total, which also became involved in other fields. South Pars development contracts involving international consortia followed in 1997, after the end of Rafsanjani's presidency. The July 1995 Sirri agreement illustrates the early implementation of this approach. [Sources: U.S. Department of State; Congressional Research Service.]

Petrochemicals formed a second pillar. Using domestic gas and petroleum feedstocks, new and restored complexes produced fertilizers, plastics, and other chemicals for domestic use and export, and the government regarded petrochemicals as a means of diversifying exports and adding value to hydrocarbon resources. The Gulf region, especially around Bandar Imam Khomeini and Asaluyeh, became an industrial zone, with employment and migration implications for Khuzestan and Bushehr.

The relationship between economic sovereignty and foreign investment was politically sensitive. Foreign partners offered technology, finance, and market access, but Iranian political culture, shaped by memories of foreign control over oil in the twentieth century (Chapters 75 and 86), made overt dependence unacceptable. The government therefore sought to attract foreign participation through contractual mechanisms and free trade and industrial zones, established by law in 1993 at Kish, Qeshm, and Chabahar, while insisting on the principles of national control. The zones were intended to attract investment and trade with fewer restrictions, though their economic results were modest in this period.

For students the key point is that oil financed reconstruction but also shaped its fragility. Because oil revenue was received in foreign exchange and controlled by the state, it gave the government great discretion over investment and imports, and it reduced the need to rely on taxation and the accountability that taxation sometimes brings. At the same time, dependence on oil meant that falling prices or production setbacks transmitted rapidly to the budget and the balance of payments. The government attempted to diversify exports, but oil continued to dominate; non-oil exports grew from a low base, and the economy remained structurally exposed.

Section 99.7

Privatization, Markets, and the Changing Ownership of the Economy

Privatization in Iran must be understood against the particular ownership structure produced by the revolution. After 1979, banks, insurance companies, and a large number of industrial enterprises had been nationalized or taken over, and many other firms had come under the control of revolutionary foundations after the confiscation of assets belonging to the former elite. By 1989 the state and quasi-state sectors dominated large-scale industry, finance, and foreign trade. The First Plan called for transferring some of these enterprises to private owners, but this was not a simple return to prerevolutionary ownership. Some former owners had left the country or lost their assets, the foundations held legal claims, and the constitutional and political environment made wholesale reversal impossible.

The program that emerged proceeded cautiously. The government offered shares in selected state-owned firms, many of which had been taken over or nationalized, through the revitalized Tehran Stock Exchange, which had existed before the revolution but had been inactive. It sold some enterprises directly to buyers, and it encouraged investors to participate in new ventures. Progress was uneven. Many of the enterprises considered most attractive, or the most important strategically, remained under state or foundation control, and the pace of sales varied with political conditions. Reports at the time and later indicated that the number of firms actually transferred and the proportion of the economy affected were modest compared with the stated intentions.

A central analytical point is that a change in the formal owner does not necessarily produce a competitive market. Several issues arose. Buyers were sometimes institutions closely connected to the state or to the foundations, so ownership shifted from one public-adjacent entity to another. State-owned banks continued to allocate credit, and access to credit often depended on political and personal connections. Domestic prices for many goods remained administered, import licenses and foreign exchange were allocated, and large firms often enjoyed protection or preferential treatment. In these conditions the identity of the owner mattered less than the surrounding structure of rules and access.

Students should therefore distinguish four categories. Government enterprises were owned and managed by ministries or state companies. Cooperative enterprises were recognized by the Constitution as a distinct sector, though their significance in practice varied. Genuinely private firms, ranging from small workshops to large commercial and industrial groups, were owned by individuals or families and operated for profit, subject to licensing and regulation. Parastatal entities, including the revolutionary foundations, were institutionally distinct from ministries and often exempt from ordinary oversight, yet they enjoyed public authority, tax and customs advantages, and political patronage. A single enterprise could move among these categories over time, and the boundaries were porous.

The foundations deserve special mention. The Foundation of the Oppressed and War Disabled, the Foundation of Martyrs, the Imam Khomeini Relief Committee, and other organizations owned or controlled substantial assets and operated businesses in agriculture, construction, manufacturing, and trade, in addition to providing welfare. They answered primarily to the Supreme Leader rather than to the president, and attempts to subject them to ordinary budgetary or tax controls were resisted. Their economic activities grew during this period, and the question of how they fit into a liberalizing economy became more pressing. Chapter 105 is responsible for their comprehensive institutional history.

A related development was the expansion of engineering and construction firms associated with the Revolutionary Guards. After the war, the government encouraged the use of military engineering units and personnel in reconstruction projects, and the Guards' construction headquarters, which was organized around 1989–1990, undertook roads, dams, pipelines, and other large works. This provided an organized and politically reliable source of project capacity and helped demobilize and employ former combatants, but it also created the basis for the Guards' later prominence in the economy. For the period 1989–1997 it is sufficient to note this beginning; the later growth of the Guards' commercial network is the subject of Chapter 105.

Trade liberalization proceeded in a similarly partial way. The government reduced some import restrictions and allowed more goods to enter, and private importers expanded their activities, but tariffs, licensing, and other controls remained. When foreign-exchange shortages emerged in the mid-1990s, many of the reforms were reversed (Section 99.8). The relationship between liberalization and political resistance also deserves attention: bazaar merchants, industrialists, labor groups, and conservative politicians objected to different aspects of reform, and the Majles and Guardian Council could slow or modify measures. As a result, privatization and liberalization advanced in fits and starts rather than as a coherent program.

One of the lasting effects was the emergence of new commercial and contracting networks. Entrepreneurs, contractors, and managers with access to state credit, licenses, and contracts built substantial enterprises, and some became prominent political and economic figures. Observers at the time, including many Iranians, associated these networks with wealth accumulation and corruption, and the perception of inequitable access became politically significant. Historians should treat claims about specific individuals with caution, but the structural point, that opportunities depended heavily on proximity to the state, is well supported.

Section 99.8

Foreign Debt, Currency Instability, Inflation, and Economic Retrenchment

The first years of reconstruction were years of unusually strong recovery. Idle industrial capacity was brought back into use, imports of machinery and raw materials rose, public and private investment grew, and consumption, long repressed, expanded. Contemporary reporting by the International Monetary Fund describes an initial period of rapid growth, with annual real GDP growth averaging about 7 percent during the First Plan, as reported in the 1995 IMF assessment. Iranians saw more goods in shops, more construction, and new roads and services. It was easy at the time to read these developments as proof that the reconstruction strategy was working.

Yet the growth rested on a financing structure that was increasingly fragile. Imports rose far faster than non-oil exports. Oil revenue, the main source of foreign exchange, was volatile and, after the brief spike of 1990, relatively low. To pay for imports the government and enterprises borrowed abroad. Much of this borrowing took the form of short-term trade credits and bank financing, in part because Iran's access to long-term loans was limited by its political isolation and by its credit standing. When short-term credits come due in large amounts, a country that has not built sufficient reserves or export capacity must find new money quickly or fail to pay. By 1993 this point had been reached.

In those years Iran's external position deteriorated sharply. Debts became due that the country could not meet on schedule, and external payment arrears accumulated. The IMF's 1998 retrospective assessment reports external arrears of about $12 billion in 1993/94, together with an overall balance-of-payments deficit of $11.7 billion. The same report estimates total outstanding external debt at $23 billion in 1993/94 and $16.8 billion in 1996/97. It is important to distinguish external debt, the total amount owed to nonresident creditors, from arrears, the portion of obligations that were overdue. The total debt was not extreme by comparison with some other developing countries, but the concentration of short maturities and the arrears indicated a liquidity crisis: the country could not pay what was due when it was due. The government negotiated with creditors, and rescheduling arrangements with several European countries and Japan were concluded in 1994–1995, easing the immediate pressure. [Sources: IMF 1998.]

Exchange-rate policy was entangled with these developments. For many years Iran had operated several exchange rates: an official rate that was very strong, in the range of 70 rials to the dollar, used for essential imports and government transactions, a more depreciated rate for other purposes, and a free-market rate that was many times weaker. This system subsidized privileged importers and consumers but distorted incentives, encouraged smuggling and rent-seeking, and discouraged exports. In March 1993 the government attempted to unify the rates by moving to a floating arrangement at a rate far weaker than the old official one. The reform was intended to eliminate distortions and increase exports, but it was introduced just as foreign-exchange availability was tightening, and the rial depreciated sharply, raising the cost of imports and contributing to inflation. Because the government lacked sufficient reserves to defend the unified rate, and because the underlying imbalance persisted, the unification did not hold. In the following years the authorities reimposed controls and reintroduced multiple rates.

Inflation accelerated. Official indices showed consumer price increases that, in fiscal 1995/96 averaged 49.4 percent annually, compared with 35.2 percent in 1994/95 and 23.2 percent in 1996/97. Wages did not keep pace, and real purchasing power fell for many households, particularly those with fixed incomes. Inflation fed political dissatisfaction, as the urban unrest described in Section 99.12 illustrates. The government attempted to contain it through price controls, import compression, administrative allocation of foreign exchange, and fiscal measures, some of which repeated the instruments of the war economy. These policy reversals demonstrate that liberalization had been neither complete nor irreversible. [Sources: IMF 1998; IMF WP 99/15.]

The Second Five-Year Development Plan, which began in 1995/96, reflected the new priorities. Its emphasis shifted toward stabilization, debt management, expanding non-oil exports, and controlling imports, while continuing investment in infrastructure and industry on a more constrained basis. Growth slowed from the early 1990s peak. The second half of the decade was also affected by the American sanctions announced in 1995 and 1996, which complicated foreign investment, and by the continuing volatility of oil prices. By 1997 the economy had stabilized to some extent but remained burdened by inflation, debt-service obligations, and structural dependence on oil.

Why did a program that began with strong recovery end in financial crisis? Several factors interacted. The government's growth targets and import-intensive investment placed heavy demands on foreign exchange. Oil prices did not rise enough to meet these demands. Foreign credit was short-term and expensive. The exchange-rate system created incentives that worked against exports and in favor of imports. Domestic political constraints made it difficult to cut spending, raise taxes, or reduce subsidies rapidly. And fragmented authority limited the government's ability to enforce a consistent policy. None of these factors alone explains the crisis, and historians continue to debate their relative weight, with some emphasizing policy mistakes, such as the timing of exchange-rate reform and reliance on short-term credit, and others emphasizing external shocks and structural vulnerabilities.

Definition: Replacing several official exchange rates with a single rate for most transactions.

Section 99.9

Demobilization, Veterans, and the Social Legacy of War

Demobilization is often imagined as a single event, the moment fighting stops and soldiers go home. In Iran it was a long process. The army, the Revolutionary Guards, and the Basij had mobilized very large numbers of people, many of them volunteers who had left schools, farms, and workshops. After the ceasefire, formal units were reduced and personnel returned to civilian life, but the war's institutions and networks did not disappear. They continued as welfare organizations, political constituencies, and, in some cases, economic actors.

The human legacy was immense. Official Iranian figures place the number of Iranians killed in the war at roughly two hundred thousand, with estimates by other researchers varying, and a far larger number were wounded. Many were permanently disabled, and a significant number had been exposed to chemical weapons, particularly mustard agents used by Iraq in several campaigns (Chapter 97). Tens of thousands of Iranians were held as prisoners of war in Iraq, and the process of prisoner exchange, which proceeded in stages from 1988 and was largely completed in 1990, returned many to their families after years of captivity. Their return was a moment of public emotion but also of adjustment: the men who came back had been absent through formative years, and families had reorganized around their absence.

Welfare responsibilities were assigned largely to revolutionary institutions rather than to ordinary ministries. The Foundation of Martyrs and Veterans Affairs and the Foundation of the Oppressed and War Disabled administered benefits, pensions, medical treatment, housing assistance, and educational privileges for the families of the dead and for the disabled, called janbazan. These benefits included preferential admission quotas to universities, hiring preferences in the public sector, loans, and access to specialized hospitals. Their purpose was both material and symbolic: to compensate sacrifice and to honor the status of "martyrs" and veterans as exemplary citizens. The existence of a privileged category also created resentment among those who had not served or who felt that preferences distorted competition for jobs and education, and the question of how far such privileges should extend remained politically sensitive.

Employment was a central challenge. Returning soldiers and volunteers had to find work in an economy that was recovering but not yet able to absorb all of them. Some entered the army or the Revolutionary Guards as professionals; some found positions in the expanding public sector and in reconstruction projects, including the engineering and construction units mentioned in Section 99.7; others returned to farming or small trade; and many struggled. Veterans with disabilities required long-term care. Those who had been very young at the front sometimes lacked schooling and vocational skills, and the government sponsored education and training programs to help them.

Families of the dead and disabled formed a distinctive social group. Widows and mothers of martyrs held a recognized status in public discourse, received benefits, and sometimes assumed visible roles in commemorative and political life. Yet public honor did not remove the practical difficulties of raising children, managing households, and coping with grief, and the experience of bereavement varied by social class, region, and family. Rural families, in particular, often lacked access to the services available in cities. Children of martyrs, who came to be a recognized category in education and employment policy, grew up in a society that valued their parents' sacrifice but also expected them to embody revolutionary ideals.

Displaced communities posed another challenge. Residents of war-damaged border cities and towns had fled to other parts of the country, and their return or resettlement depended on the repair of housing and infrastructure and on the availability of compensation. Khorramshahr and Abadan, as symbols of resistance and loss, were the focus of rebuilding efforts, and the return of residents was gradual. Some families chose not to return, and others lived in temporary housing for years. Land mines and unexploded ordnance remained a hazard in border regions, and clearing them was an extended task.

Public memory of the war became an organized field. Museums, memorials, cemeteries, and annual commemorations, the production of films, literature, and visual culture, and the naming of streets and institutions after martyrs sustained a narrative of sacrifice and resistance, which the state promoted as a source of national and revolutionary legitimacy. At the same time, many Iranians had more ambivalent memories, shaped by loss, fear, and exhaustion, and writers and filmmakers began to explore those themes. The relationship between official commemoration and personal memory is one example of how demobilization was cultural as well as administrative.

Students should distinguish formal demobilization, the reduction of military forces and return of personnel to civilian status, from the persistence of wartime institutions, political networks, and public memory. The Basij continued as a mobilization organization; veterans' associations became political constituencies; foundations retained their economic and welfare roles; and the language of sacrifice remained a resource in political debate. These continuities shaped the Republic's later politics, including the competition for the allegiance of the "war generation" that features in Chapters 100 and 101.

Section 99.10

Rural Development, Agricultural Recovery, and Regional Inequality

Reconstruction is often described through the capital and the great projects, but a large share of Iranians still lived in villages or small towns in 1989, and rural development was one of the revolution's most important promises. The revolutionary government had created the Reconstruction Jihad (Jihad-e Sazandegi) in the early 1980s, organizing volunteers and engineers to build rural roads, supply electricity and water, and provide agricultural services. The institution was later raised to the status of a ministry, and in the 1990s it expanded its work on roads, irrigation, livestock, and agricultural extension, while also becoming involved in broader development programs.

Agricultural policy emphasized food security and self-sufficiency, partly in response to the wartime experience of dependence on imports. The government offered subsidies and guaranteed prices for staple crops such as wheat, supported the mechanization of farming, expanded irrigation, and encouraged the use of fertilizers and improved seed. Wheat production grew substantially during the 1990s, though Iran remained dependent on imports in some years, and weather conditions, particularly droughts, strongly influenced harvests. Agricultural growth also depended on water, and groundwater extraction through wells increased, with long-term consequences for aquifers that later chapters address.

Rural electrification advanced rapidly, with a large proportion of villages connected to the grid by the end of the decade. Road construction reduced travel time to towns, markets, and clinics, and rural schools and health facilities expanded. The system of rural health houses (khaneh-ye behdasht), staffed by local health workers, provided basic care, vaccination, and family-planning services, and is discussed further in Section 99.11. These services contributed to measurable improvements in rural health and literacy indicators.

Rural society also experienced pressure. Mechanization and the consolidation of landholdings reduced labor demands in some areas, and young people left villages for towns and cities, seeking education and jobs. Urban growth in the 1980s and 1990s was driven in part by this migration, though natural increase within cities and administrative reclassification of settlements as urban also mattered. The shrinkage of village populations in some regions, the aging of rural communities, and the dependence of villages on remittances and public services are features of the period. Pastoral and nomadic populations, including Qashqai, Bakhtiari, Lur, Kurdish, and other tribal groups, also faced changing conditions, as pastures, migration routes, and settlement patterns were affected by state policies, market changes, and drought.

Reconstruction in the war-damaged provinces had its own character. In Khuzestan, the rebuilding of Khorramshahr and Abadan, the restoration of oil and port facilities, and the repair of agricultural and irrigation systems along the Karun and other rivers were major tasks, and the region, with its large Arab population as well as Persian, Lur, and Bakhtiari communities, was strategically and economically vital. In the western border provinces of Kermanshah, Ilam, and Kurdistan, communities that were largely Kurdish and Lur had suffered from fighting, displacement, and, in some cases, chemical attacks and mines, and the pace of reconstruction was a matter of local concern. Reports and memoirs from these regions express both appreciation for restored services and complaints that investment was slower than in central regions.

Regional inequality persisted and in some respects intensified. Tehran and a few other metropolitan areas attracted the bulk of private investment, industrial growth, and commercial activity. Peripheral provinces, including Sistan and Baluchestan in the southeast, parts of Kurdistan and the northwest, and poorer districts of Khuzestan, had lower incomes, weaker services, and higher unemployment than the national average, even as their absolute conditions improved. Baluch and Sunni communities, Kurdish communities, Arab communities in Khuzestan, and Azerbaijani and Turkmen communities experienced these disparities in different ways, shaped by geography, language, religion, and relationships with central authorities. Some of these communities also expressed concern about cultural and political recognition, which intersected with their economic grievances. Historians should avoid reducing these experiences to a single narrative of neglect or of integration.

Why did national infrastructure not eliminate regional inequality? Several reasons are apparent. Investment followed existing concentrations of population, industry, and markets, which favored established centers. The cost of providing services to dispersed or mountainous areas was high. Private investment, attracted by agglomeration economies, concentrated in a few places. Political influence and administrative capacity were unevenly distributed, and provincial elites had unequal access to the central government. And national policies that expanded education and health reduced some disparities in services while leaving gaps in income and employment. Better roads could even accelerate out-migration by making it easier to leave.

Section 99.11

Population Policy, Healthcare, Education, and Demographic Change

One of the most consequential social changes of the reconstruction era was a rapid decline in fertility. In the early years after the revolution, official rhetoric had celebrated large families and the government had dismantled the family-planning program of the monarchy, in part because of religious and ideological objections to it and in part because the war made population growth seem to be a source of strength. During the 1980s, population growth rates were among the highest in the world, exceeding three percent annually in some estimates, and the number of children entering schools and later the labor market rose steeply. By the end of the war, many officials, including some religious authorities, had come to regard this growth as a threat to development.

In 1989, as reconstruction began, the government reversed course and launched a national family-planning program. The reversal followed discussion among health officials, planners, and religious scholars, and it relied on the endorsement of senior clerics, including the Supreme Leader, who judged contraception to be permissible within Islamic law under appropriate conditions. Policy was articulated through resolutions of government bodies and, in 1993, through legislation that, among other measures, limited certain public benefits for children beyond a specified number. Public campaigns through media, health workers, and religious institutions promoted smaller families and birth spacing. Contraceptives were provided through public clinics and rural health houses at low or no cost, and vasectomy and tubal ligation were offered as well. Premarital counseling on family planning was later made a requirement for marriage licenses.

The delivery system rested on the network of primary health care established in the 1980s, particularly the rural health houses, small facilities staffed by locally recruited health workers known as behvarz, who were trained to provide basic services, record vital information, and encourage vaccination and family planning. The network reached many villages that had previously lacked any health service and is widely credited with contributing to declines in infant and child mortality as well as fertility. Women health workers and volunteers played a significant role in reaching households.

The decline in fertility was striking. The total fertility rate, which had been well above six births per woman in the mid-1980s, fell to under three by the time of the 1996 census and continued to fall, reaching about replacement level around the turn of the century (estimates vary by method). The population, which numbered about 49 million in the 1986 census, was around 60 million in 1996, and the annual growth rate fell sharply between the two censuses. The pace of change meant that the age structure shifted: the large cohorts born in the early 1980s moved into schools and, later, universities and labor markets, while the cohorts born after the mid-1990s were considerably smaller.

It would be inaccurate to attribute this change to presidential policy alone. Demographers point to several interacting factors. Education, especially female education, had been expanding for decades, and educated women tended to marry later and to want fewer children. Urbanization changed the costs and benefits of children, raising the costs of housing and education and reducing the economic value of child labor. Infant mortality had declined, reducing the need for large families as insurance. Economic pressures during the war and reconstruction years, including housing costs and inflation, influenced household decisions. The spread of mass media and changing expectations about family life also mattered. The family-planning program reduced obstacles to implementing these preferences by supplying information and services and by providing religious legitimacy, and it probably accelerated a transition that was already beginning, but it did not create the underlying demand. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of policy and social change, and the relationship between the two is difficult to disentangle statistically.

Education expanded in parallel. Literacy among those aged six and over rose from roughly three-fifths in 1986 to roughly four-fifths in 1996, with the largest gains among women and in rural areas (census-based figures; see the demographic study below). Primary enrollment approached universality, secondary enrollment rose, and the number of university students increased substantially, with the establishment of new public universities, the expansion of the Islamic Azad University, and the growth of distance and vocational education. Women's enrollment in higher education grew rapidly, and by the late 1990s women made up a majority of university entrants in many fields, a development with major implications for the later chapters of this volume.

Healthcare improved as well. Life expectancy rose, infant and child mortality fell, vaccination coverage expanded, and the number of hospitals and physicians grew. Gains were uneven, with better indicators in cities and in provinces with more resources, and with continuing challenges in rural and peripheral regions. The government's commitment to primary care, its use of community health workers, and its expansion of medical education were important contributors, and international health organizations cited the Iranian experience as an example of effective primary care in a developing country.

The demographic change had long-term consequences that unfolded after 1997. The large cohorts born in the 1980s created pressure for jobs and housing in the late 1990s and 2000s, while falling fertility reduced the dependency burden and, later, would raise questions about population aging. These consequences are treated in Chapter 106, and this chapter only introduces the origins of the transition.

Section 99.12

Urbanization, Consumer Society, Labor, and Social Inequality

By the mid-1990s a majority of Iranians lived in cities, and the urban experience of reconstruction was marked by contrasts. Tehran, a metropolitan region of many millions, grew through migration and natural increase, and other cities, including Mashhad, Isfahan, Tabriz, Shiraz, and Ahvaz, expanded as well. Rapid urban growth strained housing, transport, and services, and the government and municipalities devoted substantial resources to addressing these pressures. [Sources: Asef Bayat.]

The most prominent example is Tehran, where Gholamhossein Karbaschi, who served as mayor from 1989, pursued an ambitious program of urban development. The municipality built highways and interchanges, expanded parks and green spaces, constructed cultural centers and libraries, and financed these projects partly by selling building-density permits and municipal land, an approach that made the municipality financially autonomous from the central government. It also established the newspaper Hamshahri in 1992, which became widely read. Supporters regarded the program as a model of effective local government that improved quality of life; critics objected to the sale of density permits, which they associated with speculation and overbuilding, to the concentration of resources in wealthier districts, and to the municipality's political ambitions. Karbaschi was later tried and convicted on corruption-related charges in 1998, an episode examined in Chapter 100.

An expanding urban middle class, composed of civil servants, teachers, engineers, professionals, shopkeepers, and managers, was a principal beneficiary of reconstruction. Education and public employment offered pathways to professional status, and rising incomes in the early 1990s supported consumption of appliances, automobiles, and housing. Commercial activity grew, with new shopping centers, imported goods, and expanding services. Yet this middle-class prosperity was fragile. Inflation, rising housing costs, and the reversal of liberalization in the mid-1990s eroded purchasing power, and many households relied on second jobs or family networks.

Housing was a central concern. The cost of land and construction rose, and many low-income families could not afford formal housing. Informal settlements and illegal construction expanded on the edges of cities, including in the cities of Tehran province such as Islamshahr, which attracted rural migrants and families displaced from other places, while some middle-income families became owners of apartments in new complexes. The municipal and national governments tried to regulate informal settlement, to supply services, and sometimes to demolish unauthorized buildings, creating conflicts.

Labor conditions were uneven. Industrial workers in large state and foundation enterprises enjoyed some employment security and benefits, while workers in small private workshops and in the informal sector faced insecurity and low wages. Labor law and the system of Islamic labor councils, established after the revolution, provided some representation, though independent unions were restricted. The expansion of temporary contracts and subcontracting, the pressure of inflation, and delays in wage payments generated labor disputes. Women's participation in the formal labor force remained low relative to their educational attainment, though women worked in education, health, public administration, and increasingly in professional occupations, and many worked informally.

Urban dissatisfaction occasionally erupted into unrest. Riots occurred in Shiraz and Arak in 1991, and in Mashhad in 1992, where disputes over housing, municipal policies, and demolition of informal settlements escalated into violent confrontations with security forces. In April 1995, residents of Islamshahr, on the southern edge of Tehran, protested against increases in fares and the lack of services, and the unrest, which involved clashes and arrests, was suppressed. In 1994 there was unrest in Qazvin. The government characterized the events as disturbances instigated by troublemakers, while participants and observers pointed to economic grievances, and the incidents revealed the vulnerability of low-income urban communities to inflation and displacement. The state's response combined repression with some concessions in services. The disputed numbers of casualties and arrests in these events should be treated cautiously, as sources are limited and contradictory.

Perceptions of corruption and unequal access reinforced the sense of injustice. Many Iranians believed that people with connections to the state obtained credit, contracts, and foreign exchange on privileged terms, and that a new class of wealthy individuals had emerged while ordinary households struggled. Press commentary, popular jokes, and political rhetoric reflected these perceptions. Historians can document specific cases of mismanagement and prosecutions, and can analyze structural features such as the allocation of credit and foreign exchange, but measuring the overall extent of corruption is difficult, and the evidence is often anecdotal.

Differences of class, gender, and generation shaped how reconstruction was experienced. Educated young women entered universities and the professions in growing numbers but faced legal and social constraints and limited employment opportunities. Younger Iranians, born around or after the revolution, had no memory of the monarchy and a different relationship to the war than their parents, and their expectations about education, employment, consumption, and personal freedom increasingly diverged from the official culture. Older workers and farmers evaluated reconstruction in terms of security and services. These differences meant that reconstruction produced a society that was more educated and more connected, yet also more aware of inequality and restriction.

Section 99.13

Diplomatic Pragmatism after the Iran–Iraq War

The foreign policy of the Rafsanjani years is often summarized as pragmatic. The label is useful but requires definition. Diplomatic pragmatism here means the pursuit of practical objectives, such as foreign investment, technology, trade, regional stability, and reduced isolation, through adjustments in diplomatic method and tone, without abandoning the Islamic Republic's ideological commitments or its institutional structure. It was a change in emphasis and in tactics, not a repudiation of revolutionary principles, and it coexisted with continuing rhetorical opposition to Israel, to American policy in the region, and to the existing international order as the Republic defined it.

The objectives were closely tied to reconstruction. Iran needed imported machinery and technology, credit, and markets for non-oil exports. It also needed stability on its frontiers so that resources could be diverted from defense to development, and it needed to reduce the diplomatic isolation that had accompanied the revolution, the hostage crisis, and the war. Officials argued that normal relations with neighbors and major trading partners would serve national interests and reduce the risks of renewed conflict. At the same time, the government sought to preserve its independence and to maintain the ideological identity that distinguished it from other states in the region.

Authority over foreign policy was divided. The Constitution assigns the Supreme Leader responsibility for determining the general policies of the system and for command of the armed forces, and the president, as head of the executive, is responsible for implementing policy through the Foreign Ministry and other organs. The Supreme National Security Council, which includes the president, military commanders, and senior officials, coordinates defense and security policy, and its decisions require the Leader's approval. In practice, foreign policy was shaped by negotiation among the president, the Leader, the foreign minister, the Council, and other institutions, including the Revolutionary Guards and the intelligence services, which pursued their own activities abroad. This fragmentation meant that the same state could pursue conciliatory diplomacy through the Foreign Ministry while other organs engaged in activities that provoked alarm in other countries.

Ali Akbar Velayati, who served as foreign minister throughout the Rafsanjani presidency, was a central figure. A physician by training who had held the post since 1981, he combined experience with close relations with the Leader and the president, and he became the principal public voice of Iranian diplomacy. He emphasized the pursuit of national interests, negotiated with regional counterparts, and defended the government's positions in international forums. His continuity in office reflects both the stability of foreign-policy leadership and its dependence on the Leader's confidence.

Pragmatism was visible in several areas: in the relatively restrained response to the 1991 Gulf War, in the improvement of relations with Gulf monarchies and with Turkey, in the willingness to cooperate with the Soviet successor states, in the cultivation of European trade partners, and in the expansion of commercial diplomacy. It was also visible in the attention given to economic instruments, such as trade agreements and credit arrangements. But pragmatism had limits. Iran continued to support groups such as Lebanese Hezbollah, maintained hostility to the Arab–Israeli peace process that began in Madrid in 1991 and produced the Oslo accords in 1993, and was accused by several governments of involvement in the assassination of dissidents abroad. These activities, and the hostility of the United States, constrained the benefits that pragmatic diplomacy could obtain.

It is also important to distinguish between pragmatism in methods and the preservation of ideological commitments. Officials often described their approach as realistic and based on national interest, and they invoked both Islamic principles and the constitutional foreign-policy principles of independence, rejection of domination, and defense of Muslims. Different actors emphasized different elements. Critics of the pragmatic approach, including some conservatives, saw it as compromising principle, while critics from other directions saw it as insufficient to end isolation. The interaction between these views produced a policy that was neither fully accommodating nor fully confrontational.

Section 99.14

Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and the Persian Gulf after 1990

The most immediate foreign-policy issue after the war was relations with Iraq. The ceasefire of 1988 had not produced a peace treaty, and negotiations under United Nations auspices dragged on. Disputes concerned the exchange of prisoners, the withdrawal of forces to recognized borders, navigation in the Shatt al-Arab, and responsibility for the war. Iraq continued to occupy some Iranian territory, and its government under Saddam Hussein used the negotiations to maintain leverage. Iran, for its part, demanded recognition of Iraq's responsibility for initiating the conflict and compensation, which Iraq refused. The impasse persisted until the events of 1990 changed the context.

Iraq's invasion of Kuwait on August 2, 1990, transformed the situation. Facing a coalition formed against it by the United States and other powers, Saddam Hussein sought to secure his eastern frontier. In letters and statements in the months after the invasion, he accepted the 1975 Algiers Accord, which he had repudiated in 1980, and agreed to withdraw from remaining Iranian territory and to complete the exchange of prisoners. Iran welcomed these steps, which fulfilled some of its demands, though it did not receive recognition of responsibility for the war. The prisoner exchanges of 1990 returned most Iranian captives, though the fate of some missing persons and the return of remains remained issues for years.

Iran's position in the Gulf crisis of 1990–1991 was a careful one. The government condemned the invasion of Kuwait and called for Iraqi withdrawal, but it also opposed the presence of large Western forces in the Gulf, and it refused to join the American-led coalition. It declared neutrality, observed the embargo on Iraq to a significant degree, and benefited in some ways from the war, as oil prices rose temporarily and Iraq's military power was reduced. During the conflict, Iraqi aircraft flew to Iran to escape destruction, and Iran retained many of them, a matter of later dispute. After the war, Iran received large numbers of Kurdish and Shi'i refugees fleeing Iraq in the spring of 1991, after uprisings that the Iraqi government suppressed, placing a heavy burden on its border provinces.

The Gulf War also affected relations with the Arab monarchies of the Gulf. Iran and Saudi Arabia had broken diplomatic relations in 1988 after the 1987 incidents during the Hajj in Mecca, in which hundreds of pilgrims, many of them Iranian, died in clashes with Saudi security forces. In 1991 the two governments restored relations, and Iranian pilgrims returned to the Hajj under negotiated arrangements. The reconciliation reflected the interests of both: Saudi Arabia sought to reassure itself about its eastern neighbor in the aftermath of the Iraqi invasion, and Iran sought to reduce hostility and secure access to the pilgrimage. Relations with Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, Qatar, and Bahrain also improved to varying degrees, though suspicion remained, particularly regarding Iran's military capabilities and ties to Shi'i communities.

Territorial and political disagreements persisted. The most visible was the dispute with the United Arab Emirates over the islands of Abu Musa and the Greater and Lesser Tunbs, which Iran had occupied in 1971 as the British withdrew from the Gulf. In 1992, tensions over the administration of Abu Musa brought the dispute back to prominence, and it remained unresolved. Iran also developed its naval and missile forces in this period and acquired weapons from Russia and China, which concerned neighbors and the United States. Security in the Gulf was now structured by an enlarged American military presence in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and elsewhere, which the Iranian government criticized as a threat.

Maritime commerce was a central interest. The Persian Gulf carried Iran's oil exports and imports, and the Strait of Hormuz was the main channel for both. The restoration of safe navigation after the end of the tanker war was important for trade, and the expansion of ports such as Bandar Abbas supported this. The Gulf was also a region of economic interdependence, with Dubai in particular emerging as a major reexport hub for Iranian trade, a role that benefited both sides and illustrated how commerce could continue despite political tension.

Iran's approach during these years illustrates the pursuit of national interests without formal alignment with the United States and without ideological confrontation of the coalition. It was neither a rapprochement with the West nor a reversion to revolutionary isolation. It was a policy of calculated opportunism: reducing hostilities with neighbors while keeping options open, and using the crisis to improve Iran's relative position.

Section 99.15

Russia, Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the End of the Cold War

The dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991 removed a superpower from Iran's northern frontier and created a cluster of new states: Russia, Azerbaijan, Armenia, Georgia, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan. For Iran this was an opportunity and a risk. It opened markets, transit routes, and energy possibilities and reduced the threat of a large neighboring power, but it also raised the prospect of instability, of competition with Turkey and others for influence, and of ethnic and religious conflicts near Iran's borders.

Relations with Russia were pragmatic and mutually beneficial. Russia, seeking customers for its arms and nuclear technology and partners in a region of concern, developed cooperation with Iran in several fields. Agreements covered the sale of conventional weapons, the construction of nuclear facilities at Bushehr under a contract concluded in 1995, and trade. These links were criticized by the United States, but both governments regarded them as in their interest. The Russian government also coordinated with Iran on aspects of Caucasus and Central Asian diplomacy, and both opposed a Western or Turkish dominance in the region. Nuclear questions are treated in later chapters, and mention here is limited to the fact that the Bushehr arrangement formed part of Iran's relations with Russia.

In Central Asia, Iran sought economic and cultural ties. It established embassies, signed trade and transit agreements, and offered access to its ports and roads for landlocked states. The Economic Cooperation Organization, originally founded in 1985 by Iran, Pakistan, and Turkey, was enlarged in 1992 to include Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, and the five Central Asian republics, offering a framework for cooperation, though its practical achievements were limited. The railway link between Mashhad and Turkmenistan, opened in 1996, and plans for further transport and pipeline connections represented concrete efforts to connect Iran's economy to the north.

Caspian Sea interests were another area of activity. The disappearance of the Soviet Union ended the condominium by which Iran and the Soviet Union had managed the sea under treaties of 1921 and 1940, and the new littoral states, Russia, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan, along with Iran, contested the legal regime that should govern the sea's waters, seabed, and resources. Iran favored a division that would ensure it a substantial share or joint management, while others favored national sectors. The dispute, involving energy development and pipeline routes, remained unresolved for years.

The Caucasus presented complicated choices. Azerbaijan, whose population is predominantly Shi'i Muslim and Turkic-speaking, and which shares a long border and cultural ties with Iran's Azerbaijani population, might have seemed a natural partner. But the government in Baku, especially under the nationalist Popular Front in 1992–1993, pursued close ties with Turkey and expressed ambitions regarding the Azerbaijanis of Iran, which alarmed Tehran. Iran was also concerned about the war between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh, which sent refugees across the Iranian border and risked drawing in neighboring states. Iran adopted a policy of mediation and neutrality that, in practice, led to better relations with Armenia than many observers expected, while maintaining economic ties with Azerbaijan. Explanations of this stance cite concern about separatism and about Turkish and American influence, as well as practical economic interests, rather than religious or linguistic affinity.

The civil war in Tajikistan, which began in 1992 and involved the Tajik government and an opposition that included Islamist groups, illustrated the complexity. Tajikistan's population speaks a Persian dialect, which gave Iran a cultural connection, and Iran hosted talks and supported negotiation among the parties, working alongside Russia and the United Nations. A settlement was eventually reached in 1997. Iran's approach in Tajikistan emphasized stability and mediation over ideological export, and it kept its relations with Moscow, which had a major interest in the outcome.

To the east, Afghanistan posed a different problem. After the withdrawal of Soviet forces in 1989 and the fall of the Soviet-supported government in 1992, the country fell into civil war, and Iran supported Shi'i and Persian-speaking groups while hosting large numbers of Afghan refugees. The rise of the Taliban, a Sunni movement that took Kabul in September 1996, alarmed Iran, which regarded it as hostile, backed by Pakistan and Saudi Arabia, and a threat to the Shi'i and Persian-speaking communities of Afghanistan. Relations deteriorated sharply, culminating in the crisis of 1998, which falls outside this chapter.

These cases show that religious and linguistic affinities did not determine Iranian foreign policy. Iran cooperated with Christian Armenia and Orthodox Russia, mediated rather than backed Islamist movements in Tajikistan, and stayed cautious toward Shi'i Azerbaijan. The government acted according to a mix of economic interest, security concerns, competition with Turkey and others, and, secondarily, cultural connections. Economic results were modest, since the new states had limited resources and were often oriented toward Russia, and Iran's trade with them remained small relative to its other partners.

Section 99.16

Europe, the United States, Sanctions, and the Limits of Normalization

Europe was Iran's principal source of technology, capital goods, and credit, and European governments had an interest in trade with a large market. Relations had been damaged by the revolution, the hostage crisis, and, in 1989, by the fatwa issued by Khomeini against the author Salman Rushdie, which prompted several European governments to withdraw ambassadors. Over the following years, diplomatic ties were restored, and in December 1992 the European Council, meeting in Edinburgh, endorsed a policy known as "critical dialogue," which combined engagement and trade with discussion of European concerns, including human rights, terrorism, the Rushdie fatwa, and weapons proliferation. Germany was the main advocate, as it was Iran's leading European trading partner. Iranian officials welcomed the approach because it avoided isolation and kept commercial doors open.

Critical dialogue came under increasing strain. European governments were concerned by reports of Iranian involvement in the killing of dissidents abroad, including the 1991 murder of Shapour Bakhtiar, the last prime minister of the monarchy, near Paris, and the September 1992 assassination of Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran leaders at the Mykonos restaurant in Berlin. The Mykonos trial in Germany lasted several years. On April 10, 1997, the Berlin court issued its verdict, convicting Iranian and Lebanese defendants and stating that the order for the killings had come from the top level of the Iranian political leadership, including the Minister of Intelligence. The Iranian government rejected the finding. European Union states recalled their ambassadors from Tehran, and the policy of critical dialogue effectively ended, though trade continued and a different approach followed after Khatami's election. The case, which concerns responsibility for political violence, should be presented with care: the court's findings and the Iranian government's denials are both matters of record, and the verdict was a judicial finding in a specific proceeding.

With the United States, hostility persisted. The governments had no diplomatic relations after 1980, and the legacy of the hostage crisis, Iran's support for groups regarded by Washington as terrorist organizations, its opposition to the Arab–Israeli peace process, and its pursuit of advanced weapons shaped American policy. After the Gulf War, the Clinton administration announced in 1993 a policy of dual containment, which sought to contain both Iran and Iraq without relying on one to balance the other, as earlier policies had sometimes done. Its aim was to isolate Iran diplomatically and economically and to restrict its access to arms and technology.

This policy produced concrete legal measures. In March 1995, President Clinton issued Executive Order 12957, prohibiting American involvement in the development of Iranian petroleum resources, a step prompted by the announcement of the Conoco contract. In May 1995, Executive Order 12959 expanded the restrictions into a broad prohibition of trade and investment by American persons with Iran, with limited exceptions. Iranian officials were aware that these steps would harm their efforts to attract investment, and they protested. Congress then adopted the Iran and Libya Sanctions Act, signed by the President in August 1996, which targeted non-American firms that made significant investments in the energy sectors of Iran and Libya, a measure that raised objections from allies, who regarded it as an extraterritorial application of American law, and prompted countermeasures in the European Union. The provisions of ILSA are described in the source window below.

The sanctions affected reconstruction. They made foreign investment in petroleum and gas more difficult and politically risky, complicated financing, and encouraged Iran to rely on suppliers and partners in Europe, Russia, and Asia. However, the effects were limited by several factors. American companies had earlier traded with Iran, notably through intermediaries, and some European and Asian companies continued business, while the legislation's implementation was uneven. Iran's financial problems in the mid-1990s were primarily the result of domestic and market factors, such as debt and exchange-rate policy, rather than sanctions alone. Historians generally assess the sanctions as an additional constraint that deepened existing difficulties rather than as the sole cause of crisis, and the extent of their impact is debated. The later, more comprehensive sanctions regime and the nuclear negotiations belong to subsequent chapters (Chapter 103).

Finally, human-rights controversies and Iran's regional policies affected its international standing. Reports by international organizations about executions, restrictions on expression, and the treatment of minorities, including the Baha'is, were cited by critics, and Iranian officials denied or contested many of them. Concerns about Iran's support for Hezbollah and for Palestinian groups opposed to the peace process also shaped European and American attitudes. The result was a limited normalization: Iran maintained diplomatic and economic relations with many countries and was no longer as isolated as during the war, but it did not achieve the full acceptance sought by reconstruction planners.

The distinction between engagement and normalization is the key analytical point. Diplomatic engagement meant that governments talked, traded, and cooperated on certain issues. Normalization would have meant the removal of major political and economic obstacles and the integration of Iran into international finance and investment on ordinary terms. Engagement was achieved with many countries; normalization was not, and the gap constrained the economic strategy on which reconstruction depended.

Section 99.17

Rafsanjani, Khamenei, and the Distribution of Political Authority

The constitutional order that governed Rafsanjani's presidency had been settled in 1989 and is described in Chapters 93 and 98. Understanding how it worked requires distinguishing administrative authority, the capacity to run ministries, prepare budgets, and implement laws, from ultimate constitutional authority, the capacity to determine the general direction of the system and to command its coercive institutions. The president held the first in large measure and the second only marginally.

The presidency, as revised, was the head of the executive branch, responsible for the cabinet and the administration, for the budget and plan, and for the conduct of daily policy. With the abolition of the prime ministership, the president no longer shared executive power with a separate head of government, and Rafsanjani's office was institutionally stronger than that of his predecessor. He also benefited from the practical authority of a leader who had served as Speaker, an organizer of the war effort, and a founder of the Republic.

The Supreme Leader held the commanding position. The Leader determines general policies in consultation with the Expediency Council, supervises their implementation, commands the armed forces, appoints the heads of the judiciary and of state broadcasting, appoints the commanders of the army and the Revolutionary Guards, appoints half of the members of the Guardian Council (the jurists), and exercises authority over the foundations. Khamenei's religious credentials were initially weaker than Khomeini's, and in the early 1990s the question of whether the Leader should be a source of emulation (marja') in the ordinary sense was debated. He consolidated his position by building relations with the clergy, the security institutions, and conservative political networks.

Other institutions mattered as well. The Majles, whose members were elected, passed laws and approved budgets and plans, and its conservative majority after 1992 gave it the capacity to resist or modify the president's proposals. The Guardian Council reviewed legislation for compatibility with Islamic law and the Constitution and supervised elections, including the screening of candidates, which made it a powerful gatekeeper. The Expediency Council, composed of appointed members, arbitrated disputes between the Majles and the Guardian Council and advised the Leader. The judiciary, headed by a Leader's appointee, controlled courts and prosecutions, and the revolutionary courts and security organs had their own authority. The Revolutionary Guards and the Basij operated within the Leader’s military command structure; the Ministry of Intelligence was formally part of the executive, although the Leader exerted substantial influence over security policy, while major revolutionary foundations, as noted in Section 99.7, held assets and influence outside ordinary ministerial control. [Sources: Constitution of Iran.]

Cooperation and disagreement between Rafsanjani and Khamenei shaped the period. They collaborated in the 1989 transition and on many aspects of reconstruction, and the Leader generally endorsed the government's economic program, including the development plans. Differences emerged concerning cultural policy, with the Leader and many conservatives favoring stricter controls on media and public expression than Rafsanjani, who tolerated somewhat greater flexibility; concerning foreign policy, including the pace of engagement with the West; and concerning the control of political appointments and the institutions of the revolution. These differences were not simply personal rivalry. They reflected differing institutional positions, constituencies, and views about how to balance revolutionary and developmental goals, and the two men also needed each other. Rafsanjani needed the Leader's authority to legitimize policies; the Leader needed the president's capacity to run the state and manage the economy.

The result was a system of shared and contested authority. A decision to liberalize prices or foreign exchange required not only presidential approval but the acquiescence of the Majles and, in practice, the Leader and powerful interest groups. A cultural policy might be set by the president's ministry but constrained by the judiciary or by vigilante groups. This dispersal of authority gave the system a degree of resilience, because no single actor could dominate, but it also meant that responsibility for failures was diffuse, and that reform proposals could be blocked at several points.

Section 99.18

Elections, Political Factions, and the Executives of Construction

Elections in the Islamic Republic were competitive in some respects and restricted in others. Voters chose among candidates who had been approved by the Guardian Council, which screened for qualifications including commitment to the Constitution and to Islam, and campaigns took place under limits on organization and expression. Competition occurred among political currents, but also within a framework that excluded opponents of the system. Political factions were networks of politicians, clerics, journalists, and associations rather than parties in the conventional Western sense: they had loose structures, overlapping membership, and shifting alliances, and they often lacked formal organization or consistent platforms. Parties as such existed but were weak, and in many elections candidates ran as individuals endorsed by lists.

The 1992 parliamentary election, the first held after the death of Khomeini, illustrated these dynamics. The Guardian Council disqualified a large number of candidates, particularly from among the Islamic left, who had held considerable influence in the Majles of the 1980s and had criticized aspects of the government's economic policy. The elections produced a Majles dominated by conservatives and by supporters of the president, with the left considerably reduced. Ali Akbar Nateq-Nouri became Speaker, a position he held through the mid-1990s. The reduced presence of the left changed the political balance: it weakened internal criticism of liberalization from the redistributive side, but it also left Rafsanjani dependent on a conservative majority that did not share all his priorities.

The 1993 presidential election, held in June, returned Rafsanjani for a second term, but with a smaller share of the vote and lower turnout than in 1989. He received roughly three-fifths of the votes cast, defeating three other candidates, including Ahmad Tavakkoli, who criticized the government’s economic management and received 4,026,879 votes (23.97% of ballots cast in the Iran Data Portal compilation). Turnout, at about 50.7 percent, according to the Iran Data Portal, was lower than in 1989, and the result was widely read as an indication of a decline in enthusiasm. Some voters were dissatisfied with the economy; some regarded the contest as uncompetitive; and others may have been affected by the limitations on candidates. Turnout alone should not be taken as proof of support or opposition to the system as a whole. [Sources: Iran Data Portal.]

By the mid-1990s, supporters of Rafsanjani sought to organize themselves for the 1996 parliamentary elections. In early 1996 a group of senior officials, ministers, and associates, including figures close to the president and to the mayor of Tehran, announced the formation of the Executives of Construction (Kargozaran-e Sazandegi). The organization represented the technocratic and developmental constituency, and it sought to defend and continue the reconstruction agenda, to present candidates for the Majles, and to broaden the base of support. It included members of the president's family, among them Faezeh Hashemi, who ran for the Majles and, in Tehran, received a very large number of votes, a result that made her one of the most visible women in Iranian politics.

The 1996 parliamentary election revealed shifting alliances. Conservatives, organized around the Society of Combatant Clergy and other groups, remained the largest force, but the Executives of Construction and their allies won a substantial number of seats, especially in Tehran, and the Islamic left, which had been marginalized, began to reposition itself, ultimately moving toward a more critical stance on political issues. The result was a Majles in which the balance was more complex than before, and the conservative speaker, Nateq-Nouri, remained in office. The election also demonstrated that technocratic politicians could attract voters, at least in the capital, and that the political landscape was becoming more diverse.

Rafsanjani himself could not seek a third consecutive term under the Constitution, and there was some discussion among supporters of amending it to allow him to do so, an idea he did not pursue. The 1997 election therefore became an open contest in which Nateq-Nouri, supported by a broad conservative coalition, was widely regarded as the likely winner. The surprising victory of Mohammad Khatami on May 23, 1997, with turnout of about 79.9 percent, is analyzed in Chapter 100. Here it is sufficient to note that the 1997 turnout was much higher than in 1989 and 1993, and that the election drew on dissatisfactions and aspirations that had developed during the reconstruction years, as well as on the activity of different political currents.

Students should be cautious in interpreting these electoral developments. The restricted candidate field, the role of the Guardian Council, and the limited party structure mean that election outcomes reflected the preferences of voters among options available, not a full expression of public opinion. At the same time, elections did matter, they produced real shifts in the composition of the Majles and in the political balance, and they were occasions for debate about the direction of the country.

Section 99.19

Cultural Change, Political Restrictions, and the Demand for Reform

Reconstruction changed Iranian society in ways that did not fit the boundaries of the political system. The expansion of education created a larger and more educated population. Universities and professional schools admitted more students each year, and the number of graduates grew faster than the economy's capacity to employ them. Young Iranians, many of them born after the revolution, were more urban, more literate, and more exposed to information from abroad than their parents. They shared aspirations for jobs, housing, and personal freedom, and some were skeptical of the revolutionary slogans that had mobilized earlier generations.

Women's participation in education and public life expanded markedly. Female literacy rose, girls' enrollment reached parity with boys' in many levels, and women entered universities in growing numbers and worked as teachers, physicians, engineers, and civil servants. Women also served in the Majles, and figures such as Faezeh Hashemi advocated for women's sport and wider participation. At the same time, legal restrictions on women, including family-law provisions on marriage, divorce, and custody, restrictions on dress, and limits on certain occupations and public roles, persisted and generated debate. Women's organizations and journals discussed these issues, and religious thinkers offered competing interpretations of women's rights in Islam.

Intellectual life diversified. Newspapers and periodicals proliferated, including publications of the municipal and cultural establishments, and a number of journals provided forums for discussion of politics, religion, philosophy, and the arts. Among the most influential voices was Abdolkarim Soroush, a philosopher whose lectures and essays, including works on the historical and interpretive character of religious knowledge, argued that human understanding of religion is evolving and fallible. His ideas, published in journals such as Kiyan, attracted a large audience among students and intellectuals and provoked strong opposition from conservative clerics, who accused him of undermining religious authority. Other religious intellectuals, as well as secular writers and filmmakers, took part in discussions about the nature of Islamic governance, democracy, civil society, and the relationship between religion and politics. Mohammad Khatami, who had served as Minister of Culture and Islamic Guidance until his resignation in 1992, was associated with a more tolerant cultural policy, and his departure was seen as a victory for conservatives.

Restrictions remained substantial. Censorship by the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance and by the judiciary affected books, newspapers, films, and music. In 1994 parliamentary efforts to restrict satellite dishes began; the ban was enacted and implemented through measures spanning 1994–1995 and was difficult to enforce. It reflected concerns about foreign cultural influence. Vigilante groups, such as Ansar-e Hezbollah, intimidated opponents and disrupted gatherings, and their relationship to official institutions was a matter of controversy. On October 25, 1994, 134 writers signed an open letter calling for an end to censorship and defending freedom of expression, an event that became a landmark in the history of Iranian intellectual dissent. Later, in 1996, prosecutions and detentions of writers and journalists, including the editor Faraj Sarkuhi, drew international attention. [Sources: UN E/CN.4/1995/55; U.S. Department of State.]

Political repression and human-rights concerns also persisted. Opposition groups outside the system, such as the Mojahedin-e Khalq, leftist organizations, and ethnic and regional movements, were banned or suppressed, and the killing of dissidents abroad, as discussed in Section 99.16, damaged Iran's reputation. Religious minorities, including the Baha'is, whom the government did not recognize as a protected religion, faced restrictions and discrimination. The treatment of political prisoners and the use of the death penalty were criticized by international human-rights organizations and, increasingly, by Iranians themselves. Iranian officials rejected these criticisms and regarded them as politically motivated.

The relationship between these social changes and the later emergence of reformism needs careful treatment. There is a plausible connection: a larger educated population, expanded media, intellectual debate, and the experience of restrictions created potential support for political change. Yet correlation is not causation. The same social changes could have supported other outcomes, and many educated Iranians supported the system or were apolitical. The reform movement of 1997 depended on specific political developments, such as the exclusion of the Islamic left, its reconsideration of earlier positions, the candidacy of Khatami, and the failure of the conservative candidate to mobilize broad support, not merely on social trends. The next chapter examines how these elements came together.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Social Modernization under Political Restrictions

What Changed

  • More Iranians were literate, urban, and university-educated, and women were more visible in education and professions
  • Print culture and intellectual debate diversified despite censorship
  • Expectations about consumption, services, and personal freedom rose, particularly among the young

What Stayed the Same

  • The constitutional structure, including the authority of the Leader and the Guardian Council's screening role, was unchanged
  • Censorship, restrictions on political organization, and legal limits on women persisted
  • Security and judicial institutions retained broad powers

Pattern and pace. Social change was gradual and uneven, most visible among urban, educated groups; political change was minimal in formal structure until 1997. The difference between the two created tension, but it did not make the later political opening inevitable.

Section 99.20

The Achievements and Contradictions of the Reconstruction Era

An assessment of the Rafsanjani presidency requires judging it on several dimensions, and the results differ among them. The following paragraphs summarize them and then consider how the period ended.

Economic recovery. Reconstruction restored much of the productive capacity damaged or underused during the war. Oil production recovered, industrial plants resumed operation, and output expanded rapidly in the early 1990s. Whether this constituted development in the stronger sense of structural transformation is more doubtful, since the economy remained dependent on oil and imported inputs.

Infrastructure. Investment in transportation, energy, water, communications, housing, and public services was substantial and produced lasting assets. Access to electricity, roads, schools, and health care expanded widely, though unevenly across regions.

Liberalization. Economic liberalization was partial and uneven. Some controls were relaxed, and private activity expanded, but state and parastatal institutions retained dominant positions, and many reforms were reversed or diluted. Liberalization of the economy occurred without liberalization of politics.

Financial stability. The reconstruction model produced debt, arrears, currency depreciation, and high inflation by the mid-1990s, requiring rescheduling and the reimposition of controls. Rapid growth had not established financial stability.

Social development. Education, health care, and demographic indicators improved significantly, in part because of earlier and ongoing social policies as well as the government's actions in this period. The decline in fertility, the expansion of female education, and the spread of primary care were long-lasting achievements.

Distribution. Benefits were unequally distributed. Those with access to credit, contracts, and connections gained disproportionately, while many wage earners, fixed-income households, and peripheral regions experienced hardship. Perceptions of unfairness and corruption became politically salient.

Diplomacy. Iran reduced its isolation in the region and built economic and diplomatic ties with many states, but persistent hostility with the United States, controversies over political violence abroad and over regional policies, and sanctions limited the gains. Engagement was achieved; normalization was not.

Political institutions. The revolutionary constitutional order persisted, with the Leader, the Guardian Council, and security institutions retaining their positions. The presidency gained administrative power but remained subordinate to ultimate authority.

Rafsanjani completed two full terms, leaving office in August 1997. He did not retire from politics. He continued to hold senior positions, including leadership of the Expediency Council, where he remained influential for years, and he remained a central figure in Iranian politics. His relationship to later reformist and conservative movements, and to the Leader, continued to evolve.

The election of Mohammad Khatami on May 23, 1997, concluded the era. Khatami, a cleric and former minister who had left office in 1992, ran as an advocate of the rule of law, civil society, and expanded participation, and he won by a wide margin, with a very high turnout. Chapter 100 analyzes his campaign, his supporters, and the institutional struggles that followed. For present purposes, the election should be seen as the outcome of several converging developments, among them the contradictions described in this chapter, but not as their inevitable consequence.

Historical Debate

Was the Rafsanjani Presidency a Successful Developmental Transformation or an Incomplete Modernization Project?

The question: How should historians weigh the achievements of reconstruction against its financial instability, inequalities, and political limits?

Interpretation A — Successful transformation: Proponents emphasize the restoration of capacity, the expansion of infrastructure and services, the improvement in social indicators, and the stabilization of the state after war and revolution. They argue that the financial difficulties were largely the product of external shocks, falling oil prices, and sanctions, and that the foundations laid in these years supported later growth.
Interpretation B — Incomplete modernization: Critics emphasize the dependence on oil and imports, the failure to create a diversified, competitive economy, the unstable financing, the reversal of liberalization, and the persistence of rent-seeking and unequal access. They argue that economic modernization without political openness could not produce sustainable change.
Interpretation C — A mixed record shaped by structure: Others argue that the outcomes reflected structural features of an oil-dependent, state-centered economy and a fragmented political system, so that neither "success" nor "failure" captures the period.

Evidence: Economic indicators, plan documents, IMF assessments, census data, electoral records, and political memoirs. Disagreements concern the weight given to external versus domestic causes, the standard of comparison, and the interpretation of social change.

Why it matters: The judgment influences how historians understand the origins of reformism, the later populist reaction, and the long-term trajectory of the Islamic Republic.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 99 has examined how the Islamic Republic attempted, between 1989 and 1997, to convert a revolutionary wartime state into a developmental one. It began with the inheritance of war: damaged refineries, ports, housing, and towns; an economy managed through rationing, administered prices, and multiple exchange rates; a rapidly growing and young population; and the unfulfilled expectations of a revolution that had promised social justice. The chapter distinguished rebuilding what had been destroyed from expanding productive capacity, and both from transforming the political economy.

Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, a revolutionary insider who had helped end the war, assembled a coalition of technocrats, entrepreneurs, clerics, and administrators around the idea of sazandegi. The First Five-Year Plan combined high public investment, imported technology, foreign borrowing, and selective liberalization within a state-dominated economy. Technocratic government raised enduring questions about the relationship between expertise and revolutionary legitimacy, and historians continue to debate whether reconstruction departed from revolutionary ideals or adapted them.

Material reconstruction was substantial. Roads, railways, ports, power, water, communications, housing, industry, schools, and clinics were built or restored, though many projects spanned several administrations and benefited some regions and groups more than others. Oil financed the effort and also made it fragile. Buyback contracts reconciled foreign finance with constitutional limits on foreign ownership of resources, and sanctions later complicated their use.

Liberalization was partial. Privatization transferred some enterprises, but state, foundation, and other parastatal holders retained dominant positions, and a change of owner did not create competitive markets. Access to credit, licenses, and foreign exchange remained politically mediated, and new commercial and contracting networks emerged around the state.

The financing model proved unstable. Rapid growth in the early 1990s was accompanied by rising imports and short-term foreign borrowing. By 1993–1994 payment arrears, exchange-rate pressure, and inflation forced rescheduling and the reimposition of controls. The 1993 unification attempt failed, and the Second Plan emphasized stabilization. The period thus did not move linearly from a command economy to a market economy.

Society changed profoundly. Veterans, the bereaved, and displaced families entered civilian life through institutions that honored sacrifice but could not provide all the jobs and security they needed. Rural electrification, roads, health houses, and schools extended services, yet regional inequalities persisted, particularly in peripheral provinces. A renewed family-planning program coincided with a very rapid fall in fertility that also reflected education, urbanization, and household choices, and literacy and university enrollment, especially among women, rose markedly.

Urban life offered new amenities and new frustrations. Tehran's municipality built parks, highways, and cultural facilities, and an urban middle class expanded, while inflation, housing costs, informal settlement, and perceptions of corruption fueled discontent and, in several cities, unrest. Workers, entrepreneurs, professionals, and low-income households experienced reconstruction very differently.

Foreign policy sought to support reconstruction. Iran restored relations with Saudi Arabia, remained neutral in the Gulf War, pursued ties with Russia and the new states of Central Asia and the Caucasus, and engaged Europe through critical dialogue. Yet hostility with the United States, American executive orders and the Iran and Libya Sanctions Act, controversies over political violence abroad, and the 1997 Mykonos verdict limited normalization. Engagement and normalization were distinct outcomes.

Politically, the revolutionary constitutional order endured. The president gained administrative authority, but ultimate authority remained with the Supreme Leader, and the Guardian Council, the Majles, the judiciary, security institutions, and foundations constrained executive choices. Factions, not parties, competed within a screened electoral framework. Turnout fell in 1993 and rose sharply in 1997, but turnout alone cannot establish support for or opposition to the system.

The chapter's central conclusion is that Rafsanjani's presidency modernized the economy and expanded infrastructure and social services without liberalizing the political system, and that financial instability, uneven distribution, and political restriction created tensions that contributed to the conditions for the 1997 reformist opening. Those tensions were contributing conditions rather than determining causes, and the story continues in Chapter 100.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 99 matters because it shows how a revolutionary regime behaves when its founding emergency ends. The Islamic Republic did not collapse into either revolutionary stagnation or wholesale liberalization. It built roads, power stations, schools, and clinics, loosened some controls and restored others, and negotiated with neighbors while remaining in conflict with some great powers. The result was neither a simple success nor a simple failure.

The chapter also reveals a structural pattern: economic decisions in the Republic were made within a system of shared and contested authority in which oil income, state and quasi-state institutions, and political access shaped outcomes. That pattern did not begin or end with Rafsanjani, and later chapters will see it again in different forms under Khatami, Ahmadinejad, and Rouhani, and in the political economy of the Revolutionary Guards and foundations (Chapter 105).

Social change was the period's quieter achievement and its most lasting. Falling fertility, expanding education, rural services, and growing urbanization altered the country in ways that outlasted particular policies. They also produced a more educated and more demanding population, which later chapters on reform, protest, youth, and gender (Chapters 100, 102, 106, and 107) will treat in depth. Historians should be careful, however, not to read these developments as destiny.

The period also connects Iran to wider worlds: to a post-Cold War Gulf and Eurasia, to global oil markets, to European diplomacy, and to American sanctions policy. These links show that Iranian reconstruction was never simply domestic, and that Iranian agency operated within constraints set partly by others.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading disputed history: distinguishing recovery from stability, privatization from competition, engagement from normalization, and correlation from causation. Those distinctions will serve students in the chapters that follow, beginning with the reform era that Mohammad Khatami's election opened in 1997.
