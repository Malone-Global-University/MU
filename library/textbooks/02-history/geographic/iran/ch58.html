The Institutionalization of Twelver Shi‘ism

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

A royal proclamation can change what is said from a pulpit in a day. It cannot, by itself, produce teachers, judges, endowed colleges, trained preachers, shared habits of mourning, or the trust of a population. The history of Twelver Shi‘ism under the Safavids is the history of how those things were slowly assembled, by kings and scholars, by pilgrims and merchants, and by people who accepted, adapted, and sometimes resisted what was asked of them.

On the eastern side of the great square of Isfahan, the Maydan-i Naqsh-i Jahan, stands a mosque with no courtyard and no minarets. It is known as the mosque of Shaykh Lutfallah, and its construction, begun in 1602–1603 and completed in 1618–1619 under Shah Abbas I, was bound up with a person as much as a place. It was a private royal mosque rather than the public Friday congregational mosque or a madrasa. Lutfallah al-Maysi was a scholar from Jabal Amil, a hill country in what is now southern Lebanon, a region whose Twelver learned families had maintained a tradition of legal scholarship for centuries before the Safavids existed. He had come to Isfahan, taught there, and was honored by royal architectural patronage. His teaching activities must be distinguished from the function of the mosque that bears his name. A visitor standing before that building a century after Shah Ismail's conquest of Tabriz would have seen, in one place, a royal capital, a scholar from a transregional network, a private royal place of worship, and the architecture of a monarchy that had chosen to put Twelver Shi‘ism at the center of its public identity.

Nothing about that scene was inevitable in 1501. In that year the young leader of the Safavid Sufi order took Tabriz and, according to the chroniclers, had the Friday sermon recited in the names of the twelve imams of the Twelver tradition. The act was dramatic and the subsequent history was long. The Safavids ruled territories whose population was overwhelmingly Muslim but not overwhelmingly Twelver Shi‘i. Many of their subjects were Sunni, belonging to the Hanafi or Shafi‘i schools of law; many others belonged to Sufi lodges, practiced devotion to the family of the Prophet within a broadly Sunni framework, or held beliefs that neither Sunni nor Twelver jurists would have recognized as their own. There were Armenian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other non-Muslim communities as well. A proclamation told these people what the state expected. It did not tell the historian what they believed.

This chapter studies the process by which the proclamation became a durable religious order, and it argues that the process was neither instantaneous nor uniform. It was prolonged, stretching across the two centuries from 1501 to the end of the dynasty's effective rule in 1722. It was uneven, differing sharply among cities, villages, tribal zones, and frontiers. And it was the work of many agents: a monarchy that coerced and patronized, scholars who came from Lebanon, Iraq, the Gulf, and Iran itself, jurists who disagreed with each other about fundamental questions, preachers and reciters who made the story of Karbala a public experience, shrine custodians, endowment managers, merchants, guilds, women of learned and royal households, and ordinary people whose compliance, conviction, or evasion left only scattered traces in the sources.

The chapter's governing distinction is among four developments that are related but not identical. The first is an official religious declaration, the act of 1501 and its repetition in decrees, coinage, and ceremony. The second is the construction of an institutional framework, the offices, courts, colleges, and endowments through which a religious tradition reproduces itself. The third is change in popular belief and practice, which depends on teaching, ritual, and social pressure, and which is the hardest to measure. The fourth is the growing authority of jurists, whose claims to interpret law and guide the community in the absence of the Twelfth Imam were never simply the same as the state's claims. Each could advance without the others. A government could declare a religion without building colleges; colleges could exist without winning the people; and jurists could gain prestige without gaining independence from the throne.

The evidence for this history is rich and uneven, and students should learn its characteristics early. Chronicles written for the dynasty, such as Hasan Rumlu's Ahsan al-Tawarikh and Iskandar Beg Munshi's history of Shah Abbas, narrate royal acts and tend to present the Shi‘i transformation as a work of royal piety. Juristic treatises, commentaries, and legal opinions reveal the arguments of scholars but often reflect scholarly ideals more than court practice. Administrative manuals such as the Tadhkirat al-Muluk, compiled around 1725, describe offices as they stood at the dynasty's end. Endowment deeds, coins, inscriptions, and buildings document patronage. European merchants, diplomats, and missionaries, including the Carmelite fathers settled in Isfahan from 1608, wrote about what they could see, usually in cities and usually as outsiders. Ottoman, Uzbek, and Sunni writers describe the Safavids from hostile positions. Almost none of this evidence comes from poor households, rural women, or those who concealed their beliefs, and every account must be asked whose purposes it served.

The chapter also takes care to separate itself from its neighbors. Chapter 57 narrated the founding of the Safavid state and the immediate religious claims of Ismail's conquest. Chapter 59 will narrate Chaldiran and the Ottoman-Safavid struggle, and Chapters 60 through 62 will return to the political histories of Tahmasp, Abbas I, and Isfahan. Here the political reigns appear only as the changing settings in which a religious institutionalization took place. Chapter 64 will examine tribes, slavery, gender, and minorities as social history, and Chapter 65 will return to the ulama, shrines, and the growth of clerical authority. The present chapter is the first, foundational treatment of how the religious system was built; it does not pre-empt the later and more detailed accounts of clerical authority and religious society.

Finally, as Chapter 1 argued, the Iranian world cannot be reconstructed within modern borders. The scholars of this chapter came from places now in Lebanon, Iraq, Bahrain, and Saudi Arabia; the shrines that mattered most to Twelver devotion included Najaf and Karbala, which lay outside Safavid control for most of the period; the Sunni populations the Safavids ruled included Kurdish, Baluch, and eastern Iranian communities whose neighbors were Ottomans and Uzbeks. The chapter's central question is therefore this: how did Twelver Shi‘ism develop from the official religion proclaimed by the Safavid monarchy into an extensive system of religious institutions, legal authority, education, pilgrimage, public ritual, and everyday communal practice, and why did this transformation remain uneven and contested?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish an official religious declaration from the construction of institutions, the change of popular practice, and the growth of juristic authority, and explain why they must be studied separately.
  2. Describe the religious landscape the Safavids inherited, including Sunni legal communities, Sufi lodges, existing Twelver communities, Zaydi and Alid devotional traditions, and Qizilbash beliefs.
  3. Analyze the coercive measures of the early sixteenth century, including public denunciation, and distinguish documented local acts from claims of uniform policy.
  4. Explain how Sunni communities responded through resistance, migration, concealment, accommodation, and survival, and why responses differed by region.
  5. Evaluate the role of transregional Twelver scholarly networks and of Iranian scholars, avoiding the picture of a single mass migration.
  6. Assess the career of Ali al-Karaki and the adaptation of Imamite jurisprudence to the needs of a Shi‘i monarchy.
  7. Distinguish the administrative offices of sadr, shaykh al-Islam, qadi, and endowment trustee from the scholarly authority of leading jurists.
  8. Explain the doctrine of the occultation and the meanings of ijtihad and mujtahid, without projecting modern doctrines of clerical government backward.
  9. Interpret the Friday prayer dispute and the Usuli-Akhbari disagreement as debates within the emerging Shi‘i establishment.
  10. Trace the development of Muharram commemoration, shrine patronage, and Persian religious literature, and distinguish them from later theatrical forms.
  11. Assess religious diversity, the position of non-Muslim communities, and the participation of women, households, and merchants in religious life.
  12. Evaluate competing historical arguments about whether Shi‘itization was imposed from above and whether scholars became independent of the monarchy, and critique the sources on which each argument rests.

Historical Context

The Safavid movement, treated in Chapters 56 and 57, grew out of a Sufi order centered on the shrine of Shaykh Safi al-Din at Ardabil. Over the fifteenth century its leaders acquired a militant following among Turkmen tribesmen of Anatolia, Azerbaijan, and Syria, who became known as the Qizilbash, "red heads," for their distinctive headgear. As Chapter 55 explained, the order had been tied by marriage to the Aq Qoyunlu house, and it emerged as a political force in the vacuum that house left behind. Ismail, who was a boy when he was raised to leadership, took Tabriz in 1501 and was crowned shah. The Qizilbash revered him as a spiritual guide, and in some circles as more than that. This devotional relationship, between a murshid (spiritual master) and his disciples, is the context in which the proclamation of Twelver Shi‘ism must first be understood.

The religious world Ismail entered was complicated. For most of the preceding six centuries, Iran had been governed by Sunni dynasties or by rulers who patronized Sunni law. Hanafi jurists predominated in the northeast and Transoxiana, Shafi‘i jurists in much of the south and west, and the madrasas, endowments, and judicial offices of cities were largely staffed by Sunni scholars. Yet Iran also had a long tradition of Shi‘i devotion and, in certain places, of organized Shi‘i communities. Qum had been a Shi‘i center since the early Islamic centuries. Kashan, Rayy, Varamin, and Sabzavar had Twelver populations, and the Sarbadars of Sabzavar, discussed in Chapter 51, had ruled with Shi‘i sympathies in the fourteenth century. The Mar‘ashi sayyids governed parts of Mazandaran for generations, and Zaydi Shi‘i communities lived on the Caspian coast. The Ilkhan Oljeitu had briefly leaned toward Shi‘ism, and the great Imamite jurist al-Hilli, who died in 1325, had taught in Iraq under the Mongol rulers. Devotion to ʿAli and the Prophet's family, moreover, ran through Sunni piety and Sufi practice, so that reverence for the imams was not by itself a marker of Twelver identity.

Outside Iran, the centers of organized Twelver learning lay mainly in Arabic-speaking lands. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala in Iraq, the town of Hilla, the villages of Jabal Amil in southern Lebanon, Bahrain, and the oasis districts of eastern Arabia had maintained scholarly families, teaching circles, and jurisprudential traditions that were largely independent of any Shi‘i state. Twelver scholarship had developed over centuries as a minority tradition within larger Sunni polities, and its jurists had grown accustomed to teaching, issuing opinions, and managing communal affairs without governmental power. This experience would matter greatly when a Twelver monarchy appeared and asked such scholars to join its service.

The outline of this chapter therefore rests on a set of problems that the Safavids faced and could not solve by decree. A dynasty whose founding legitimacy rested on Sufi charisma and descent needed to place itself within a juristic tradition that defined Twelver law and ritual. A state with few trained Twelver scholars needed teachers, judges, and administrators. A population mostly accustomed to other religious practice needed to be taught, persuaded, pressured, or simply habituated to new forms of worship. And a monarchy whose claims to sacred authority overlapped those of jurists needed to decide how far to share religious leadership. These problems were never fully resolved, and the history of the institutionalization of Twelver Shi‘ism is the history of how successive generations negotiated them.

The period covered runs from 1501 to about 1700, with a concluding assessment through the fall of Isfahan in 1722. It begins with the proclamation and its immediate coercive effects, follows the arrival and rise of scholars and the construction of offices and colleges, examines the legal and theological disputes that arose within the Twelver community, turns to rituals, shrines, and religious literature, and finally assesses the social distribution of religious change and the late-Safavid settlement. At its close, Twelver Shi‘ism had acquired an extensive institutional presence across much of Iran, but substantial Sunni and non-Muslim communities persisted, jurists and monarchs remained in tension, and the boundaries of orthodoxy remained disputed.

From Official Proclamation to Religious Transformation

The religious situation in 1501, the first measures of Safavid religious policy, and the uneven responses of Iranian communities.

Section 58.1

The Religious Proclamation of 1501 and the Problem of Implementation

When Ismail entered Tabriz in 1501, the city had been the capital of the Aq Qoyunlu and, before them, of the Ilkhans and Jalayirids, as Chapters 47 and 55 explained. It was a large and commercially rich city with a long-established Sunni religious life, and it was an appropriate place to announce a change. The chronicles written under the dynasty, among them Hasan Rumlu's Ahsan al-Tawarikh, describe the proclamation as a set of public acts: Ismail ascended the throne, the Friday sermon was recited in the names of the twelve imams of the Twelver tradition, and the new religion was declared the religion of the state. Coins were struck bearing Shi‘i formulae, and the call to prayer was altered to include phrases characteristic of Shi‘i practice. Each of these acts was visible and audible to the whole population, and each was an assertion of sovereignty as much as of belief.

The choice of such acts reflects the way religious recognition worked in the Islamic world generally. As Chapter 53 showed for the Timurids, the Friday sermon and the coinage were the two principal means by which a ruler's sovereignty was publicly acknowledged. A ruler whose name was read in the mosques and stamped on the coins of a city was recognized as its sovereign. Ismail's innovation was to attach to these familiar instruments a new religious content. The sermon would name the twelve imams; the coins and the call to prayer would carry formulae that Twelver Shi‘is used and that most Sunnis did not. The declaration thus converted the ordinary machinery of royal recognition into a means of announcing a confessional identity, a step with few precedents on this scale in the central Islamic lands.

Yet a proclamation is only a beginning. To make a religion the religion of a state, a ruler needs persons who can lead the prayers, preach the sermons, judge disputes according to the law of the declared school, teach the law to the next generation, manage the endowments that pay for all of this, and explain the new practices to a population that has never heard of them. In 1501 the Safavid state possessed few such persons. Its leaders were military commanders, Qizilbash chiefs, and the officials of the dynasty's Sufi organization. Twelver jurisprudence had, as Section 58.5 will show, a long and sophisticated tradition, but that tradition was housed mainly in Iraq, Lebanon, and eastern Arabia, not in the Iranian cities the Safavids now ruled. The first problem of the new order was therefore a shortage of personnel and institutions, and that shortage shaped almost everything that followed.

A second difficulty lay in the nature of Ismail's own authority. To the Qizilbash he was the head of the Safavid order, a murshid-i kamil or "perfect spiritual guide," and in some circles he was credited with more than human qualities. The poems attributed to him, written under the pen name Khata‘i, and the reports of foreign observers suggest that devotion to him could take forms that a jurist trained in Twelver law would not recognize as correct. A juristically defined Twelver system, by contrast, located religious knowledge in the Qur‘an, the traditions of the Prophet and the imams, and the reasoning of qualified scholars, and it placed the final authority in the absent Twelfth Imam, not in any living ruler. The dynasty's sacred claims and the jurists' claims were therefore never quite aligned, and the tension between a charismatic Sufi monarchy and a legalistic religious tradition would shape the history of Safavid religion until the end.

For this reason, students should keep apart what the government demanded and what its subjects believed. A decree could require that the sermon be read in a given form; it could not require that listeners understood or accepted it. Chroniclers in the dynasty's service tended to describe the new religion as having been received with enthusiasm, or, where they admitted resistance, as having been overcome. Hostile Sunni and Ottoman writers described the same events as an imposition. Neither is a neutral witness. The historian has instead to reconstruct, from scattered evidence of buildings, appointments, endowments, legal documents, and travelers' notes, how a declaration became, over decades, a lived reality for some communities and remained a formality or a pretext for resistance in others.

The governing question of this section therefore frames the whole chapter: what institutions must a state create or reorganize before an official religious declaration becomes an enduring social reality? The sections that follow answer it in stages: by describing the religious landscape the Safavids inherited, by examining the coercion they used, by tracing the scholars, offices, and colleges they built, and by following the disputes and devotions through which Twelver Shi‘ism became part of the daily life of many, though never all, of their subjects.

Context: Rumlu belonged to the Qizilbash milieu and wrote under the Safavid dynasty. His chronicle describes the events of Ismail's accession at Tabriz, including the recitation of the Friday sermon in the names of the twelve imams and the establishment of the Twelver religion as the religion of the state. Students should consult the Seddon translation or a scholarly edition of the Persian text, identify the passage they use, and compare it with other accounts, including contemporary foreign reports and later Ottoman narratives. No excerpt is reproduced here.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The chronicle presents Ismail's accession at Tabriz as the occasion on which the Twelver faith was publicly established. It reports the reading of the sermon in the names of the twelve imams and the changes made to the formulae of public worship, and it portrays the transformation as a work of royal piety. Where it acknowledges opposition from the city's Sunni notables, it describes the opposition as overcome.

Analysis: The chronicle is the best-known narrative witness for the acts of 1501 and shows how the dynasty chose to remember them: as a decisive, public, ceremonial moment. It is useful for the sequence of events and for the connection between sovereignty and religious formulae. Comparison with contemporary European and other sources helps establish that the proclamation was a real and remembered event, not a later invention.

Limitations: Rumlu wrote decades after the events and in the dynasty's service. His account presents the new religion as foreordained and tends to minimize the difficulty and the cost of its establishment. The chronicle cannot show what ordinary people in Tabriz believed, how many accepted the new formulae willingly, or how quickly the personnel and institutions required to sustain them were assembled. Later accounts often repeat or expand the early narratives, so apparent corroboration may reflect copying.

Perspective: The text represents the viewpoint of a Qizilbash-connected official of the dynasty. The perspectives of Sunni scholars and residents of Tabriz, of Armenian and Jewish communities, and of the rural population are largely absent or filtered through hostile or triumphant narration.

Section 58.2

Sunni, Shi‘i, and Sufi Traditions in Iran before the Safavids

To understand what the Safavids changed, it is necessary to reconstruct what they found. The religious landscape of Iran in 1501 was varied, and describing it as a division between a Sunni majority and a Shi‘i minority, however convenient, misrepresents it. Religious identity in the late medieval Islamic world was less sharply bounded than later confessional history suggests. Communities shared shrines, saints, and devotional practices across what modern observers might call sectarian lines, and a person's attachment to a legal school, a Sufi master, a local shrine, and the family of the Prophet could coexist without any sense of contradiction.

The institutional backbone of public religious life was Sunni. Hanafi jurists predominated in the northeast and in the cities of Transoxiana and Afghanistan, and Shafi‘i jurists in much of the south and west, including Shiraz and the Persian Gulf littoral. Colleges, mosques, judicial offices, and endowments, as Chapter 53 described for the Timurid world, were largely staffed by Sunni scholars, and the great scholarly families of Herat, Shiraz, Tabriz, and Samarqand belonged to this tradition. Rulers from the Samanids to the Timurids and Aq Qoyunlu patronized Sunni jurists, preachers, and colleges, and the Aq Qoyunlu court, as Chapter 55 noted, had supported scholars such as Jalal al-Din Davani of Shiraz. This Sunni establishment was the immediate target of the Safavid transformation, and it was also the source of the administrative expertise that any government needed.

Sufism formed a second great stratum of religious life. Lodges (khanqahs) and the orders attached to them, the Naqshbandiyya in the east, the Nimatullahiyya in the south, and many local brotherhoods elsewhere, attracted followers from every rank of society. Sufi shaykhs mediated disputes, offered charity, and supplied rulers with spiritual blessing. Many Sufi orders traced their spiritual genealogies through ʿAli, and veneration of ʿAli and the Prophet's family was a prominent feature of Sufi devotion. The Safavid order itself was one such brotherhood. It had begun as a Sunni Shafi‘i order, and the historical process by which its leaders and followers came to place the imams at the center of devotion is the subject of Chapter 56.

Organized Twelver communities existed as well, and they provided the first foundation on which the Safavids could build. Qum had been a center of Shi‘i learning and devotion since the early Islamic centuries and housed the tomb of Fatima al-Ma‘suma, a sister of the eighth imam. Kashan, Rayy, and Varamin had Twelver populations, and Sabzavar in Khurasan had been the seat of the Sarbadars, whose fourteenth-century rule had Shi‘i sympathies. In Mazandaran, the Mar‘ashi sayyids, who claimed descent from the imams, held power into the sixteenth century. The Ilkhan Oljeitu had at one point inclined toward Shi‘ism, and Twelver scholars of Hilla in Iraq, above all al-Hilli, had written authoritative works under Mongol rule. These communities were small compared with the Sunni majority, but they possessed texts, traditions, and families of learning, and their existence meant that the Safavids did not create Iranian Twelver Shi‘ism from nothing.

Other traditions overlapped and complicated the picture. Zaydi Shi‘ism, which recognizes a different line of imams and a more activist conception of leadership, had a presence on the Caspian coast, in Gilan and Daylam, and its followers should not be confused with Twelvers. A broad devotion to ʿAli and the Prophet's family, sometimes called Alid loyalism, ran through Sunni piety, so that a person might venerate the imams, visit their shrines, and mourn Husayn while following Sunni law. The Qizilbash themselves held beliefs that blended Sufi, Turkmen, and Alid elements, including a veneration of Ismail as a holy figure, which differed from the beliefs a Twelver jurist would defend. Anatolian communities of similar background, later called Alevi, had their own traditions, and the Safavids' appeal to them would trouble the Ottomans, as Chapter 59 will describe. These strands could not be neatly sorted into two sects.

The Timurid shrine of Imam Reza at Mashhad illustrates the complexity. Gawhar Shad, wife of Shah Rukh, built a mosque there that was completed in 1418, as Chapter 53 noted. The patron belonged to a Sunni ruling dynasty and the shrine honored the eighth Shi‘i imam, yet the foundation caused no conflict. Pilgrimage to the tombs of the imams was widely practiced across the confessional boundary, and rulers who patronized them gained respect from many constituencies. The Safavids inherited this tradition of shared sacred geography and would later redirect it, but they began with a landscape in which the boundary between Sunni and Shi‘i devotion was porous.

Students should therefore carry from this section an important correction. The Safavids encountered a varied religious landscape, not a population belonging to two internally uniform camps. Sunni jurists were divided among legal schools and between rationalist and traditionalist theologians; Sufi orders competed with one another; Twelver communities differed in their relation to learned authority; and the beliefs of tribal followers and urban notables did not always match the doctrines of the scholars. A proclamation addressed to this diversity could not produce uniformity, and the work of institutionalization was in part the work of drawing boundaries that had previously been porous.

Section 58.3

Coercion, Public Ritual, and the Suppression of Sunni Authority

The early Safavid state did not rely on persuasion alone. The sources, including Safavid chronicles, European reports, and Ottoman and Sunni narratives, record coercive measures against Sunni religious authority and those who resisted the new order. The evidence is uneven, comes largely from interested parties, and must be handled with care, but it leaves no doubt that violence, intimidation, dismissal, and humiliation were part of the process, particularly in the first decades. Students should neither dwell on these measures in a sensational way nor minimize them. They were instruments of a political and religious project, applied with varying severity in particular places.

One of the most distinctive instruments was the public ritual of tabarra, the disavowal or cursing of those regarded in Twelver Shi‘i tradition as having usurped the rights of ʿAli and the Prophet's family, above all the first three caliphs, Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman. Such cursing offended the vast majority of Sunnis, for whom these men were revered Companions of the Prophet. In Safavid practice, the ritual was institutionalized: groups known as tabarra’iyan were assigned to move through streets and bazaars performing the denunciations, and ordinary people could be required to take part or at least to listen. The practice made the new religious identity visible and audible in public space, and it created a test: those who refused, or who showed displeasure, risked identification as opponents. It thus served simultaneously as ritual, propaganda, and surveillance.

Historical Vocabulary
Tabarra and the Tabarra’iyan

Definition: Tabarra (Arabic, literally "dissociation") is the Shi‘i doctrine and practice of publicly disavowing those held to be enemies of the Prophet's family, most controversially the first three caliphs. The tabarra’iyan were persons or groups charged in the Safavid period with performing public denunciations.

Historical context: Disavowal had a place in earlier Shi‘i devotion, but the Safavids made it a public and organized instrument. Its practice and intensity varied by period; later Safavid rulers and scholars moderated or rejected public cursing in some contexts, and it did not remain at the same pitch throughout the dynasty.

Why it matters: The practice shows how the Safavid state used ritual to define communal boundaries and test loyalty, and why Sunni communities in Iran and neighboring Ottoman and Uzbek states experienced the new order as an insult as well as a political change.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that every Twelver scholar endorsed public cursing, or that it was practiced continuously and uniformly across Iran. Its extent and form changed over time and place, and some jurists sought to limit it.

The regime also removed or displaced Sunni religious officials and transferred their functions to men who would accept the new religion. Prayer leaders, preachers, judges, and teachers who refused to adapt were dismissed, and in some places punished more severely, including by execution. Sunni mosques and colleges were in some cases taken over, converted to Shi‘i use, or deprived of endowment income, and the revenues of endowments could be redirected. Property was confiscated from opponents, and those who resisted on political as well as religious grounds, including rebels and rivals, were punished. Foreign and Sunni sources describe massacres and executions in connection with Ismail's conquests of Tabriz and of other cities, and later conquests such as that of Herat, but the precise scale of such events is disputed and numerical claims in the sources should be treated with great caution.

The documentary base requires a distinction between acts recorded in specific places and general claims about policy applied uniformly across Iran. For Tabriz, the first major city to experience the new order, the sources agree that the proclamation was accompanied by the reorganization of public worship and by reprisals. For other cities, such as Shiraz and Isfahan, accounts report disruptions of Sunni religious life, the replacement of officials, and acts of violence, though the timing and degree differ and are sometimes attested only in later or hostile texts. For Yazd and other cities there is evidence of tension and local resistance, but the picture is fragmentary. The historian should therefore avoid describing a single, coordinated campaign executed everywhere in the same manner. What the evidence supports is a pattern of coercion that varied with the strength of the Safavid hold on a city, the attitude of local elites, the proximity of rival powers, and the personal character of local commanders.

The purposes of coercion were several. It aimed to dismantle the social and institutional networks of the old Sunni elites, which could serve as centers of resistance and which were tied to the dynasties Ismail had overthrown. It sought to create a body of officials and religious personnel loyal to the new regime. It served a disciplinary function toward the Qizilbash, whose own devotional enthusiasm needed channeling into a state religion. It marked a boundary against the Ottomans to the west and the Uzbeks to the northeast, both Sunni powers, so that religion became a symbol of political difference. And it expressed the religious convictions of the dynasty, which should not be reduced to cynical calculation. Historians disagree on how to weigh these motives, and they probably operated together.

The consequences were equally varied. Coercion forced many Sunni scholars and officials to flee, some to Ottoman, Uzbek, and Mughal territories, where Iranian scholars continued to be prominent. It drove other communities to conceal their allegiances or to comply outwardly. It generated lasting resentment and a literature of polemic on both sides, and it hardened the confessional boundary between the Safavids and their neighbors. At the same time it was not sufficient, by itself, to create a Twelver society. Terrorized communities could comply in public while preserving their practices in private, and the decades of institution-building described in later sections were necessary precisely because coercion could not produce understanding or consent. The Safavids could dismiss a Sunni judge in a day; they could not produce a trained Twelver judge so quickly.

From the perspective of the Safavid ruler and his officials: Public denunciation announced that the city now belonged to a new order, identified opponents, and demonstrated the loyalty of those who joined in. It converted religious conviction and political discipline into a visible civic ritual.

From the perspective of a member of an existing Twelver community: The new regime offered recognition and protection that a minority had rarely enjoyed, and the community's scholars and families could expect patronage. From the perspective of a merchant or artisan: The central concerns were the safety of trade, relations with officials, and reputation. Taking part, standing aside, or complying outwardly were calculations about livelihood as much as faith, and the sources record such choices only indirectly.
From the perspective of Armenian or Jewish residents: Non-Muslim communities were not the main targets of the ritual, but they lived beside its disruptions and had to judge how the change in rulers would affect their legal standing and their relations with Muslim neighbors.
Section 58.4

Accommodation, Resistance, and the Survival of Sunni Communities

Communities faced with the Safavid religious order did not respond uniformly or once for all. Over the sixteenth century they chose among resistance, flight, concealment, accommodation, and gradual adoption, and their choices changed as political fortunes changed. The history of these responses is as important as the history of royal policy, because it determined how much of the declared religious order was realized in practice.

Open resistance occurred, but it was rarely sustained within cities that the Safavids firmly controlled. Revolts and plots are recorded in some places, and tensions between Qizilbash commanders and local notables sometimes broke into violence. More commonly, resistance took the form of flight. Scholars, officials, and merchants left for the Ottoman lands, Transoxiana under Uzbek rule, and the Mughal and Deccan courts of India, where the demand for educated Persian speakers was strong. This migration deprived Iran of members of established scholarly families, but it also spread Persianate culture, as Chapter 53 noted for the Timurid diaspora, and it created communities abroad with grievances against the Safavid order.

Concealment and accommodation were more widespread than flight. Many Sunni families kept their allegiances private, taking advantage of the practice of taqiyya, the concealment of belief under duress, a doctrine ironically better known in Shi‘i tradition. Others adapted to the new institutions in ways that preserved their social position. Experienced secretaries, accountants, and local officials, whose skills the state needed regardless of their religion, often retained their posts, and the continuity of administrative personnel that Chapter 55 emphasized for the Turkmen period persisted under the Safavids. A family could change its public religious observance across a generation or two while maintaining the same network of kinship, marriage, and patronage. Gradual adoption of Shi‘i practice, through marriage, education, employment, and habit, could thus occur without a dramatic moment of conversion, and the chroniclers' language of mass conversion obscures a slower process.

Evidence for the persistence of Sunni communities during the sixteenth century is substantial. Rosemary Stanfield Johnson, whose research on Tahmasp's reign is particularly useful, has documented continuing Sunni adherence and opposition in this period rather than a comprehensive conversion. Sources indicate Sunni scholars and officials still present in cities such as Qazvin, which became the capital in the mid-sixteenth century, and Hamadan, as well as in eastern Iranian regions including Khurasan, where the Uzbek threat, Sunni neighbors, and contested sovereignty kept confessional boundaries fluid. Students should note that the evidence is stronger for some places than others, and that the size of Sunni communities cannot be measured. Even late Safavid estimates of religious adherence require scrutiny, since observers had limited information and their own purposes.

Differences among kinds of communities were profound. Urban religious institutions, which had buildings, endowments, and office-holders that the state could seize or reassign, were the most exposed to Safavid intervention, and the shift in public religious personnel in major cities was consequently the most rapid. Villages were harder to reach. Rural religious life centered on local shrines, saints, and lodges, on the village prayer leader, and on customary practices, and it changed more slowly. Tribal populations followed their chiefs, and their religious identity depended on the chief's relationship with the dynasty. Frontier societies, whether on the Ottoman border, in Kurdistan, or toward the Uzbek lands, lived with competing sovereigns and could shift allegiance when circumstances changed. Regions where Safavid rule was contested, such as parts of Khurasan, experienced repeated reversals, and the religious order imposed in one period could be undone in the next.

Inherited Sunni social networks could survive even where public religious practice had changed. A family's authority rested on its position in a web of marriages, endowments, shrines, and learned connections, and these ties did not disappear when its members adapted outwardly. Sufi lodges continued to function, and in some cases to attract Shi‘i devotees, so that the Sufi orders became arenas of overlapping identity. Endowments founded by Sunni donors remained in force, though their management could be altered. The persistence of such networks helps explain why the religious map of Iran remained patchwork well after 1501, and why, as Section 58.17 will show, Sunni populations survive in parts of the country to the present day. It also warns against reading the Safavid religious program as a simple replacement of one set of institutions by another.

The overall pattern of Division I can now be stated. The proclamation of 1501 established a legal and symbolic framework and was reinforced by coercion in the early decades. But the Safavids inherited a varied religious landscape, lacked the personnel and institutions to administer a Twelver order, and encountered a wide range of responses. The transformation therefore depended on construction: of a body of scholars, a system of offices, a network of colleges and endowments, and a body of law, ritual, and literature that could give the declaration substance. Division II turns to the first of these tasks, the assembling of scholars and offices.

Section 58.5

The Transregional Networks of Twelver Scholarship

The Safavid court's need for Twelver scholars led it to the places where such scholars already worked. Imamite jurisprudence had developed, from the medieval period, in communities that were minorities within larger Sunni states, and its centers were in Arabic-speaking regions. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala in Iraq had attracted students and teachers around the tombs of the imams. The town of Hilla, in central Iraq, had been the seat of the great legal school of al-Hilli and his family in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. In Lebanon, Jabal Amil, the hill country of the south, and the Bekaa valley and Baalbek, sheltered a number of learned Twelver families. In eastern Arabia, Bahrain and the neighboring districts of Qatif and al-Ahsa had their own traditions of scholarship. Each of these places had teachers, libraries, and chains of transmission that stretched back for generations.

These networks were not designed to serve a state. Their scholars had been accustomed to working without political power, teaching students, issuing legal opinions to their communities, and managing local endowments, often in cautious relation to Sunni rulers. When the Safavids appeared, they offered something new: a Twelver monarchy that wished to place scholars in positions of public authority, endow their institutions, and reward their learning. The attractions were considerable. Royal patronage brought salaries, endowed teaching posts, honorific titles, and the prospect of influence over law and worship. But they came with costs: dependence on a ruler whose religious claims were sometimes unlike the scholars' own, exposure to the changes of politics and court factions, and the demands of administrative service.

Students should be careful not to describe the movement of scholars as a single mass migration. The evidence indicates a variety of patterns. Some scholars settled permanently in Iran, took posts, married into local families, and founded dynasties of teachers. Others traveled repeatedly between Iran and their homelands, serving for a period and returning. Some declined invitations or left after disputes, and some, like the great scholar Shaykh Husayn b. Abd al-Samad, who is reported to have arrived in the reign of Tahmasp, brought families who became prominent. Their son Shaykh Baha al-Din al-Amili, known as Shaykh Baha‘i, became one of the most eminent figures of Abbas I's Isfahan. The scholar Lutfallah al-Maysi, mentioned in the chapter opening, is another example. The total number, the timing, and the permanence of these movements remain matters of scholarly debate, and Encyclopaedia Iranica describes the networks as multidirectional and cautions against exaggerating their scale.

Equally important, Iran was not empty of Twelver scholarship. Qum, Kashan, Rayy, and other centers had maintained Shi‘i learning, and Iranian scholars studied in Iraqi and Arab centers and returned. Muqaddas Ardabili, an Iranian scholar of the sixteenth century who taught at Najaf while it was under Ottoman rule, shows that scholars of Iranian origin worked across the political boundary. In the seventeenth century the philosophical and theological circles of Isfahan and Shiraz, associated with names such as Mir Damad and Mulla Sadra, were composed largely of Iranian-born thinkers. The scholarly families who rose in Safavid Iran, such as the Majlisis of Isfahan, were of local origin. A history that credits the imported clerics alone with building Safavid Shi‘i learning would therefore misrepresent a more complex interaction between imported and indigenous traditions, in which books, students, and ideas moved in several directions.

Scholars differ on how much weight to give each element. Some studies, notably Rula Jurdi Abisaab's work, stress the importance of scholars from Jabal Amil and other Arab lands in shaping Safavid religious institutions and legal thought. Others emphasize the Iranian foundations and the diversity of the scholarly community. The disagreement reflects limited evidence and different judgments about how to define influence, since a scholar's importance can lie in teaching, writing, holding office, or simply being cited. For students the point is that the networks mattered, that they were not one-way, and that their significance was gradual rather than immediate.

The role of books and students deserves emphasis alongside that of teachers. Legal and devotional texts circulated in manuscript from Najaf, Hilla, and Jabal Amil to Iranian libraries, and Iranian students traveled to study in the older centers. The works of al-Hilli, of the early jurists al-Tusi and the Four Books of Shi‘i traditions, and of later commentators formed the shared textual foundation. A scholar who stayed in Iraq could shape Iranian religious life through his writings, and a text could travel much farther than its author. Section 58.8 will return to the educational mechanisms through which this learning was reproduced.

Section 58.6

Ali al-Karaki and the Adaptation of Twelver Jurisprudence to Government

No scholar of the early Safavid period is more closely associated with the adaptation of Imamite law to the needs of a Twelver monarchy than Nur al-Din Ali al-Karaki, also known as Muhaqqiq al-Thani, "the Second Investigator," a title that places him beside the earlier jurist al-Muhaqqiq al-Hilli. He died in 1533–1534. Born in the Lebanese Bekaa region, in the village of Karak Nuh from which his name derives, he received his training in the established scholarly networks of the Arab lands, studying with leading teachers in Jabal Amil and elsewhere and later at Najaf, where he became a recognized authority. His commentary on a major legal text of al-Hilli, and his treatises on specific legal questions, circulated widely. When he came into the orbit of the Safavid court, he did so as a mature scholar with an established reputation.

The chronology of his relationship with the dynasty needs to distinguish travel from royal recognition. According to Rula Jurdi Abisaab's specialist study, al-Karaki left Jabal Amil for Najaf around 1504; this was a move to an established Iraqi scholarly center, not proof that he had settled at the Safavid court in Iran. Around 1510 Shah Ismail honored him while he was residing at Najaf. He later visited the Safavid court, including during preparations for Chaldiran in 1514. These relationships developed over years rather than beginning with a securely documented Iranian court appointment in 1504. Under Tahmasp, al-Karaki's position became more prominent. According to the traditions preserved about him, Tahmasp issued orders recognizing his authority and instructing officials to follow his guidance in religious matters, and he is reported to have appointed prayer leaders and teachers in cities and villages, to have instructed communities in correct ritual practice, and to have attended to matters such as the orientation of mosques toward Mecca. Some of these reports rest on later hagiographical sources, and the more dramatic anecdotes, such as the story that the shah deferred to him as more worthy of rule, should be treated as tradition rather than as established fact.

What al-Karaki did and what he stood for are better established than the narrative detail. He worked to apply the rules of Imamite jurisprudence to the practical problems of a Shi‘i state: how and by whom Friday prayer should be led, how the land tax (kharaj) should be treated, who might act in matters of law and communal administration while the Twelfth Imam was absent, and how worship should be organized. His position was that a qualified jurist could act as a general deputy of the Imam during the occultation, with authority in a range of legal matters. He wrote on the permissibility of accepting land revenue and appointments from a ruler who acted justly, which gave a juristic basis for scholars to serve the Safavid state. This was a considerable adaptation. Twelver scholars had long been wary of association with governments, and the question of whether a pious jurist might lawfully hold office under a ruler was disputed.

His most contested position concerned Friday prayer, discussed fully in Section 58.10. He argued that Friday congregational prayer could be held during the occultation under the leadership of a qualified jurist or his appointee. This position provided a religious foundation for the public worship of the Safavid cities and made the jurist the indispensable organizer of communal religion. It also drew objections from other scholars, notably Ibrahim al-Qatifi, who disputed al-Karaki's claims on this and other matters. The existence of such objections reminds students that al-Karaki's authority did not rest on universal assent but on the judgment of the court and of those scholars who agreed with him.

The nature of his authority needs careful definition. Al-Karaki's influence derived primarily from his learning and reputation, which were recognized across the scholarly networks of the Arab lands and Iran. The powers he exercised in Iran derived additionally from the shah's support, which could be given and withdrawn. The two should be kept distinct. A scholar with a reputation commanded respect among students and fellow jurists whatever his relationship with the throne, whereas the power to appoint officials or to order the conduct of worship in a city depended on royal commission. His career thus illustrates a recurrent pattern of Safavid religious history: scholarly prestige and royal favor reinforced one another, but they were not identical, and the relationship between them could shift.

His importance lies partly in helping an established tradition of Imamite legal scholarship respond to the practical problems of a newly founded Shi‘i monarchy, not in inventing a new religion or a new doctrine of clerical rule. He supplied arguments by which scholars could justify cooperation with the state, and by which the state could claim juristic support. Later generations built on his work, and Encyclopaedia Iranica regards him as one of the most influential Imamite jurists of the early Safavid period. At the same time his career should not be exaggerated into a model of the later clerical establishment. In his lifetime the institutions that would support a stable body of jurists, colleges, endowments, and offices, were only beginning to take shape, and the Safavid order he served was fragile, as Tahmasp's early reign would show.

Section 58.7

The Sadr, Shaykh al-Islam, Judges, and the Government of Religious Institutions

The Safavid state governed religion through a set of offices that were in part inherited from earlier Iranian administrations and in part reconfigured for the new order. This section is the chapter's principal administrative analysis, and it makes a point that is essential to everything else: the holder of a religious office and a scholar of great authority were not necessarily the same person, and the expansion of religious administration did not create a fully independent, centrally organized clerical hierarchy.

The central office was the sadr. The term, as Chapter 53 noted for the Timurids, designated an official associated with the supervision of religious endowments and religious appointments, and earlier Iranian states had used it. Under the Safavids the sadr became a major office of state, with responsibility for the administration of endowments (waqf), the appointment and supervision of religious personnel such as prayer leaders, preachers, and mosque and shrine staff, and certain judicial functions. Since endowments held large amounts of land and property and funded colleges, shrines, and charities, the sadr controlled substantial resources. Many early sadrs were drawn from sayyid families, descendants of the Prophet, whose status carried prestige, and some were men of learning, but the office was an appointment of the crown and the sadr served at the shah's pleasure.

Beside the sadr stood other officials. The qadi, or judge, administered Islamic law in the courts and registered contracts, marriages, and property transactions. The shaykh al-Islam was the leading religious authority of a city or district, with duties of legal and religious oversight that varied from place to place; the post of shaykh al-Islam of Isfahan, held by Majlisi from 1687, was among the most prominent. The imam-i jum‘a led the Friday congregational prayer in a city, a post of considerable symbolic importance since the Friday sermon named the ruler. Trustees (mutawallis) managed individual endowments and shrines. And the courts of the sharia coexisted with administrative tribunals applying customary law and royal decree. Together these offices formed a network that touched religion, law, property, and public worship.

According to the specialist treatment, the central sadr's jurisdiction was divided under Tahmasp in 1562–1563 into two parts, a division that the administrative manual Tadhkirat al-Muluk describes in its picture of the late Safavid system, with one sadr handling the endowments and religious affairs attached to the crown lands and the royal household, and another responsible for the general endowments of the realm. The details of how this arrangement worked and how it changed over time are known imperfectly, and arrangements shifted across the dynasty's history. Under Abbas I and his successors the office was reorganized, its relation to other officials altered, and its influence at times reduced or enlarged by court politics. Students should not imagine a single, fixed structure maintained for two centuries, but rather an evolving set of arrangements.

The distinction between administrative position and theological authority is crucial. The sadr was an official, appointed and dismissed by the shah, whose power derived from the office. A leading jurist or mujtahid, by contrast, derived authority from learning, from the recognition of students and peers, and from the trust of the community, and such authority did not depend on holding any post. A great scholar might serve as shaykh al-Islam, as Majlisi did, or hold no office at all and still command a following. Conversely, an official might have little scholarly standing. The two spheres overlapped, and the Safavids often tried to bring scholars into office to capture their prestige for the state, but they remained conceptually and practically distinct. Encyclopaedia Iranica's treatment of the sadr documents this distinction explicitly, and it should shape how students read the sources.

The expansion of religious administration thus did not create an independent clerical establishment. The offices were creatures of the crown; their holders could be appointed, transferred, or removed, and their incomes depended on royal grants and endowments the state supervised. There was no single head of the religious institution, no hierarchical chain of authority from village to capital, and no body of clergy with its own sources of revenue and discipline comparable to that of a church. Scholars who held no office relied on private teaching, gifts, and endowments, and their position varied with the favor of patrons. The overall picture is of a plural, overlapping system of offices and authorities, held together partly by royal supervision and partly by the shared training and kinship of the scholarly families. Section 58.8

Madrasas, Teachers, Students, and the Reproduction of Shi‘i Learning

A religious system survives only if it can reproduce its learned members. Offices can be filled by appointment, but the knowledge that makes a judge, a jurist, or a preacher competent must be passed on through years of teaching, and building the educational infrastructure to do so was a central, slow task of the Safavid centuries. Appointments could be made in a day; colleges took generations.

The principal institution was the madrasa, a college in which students studied the religious sciences: the Qur‘an and its interpretation, the traditions of the Prophet and the imams, jurisprudence and its principles, theology, Arabic grammar and rhetoric, and often logic, philosophy, and mathematics. Madrasas were supported by endowments, which paid teachers, provided housing and stipends for students, and maintained libraries. Iran already possessed many madrasas, most of them Sunni and many of them founded under Timurid and earlier rulers. The Safavids converted some of these to Twelver use and founded new ones, and the emphasis of their curriculum changed to include Twelver jurisprudence and traditions. In Isfahan, under Abbas I and his successors, a cluster of colleges grew up near the royal square and the bazaars, and a large college was begun under Sultan Husayn in the early eighteenth century. Colleges at Mashhad, Qum, Shiraz, and other cities also expanded.

Much of the instruction took place outside formal institutions. Scholars taught in their homes, in mosques, and in the study circles that formed around a famous teacher, and a student's training often consisted of attending the lessons of several masters in turn, sometimes in different cities. The quality of a student's education depended on the teachers he could reach. The teaching was oral and textual: a teacher read and commented on a text while students listened, annotated, and questioned. Libraries, which could be attached to colleges, shrines, or private houses, held the manuscripts on which this teaching depended, and the copying of manuscripts was itself a significant scholarly activity.

Authority was reproduced through a system of licenses and lineages. A student who had mastered a text or a branch of learning could receive an ijaza, a written authorization from the teacher permitting him to transmit the text or to issue legal opinions. These licenses formed chains linking a scholar to a succession of teachers reaching back to the great jurists of the past, and the prestige of a scholar depended partly on the quality of his chain. The system gave the scholarly community a form of self-regulation independent of the state: a license rested on a teacher's judgment, not on a royal commission. At the same time, scholarly families, in which fathers taught sons and sons-in-law, formed dynasties that held posts across generations. The Majlisi family of Isfahan is a well-known example, and family connection was a major route to office and influence.

The languages of instruction mattered. Arabic was the language of scripture, law, and advanced scholarship, and a jurist needed to read and write it fluently. Persian, however, was the language of administration, literature, and the daily life of most Iranians, and it was the means of reaching audiences who could not read Arabic. Under the Safavids, scholars increasingly wrote in Persian for broader readers, producing legal manuals, devotional texts, and translations of traditions, a development Section 58.15 examines. Shaykh Baha‘i's Jami‘-i Abbasi, a Persian manual of legal and ritual rules associated with the court of Abbas I, is an example of how a leading scholar produced a text for readers outside the advanced circle. This dual-language pattern, Arabic for the learned and Persian for the wider public, shaped how Twelver doctrine spread.

Access to learning was unequal. A student needed time, money, and a patron. Endowed stipends and the hospitality of teachers helped, and talented students from modest families could rise, but the long course of study favored the sons of scholarly and wealthy families who could support years of unremunerative training and who had connections to teachers and patrons. Geography mattered: students in Isfahan, Qum, or Mashhad had greater access to teachers than those in provincial towns. Women were generally excluded from the colleges, although educated women of learned households, who might receive instruction from fathers or husbands, appear in the record, and the evidence for their learning is thin. Poor families and villagers acquired their religious knowledge from local prayer leaders, preachers, and the customs of their communities rather than from formal institutions.

Students should draw from this section the conclusion that building a religious system required a sustained educational infrastructure, not merely government appointments. The Safavids could appoint a sadr or a judge, but the competence of those officials, the continuity of their learning, and the authority of their opinions depended on a system of colleges, teachers, books, licenses, and families that took generations to establish. By the late seventeenth century that system was in place in Isfahan and other major cities, and it supplied the personnel for the offices of the state and for the preaching and teaching that reached the wider population. But it had grown unevenly, depended on the patronage of kings and the generosity of donors, and left large areas of the country with little formal religious education. Section 58.9

The Hidden Imam and the Problem of Legitimate Authority

Every attempt to build a Twelver religious order had to answer a question inherited from the ninth and tenth centuries: who may speak for the Twelfth Imam while he is absent? Twelver tradition holds that the line of imams descended from Ali and Fatima ended with Muhammad al-Mahdi, born in the later ninth century according to the tradition, who went into concealment. The tradition distinguishes a Lesser Occultation, the period of the four successive deputies who communicated between the community and the Imam, from the Greater Occultation, which began in 941 (329 in the Islamic calendar) when the last deputy died without naming a successor. From that time the community had no living infallible guide. Those who sought to organize Twelver life in the Safavid period inherited seven centuries of reflection on what, in the Imam's absence, could legitimately be done.

The question had practical dimensions that the Safavid proclamation of 1501 made urgent. Who could lead the Friday congregation, which Twelver law had long treated as requiring the Imam or his appointee? Who could collect and distribute religious taxes, enforce penalties, command a holy war, or adjudicate disputes? A king, however devout, claimed his legitimacy partly from descent from the seventh imam, Musa al-Kazim, and from the spiritual office of the Safavid order, but a descent claim did not by itself resolve whether a ruler could legitimately exercise functions that the tradition reserved to the Imam. The Safavid answer was never a single doctrine. Ismail's early supporters among the Qizilbash treated him as a quasi-divine guide, and this view faded as the dynasty reoriented toward the legal learning of the jurists. Later Safavid rulers sought legitimacy through genealogy, patronage of shrines, and the endorsement of scholars who recognized their authority as just rulers acting on behalf of the absent Imam. Some jurists accepted this arrangement. Others regarded all rule before the Imam's return as at best tolerable and at worst usurpation.

Jurists developed a body of reasoning to describe what remained possible. Earlier scholars of Twelver law, among them al-Shaykh al-Tusi in the eleventh century, al-Muhaqqiq al-Hilli in the thirteenth, and al-Allama al-Hilli in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth, laid the foundations. They treated the legal tradition as a body of knowledge transmitted from the imams and held that a qualified scholar could derive rulings from the sources of law when the Imam was unavailable to resolve difficult cases. This derivation of rulings by a qualified scholar is called ijtihad, and its practitioner a mujtahid. Al-Allama al-Hilli is generally credited with giving the principle systematic form and with distinguishing within the community those who exercise ijtihad from those who follow their rulings (taqlid). The terms are Arabic, and the underlying idea is not unique to Twelvers, since Sunni jurisprudence also used ijtihad, but the particular Twelver version took its shape from the circumstances of the Occultation and the need to rely on the traditions of the imams.

Historical Vocabulary
Ijtihad, Mujtahid, and Taqlid

Definition: Ijtihad (Arabic, "exertion") is the effort by a qualified scholar to derive a legal ruling from the recognized sources of law. A mujtahid is a scholar judged capable of this effort. Taqlid (literally "imitation") is the practice by which a person who cannot derive rulings follows the ruling of a qualified scholar.

Historical context: In Twelver thought the sources typically include the Quran, the reported sayings and actions of the Prophet and the imams, consensus under specified conditions, and reason. The weight to be given to each, and above all to reason and to the opinions of later jurists, was disputed. The vocabulary became common in Safavid Iran as scholars of the Arab world and Iran sought to explain what religious authority could exist during the Occultation.

Why it matters: The distinction between the scholar who derives rulings and the believer who follows them became a central feature of Twelver religious organization. It allowed ordinary believers to practice a complex law without becoming scholars, and it gave the learned a role that no royal appointment could confer or revoke.

Potential misunderstanding: Ijtihad was not a license for free opinion. It was a disciplined technique governed by rules, learned over many years and constrained by the texts. Nor was there a formal clerical hierarchy with fixed ranks in the Safavid period. Recognition as a mujtahid came gradually, through the judgment of teachers, peers, and followers, and the term could be disputed.

A further question concerned the scope of the jurist's authority. A narrow view held that the jurist could give legal opinions and settle disputes between consenting parties but nothing more. A broader view held that, in the absence of the Imam, the qualified jurist inherited at least some of his functions, such as leading the Friday prayer, collecting religious dues, and ordering penalties, and could appoint others to do so. The idea that jurists collectively served as the general deputy (na‘ib ‘amm) of the Imam, in contrast to the four special deputies of the Lesser Occultation, appears in Safavid-period writing, and the work of Ali al-Karaki, discussed in Section 58.6, advanced it. The idea was elaborated in different ways by different scholars and should not be equated with later claims, from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, concerning the political authority of the jurist. Students should resist the temptation to read the Safavid discussion as an early version of those later doctrines. Its contemporary concern was how to organize a functioning community of Twelver believers under a ruler who was not the Imam.

The scholarly literature agrees that Safavid Twelver authority was in practice a negotiated arrangement among the monarchy, the officials of the religious administration, and the learned community of jurists, and it did not settle into a single formula. The king and his officials appointed to office and paid salaries, and the jurists supplied the legal reasoning, the learning, and in many cases the legitimacy. Each side needed the other, and the balance shifted over time and with personalities. Where the monarchy was strong, as under Abbas I, jurists tended to accept the arrangement and receive favor. Where the monarchy was weak, as in the later seventeenth century and especially in the decades before 1722, the jurists acquired more independence of action and also, some scholars have argued, more room to criticize the court. The relationship was not a simple opposition of church and state, because the institutions in question did not operate as separate bodies in the way that language suggests.

Evidence for these arrangements comes mainly from the writings of the jurists themselves, from administrative manuals such as the Tadhkirat al-Muluk, from chronicles, from endowment deeds, and from the reports of European visitors. Each has limits. Juristic treatises state positions, not necessarily practices, and they often respond to specific disputes whose details are lost. Administrative manuals describe offices as they were idealized by officials. Chronicles favor the court. European observers, who included merchants, diplomats, and missionaries, reported what they saw and were told in the capital, often with limited command of Persian and with their own purposes. Reconstructing practice therefore requires combining these sources and accepting that the result will be provisional.

Section 58.10

The Friday Prayer Controversy: Law, Kingship, and Congregational Life

The congregational prayer held at noon on Fridays is, in Islamic practice, the central weekly act of public worship, and the sermon (khutba) delivered during it had since the early caliphates been an announcement of sovereignty. Reading the name of a ruler in the Friday sermon, and minting coins in his name, were the two classic marks of legitimate rule. The Safavid proclamation at Tabriz had altered the formulae of worship and placed the names of the imams in the sermon, as Section 58.1 explained. But the legal question of whether Twelvers could hold a Friday prayer at all in the Imam's absence had been disputed within the tradition, and Safavid rulers who wanted to hold such prayers in their capitals and cities needed legal grounds.

Twelver jurists before the Safavid period generally took one of three positions. Some held that the Friday prayer was obligatory only when the Imam or his specific representative presided, so that during the Occultation it could not properly be held. Others held that it was permitted but not obligatory in his absence, so that the noon prayer was a valid substitute. A third group held that it was obligatory when a qualified jurist could lead it. The practical effect of the first position was that Twelvers did not hold the Friday prayer with the regularity and public character of their Sunni neighbors. The Safavid state, building a capital with a congregational mosque at its center, had an interest in the third.

The controversy came to a head in the reign of Tahmasp, when al-Karaki, whom the shah had honored and empowered, took the view that jurists could lead and, in some versions of his argument, should lead the Friday prayer. His position was disputed by other jurists. The best-known opponent was Ibrahim al-Qatifi, a scholar from the Gulf shore who was prominent in the same circle of Arab Twelver scholars and who criticized al-Karaki's positions on several matters, including the legitimacy of Friday prayer under a ruler whom some regarded as unjust and the status of land revenue (kharaj) that the jurist accepted from the ruler. The dispute produced treatises on both sides. It is generally dated to the second quarter of the sixteenth century, but the exact chronology of the individual treatises and the order in which they responded to each other is not fully settled.

In towns where a Friday prayer was organized under a leader with the support of the state, townspeople had a weekly occasion for gathering, for hearing the sermon, and for displaying communal belonging. Where the prayer was not held, as in many smaller towns and rural districts, worshippers prayed in their local mosques or at home, and the weekly congregation was less prominent. The pattern was uneven and depended on whether there was a qualified leader, a suitable building, a source of funding, and the interest of local officials. The sermon itself carried the Safavid religious message to those who attended, though it is not known how many, or how often, in any given place.

Section 58.11

Religious Law in Daily Life: Courts, Endowments, Family, and Ritual Practice

Religious law reached most people through disputes over land and inheritance, marriage contracts, debts, and the endowments that supported local mosques and shrines, not through juristic treatises. Safavid Iran inherited a dual structure in which the judge applying religious law (qadi, often called qadi-yi shar‘) and officials applying customary and royal law (‘urf) both had jurisdiction. The qadi heard cases involving marriage, divorce, inheritance, contracts, and endowments and issued documents that carried legal weight when sealed. Customary jurisdiction, exercised by royal officials such as the chief judicial officer known as the divanbegi and by provincial governors, covered criminal matters, disputes touching state interests, and cases that the parties preferred to bring before secular authority. The line between the two was not fixed, and litigants sometimes chose among forums. The result was a legal landscape in which religious law was an important but not exclusive source of order.

Under the Safavids the judges applying religious law were of the Twelver school, and the substance of the law they applied was drawn from Twelver jurisprudence. Differences from Sunni practice affected the lives of ordinary people. Twelver law of inheritance differs in its treatment of the shares of daughters and other relatives, so that the distribution of an estate in Safavid Iran followed rules distinct from those of neighboring Sunni states. Twelver law recognizes temporary marriage (mut‘a or sigha), a contract for a fixed term and a stated payment that Sunni jurists generally reject, and its use in Safavid society is attested in the accounts of European visitors, though how common it was, and in which classes and regions, is not firmly established. Twelver law also has its own rules on ritual matters, such as the position of the body in the prayer and the formulas of the call to prayer. Some Safavid-era communities added the invocation of Ali to the call, a practice that scholars debated and that was not uniformly accepted as a required part of the call.

Endowments (waqf) were the means by which property was dedicated to religious and charitable purposes, and they were a main channel through which religious institutions were funded. A donor, who might be a king, an official, a merchant, or a woman of means, set aside the income of land, shops, baths, or other property for a stated purpose, such as the upkeep of a mosque, a college, or a shrine, the salaries of its staff, or the feeding of the poor. The terms were written in a deed, and a manager, called a mutawalli, administered the property. The royal endowments associated with Abbas I are among the best known. His grants to the shrine of Imam Riza at Mashhad and to the Safavid family shrine at Ardabil, where the gift of a major collection of Chinese porcelain is dated to 1608 (1017 AH) in specialist ceramic and architectural research; related gifts, books, and endowment transactions should not all be assigned to that single year, show a ruler using endowment to link his own legitimacy to the holy places of the dynasty and of the Twelver community. Such grants also placed property under religious management and sheltered it, in principle, from confiscation, although the state sometimes interfered with the administration of endowments, particularly when they were large.

The economic role of endowments was considerable. Properties dedicated as waqf supported not only worship but also teaching, the maintenance of water systems and roads, and in some cases the feeding of the poor. They gave religious institutions an income independent of the state's yearly budget, though in some cases they also made institutions dependent on a founder's family or on the continuing goodwill of the managers. Managerial positions were often held within families, and a family might derive income and standing from controlling an endowment for generations. This helps explain how families of scholars, descendants of the Prophet, and managers of shrines accumulated influence. It also helps explain disputes, since competition over the right to manage a lucrative endowment was a frequent cause of litigation.

Religious taxes raised questions of their own. The Quranic alms tax (zakat) was due on certain kinds of property, and Twelver law adds a levy known as khums, one-fifth of certain gains, a portion of which tradition assigns to the Imam. During the Occultation, the question of who should receive the Imam's share became disputed, and jurists proposed that it be given to qualified jurists to disburse. The extent to which Safavid Iranians actually paid it, and to whom, is not clear from the sources, and later developments, especially in the nineteenth century, made the payment of khums to leading jurists a much more systematic practice than it appears to have been under the Safavids. Historians should therefore be careful not to retroject later arrangements for the financing of the clergy onto the seventeenth century.

The evidence for the daily operation of law comes from documents more than from treatises: sales, marriage contracts, endowment deeds, petitions, and judgments, many preserved in the archives of shrines and in family papers. They are valuable because they show actual transactions and the language in which ordinary parties and officials expressed them. They also show that conformity to Twelver legal forms was spreading, for example in the invocation of the imams in the opening formulas of documents and in the notarial vocabulary. But surviving documents come disproportionately from towns, from propertied families, and from institutions with the means to preserve archives. The legal experience of villagers, nomads, and the poor is much less visible, and in many regions customary practice, tribal law, and local arbitration continued to govern daily life with little reference to the formal courts.

The lesson for students is that the institutionalization of law was the least visible and perhaps the most consequential part of the process. A population that increasingly drew up its contracts in Twelver forms, divided its inheritances by Twelver rules, endowed its mosques in the names of the imams, and went to judges trained in Twelver law lived in a legal order that was recognizably Twelver, even if its members did not read the treatises or share the scholars' disputes. Yet the extent of this legal Twelverization varied across towns and regions and over time, and the sources do not permit confident statements about how fully it reached the countryside before the end of the seventeenth century.

Section 58.12

Usulis and Akhbaris: The Seventeenth-Century Argument over Sources of Law

In the early seventeenth century a controversy broke out among Twelver scholars about the very method that ijtihad represented. The scholars who defended it were later called Usulis, after the discipline of the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) that provided the tools of legal reasoning. Their critics were called Akhbaris, after the akhbar, the reports of the sayings and actions of the Prophet and the imams, which they regarded as the only secure basis of law. The labels and the sharpness of the division are partly the product of later polemic, and historians now emphasize that the positions of individual scholars varied. Many scholars of the period combined elements of both approaches and did not belong comfortably to either camp.

The critique is most closely associated with Muhammad Amin al-Astarabadi, a scholar who studied in Iran and the Hijaz and who died at Mecca in 1623–1624 or 1626–1627 (the sources preserve both dates). In a work on the principles of the law, often cited under the title al-Fawa‘id al-Madaniyya, he argued that the speculative reasoning of the jurists introduced uncertainty into a law that the imams had already conveyed, that the Quran could be understood only through the traditions of the imams and not through independent interpretation, and that jurists who relied on rational principles borrowed from Sunni jurisprudence had departed from the path of the imams. The precise dating of his works and the details of their reception are matters on which the scholarly literature gives somewhat different accounts, and students should treat the dates as approximate.

The argument did not remain a purely academic quarrel. It touched on the authority of the jurist as such. If law could be known with certainty from the reports of the imams, then the role of the mujtahid as an authoritative interpreter was reduced, and the ordinary believer could, at least in principle, consult the reports directly or through a transmitter without reference to a jurist's rational judgment. Akhbari scholars in practice still relied on learned scholars to collect, authenticate, and explain the traditions, so their approach did not abolish scholarly authority. But it shifted its basis from reasoned derivation to faithful transmission, and it made scholars more cautious about claiming general powers on behalf of the Imam.

The seventeenth-century scholarly scene shows how many positions were possible. Muhsin Fayz Kashani, a prolific scholar of Safavid Iran who died around 1680, is often classed as moderately Akhbari while working in philosophy, mysticism, and the collection of traditions as well. Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, whose compendium of traditions made him a central figure in the Twelver scholarly literature, is often described as leaning toward the use of traditions without being a strict Akhbari. Shaykh Baha‘i, Mir Damad, and their circle in Isfahan cultivated juridical, philosophical, and scientific learning and were not firmly on either side. Isfahan therefore supported a variety of methods, and the court's patronage extended to scholars of differing inclinations.

The prestige of the Akhbari position in the Safavid centers, and its stronger presence in the Arab Twelver shrine cities and the Gulf, mean that the dispute should not be mapped neatly onto the later victory of Usuli methods. That victory, which took place in the eighteenth century in the shrine cities of Iraq under Ottoman rule after the fall of the Safavids, lies beyond this chapter and belongs to the discussion of the post-Safavid period in later chapters. The point for students here is that, at the moment of the Safavid peak, Twelver legal method was an open question. The institutionalization of Twelver Shi‘ism did not follow a single path from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, and some of the institutions that later seemed to follow naturally from Usuli premises developed only after the Safavid state had collapsed.

The controversy also illustrates the social conditions in which scholarship operated. Scholars competed for students, patrons, and offices, and methodological disputes often became entangled with personal rivalries and with competing networks, the circles of Isfahan, the Arab shrine cities, and the Gulf. Writers on both sides produced treatises, refutations, and refutations of refutations, and these texts are valuable evidence for the intellectual life of the period. They also warn historians against assuming that the learned community spoke with a single voice. A Safavid king who wished to appeal to scholarly consensus found a community that was often divided, and this division contributed to the limited and negotiated character of juristic authority discussed in Section 58.9.

Ritual, Pilgrimage, Literature, and Devotional Life

Section 58.13

Muharram, Ashura, and the Remembrance of Karbala

The event on which Twelver devotional life centers took place on the tenth of the month of Muharram in the year 61 of the Islamic calendar, which corresponds to October 680 CE. Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet and son of Ali and Fatima, was killed with a small band of family members and companions near Karbala in present-day Iraq by forces acting for the Umayyad caliph Yazid. The women and children of Husayn's party were taken captive. Shi‘i tradition, and many Sunni Muslims in different ways, have remembered the event as a moment of injustice and sacrifice. For Twelvers it became the foundation of a calendar of mourning, preaching, recitation, and processions that gave the community a shared emotional language.

Mourning for Husayn long preceded the Safavids. Sources from the tenth century record public mourning ceremonies in Baghdad under rulers sympathetic to Shi‘ism, and a tradition of elegy and commemoration developed among Twelver and other Shi‘i communities across the following centuries. The Safavids did not invent the remembrance of Karbala. What the dynasty did was to give it an official setting, to support it with money and public order, and to place it at the center of the religious year of an entire realm. In the cities of Safavid Iran, the first ten days of Muharram, and above all Ashura, became a season of black cloth, communal meals, gatherings in mosques and private houses, preaching in the open, and processions. These practices were supported by officials, merchants, and wealthy families, and in the capital by the court.

A specific literary development shaped the ceremonies. Husayn Va‘iz Kashifi, a preacher and writer of Timurid Herat who died in 1504 or 1505, composed in Persian a prose work with verse, the Rawzat al-Shuhada ("Garden of the Martyrs"), which narrates the sufferings of the Prophet's family culminating at Karbala. It was written around 1502, in the last years of Timurid rule and in a religious milieu that was not narrowly Twelver. The book became so popular that the term rawza-khani, recitation of the rawza, came to designate the practice of reciting accounts of the martyrdom of Husayn, and the person who recited it was a rawza-khan. The case is instructive because the text that gave the Safavid-period ceremony its name was composed just before the Safavid conquest of Herat and was adopted by the new order.

Historical Vocabulary
Rawza-khani, Ta‘ziya, and the Majlis

Definition: Rawza-khani is the recitation or narration of the sufferings of Husayn and his companions, named after Kashifi's Rawzat al-Shuhada. A majlis (plural majalis) is a mourning assembly at which such narration takes place. Ta‘ziya (literally "consolation" or "condolence") refers in later centuries to dramatic enactments of the events of Karbala and, more broadly, to mourning rites.

Historical context: Rawza-khani and mourning assemblies are well attested in the Safavid period. The fully developed dramatic form of the ta‘ziya, with scripted roles, specially built performance spaces, and a regular repertoire, is documented mainly from the eighteenth and especially nineteenth centuries. The evidence for what Safavid-era mourning looked like comes from European travelers, chronicles, and later memory.

Why it matters: The terms show how a literary text, a form of public speech, and a devotional rite became linked. They also help students distinguish the Safavid practice of recitation and procession from the later theatrical tradition often called the Persian passion play.

Potential misunderstanding: Descriptions of Muharram in modern Iran, which include elaborate ta‘ziya performances, should not be read back unaltered into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The practices changed substantially over time, and the sources for the earlier period are thinner.

Muharram ceremonies served several purposes at once. They commemorated a sacred event, taught the community who Husayn and his enemies were, and connected the sufferings of the imams to the worshipper's own sorrows and hopes. They offered an occasion for the distribution of food and charity, a duty that wealthy patrons were expected to fulfill. They linked neighborhoods, since processions and assemblies were organized by quarters of the city and by guilds and associations, so that mourning also expressed local loyalties and sometimes rivalries. They supplied a framework for popular preaching, in which preachers appealed to emotion and to the narrative of the sufferings of the imams to encourage devotion and the avoidance of sin. And they gave rulers an occasion to display piety, as when the court sponsored gatherings or the shah attended.

The ceremonies also marked boundaries. For Twelvers the denunciation of the enemies of the imams was part of the traditional cycle of reverence and disavowal, and in the early Safavid period elements of tabarra were incorporated in public rites. Sunni residents, and non-Muslim minorities living in the same cities, experienced the season differently, and in times of heightened feeling they could be vulnerable to harassment. European visitors, among them Italian and French travelers who were in Isfahan in the seventeenth century, describe processions, flagellation and other expressions of grief, and the intensity of public emotion. Their accounts are valuable but must be read with caution. These writers were outsiders, often hostile to or fascinated by what they saw as exotic, and they described what they could observe in the capital, which may not have been typical.

Not all scholars approved of every form of mourning. Jurists debated which practices were permissible and which, such as certain forms of self-injury, were questionable. Some Safavid-period religious authorities criticized excesses, while others defended emotional expression as a form of devotion. The discussion shows that the shape of Muharram was itself a field of argument, in which preachers, jurists, officials, and ordinary participants had different interests. The sources cannot reveal what individual participants felt. They show a public ritual that was supported, shaped, and sometimes restrained by the authorities and that nonetheless belonged to the community that performed it.

The overall effect of Muharram was to make Twelver Shi‘ism a religion of public participation and emotion as well as law. It was probably the single most effective means by which the faith became part of the lives of those who never opened a legal treatise. But its development was gradual, and the grand ceremonies of the nineteenth century were the result of further elaboration. In the Safavid period, the ceremonies were already widespread in the cities, supported by the state and the wealthy, and increasingly central to communal life, though the evidence does not allow a confident estimate of their reach in villages and among nomadic groups.

Section 58.14

Shrines, Pilgrimage, and the Sacred Geography of Safavid Iran

A religious order that remembers the imams must also locate them, and the geography of Twelver devotion is a geography of tombs. Of the twelve imams, Ali is buried at Najaf and Husayn at Karbala, and others at Medina, Baghdad (Kazimayn), and Samarra, all in the Arabian and Mesopotamian lands. In Iran itself lies the tomb of the eighth imam, Ali al-Riza, at Mashhad in Khurasan, where he died in 818 and where his shrine grew over the centuries into a major complex and the main place of Twelver pilgrimage within the Iranian lands. Qum, roughly 150 kilometers southwest of modern Tehran, holds the tomb of his sister, Fatima al-Ma‘suma, and became a destination of pilgrimage and a center of learning. Throughout Iran lie the burial places of numerous descendants of the imams, the imamzadas, ranging from large and well-endowed shrines such as Shah Abd al-Azim near Rayy and the Shah Chiragh at Shiraz to small local tombs tended by villagers.

This geography interacted with political boundaries in a way that mattered greatly to Safavid policy. The principal shrines of Iraq, at Najaf, Karbala, Kazimayn, and Samarra, lay outside the Iranian plateau. They were held by the Safavids only briefly, from about 1508 to 1534 and again from 1623 to 1638, and for most of the period they lay in the Ottoman Empire, which had conquered Iraq in the 1530s and confirmed its control in the settlement of 1639. The most sacred places of Twelver Shi‘ism were thus under the control of the empire's principal Sunni rival. For the Safavids this was a constraint and an opportunity. It meant that Iranian pilgrims who wished to visit the tombs of Ali and Husayn needed to cross the frontier and rely on Ottoman tolerance, which was uneven, and it gave added importance to the shrines within Safavid territory. It also meant that the Safavid rulers invested heavily in Mashhad and Qum, and that the Arab shrine cities became an important arena of competition between the two empires.

Shrines were more than destinations. They were centers of landed wealth, of employment, and of religious and legal activity. The great complexes at Mashhad, Qum, and Ardabil held extensive endowments, employed attendants, reciters, and scholars, and provided hospitality for visitors. They also offered sanctuary (bast), a place where those in danger of arrest or revenge could take refuge, which gave them a role in conflict resolution and also a role in tensions with the state. The custodianship (tawliyat) of the major shrines was in the hands of powerful officials, and the post carried wealth and influence. Pilgrims came from far and near and contributed to the local economy by purchasing lodging and food and by making donations, and the markets around shrines grew accordingly.

Pilgrimage practice also built ties across social boundaries. People of different regions and classes met at shrines, and the rituals of visitation (ziyara), including prayers addressed to the imams and the recitation of visitation texts, were shared across the Twelver world. Local shrines of imamzadas gave villagers their own sacred places, where vows were made and fulfilled, where the sick sought healing, and where communities celebrated saints' days. Many of these practices were more local and less textual than the formal rites of the great shrines and did not depend on the approval of jurists. The attachment of Twelver identity to these places is among the most durable results of the Safavid period, and many continued as sites of devotion long after the dynasty had fallen.

Section 58.15

Writing the Faith: Persian Religious Literature and the Collection of Traditions

An institutional religion depends on texts, and the Safavid period saw a large production of religious writing in both Arabic and Persian. The learned continued to write in Arabic, the language of scripture and law, but increasingly scholars wrote in Persian for wider readerships, translating and summarizing the traditions of the imams, composing legal manuals, and producing devotional and polemical works. This development made Twelver doctrine accessible to readers who could read Persian but not Arabic, including officials, merchants, and educated women, and it gave Twelver Shi‘ism a Persian literature that shaped the religion in Iran in the following centuries.

The largest single work of the period is the Bihar al-Anwar ("Oceans of Lights") of Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, a vast Arabic compilation of traditions of the Prophet and the imams on theology, history, ethics, and law, which in modern printed editions fills over a hundred volumes. Majlisi drew on a wide range of earlier collections, many of which he sought out and had copied, and the compilation preserved material that might otherwise have been lost. His purpose was in part to gather a comprehensive record of the sayings and actions of the imams, and the work became a standard reference for later scholars. Because it is a compilation, it reproduces reports of varying reliability, and its use as evidence for early Islamic history requires critical care. As a monument of Safavid scholarship it is of the greatest importance.

Majlisi also wrote extensively in Persian, producing works of devotion, ethics, biography, and legal guidance aimed at a broad audience. His Persian writings on the lives of the imams and on the sufferings of the Prophet's family, along with manuals of ritual, supplied preachers and educated laypeople with material in their own language. Other Persian works of the period included Shaykh Baha‘i's Jami‘-i Abbasi, a manual of ritual and legal rules, and Persian commentaries on the Quran, among them that of Mulla Fath Allah Kashani, who died around 1580. These works did not replace Arabic scholarship but extended its reach, and their circulation in manuscript, copied by professional scribes and private readers, shows a sizeable readership. The printing press was not yet used for Persian religious texts in Iran, so circulation depended on manuscript production, which limited the number of copies and tied the spread of texts to the economics of scribal labor and the patronage of the wealthy.

Collections of poetry and devotional verse expanded the literature of Twelver Shi‘ism. Elegies for Husayn and the imams, panegyrics in praise of Ali, and religious verse in Persian were composed by poets of the period, including those who worked in the circle of the court and others who wrote for local assemblies. Poets of the Safavid period, working in what later scholars call the Indian style, were often active at the Mughal and other courts to which many left, and scholars debate how much the Safavid court's patronage of poetry differed from earlier Timurid practice. For students the main point is that religious poetry provided a lasting means of transmitting the memory of the imams in an accessible form, memorized and recited by those who could not read.

The patterns of authorship and circulation show the social structure of religious knowledge. Learned authors belonged to a small elite, often drawn from scholarly families and in many cases from the Arab Twelver centers or their descendants. Their works circulated chiefly among other scholars, officials, and wealthy readers. Women could be addressees and patrons, and some devotional works were written with women readers in mind, but the evidence for women as authors is limited. The effect of the literature on the general population was mediated by preachers, reciters, and teachers, who drew on the texts and delivered their contents orally. This is a reminder that written texts were only one channel of transmission, and often a minor one for most Iranians.

Section 58.16

Sufism, Qizilbash Practice, and Philosophy in a Legalizing Order

The Safavid dynasty arose from a Sufi order, the Safaviyya, founded by Shaykh Safi al-Din of Ardabil, who died in 1334. The order was originally Sunni, of the Shafi‘i legal school, and acquired a more Shi‘i and then increasingly militant character over the fifteenth century. The Qizilbash who brought Ismail to power were his devoted followers, and their relationship to him combined military loyalty with a form of religious devotion that treated him as a spiritual guide. As the dynasty consolidated and as scholars of law gained influence, a tension developed between this heritage and the legalistic, scholarly religion that the jurists promoted. Chapter 55 and the earlier chapters of this volume have described the origins of the Qizilbash. The question here is what became of the Sufi and heterodox elements within an institutionalizing Twelver order.

The relationship changed over time. In the sixteenth century the dynasty continued to have close ties to Sufi practice, and Qizilbash religious ideas, including reverence for Ismail and for the lineage of the Safavid shaykhs, persisted. Under Tahmasp, who emphasized his own piety and relied on jurists such as al-Karaki, the court moved away from open claims of Ismail's divinity, and the regime made efforts to restrain behavior that scholars regarded as improper. Under Abbas I the court reduced the Qizilbash's role in government and military affairs, as Chapter 57 and the surrounding chapters discuss, and with it reduced the political weight of the heritage that had given Sufi devotion its position. Abbas continued to honor the family shrine at Ardabil and to present himself as heir to the Safavid shaykhs, so the Sufi heritage was transformed rather than abandoned.

Attitudes of the scholars toward Sufism varied. Some jurists were hostile to organized Sufi orders, to the veneration of living shaykhs, and to practices they regarded as unlawful innovation. In the later seventeenth century, hostility to Sufis was prominent among a number of scholars in Isfahan, and some wrote polemics against Sufi doctrines and practices. Others saw no contradiction between law and mysticism and combined the two in their own lives and work. Fayz Kashani is an example of a scholar who wrote on law and traditions and also on mystical themes. Majlisi, while hostile to certain Sufi positions, was not opposed to every form of spiritual discipline. The attitudes of Safavid scholars thus formed a spectrum, and generalizations that Safavid Twelver scholars were either anti-Sufi or pro-Sufi are misleading.

Some Sufi orders left Iran, and others were suppressed or declined. The Nimatullahi order, which had been centered at Mahan in Kirman after its founder's death in the early fifteenth century, saw its leaders move to India, where they enjoyed patronage in the Deccan, and its presence in Iran faded and then revived in the later eighteenth century. The Nuqtavi movement, a heterodox current that had developed in the sixteenth century, was persecuted under Abbas I. Other groups, including wandering dervishes and some Qalandar communities, continued to exist at the margins, sometimes tolerated and sometimes harassed. Popular devotion to the saints and to the imams often retained Sufi elements, such as the veneration of tombs and the practice of rhythmic recitation, even as the formal orders weakened. The history of Sufism in this period therefore combines suppression, migration, accommodation, and persistence, and the sources, which mainly record the views of hostile authors and officials, make it hard to reconstruct from the inside.

Philosophy flourished alongside the law. The school associated with Isfahan in the early seventeenth century, whose best-known figures include Mir Damad, who died in 1631, Mir Findiriski, who died around 1640, and Mulla Sadra of Shiraz, who died in 1640 while traveling to the pilgrimage, produced a body of work that combined Islamic philosophical traditions with Twelver theology and mystical themes. Mulla Sadra's synthesis, often called transcendent philosophy, had a limited circle of readers in his own time and became much more influential in later centuries. Isfahan's philosophers worked within institutions supported by the court and the patronage of the capital, and they sometimes faced hostility from jurists who regarded their speculation with suspicion. The existence of this intellectual tradition shows that institutionalization of Twelver religion did not eliminate intellectual diversity and that the capital supported legal, philosophical, and mystical learning at the same time.

The material setting of Isfahan reflects these relationships. The Shaykh Lutfallah Mosque, begun in 1602–1603 and completed in 1618–1619, was named for a scholar from Jabal Amil who taught in the capital. Its single-domed, courtyardless plan served private royal worship rather than functioning as the large public Friday mosque or as a madrasa. By contrast, the monumental royal congregational mosque on the Maydan, begun around 1611 and completed over subsequent decades, provided a much more public setting for worship. Together, these distinct types of royal patronage show how scholarly prestige, religious practice, and the dynasty's claims were expressed in Isfahan's urban design, without turning every religious building into an identical institution.

Students should draw the following conclusion from this section. The institutionalization of Twelver Shi‘ism did not mean the replacement of mysticism, philosophy, and Qizilbash devotion by a uniform legalism. It meant the creation of a framework in which law, preaching, shrines, and learning were prominent, and in which older Sufi and heterodox elements were reshaped, marginalized, or absorbed. Some did persist at the popular level, others were driven out, and others turned into intellectual traditions of high prestige. The resulting religious landscape was layered, and the layers were not always in harmony with each other.

Diversity, Households, Late Safavid Orthodoxy, and Historical Synthesis

Section 58.17

Regional Diversity, Sunni Populations, and Non-Muslim Communities

The institutions described so far were concentrated in cities, above all Isfahan, Qum, Mashhad, Qazvin, Tabriz, and Shiraz. Safavid Iran was a larger and more varied country. Its population included Persian-speaking cultivators and townspeople, Turkic-speaking pastoralists and villagers, Kurds, Baluch, Lurs, Arabs of Khuzestan and the Gulf shore, Armenians and Georgians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and other communities, and their religious experience differed accordingly. A history that follows only the capital will misjudge how far an institutionalized Twelver order had reached by the later seventeenth century.

Sunni populations remained substantial in several regions. Kurdistan, much of the Baluch country, the Turkmen steppe, parts of the Gulf coast, and districts along the eastern and northwestern frontiers kept a Sunni character, sometimes within the Safavid state and sometimes beyond its edge. Their religious leaders often continued to teach and judge in the Sunni legal schools, and the state's willingness to tolerate this varied with its political needs. Frontier regions with Ottoman or Uzbek neighbors were sensitive because a Sunni population could be seen as a security risk, and rulers could treat local religious leaders either as threats or as useful intermediaries. Kurdish tribal leaders, who commanded military resources, had to be bargained with, and religion was only one of the considerations that shaped the bargain.

Twelver Shi‘ism itself was not uniform. Communities in Gilan and Mazandaran along the Caspian had a regional religious history that included Zaydi and other Shi‘i traditions and had been drawn into the Twelver framework unevenly. Villages in the mountains and deserts had their own saints, shrines, and customs, which they understood as part of Islam without necessarily relating them to juristic debates. Heterodox currents associated with the Qizilbash and related groups, whose beliefs ranged across veneration of Ali and the imams that other Muslims regarded as exaggerated, persisted in some Turkic tribal and village settings, as did communities in Anatolia, outside Safavid rule, that retained Qizilbash traditions under Ottoman authority and became the ancestors of later Alevi communities. These variations remind students that the institutional Twelver order was a framework laid over many existing practices and did not replace all of them.

Non-Muslim communities lived under Muslim rule in the legal status familiar from earlier Islamic states, protected in principle and subject to special taxes and restrictions. Armenians held a prominent place. After the campaigns against the Ottomans in the early seventeenth century, Abbas I relocated large numbers of Armenians from Julfa on the Araxes and other northwestern districts to Isfahan, where they founded the quarter called New Julfa across the river from the capital. The measure, usually dated to 1604–1605, had strategic and economic purposes, including denying territory to the Ottomans and drawing a commercial community into the capital's service, and it caused great suffering and loss in the course of the move. The Armenians of New Julfa became important silk merchants with networks extending across Eurasia, built churches, and maintained their own institutions, and Abbas and his successors gave them protection while retaining control. Their situation shows that the Safavid state could treat a religious minority as an economic asset and a political client at the same time that it promoted Twelver orthodoxy.

Jews and Zoroastrians experienced the period unevenly. Jewish communities existed in Isfahan, Kashan, Shiraz, and other cities, and Zoroastrians were concentrated in Yazd and Kirman and their villages. Both were subject to restrictions, including those arising from notions of ritual purity in Twelver law, which some jurists applied with particular rigor. The documentary record shows episodes of persecution as well as long periods of coexistence. In the mid-seventeenth century, under Abbas II, a campaign of forced conversion affected the Jews of Isfahan and other cities for several years before it was reversed, and in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, under Sulayman and Sultan Husayn, pressure on non-Muslim communities and on Sufis and others increased in some places, though the extent and consistency of this pressure are debated and varied from one region to another. Students should avoid both extremes: the picture of constant persecution does not fit the evidence, and neither does that of uniform tolerance. Official policy, local officials, jurists' opinions, and popular attitudes interacted, and the experience of a given community depended on the time and place.

European missionaries, including Carmelites, Augustinians, Capuchins, and Jesuits, established small houses in Isfahan and other cities and sometimes won the favor of the court, which valued European diplomatic and commercial contacts. They also provide some of the most detailed outside descriptions of Safavid religious life, which they sometimes wrote with an eye to polemic. Their reports should be read with the caution due to any partisan source, but they are indispensable for the capital and for the position of Christians.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Iranian Twelver Shi‘ism Imposed, Gradual, or Both?

The question: How should historians explain the shift of most of the population of the Iranian plateau to Twelver Shi‘ism between 1501 and the early eighteenth century? Was it the product of coercion by the state, of gradual and negotiated change, or of something else?

Interpretation A — A rapid imposition from above: Some older and popular accounts emphasize Ismail's decree, the violence against Sunni scholars and communities in cities such as Tabriz, and the use of tabarra. On this reading, a Sunni majority was forced within a generation or two into a new confession by the power of the state, and the Safavid dynasty created Iranian Shi‘ism.
Interpretation B — A gradual and uneven conversion: Other scholars stress that Shi‘i communities and Shi‘i devotion existed in Iran long before 1501, that the proportion of Sunnis at that date is uncertain, that the new learned class and its institutions took generations to build, and that conversion varied by region and was only completed over a century or more through teaching, preaching, patronage, and the passage of time. Coercion mattered, but it was one factor among several.

Evidence: The argument rests on chronicles, biographical dictionaries, juristic writings, endowment and legal documents, accounts by European and Ottoman observers, and the survival of Sunni communities in certain regions. The evidence is unevenly distributed and rarely allows quantitative estimates of how many people held which beliefs at a given time.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology, scale, and emphasis, and also what counts as conversion. A person who attended a Muharram assembly, drew up documents in Twelver forms, or followed a Twelver judge might not have changed his private conviction in the same way as one who recited a new confession, and the sources seldom distinguish these.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the Safavid achievement is understood. If it was an imposition, its legacy is one of state power. If it was gradual and negotiated, its legacy also includes the agency of scholars, communities, and ordinary people who adopted and adapted the faith. Neither view removes the importance of violence in the early years.

Section 58.18

Women, Households, Merchants, and the Social Experience of the Religious Order

Religious institutions were run largely by men, but women took part in the religious life of Safavid society as worshippers, donors, mourners, and transmitters of devotion within the household. The sources, written overwhelmingly by men, record these roles unevenly, and historians must infer much from documents of endowment, from the reports of European travelers who could rarely enter women's spaces, and from later memory. What the evidence does show is that women of means acted as patrons. Women of the royal house and of elite families endowed mosques, colleges, and charitable foundations. The Madrasa-yi Madar-i Shah, a large college in Isfahan completed in the early eighteenth century during the reign of Sultan Husayn, is associated with the endowment of his mother. Women of more modest means donated smaller properties and made vows at shrines, and the records of endowments include many that were established by women, a reflection of Islamic law's recognition of a woman's right to hold and dispose of property.

Women also participated in Muharram assemblies, which often had separate gatherings for them, and in pilgrimage to local and regional shrines, where vows, prayers for children and health, and visits to the tombs of holy figures formed a part of ordinary religious life. Women's assemblies were sometimes led by female reciters, and the devotional memory of Fatima and the women of the Prophet's household, including Zaynab, who is prominent in the Karbala narrative, gave women a specific place in Twelver devotion. How far women's religious life was shaped by formal juristic teaching and how far by customary and domestic practice is difficult to say, and it surely varied by class and region.

Twelver law shaped women's legal position in marriage, divorce, inheritance, and property in ways that differed in detail from Sunni law and that the courts applied in practice. Documents of marriage, divorce, and property show women appearing before judges and contracting in their own names, and they also show the constraints under which women lived, including the authority of fathers and husbands and the unequal rules of inheritance and divorce. Temporary marriage, discussed in Section 58.11, was a contested subject, and its social meaning, whether as a regulated union, a source of income for some women, or a form of exploitation, is hard to establish from the available sources, which are dominated by male views. The sources rarely allow us to hear women describe their own experience.

Merchants held a prominent place among the supporters of the religious order. The bazaar economy brought together wholesale and retail traders, artisans, moneylenders, and the guilds that organized crafts. Prosperous merchants were among the principal donors of mosques, schools, and charitable endowments, and they supported preachers and the organizers of Muharram processions, and many held religious dues in trust or paid them voluntarily. Their economic interests aligned with an order that offered stable law and the protection of contracts and property, and some of the scholarly families that later played major roles in Iran were closely connected to the commercial world through marriage and shared endowments. The relationship between the bazaar and the clergy, a prominent feature of later Iranian history, had roots in this period, though the evidence does not support identifying Safavid arrangements with those of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The Armenian and Indian merchants of Isfahan and the Gulf trade, who were not Twelver, were part of the same commercial world, and Twelver merchants competed and cooperated with them. The state encouraged trade and protected it in the interest of its revenue, and religious difference between traders did not prevent commercial partnership. At the same time, jurists' rules on ritual purity and on dealings with non-Muslims shaped some social interactions, and the sources record incidents of tension. The overall picture is of a society in which religious boundaries were real and consequential but crossed daily in commerce.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: A Year of Twelver Observance in a Safavid City

Note: this is an analytical composite drawn from many kinds of evidence. It does not describe any named individual or a particular documented neighborhood.

For an urban household in a major Safavid city in the later seventeenth century, the religious year was structured by the Islamic lunar calendar. The month of Muharram brought mourning assemblies, black cloth, charitable meals, and the processions of Ashura, in which the neighborhood and its guilds took part. The month of Ramadan brought the fast and nightly prayers. Festivals commemorating the birth of the imams and the Prophet were occasions for gatherings and gifts, and the anniversaries of their deaths were marked with recitation and charity. The household's finances included alms and, among the more observant, dues to religious figures, and a family of means might make a vow to endow a lamp or a copy of the Quran to a local shrine.

The weekly rhythm centered on the Friday prayer where it was held, and on visits to the neighborhood mosque and to the shrines of local imamzadas. Marriage contracts were drawn up before a judge or a legal specialist, inheritances were divided under Twelver rules, and disputes over property were taken to the judge of religious law or settled by respected neighbors. Children learned prayers and the names of the imams at home and attended elementary schools, where they learned to read the Quran and, if the family could afford it, Persian texts. For the most part, the religious expert a family met was the local prayer leader or preacher, not a famous jurist.

The experience was not the same for everyone. A villager far from the capital might have seen the great ceremonies rarely, relied on a local saint's shrine, and kept customary practices that scholars viewed with reserve. A Sunni or non-Muslim household in the same city lived alongside this calendar, with its own festivals and obligations, and had to take account of the public mood during the days of mourning. A poor household might have participated in the processions as a spectator or recipient of charity. These differences show why generalizations about the religious life of Safavid Iran should be qualified by place, class, gender, and community.

Section 58.19

Muhammad Baqir Majlisi and the Late Safavid Consolidation of Orthodoxy

No individual is more closely associated with the late Safavid consolidation of Twelver religious life than Muhammad Baqir Majlisi (1627–1699 or 1700). He was the son of Muhammad Taqi Majlisi, a respected scholar of Isfahan associated in some accounts with mystical learning, and he grew up in a scholarly family with extensive connections. He taught and wrote prolifically, becoming a prominent figure under Shah Sulayman and Sultan Husayn. His appointment as shaykh al-Islam of Isfahan in 1687, and his tenure in that office until his death, are documented. Later accounts sometimes wrongly assign him the title mulla-bashi, or head of the learned class. That Safavid office was established only in 1712, after Majlisi had died. It subsequently institutionalized some of the elevated religious authority that his office and standing had exemplified; it was not a post he held. Nor is there secure evidence that Majlisi simultaneously occupied both principal sadr positions sometimes attributed to him.

His importance rests on three kinds of activity. The first was scholarship: the compilation of the vast Bihar al-Anwar, discussed in Section 58.15, and other works on traditions and law. The second was popularization, through works in Persian on the lives of the imams, on devotion, ethics, ritual, and pilgrimage, that carried Twelver teaching to readers outside the learned circle. The third was institutional and polemical influence. Majlisi used his standing at court to support preachers and teachers, to promote observance of the law, and to oppose what he regarded as errors, including certain Sufi practices and philosophical doctrines, and he is associated with the more restrictive atmosphere of the late Safavid court toward some of these currents. His writings against non-Muslims and against certain Sufi positions are well known.

His position matters because the shaykh al-Islam of the Safavid capital was not merely an honorific scholar. It carried public responsibilities for the application of religious law and for the supervision of practices presented as Islamic obligations. Yet the authority of the office should not be confused with a monopoly over Shi‘i religious knowledge. Majlisi also possessed scholarly standing earned through teaching and the circulation of his works. Biographical notices preserve the names of numerous students and recipients of his authorizations to transmit learning (ijazat), although later assertions about the total size of his student body cannot be independently verified. His influence therefore rested on several partly overlapping foundations: recognition among scholars, access to the ruler, an official post, and a network of students and readers. Those sources of power could reinforce one another, but neither an appointment nor a royal decree compelled every jurist or community to accept his arguments.

Claims about the consequences of his campaigns also require discrimination between contemporary records and later polemics. Majlisi argued forcefully against Sunni doctrine and against particular Sufi beliefs and practices; nevertheless, his polemical attacks were not proof that all Sufi activity ceased or that philosophy vanished from Safavid education. Accounts of an image venerated by visitors to Isfahan being destroyed in the year of his appointment, for example, appear in later biographical traditions, whose description of the object and community is unclear. His support for stricter religious observance must be evaluated against the limits of actual enforcement. Even favorable biographers acknowledge measures that did not succeed in transforming public conduct as intended. Nor can royal measures under Sultan Husayn automatically be attributed to Majlisi personally without identifying the decree, its date, and the evidence connecting him to it. The historical issue is not whether he mattered—his office, writings, and teaching make that clear—but how authority exercised through a court differed from the broader social acceptance of religious prescriptions.

Several edicts attributed to the reign of Sultan Husayn, including measures against wine and other practices judged improper, are often connected to Majlisi's influence. The exact scope of these measures, the degree to which they were enforced, and the extent of Majlisi's personal responsibility are all matters on which the sources, mostly written after the fall of the dynasty, are not fully reliable. Later writers, including some who sought to explain the fall of Isfahan in 1722, held the supposed bigotry or weakness of the late Safavid court responsible, and some blamed the clergy. Historians now treat such moralizing explanations with reserve, because the causes of the collapse were chiefly military, fiscal, and political, as the chapters on the late Safavid state will show, and because the later literature that attributes the fall to clerical influence had its own purposes.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Independent Was the Twelver Learned Class from the Safavid State?

The question: Did the Twelver scholars of the Safavid period act mainly as dependents of the monarchy, holding offices and incomes by royal favor, or did they develop a measure of autonomy that outlasted the dynasty?

Interpretation A — Integration into the state: Some influential studies, notably that of Said Amir Arjomand, emphasize the creation under the Safavids of a state-sponsored religious establishment in which jurists held offices, drew salaries, and depended on royal patronage, and they see the more independent clerical authority of later centuries as largely a post-Safavid development in changed conditions.
Interpretation B — Growing autonomy: Other scholars point to the independent sources of scholars' standing: their licenses and learning, their family networks, their endowment income, their following among townspeople, and the disputes in which they criticized the court. On this view, the learned class had a measure of autonomy even at the height of Safavid power, and the Safavid period laid foundations for the later authority of jurists.

Evidence: Both positions draw on juristic treatises, administrative manuals, chronicles, endowment documents, and biographical sources, and on comparison with the post-Safavid period. The evidence is difficult because the same scholar could be both a salaried official and an independent teacher.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis, and also chronology: when, and in what conditions, did the learned class acquire the independence it later displayed? The two views are not mutually exclusive, since a scholar could be dependent in some respects and autonomous in others.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one traces the history of clerical authority in Iran, whether as a development that began under the Safavids or as one that arose after their collapse, and how one reads the relationship between religion and government in later periods.

Section 58.20

Historical Synthesis: What Had Been Institutionalized by 1722, and What Had Not

By the time Afghan forces besieged Isfahan and the dynasty fell in 1722, the Iranian plateau had been ruled for two centuries by a government that had proclaimed Twelver Shi‘ism as its religion. This chapter has asked what that proclamation became. The answer is not a single institution or a single policy. It is an accumulation: a body of scholars who taught in colleges supported by endowments; officials who administered religious offices and endowments; a set of legal practices that shaped marriage, inheritance, and property; a public ritual calendar centered on the mourning for Husayn; a geography of shrines that connected the dynasty and its subjects to the imams; and a growing body of Persian and Arabic religious literature. These elements reinforced each other, and together they made Twelver Shi‘ism a deeply embedded part of Iranian public life.

The process was uneven in time. In the first generations after 1501, the proclamation and its enforcement dominated, accompanied by the recruitment of scholars from abroad and the beginnings of juristic adaptation. In the middle decades of the sixteenth century, the question of how jurists should relate to the state occupied the learned, and the offices of the religious administration took shape. In the early seventeenth century, with the capital at Isfahan, the scholarly infrastructure reached its fullest development, and the cult of shrines and the public ceremonies of Muharram became prominent. In the late seventeenth century, the consolidation of orthodoxy associated with Majlisi and others brought both a rich literature and a more restrictive attitude toward Sufis, philosophers, and non-Muslims. Each of these phases left layers that persisted.

It was also uneven in space. The capital, and a few other cities, had a density of institutions that most of the country lacked. Many villages, tribal groups, and borderland regions experienced the Twelver order mainly through occasional preachers and local shrines. Sunni communities persisted in several regions, non-Muslim communities maintained their own institutions, and heterodox and Sufi currents continued at the margins and in popular practice. What can be said of the country as a whole is that Twelver Shi‘ism had become the dominant public religion and that the institutions that sustained it were in place in the cities, not that every Iranian shared a single religious experience.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why the Proclamation of 1501 Became an Institutional Order

Long-term causes

Existing Twelver communities and learned traditions in Iran and the Arab lands; a Persian-language culture of devotion to the Prophet's family; the medieval development of Twelver jurisprudence; the weakness of rival Sunni dynasties and institutions in the region.

Short-term causes

Ismail's need to secure legitimacy beyond the Qizilbash; the shortage of Twelver scholars in Iran; the dynasty's willingness to recruit and pay jurists from Jabal Amil, Bahrain, and Iraq; the Ottoman and Uzbek rivalry that gave religious identity strategic importance.

Immediate trigger

The proclamation at Tabriz in 1501 and the demands of governing a diverse population under a newly declared state religion.

Immediate effects

Coercive measures against Sunni officials; the migration of scholars; the creation of offices such as the sadr; early juristic arguments over the authority of the jurist and the Friday prayer.

Long-term consequences

A durable system of colleges, endowments, courts, public rituals, and shrine pilgrimage; a Twelver majority in much of the plateau; a clerical class with its own networks; and a body of literature in Arabic and Persian that continued to shape Iranian religion after 1722.

Historical complexity

No single actor made the system. The monarchy, the Arab and Iranian scholars, merchants and donors, preachers, local communities, and the wider geopolitical situation each contributed. Different outcomes were possible, and the system's extent and character at any moment remained uncertain.

Why a single cause is insufficient: Royal decree, scholarly migration, devotional appeal, and economic interest each explain part of the process. None explains the whole, and the sources do not permit a precise weighting.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Religious Life on the Iranian Plateau, c. 1500–1722

What changed?

  • The public religion of the state, from varied Sunni and Sufi-inflected forms to an officially Twelver order
  • The personnel of religious office and law, which came increasingly to be trained Twelver scholars
  • The ritual calendar, with Muharram and the cult of the imams at its center
  • The geography of devotion, with Mashhad, Qum, and Ardabil elevated by state patronage
  • The language of religious literature, which increasingly included Persian alongside Arabic

What remained continuous?

  • Local saints, shrines, and customary practice in villages and among pastoral groups
  • The role of endowments, judges, and notaries in daily legal life
  • Sunni, Jewish, Armenian, Zoroastrian, and other communities, with their own institutions
  • Sufi devotion in many forms, despite the weakening of the formal orders
  • Devotion to the family of the Prophet that had spread across confessional lines before 1501

When did change occur, and what was its character? The change was gradual and uneven, with a violent opening, a long middle of institution-building, and a late phase of consolidation that was also more restrictive. It was contested at every stage.

Who experienced change most directly? Sunni scholars and officials in the early decades, Arab and Iranian scholars who entered the new system, and urban residents who encountered the new ceremonies and offices. Villagers, pastoralists, and many non-Muslims experienced greater continuity in their daily routines, though they too felt its effects.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against treating the Safavid religious transformation either as an instant conversion or as a mere change of label. It was a long process of institution-building whose results were substantial but incomplete.

An assessment of the Safavid religious achievement should also note what remained unsettled in 1722. The authority of the jurist in relation to the state had been argued without being fixed. The dispute between Usulis and Akhbaris was unresolved, and its resolution would take place after the dynasty's fall and largely outside Iran, in the shrine cities of Iraq. The clerical class had no formal hierarchy, and the sources of its income were varied and uncertain. The collapse of central authority after 1722 would test what had been built. That the Twelver order survived the fall of the dynasty, and that the scholars, shrines, and rituals continued under later rulers, is evidence that it had acquired a life of its own. How it developed afterward belongs to later chapters.

A word of caution about terminology completes the chapter's argument. Historians sometimes describe the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century process as confessionalization, a term borrowed from the study of early modern Europe for the way states and churches together defined and enforced religious identities. The term is a modern analytical one, not a historical actor's category, and it fits some features of Safavid Iran, such as state sponsorship of a confession, the recruitment of religious specialists, and the creation of public rituals, while fitting others poorly, such as the absence of a formal church hierarchy and the persistence of religious diversity. Students may use it as a comparative tool without assuming that Iran followed a European pattern. The history of Twelver Shi‘ism in Safavid Iran has to be understood on its own terms, in the light of its own evidence, which this chapter has tried to present with its limitations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked how Twelver Shi‘ism developed from the religion proclaimed by Shah Ismail at Tabriz in 1501 into a system of institutions, legal authority, education, pilgrimage, ritual, and everyday practice, and why that development remained uneven and contested. Its central argument is that the proclamation was a beginning and not an accomplishment. A decree could change the Friday sermon and the coinage, but it could not by itself supply judges, teachers, textbooks, endowments, or the habits of a population. Those had to be built over generations, by many actors, under changing political conditions.

The starting point was a religious landscape that was already varied. Sunni law and learning flourished in many cities, Twelver communities and scholarly traditions existed in parts of Iran and the Arab lands, Sufi orders attracted followers across social ranks, and devotion to the family of the Prophet crossed confessional lines. The early Safavid measures, including the replacement of Sunni officials, the ritual of tabarra, and the use of force in some cities, were real and in places violent, but the sources do not support a picture of uniform and simultaneous coercion. Accommodation, flight, concealment, and the survival of Sunni communities were part of the story from the beginning.

Because Iran had few Twelver jurists in 1501, the dynasty recruited scholars from Jabal Amil, Bahrain, and Iraq, as well as from Iranian centers such as Qum and Kashan. They arrived over generations through scholarly and family networks and not in a single migration. The best-known of them, Ali al-Karaki, argued that the qualified jurist could act as general deputy of the absent Imam, and his work, and the disputes it provoked, show jurists adapting the law to the realities of a Twelver monarchy while disagreeing about how far such cooperation should go.

The Safavid state created a religious administration with offices such as the sadr, the shaykh al-Islam, the judge, and the prayer leader of the Friday congregation. These offices were filled by appointment and sometimes by inheritance, and their relations to one another and to the monarchy changed with reigns and personalities. The learned class that filled them was formed in colleges supported by endowments and organized around teachers, licenses, and scholarly families. Arabic remained the language of advanced scholarship, while a growing literature in Persian carried teaching to wider audiences.

The central legal and conceptual problems concerned authority in the Imam's absence. The doctrines of ijtihad and taqlid distinguished the scholar who derived rulings from the believer who followed them, and the dispute over the Friday prayer showed that even the basic question of whether and under whom Twelvers could hold the weekly congregation remained open. In the seventeenth century the Akhbari critique, associated with al-Astarabadi, challenged the rational methods of the Usulis, and the resulting controversy showed that Twelver legal method was unsettled at the peak of Safavid power. The Usuli triumph came later, largely outside Iran.

Law reached ordinary people through courts, notaries, endowments, and the forms of marriage, inheritance, and contract. Waqf gave religious institutions an income independent of the treasury and gave families and officials stakes in their management. Documents show the spread of Twelver legal forms in towns, though the evidence for villages and pastoral groups is thin and customary law remained important. The financing of the clergy by religious dues was far less systematic than in later periods.

Ritual and pilgrimage made the faith a public and emotional experience. The remembrance of Karbala in Muharram, supported by Persian narrative literature such as Kashifi's Rawzat al-Shuhada, gave the community a shared language of mourning, while the shrines of Mashhad, Qum, Ardabil, and the many imamzadas supplied a sacred geography. The greatest shrines of Iraq lay mostly under Ottoman rule, which gave added weight to the shrines within Safavid territory and to royal acts of pilgrimage and endowment. Sufi orders, philosophers, and Qizilbash devotion were reshaped, marginalized, or absorbed in this order, though some persisted in popular practice and intellectual life.

The religious order was not uniform in space or society. Sunni populations remained in Kurdistan, the Baluch country, the Gulf coast, and frontier districts. Armenians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others lived under changing conditions of protection and pressure. Women acted as donors, mourners, and pilgrims, though the sources rarely record their own voices, and merchants were major supporters of institutions. The late seventeenth century, associated with Majlisi, brought both a great consolidation of literature and a more restrictive attitude toward some currents, though the extent and effects of this are debated.

By 1722 Twelver Shi‘ism was the dominant public religion of the plateau, embedded in colleges, courts, endowments, rituals, and shrines. Yet the relationship of jurists to the state, the method of the law, the finances and hierarchy of the clergy, and the reach of the faith into the countryside remained unsettled. The historical debates in the chapter, on whether Twelverization was imposed or gradual and on how independent the learned class was, show that these questions remain open. The chapter's conclusion is that institutionalization was a long, collaborative, and uneven process whose results survived the dynasty that began it, and that it is best understood as such and not as the realization of a plan.

The Shi‘i practice of public disavowal of those held to be enemies of the Prophet's family; its intensity and form varied over time.
Sadr
The senior Safavid official responsible for endowments and the religious establishment, whose office changed over time.
Shaykh al-Islam
A senior religious office, in Safavid cities often held by a leading scholar with legal and administrative duties.
Qadi
A judge applying religious law, who issued sealed documents of marriage, inheritance, contract, and endowment.
‘Urf
Customary and royal law, applied by officials such as the divanbegi, alongside religious law.
Imam-i jum‘a
The leader of the Friday congregational prayer in a city, an office supported by the court.
Ijaza
A written license from a teacher authorizing a student to transmit a text or issue legal opinions.
Madrasa
A college for religious and related learning, usually supported by endowment.
Waqf
An endowment dedicating the income of property to a religious or charitable purpose, managed by a mutawalli.
Khums
A levy of one-fifth on certain gains in Twelver law, part of which tradition assigns to the Imam; its collection under the Safavids is uncertain.
Mut‘a (sigha)
Temporary marriage, a fixed-term contract recognized in Twelver law and rejected by most Sunni jurists.
Muharram and Ashura
The first Islamic month and its tenth day, when Husayn's death at Karbala in 680 is commemorated.
Rawza-khani
Recitation of the sufferings of Husayn and his companions, named after Kashifi's Rawzat al-Shuhada.
Ta‘ziya
Mourning rites and, in later centuries, dramatic enactments of Karbala, fully documented mainly from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
Majlis
A mourning assembly at which the sufferings of the imams are narrated.
Ziyara
Visitation of the tombs of the Prophet, imams, and their descendants, with prescribed prayers.
Imamzada
The tomb of a descendant of an imam, a focus of local devotion across Iran.
Bast
Sanctuary offered at shrines to those in danger of arrest or vengeance.
Qizilbash
The Turkmen military supporters of the Safavids, whose devotion to the dynasty combined loyalty and religious reverence.
Jabal Amil
A region of southern Lebanon that supplied many Twelver scholars to Safavid Iran.
Confessionalization
A modern analytical term for the joint work of state and religious bodies in defining and enforcing religious identity; applicable to Iran only with qualification.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a religious identity that later seemed natural to much of Iran was in fact assembled. The proclamation of 1501 gave it a public beginning, but colleges, courts, endowments, rituals, and shrines gave it durability, and each of these depended on the work of scholars, officials, donors, preachers, and ordinary participants. Seeing the work that was done is a corrective to both the view that Iran was always Shi‘i and the view that it became so by decree.

The chapter also reveals a larger process, the way states and religious communities need each other and yet remain distinct. The Safavid monarchy needed the legitimacy and competence of the jurists, and the jurists needed the patronage and protection of the monarchy. Their disagreements over the Friday prayer, over cooperation with rulers, and over the method of law show that the relationship was negotiated and never settled. That tension would shape Iranian history long after the dynasty had gone.

Several continuities survived 1722. The shrines, the Muharram calendar, the scholarly networks, and the Persian and Arabic literature continued under later rulers, and the unresolved questions about juristic authority and legal method were resolved, in the following century, in ways the Safavid scholars had not foreseen. Students should be careful to treat the later history on its own terms and not as a fulfillment of Safavid arrangements.

Finally, the chapter prepares for what follows. Later chapters return to the political history of Tahmasp, Abbas I, and Isfahan, and to the social history of tribes, gender, and minorities, and Chapter 65 takes up the ulama, shrines, and the growth of clerical authority in more detail. Readers who carry forward the lessons of this chapter, about the difference between proclamation and institution, between official claim and lived practice, and between evidence of intention and evidence of effect, will be well prepared for those chapters.
