Iranian Elites, Secretaries, and the Abbasid Imperial System

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Abbasid revolution brought a new dynasty to power, but dynasties do not govern by victory alone. Governing required registers, assessors, couriers, accountants, governors, and the cooperation of landholding families in every province, and in the decades after 750 a remarkable share of that expertise, and of the military and political leadership that protected it, came from Iranian lands. This chapter asks how that happened, what it changed, and where it stopped.

In the summer of 762 the caliph al-Mansur began construction on the banks of the Tigris of a new capital, a circular city that its contemporaries called Madinat al-Salam, the City of Peace, and that later generations knew simply as Baghdad. According to the historical tradition preserved by later writers, the foundation was no merely ceremonial act. The caliph is said to have consulted astrologers, engineers, and administrators about the site, and the persons reported to have advised him included men whose names and family backgrounds point toward Iraq, Khuzestan, Fars, and Khurasan. Even allowing for the later embellishment that surrounds the story, the episode illustrates something that is well established. The early Abbasid court was a place where Arab, Iranian, Aramaic-speaking, and other expertise met, and where decisions about taxation, armies, communications, and the siting of the capital itself were made by officials of widely differing origins.

Chapter 30 explained how a revolutionary movement organized in Khurasan overthrew the Umayyad dynasty between 747 and 750. That chapter's story ended in victory. This chapter begins with its administrative aftermath. A movement that had mobilized Arab settlers, Iranian converts, local notables, and discontented villagers around the claim of rule by a member of the Prophet's family now had to collect revenue from Egypt to Sogdiana, pay soldiers, answer petitions, appoint governors, and prevent the many forces that had supported the revolution from turning against one another. Revolutionary leadership and imperial administration are different skills, and the Abbasids drew on whatever personnel and institutional practices could supply the second.

Many of those practices were older than the dynasty. The Umayyad caliphate had already built diwans, a postal system, a coinage, and a cadre of secretaries. Before the Arab conquests the Sasanian state, examined in Chapters 22 and 23, had maintained scribes, tax registers, and provincial administrators across Iran and Mesopotamia, and Byzantine, Aramaic, and Coptic-speaking administrative traditions persisted in the western provinces. The central historical problem of this chapter is how Iranian participants, as military commanders, landholders, secretaries, and ministers, shaped the way these inheritances were combined in the early Abbasid state, and how their influence, which was real and substantial, was also conditional, contested, and often precarious.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, "Iranian" here is a historical-civilizational term in the sense established in Chapter 1, and it covers people who spoke Middle Persian, Sogdian, Bactrian, Parthian-derived dialects, and Arabic, who were Muslims, Zoroastrians, Christians, Jews, and Buddhists by origin or conviction, and who did not necessarily share a political program. Second, the chapter does not describe a Persian restoration. The Abbasid caliphs presented themselves as Muslim rulers descended from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas, and their state was an Islamic imperial system in which Arabic became the language of government. Third, language and ethnicity are kept separate: an Arabic-writing secretary of Iranian descent was not thereby an Arab, and an Arab official might use Persian administrative methods.

The evidence for these developments is uneven, and the chapter says so as it goes. The principal narrative sources, such as the historical work of al-Tabari (d. 923), the works of al-Yaʿqubi and al-Baladhuri, and the compilation of biographies of viziers and secretaries by al-Jahshiyari (d. 942 or 943), were written in Iraq a century or more after the events they describe. They preserve official lists, genuine documents, and also dramatic anecdotes shaped by later literary tastes. Against these stand a smaller body of contemporary or near-contemporary evidence: legal and fiscal writing such as Abu Yusuf's treatise on land tax prepared for Harun al-Rashid, the advice literature of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, coins and seals, papyri and parchment documents from Egypt, Bactria, and Sogdiana, and the sparse physical remains of the Abbasid cities. Students should learn to ask which kind of evidence supports each claim.

The chapter proceeds in five movements. The first (31.1–31.3) examines the transition from revolution to government and the continuing role of the Khurasani military coalition. The second (31.4–31.7) examines Iranian landholders, the question of Sasanian inheritance, the dīwāns, and the secretarial profession. The third (31.8–31.10) examines the machinery of rule: taxation, provincial governance, communications, and the new capital at Baghdad. The fourth (31.11–31.14) follows the intellectual and political careers of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ and the Barmakid family. The fifth (31.15–31.16) turns to al-Maʾmun, al-Fadl ibn Sahl, and the renewed importance of Khurasan, and then assesses the limits of Iranian participation. The chapter closes where Chapter 32 begins: with the question of how religious change and Iranian identity unfolded among the populations governed by this system.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Abbasid victory of 750 created new administrative and political problems distinct from those of revolutionary mobilization.
  2. Trace the consolidation of caliphal authority under al-Mansur, including the control of powerful commanders such as Abu Muslim and the founding of Baghdad.
  3. Describe the role of Khurasani soldiers and commanders in the early Abbasid state and explain why "Khurasani" is not a synonym for "Iranian" or "Persian."
  4. Analyze the position of dehqāns and other provincial notables as intermediaries between the state and local society.
  5. Distinguish documented administrative continuities among Sasanian, Umayyad, and Abbasid institutions from retrospective claims of Persian influence.
  6. Describe the principal dīwāns and explain how records, correspondence, and accounting made imperial control possible.
  7. Assess the training, recruitment, patronage, and political vulnerability of the kuttāb as a professional class.
  8. Explain how land revenue was assessed and collected and identify whose labor supported the imperial fiscal system.
  9. Interpret Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's political advice as evidence for the relationship between administrative expertise and ideas of rulership.
  10. Compare the careers of the Barmakids and al-Fadl ibn Sahl as examples of the opportunities and hazards of Iranian political advancement.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the fall of the Barmakids in 803, distinguishing established events from later embellishment.
  12. Evaluate the extent and limits of Iranian influence on the early Abbasid imperial system without describing it as either a Persian restoration or an unchanged Arab administration.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is winning a revolution different from governing an empire, and what did the Abbasids need that revolutionary mobilization could not supply?
  2. How did Iranian landholders, soldiers, and secretaries come to occupy positions of influence in a caliphate whose official language and religion were Arabic and Islamic?
  3. What evidence allows historians to separate genuine Sasanian administrative inheritance from later claims that the Abbasid state was "Persian"?
  4. How did administrative expertise, such as accounting, drafting, and tax assessment, become a source of political power, and why was that power so fragile?
  5. Whose labor and agricultural output supported the early Abbasid fiscal system, and how did they experience it?
  6. How should historians read the story of the Barmakids, given that most of the narrative was written long after their fall and shaped by literary taste?
  7. What did Khurasan mean for the Abbasid political order after 750, and why did it again become politically central under al-Maʾmun?
  8. Why should historians avoid describing Iranian participation in the caliphate as either a restoration of Persian rule or mere employment within an Arab system?

Historical Context

Students arriving at this chapter should carry forward three bodies of earlier history. The first is the Sasanian administrative world described in Chapters 22 through 24. The Sasanian empire governed a large territory through a combination of royal officials, a scribal class whose members are called dibīrān in Middle Persian, provincial governors, and landholding families of the lesser nobility known as dehqāns, and tradition credits Khosrow I (examined in Chapter 27) with a systematic land survey and a fixed system of land and poll taxes. How much of that system functioned as later sources describe is debated, but it is clear that fiscal records, written correspondence, and a trained scribal profession existed and that many of their practitioners remained in place after the Arab conquests.

The second body of background is the conquest-era and Umayyad settlement described in Chapters 28 and 29. In many provinces the conquerors initially left existing local administration intact, collecting tribute through established intermediaries and conducting business in Middle Persian, Greek, Aramaic, Bactrian, Sogdian, or Coptic. Over time the caliphate imposed its own structures. Papyri and reports in later chronicles show that in the late seventh century, under the caliph Abd al-Malik and his governor al-Hajjaj, the central fiscal bureaus of Iraq were Arabized, a change associated by tradition with a Persian-speaking secretary from Sistan named Salih ibn Abd al-Rahman, who is reported to have translated the registers into Arabic. In Khurasan, by contrast, the sources point to Arabic replacing Persian in official registers only in the 740s, much later than in Iraq. Administrative Arabization was therefore a gradual and regionally uneven process, and the secretaries who carried it out were often themselves of Iranian or Aramaic-speaking background.

The third body of background is the political situation after 750. The Abbasid movement had been a coalition. It included Arab settlers in Merv and other Khurasani garrison towns, Iranian clients (mawālī) and local landholders, Alid sympathizers who understood the cause as one of the family of Ali, and villagers whose grievances were fiscal and local. Once the revolution had succeeded, these groups wanted different things. The Abbasid family, itself a minority within the Prophet's extended kin, had to decide whom to reward, whom to restrain, and whose expertise to rely upon. The solutions arrived at between 750 and the early ninth century were not planned in advance. They emerged through improvisation, conflict, and negotiation, and they involved the executions and rebellions that this chapter describes alongside the quieter story of bureaucratic growth.

Finally, students should keep in mind the geography of the empire. The Umayyad empire that the Abbasids overthrew had stretched from Iberia and North Africa across Egypt, Syria, Iraq, and Iran into Transoxiana and the Indus region. The Abbasids inherited most of that realm but not all of it: an Umayyad survivor established an independent emirate in al-Andalus in 756, and Abbasid authority in the far west remained slight. Effective Abbasid government centered on Egypt, Syria, Iraq, Iran, and Transoxiana, and the dynasty moved the center of gravity of the empire eastward from Damascus toward Iraq. The lands of the former Sasanian empire, with their large agricultural populations, their irrigated plains, and their tax registers, supplied a larger share of caliphal revenue than the western provinces. This is part of the explanation, though not the whole of it, for the weight of Iranian and Mesopotamian administrators in the early Abbasid court.

Section 31.1

From Revolution to Administration: The Abbasid State after 750

The Abbasid movement that triumphed in 750 was an alliance built for a single purpose. It had overthrown the Umayyad dynasty by combining the organizational network of its missionaries, the military strength of the Khurasani armies, and the discontent of many groups who believed the Umayyads had governed unjustly. Once the Umayyad caliph Marwan II had been defeated and killed, the alliance lost its unifying objective, and its component groups faced the question of what they would gain from victory. The first Abbasid caliph, Abu al-Abbas, known by the later epithet al-Saffah, ruled from 749 to 754 and spent much of his reign eliminating Umayyad rivals and securing recognition in Iraq, Syria, and the east. Later sources report that many members of the Umayyad family were killed in these years, though the scale and the individual episodes were elaborated in subsequent retellings.

Governing required more than eliminating rivals. The Abbasids inherited the physical and institutional apparatus of the Umayyad caliphate: a coinage struck in gold, silver, and copper, a network of garrison cities, a system of registers for the army and for taxation, a courier service, and a body of secretaries who understood how these things worked. The surviving evidence suggests that the Abbasids did not dismantle this machinery. They retained much of its personnel, relocated its center of gravity from Syria to Iraq, and then adjusted its operation to the needs of a dynasty whose legitimacy rested on a family claim and on the loyalty of a particular army. The result was neither a continuation of Umayyad practice nor a clean break from it.

The early months of Abbasid rule also show that the new dynasty did not yet control its own coalition. According to the later historical tradition, a Kufan secretary named Abu Salama al-Khallal, who had acted as a kind of coordinator for the movement in Iraq and was styled the "wazīr of the family of Muhammad," was put to death in 750 on the caliph's behalf, with the involvement of Abu Muslim reported but its details disputed. Whatever the full story, the episode indicates that ministerial office in these years was an informal position resting on personal trust rather than a defined post, and that the early Abbasids were prepared to remove men who had been instrumental in bringing them to power. This is a pattern that recurs throughout the chapter.

The Abbasids' problem was therefore double. They had to build a stable administrative system for an empire that now centered on Iraq and the Iranian plateau, and they had to manage the political forces, military, tribal, religious, and regional, that had made the revolution and expected to share in its rewards. The Khurasani army expected pay and status. The Arab garrison populations of Syria, who had been the mainstay of the Umayyads, had to be reconciled or sidelined. The Alids, descendants of the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law Ali, had been promised, in the movement's early propaganda, a rule in the name of the Prophet's family, and in the decades after 750 some of them concluded that they had been displaced by their Abbasid cousins. The rebellion of Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya in Medina and of his brother Ibrahim in Basra in 762–763 showed that this view could produce armed challenge.

The evidence for these years is largely narrative and retrospective. Writers of the ninth and tenth centuries, working in Baghdad and under the patronage of later caliphs, describe the founding generation with a mixture of reverence and critical distance. They supply names, offices, and sequences of events, and some of this can be cross-checked against coins, which bear the names of caliphs and often governors, and against surviving papyri. The reader should treat reported conversations and motives with caution, and treat structural conclusions, such as that secretaries and military leaders both sought influence and the caliph sought to balance them, as better supported than the details of individual intrigues.

Section 31.2

Al-Mansur and the Consolidation of Caliphal Authority

Abu Jaʿfar al-Mansur, who ruled from 754 to 775, is generally regarded as the true founder of the Abbasid state in the sense that he turned a victorious movement into a functioning monarchy. His accession was challenged at once by his uncle Abdallah ibn Ali, who had led the Syrian campaign against the Umayyads and who claimed the succession on the basis of a reported understanding with the previous caliph. Al-Mansur sent Abu Muslim, the leader of the Khurasani army, to confront him, and Abu Muslim defeated Abdallah in 754. The immediate result was to strengthen al-Mansur against one rival while leaving him confronting a more dangerous one: a commander who held the loyalty of the Khurasani soldiers and enjoyed an independent reputation among supporters of the revolution.

In 755 al-Mansur summoned Abu Muslim to his camp near the former Sasanian capital region of Ctesiphon and had him killed. The sources agree on the broad outline, though they differ on the exact circumstances and on how far the caliph's accusations against Abu Muslim were justified. The consequences reveal how complicated the Khurasani movement had been. Soon after the execution, an Iranian commander named Sunbadh rose in revolt in the region of Rayy and Nishapur, apparently in the name of avenging Abu Muslim, and was defeated within a few months. Later, in 767–768, a revolt led by Ustadhsis began in the region of Herat, Badghis, and Sistan and was suppressed only after substantial fighting. These movements are only partly understood. They combine loyalty to a murdered leader, local resistance to Abbasid fiscal and political control, and, in some reports, religious ideas about prophecy and succession that Chapter 32 will discuss. They show that Iranian regions did not support the new dynasty uniformly.

Al-Mansur's relationship with the men who ran his administration illustrates his method. He relied on talented officials, many of them from families whose members had served earlier regimes, and he also watched them closely and removed them when he judged them dangerous or dishonest. Abu Ayyub al-Muriyani, a secretary from the Khuzestan region who rose to be the caliph's chief administrator, was dismissed and killed around 770. Reports from the period describe a caliph who audited his officials' accounts and confiscated the wealth of those suspected of embezzlement or disloyalty, a practice known as muṣādara that became a routine tool of Abbasid government. Later tradition remembered al-Mansur as intensely frugal, giving him a nickname that alluded to counting small coins, and as a ruler who left a large treasury. These characterizations may be exaggerated, but they point to a real concern with fiscal discipline.

One often-quoted tradition has al-Mansur saying that his rule depended on four kinds of men: a judge, a police chief, a tax official, and a head of the courier service who would tell him the truth about what was happening in the provinces. The attribution cannot be verified, and the story is best read as a later formulation of how the early Abbasid state was understood to work. It does, however, correspond to practices that are otherwise documented. The caliph depended on information, on honest tax collection, and on the personal oversight of the provincial governors who were his agents.

Al-Mansur's reign also saw the extension of Abbasid power into regions that had remained partly outside caliphal control. The mountainous province of Tabaristan on the Caspian coast was ruled by a local dynasty of ispahbads who had maintained a degree of autonomy since the conquests. In the campaigns of about 759 to 761, Abbasid armies, in which Khalid ibn Barmak is reported to have participated, brought Tabaristan under direct caliphal control. The case illustrates a larger point. The consolidation of authority in this period was not only a matter of defeating rivals at the center but also of extending the reach of a fiscal and military administration into regions whose rulers had earlier negotiated their own terms with the caliphate.

Section 31.3

Khurasani Soldiers, Commanders, and the New Political Elite

The army that had carried the Abbasid revolution westward is usually called the Khurasaniyya, and its descendants in the capital region are often called the abnāʾ al-dawla, "sons of the dynasty" or "sons of the state." The term "Khurasani" in this context refers to origin in the region of Khurasan and to participation in the revolutionary movement, not to ethnicity or language. The soldiers included Arab settlers whose families had lived for generations in Merv and other garrison towns of Khurasan, as well as local converts and clients of various origins. Commanders such as Qahtaba ibn Shabib, Abu Dawud Khalid ibn Ibrahim, and Khazim ibn Khuzayma were themselves Arab by descent or tribal affiliation, while Abu Muslim's own origins were disputed in the sources, which attributed to him an Iranian, Arab, or client background. Describing the Khurasani military as simply "Persian" would misrepresent a group that was ethnically mixed and defined above all by loyalty to the Abbasid cause.

After the revolution, the Khurasani soldiers became the dynasty's principal military force. They were paid through the register of the army, the dīwān al-jund (examined in 31.6), and the caliphs rewarded their leaders with governorships, estates, and positions in the court. Many were settled in and around Baghdad, in quarters named after commanders and associated with particular groups; the district of al-Harbiyya, for example, took its name from Harb ibn Abdallah, one of al-Mansur's Khurasani commanders. The design of the new capital reflected this reliance: one of the four principal gates of the Round City, discussed in 31.10, was named for Khurasan. Their descendants, the abnāʾ, remained a political force into the ninth century.

Military patronage created its own tensions. The Khurasani commanders expected deference, and the caliph needed their loyalty without allowing them to become independent. Al-Mansur's execution of Abu Muslim was the clearest expression of that tension, but it was not the only one. In 758 a group known as the Rawandiyya, composed in part of Khurasani soldiers, attacked the caliph's palace at Hashimiyya in the name of beliefs that treated him as an object of religious veneration; the episode was put down by loyal commanders, and it shows how revolutionary religious enthusiasm could become a security problem for the regime it had created. At about the same time, around 758–759, Abd al-Jabbar, the governor of Khurasan, rebelled against the caliph and was defeated by forces under the heir apparent, al-Mahdi, sent from the west. The relationship between provincial armies and the caliphal court was therefore unstable.

The political rise of the Khurasani military did not occur apart from the administrative changes described in this chapter. Military leaders and secretaries competed for influence, and the competition sometimes took the form of rivalry between men who claimed legitimacy through service in the revolution and men who claimed it through administrative competence. A caliph who relied too heavily on the army risked dependence on its commanders; a caliph who relied on secretaries risked the resentment of soldiers. The solutions varied. Al-Mansur used both groups and played them against one another. His successor al-Mahdi, who ruled from 775 to 785, broadened the circle of men who might hold high office and relied increasingly on civilian officials. By the reign of Harun al-Rashid the military and administrative elites overlapped, as many of the soldiers' sons had become officials.

For the history of the Iranian lands, the significance of the Khurasani soldiers is double. They tied Khurasan, one of the most important regions of the Iranian world as Chapter 1 emphasized, to the political center of the caliphate in ways that lasted for generations. At the same time, because the Khurasani soldiers were ethnically mixed and increasingly based in Iraq, they did not represent a distinct Iranian political project. The politics of the period are better understood as a competition among elites using regional, religious, and personal networks than as a contest between Arabs and Persians.

Section 31.4

Iranian Landholding Families and the Survival of Local Authority

Beneath the military and court elites stood a wider class of local notables whose role in this period is easy to overlook because they rarely appear in the chronicles. In the Sasanian world, the lesser landholding nobility are called dehqāns in the later Persian and Arabic sources, from a word meaning roughly "village lord." They held and managed land, supervised cultivators, maintained local records, and stood between the peasantry and the state. After the conquests, the new rulers needed them. A caliphate that lacked a large cadre of its own officials in every district could not have collected taxes in the Iranian countryside without men who knew who owned which fields, who could vouch for the village headmen, and who understood the irrigation schedules.

The mechanisms of this dependence varied across regions, and it is a mistake to speak of a single class. In the irrigated plains of southern Iraq, known as the Sawad, local landholders and village leaders served as tax guarantors and collectors. In Khurasan and Transoxiana the system was more varied, since local rulers with titles such as the Bukhar-khudah of Bukhara or the Ikhshid of Ferghana retained princely status and collected revenue under agreements with caliphal governors. In the mountains of Tabaristan, as noted, a ruling dynasty held out until the late 750s. In Fars and Khuzestan, the heartlands of the Sasanian administrative tradition, many local scribal and landholding families had continued in office since the conquests.

Documents from the eastern provinces offer rare direct evidence of these relationships. The Sogdian archive found at Mount Mugh in Tajikistan in the 1930s includes letters and contracts from the early eighth century that show a local ruler, Dewashtich, corresponding with Arab governors and with other local authorities in a Sogdian world that continued to function in its own language. Parchments in the Bactrian language from northern Afghanistan, the latest of which date to the eighth century, record sales, loans, and tax matters among local landholders. Narratives of Bukhara and Khurasan composed in Persian in the tenth century and later preserve traditions about the relations between local lords and Arab governors, though these are later and shaped by their own concerns. Together these sources show that local elites often continued to operate through their own languages, networks, and customary law long after the arrival of Arab rule.

The fortunes of these families after 750 were far from uniform. Some prospered by converting to Islam, aligning with Abbasid officials, and entering government service as ʿāmils (local officials) or secretaries. Some lost land and status in the upheavals of the revolution and in the revolts that followed. Some retained local power by cooperating with Arab governors while preserving a separate identity in family tradition. Others, especially in mountainous regions, resisted until military force brought them into the fiscal system. The Barmakids of Balkh (31.12) and the family of al-Fadl ibn Sahl (31.15) represent the successful end of this spectrum, but the existence of such exceptional careers should not obscure the many local notables who simply remained in their districts, supervising cultivation and paying what was demanded.

The mechanism by which local influence became imperial service is worth stating plainly. A family with local knowledge and standing was useful to a governor; a governor who valued such a family might appoint its members to fiscal posts; if the family's members performed well, they might be recommended to the central dīwāns or the court; and once there, they could use their networks to bring in kinsmen and clients. The movement from village or provincial position to the center was therefore a matter of patronage and demonstrated competence rather than formal examination. It also meant that the fortunes of an individual official were tied to those of a patron, a point that matters for understanding the Barmakid case.

Section 31.5

Sasanian Administrative Inheritance and Institutional Transformation

Did the Abbasid administration inherit from Sasanian Iran? The question cannot be answered with a simple yes or no, because the answer depends on what is meant by inheritance, what channels carried it, and what the Abbasids changed. The Sasanian state, as described in Chapter 23, had provincial governors, a scribal class, a system of land and poll taxes, and written records. Many of these functions continued in the territories of the former Sasanian empire after the conquests, and some persisted through the Umayyad period. It is therefore certain that Sasanian administrative practice contributed to the early caliphate. What is less certain is the extent of that contribution and the form it took.

Some specific evidence supports the claim of continuity. Several important administrative terms in Arabic are borrowed from Persian, including dīwān itself, which is commonly explained as a Persian loanword, and zimām, the term for an account or control register. The use of cadastral surveys and fixed land assessments in Iraq and western Iran drew on practices that the Arabic tradition connected to the Sasanian reforms associated with Khosrow I. Officials such as Zadanfarrukh and his son Mardanshah, who headed the fiscal bureau of Iraq under al-Hajjaj around the turn of the eighth century, were Persian speakers who preserved Middle Persian accounting before its translation into Arabic. When the Abbasids came to power, many of the families who staffed these offices remained in service.

Other evidence cautions against overstating the case. The Umayyads had adapted Byzantine and Sasanian practices alike, and papyri from Egypt and Syria show that Greek and Coptic traditions of administration also continued. The Abbasid administration operated in Arabic, followed Islamic principles about taxation, and was shaped by the specific needs of a dynasty claiming rule in the name of the Prophet's family. Many of the Persian-derived terms and practices reached the Abbasids through the Umayyad bureaus rather than directly from the Sasanian court. Moreover, some of the claims about Sasanian practice in the Arabic sources, such as the reputed fixed system of Khosrow I, were made by writers in the ninth and tenth centuries who wished to present earlier models of just government and who may have idealized them.

A further channel of influence was literary rather than institutional. Secretaries such as Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ translated from Middle Persian into Arabic works on royal conduct, history, and statecraft, and writers of the ninth century attributed to Sasanian precedent a set of ideals about the justice of the king, the relationship between ruler and officials, and the structure of society. These translated works, discussed in 31.11 and in Chapter 33, helped provide the secretarial profession with a language of legitimacy. They do not prove that Abbasid offices copied Sasanian ones, but they show that Sasanian ideas about rulership were available to the court and that the secretaries found them useful.

The most defensible conclusion is that the Abbasid administrative system was a composite. It combined an inheritance from late antique Iran and Mesopotamia, an Umayyad adaptation of that inheritance and of Byzantine practice, Islamic legal and fiscal categories, and innovations of the Abbasid period itself. Persian-speaking secretaries were important carriers of this composite, but they were not the only ones, and what they carried had been transformed by two centuries of change.

Historical Debate

How Much Did the Abbasid Administration Inherit from Sasanian Iran?

The question: Historians agree that Iranian secretaries and Iranian administrative terms were prominent in the early Abbasid state. They disagree about how far this reflects the survival of Sasanian institutions as opposed to the work of Umayyad adaptation, late antique Near Eastern traditions shared across the region, and Abbasid innovation.

Interpretation A — strong Sasanian continuity: Some earlier scholarship, and much popular writing, treated the Abbasid state as substantially a continuation of Sasanian governance under Islamic rule, pointing to Persian loanwords, the role of Persian-descended secretaries, the reported adoption of Persian court ceremonial, and the translation of Persian royal literature into Arabic.
Interpretation B — a shared late antique inheritance transformed by Umayyad and Abbasid practice: Other historians stress that the Umayyads had already created a bureaucratic state drawing on Byzantine, Sasanian, and local traditions, and that Abbasid institutions evolved from the Umayyad base. In this view the Sasanian contribution is real but is only one strand among several and reached the Abbasids largely through existing Arabic-language offices.
Interpretation C — a selective use of Sasanian models for legitimacy: A third approach emphasizes that Persian precedent was often invoked retrospectively. Secretaries and later writers presented Sasanian practice as a model of just rule, and these claims served the secretaries' own status and the caliphs' desire for royal authority more than they described what offices actually did.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on loanwords and administrative vocabulary, papyri documenting Umayyad bureaus, accounts in later Arabic and Persian chronicles of the Sasanian tax system, the biographies of secretarial families, and surviving translations from Middle Persian. Each category has limits. Loanwords can prove contact without showing institutional continuity; later chronicles may idealize the past; and papyri come mostly from Egypt rather than Iran or Iraq.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology (whether Sasanian influence was greatest before or after 750), causation (whether continuity resulted from personnel or from deliberate imitation), terminology (what counts as "Persian" or "Sasanian" influence), and scale (whether the influence was central or marginal).

Why it matters: The answer affects how Iran's place in the caliphate is understood. If the Abbasid state is read as largely Sasanian, it becomes a story of Persian resurgence; if read as purely Arab-Islamic, Iranian contributions are minimized. This textbook's treatment, in line with its editorial principle on continuity and rupture, treats Iranian participation as significant but mediated and transformed.

Student task: Choose one administrative practice discussed in this chapter, such as land assessment, the use of registers, or the drafting of correspondence. Explain what evidence would be needed to show that it was inherited directly from the Sasanians rather than from the Umayyads or from a shared late antique tradition.

Section 31.6

The Dīwāns: Registers, Revenue, Correspondence, and Imperial Control

The central instrument of Abbasid government was the dīwān. The word referred both to a register and to the office that kept it, and it came to designate the whole system of bureaus through which the caliphate recorded what it owed, what it collected, and what it spent. The major bureaus differed in function but shared a common basis in written record. A bureau without a register could not demand payment, and a register that was not updated could not control officials.

The best-documented dīwāns of the early Abbasid period include the following. The dīwān al-kharāj recorded land assessments and tax receipts and supervised the collection of revenue from the provinces. The dīwān al-jund maintained the register of soldiers and their stipends, which determined who belonged to the army and what they were owed. The dīwān al-rasāʾil, the chancery, drafted and dispatched official correspondence, and a bureau associated with the caliphal seal ensured that documents were authenticated. A controller bureau, the dīwān al-zimām, appears in the sources of the later eighth century and checked the accounts of other offices. Other bureaus handled expenditure, the caliph's private estates, the courier system, and the affairs of particular provinces. The exact number, names, and responsibilities of these offices changed over time and are not always clear from the sources, and the list should not be read as a fixed organizational chart.

Records made imperial control possible by allowing officials in Baghdad to know, in principle, what the provinces owed and what they had sent. Provincial governors submitted accounts; tax collectors issued receipts; the central bureaus compared the figures and demanded explanations for shortfalls. An accounting culture of this kind produced specialized practices such as the use of daily ledgers, summary registers, and standardized forms. A major technological development of the period was the spread of paper. Egyptian administration relied on papyrus and the eastern provinces on parchment or paper, and Arabic sources report that a paper mill was established in Baghdad in the 790s, an initiative associated with the Barmakid al-Fadl ibn Yahya. The traditions about the introduction of paper from Samarkand and Central Asia are plausible but are partly legendary, and the details are not securely established. The broader point is that cheaper writing material made it easier to maintain records on a large scale.

The information held by the dīwāns was always incomplete. Registers could be falsified, and officials could conceal receipts or inflate expenditure. Mechanisms to prevent this included audits, confiscations, comparison of accounts across bureaus, and the personal inspection of the caliph. Later authors who wrote treatises on administration, including the tenth-century writer Qudama ibn Jaʿfar, described correct procedure in detail, but their prescriptions reflect ideals and later developments as much as the practices of the late eighth century. Budgets preserved in writers such as Ibn Khurradadhbih and al-Jahshiyari give figures for provincial revenues in the time of Harun, but these are of uncertain reliability and are best treated as indications of relative importance rather than precise accounts.

Historical Vocabulary
Dīwān

Definition: A register or account book, and by extension the bureau that maintained it; later also an anthology of poetry or a council. In the early caliphate the term was applied to the major financial, military, and administrative offices.

Historical context: The word is commonly explained as a loanword from Persian, and the tradition holds that the earliest caliphal dīwāns were organized under the second caliph Umar on the model of existing registers, though the details of that tradition are debated. Under the Umayyads, central bureaus in Iraq and Syria were Arabized, and under the Abbasids the system expanded and became more specialized.

Why it matters: Dīwāns made it possible for a distant caliph to exercise authority through written records. They also created a profession, the kuttāb, whose members controlled the information the state needed.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not equate Abbasid dīwāns with modern ministries. They were not always sharply separated, their functions overlapped, and their organization changed with circumstances and with the preferences of caliphs and ministers.

Section 31.7

The Kuttāb: Secretaries, Literacy, and the Professional Bureaucracy

The people who staffed the dīwāns, the kuttāb (singular kātib), formed what is best described as a professional class rather than a formal civil service. There was no examination and no fixed hierarchy of ranks comparable to the later Chinese or Ottoman systems. Entry depended on family connection, apprenticeship, and the patronage of an established official, and success depended on competence and on the favor of those higher up. Yet the profession had recognizable standards. A secretary needed to write a clear hand, to compose correct and elegant Arabic prose, to calculate and keep accounts, to understand the system of land measurement and taxation, and, at the higher levels, to know the forms of correspondence appropriate to different recipients.

Training occurred largely through families and masters. Boys of secretarial families learned reading, writing, and arithmetic at home or in the offices where their fathers and relatives worked. The tradition of secretarial advice literature, represented by the Umayyad chief secretary Abd al-Hamid ibn Yahya (d. 750), whose epistle to the secretaries described the qualities and conduct expected of the profession, gave the craft a set of ideals. These included loyalty, discretion, learning, and a refined style, and they became part of the secretarial self-image. Secretaries took pride in their ability to write the formal prose of the chancery, and Arabic epistolary style developed in these years into a distinctive and influential literary form, a subject treated more fully in Chapter 33.

The kuttāb were not an ethnic group. Many came from Iraqi, Iranian, and Khuzestani families that had served before the conquests or were drawn into service afterward. Others were Syriac-speaking Christians whose families had long dealt with accounts and administration, and Jewish and Zoroastrian secretaries and accountants are also attested. Some were Arabs, though they were less prominent in the bureaus than in the army. Religion also varied: some secretaries were Muslim converts or sons of converts, while others remained Christian, Jewish, or Zoroastrian, and in the early Abbasid period it was not unusual for non-Muslims to hold senior financial positions, a situation that later generated controversy and periodic attempts to remove them. The central point is that the profession was defined by function and training, and its members cannot be assigned a single ethnicity or confession.

The shift to Arabic as the language of administration was central to the profession, but it did not eliminate non-Arab secretaries. Rather, it made Arabic literacy a condition of advancement. Secretaries of Iranian background became fluent and often distinguished Arabic stylists, and the use of Arabic did not require abandoning Persian, which continued to be used in local administration, in family life, and in some provincial bureaus. The distinction between language of work and language of origin should be preserved: an Arabic-writing secretary of Persian descent worked within an Arabic administrative system while retaining connections to Persian culture and often to Persian-speaking networks.

The position of a secretary was also insecure. Because their power derived from proximity to rulers and from access to the information that the state required, they depended on the goodwill of the caliph and of the patrons who had appointed them. The histories of the period are filled with accounts of secretaries who were dismissed, tortured, or executed, and whose property was seized, and this pattern, evident in the fates of Abu Ayyub al-Muriyani, Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, and the Barmakids, formed an important part of the profession's collective memory. A career in the dīwāns could bring wealth and influence, but it could end abruptly.

Historical Vocabulary
Kātib (pl. kuttāb)

Definition: A secretary, clerk, or scribe; in the Abbasid administration, a member of the profession that staffed the dīwāns, drafted correspondence, and kept accounts.

Historical context: The Middle Persian counterpart is dibīr, and the Sasanian scribal profession was an important antecedent, though the Abbasid kuttāb also drew on Aramaic, Greek, and Arabic administrative traditions and on the secretarial culture of the Umayyad court.

Why it matters: The kuttāb created the continuity of administration that a changing dynasty needed, and their expertise became a base for political influence, as the later sections of this chapter show.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that every kātib was Persian or that the profession represented an organized Iranian bloc. Secretaries competed with one another, served different patrons, and came from varied religious and linguistic backgrounds.

Section 31.8

Taxation, Land Revenue, and the Fiscal Foundations of Power

The caliphate's wealth came overwhelmingly from agriculture. The principal tax was the kharāj, a land tax assessed on cultivated land and its produce, supplemented by the poll tax (jizya) on non-Muslims who had been granted protection, by taxes on trade, and by a variety of lesser levies. The kharāj was the economic foundation of the state, and the officials of the dīwān al-kharāj were among the most influential in the empire. Taxes on land fell on those who worked it or held title to it, and the burden therefore fell on cultivators, tenants, and the landholders who employed them, rather than on the soldiers, officials, and religious scholars who consumed much of the revenue.

The assessment of land took several forms. In some regions a fixed sum was demanded for each unit of land, calculated from a survey of the area and the type of crop. In others, assessments were based on a share of the harvest. In the Sawad of Iraq, reports credit al-Mahdi, and in some accounts al-Mansur, with a shift from fixed assessments to a proportional division of the crop known as muqāsama, a change thought to have reduced the risk to farmers in poor years while increasing the state's dependence on inspectors who could estimate yields. The detail of these changes is debated and varied by region, and it would be incautious to treat the Sawad as typical of Khurasan, Fars, or Egypt. A treatise on land tax written for Harun al-Rashid by the jurist Abu Yusuf, the Kitāb al-Kharāj, discusses assessment, collection, and the treatment of taxpayers, and it urges the caliph to avoid cruelty and to protect cultivators, which suggests both that the treatment of taxpayers was a concern and that abuses were common.

Questions of religion and tax were also bound together. Under the Umayyads, as Chapter 29 explained, many converts to Islam expected to be relieved of burdens that had marked non-Muslim subjects, and disputes over this question contributed to unrest. Abbasid legal and administrative opinion generally held that the poll tax did not apply to Muslims, while the land tax tended to remain attached to the land regardless of the religion of its holder. In practice, the enforcement of these principles varied with governors, regions, and the vigilance of local officials, and the larger fiscal question of how conversion affected the revenue base is revisited in Chapter 32.

Revenue moved from the provinces to the center in cash and in kind. Governors forwarded surpluses to Baghdad after meeting local expenses, including the pay of troops. Provincial governors also sent gifts, notably at the Persian festivals of Nawruz and Mihrajan, a custom that shows the survival of Iranian court practices within the Abbasid system. Figures for the total revenue of the caliphate under Harun exist in later sources, but, as noted in 31.6, they are of uncertain reliability and should not be read as exact. What is clear is that the Iranian and Iraqi provinces provided the great majority of the revenue.

The fiscal system was also a system of inequality. The agricultural population produced the revenue, and in many areas it consisted of dependent tenants, sharecroppers, and small proprietors who owed rents or taxes to landlords, officials, or the state. Large estates belonging to the caliph's family, to leading officials, and to military commanders absorbed much of the surplus. Irrigation works required maintenance, often through compulsory labor organized by local officials, and a poor harvest, a canal breakdown, or a rapacious collector could drive a family into debt or off the land. Alongside the historical evidence that officials were proud of their competence in assessing land, there is also evidence in legal and administrative writing that taxpayers were beaten, imprisoned, and dispossessed. The prosperity of the court and of the elite secretaries rested on this agricultural labor.

Multiple Perspectives

The Tax Year: Four Viewpoints on Land Revenue in an Iranian or Iraqi Province

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on administrative and legal writing of the period. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or knowledge.

From the perspective of the caliph and his court: Revenue is the foundation of military power and royal generosity. A dependable flow of tax revenue allows the caliph to pay the army, reward supporters, and finance building projects, but the central authority can verify provincial performance only through reports, audits, and informants, and therefore worries constantly about concealed receipts and disloyal officials.
From the perspective of a kātib in the dīwān al-kharāj: The secretary's expertise lies in assessment, accounting, and drafting, and his advancement depends on producing revenue that satisfies his superiors while maintaining enough goodwill among local notables to continue to obtain accurate information. He knows that a mistake or a rival's accusation can bring dismissal and confiscation.
From the perspective of a provincial landholder (dehqān): The landholder acts as an intermediary, vouching for the village's assessed obligations and collecting from cultivators. His status depends on the goodwill of the governor, and he must weigh obligations to his neighbors and dependents against pressure from officials. He may profit from the position but also bears the risk of being held responsible for shortfalls.
From the perspective of a taxpaying cultivator: The cultivator experiences taxation as a claim on the harvest, as the arrival of surveyors and collectors, and as a source of debt in bad years. The fixed or proportional assessments are less important than the conduct of the officials who apply them. Cultivators have little direct access to the caliph, though petitions and collective complaints were sometimes possible.
Section 31.9

Governors, Provincial Administration, and Communications

The caliph governed provinces through governors, usually called amīrs or wālīs, whom he appointed, instructed, and removed. A governor typically held military command, led the congregational prayer in the provincial capital, maintained order, and oversaw the collection of revenue. These combined powers made governors potentially dangerous, and the Abbasids, like their predecessors, tried to limit them. Sources suggest that at various times the caliphs separated fiscal responsibility from military command by appointing a distinct head of taxation, and that they rotated governors, kept their relatives or confidants in important posts, and monitored them through informants. Princes of the Abbasid house, trusted commanders, and secretaries all served as governors, and the choice varied by province and by moment. Khurasan, as the most militarily and politically sensitive eastern province, was entrusted to men of particular standing, and later to the heir apparent himself.

Governors worked with a provincial staff of secretaries, tax collectors, judges, and local notables. Their provincial dīwāns replicated at smaller scale the central bureaus, so that a governor's secretary could play a decisive role in determining what revenue was reported and what was forwarded. The relationship between governor and secretary was often one of mutual dependence: a governor needed an experienced kātib to run his accounts, while the secretary's advancement depended on the governor's favor and on his being in office. When a governor fell, his secretaries and associates often fell with him, and when a governor rose to the center, he commonly brought his provincial circle with him. This mechanism of patronage helped to carry officials with local knowledge, many from Iranian provinces, into the central dīwāns.

Communications and intelligence were closely linked in the Abbasid system. The barīd was the courier and post service, a network of stations along principal roads where official messengers could change mounts and where the caliph's agents could report on conditions. The word's origin lies in the Latin and Byzantine postal tradition, though the Sasanian state had also maintained a courier service, and the Umayyads and Abbasids developed the institution for their own purposes. In each province the head of the barīd, who answered to the caliph rather than to the governor, was expected to report on the governor's conduct, the condition of the roads, the harvest, and the mood of the population. The system could convey reports with considerable regularity, though the sources do not permit precise claims about speed, and the quality of information depended on the honesty and courage of local officials.

The limits of central oversight were nonetheless real. Distance mattered, since a governor in Khurasan or Transoxiana could take decisions that would be known and reversed in Baghdad only after a delay. Regional elites, including Arab settlers, local landholders, and military leaders, could obstruct or manipulate caliphal policy. The reign of Harun al-Rashid illustrates the problem. Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan, appointed governor of Khurasan in 796, held the province for roughly a decade and was reported to have accumulated great wealth and to have governed harshly. A rebellion led by Rafiʿ ibn al-Layth began in Samarkand in about 806 and spread across Transoxiana. Harun removed Ali ibn Isa around 806–807 (the sources vary slightly) and sent Harthama ibn Aʿyan to the east, and then left Iraq himself in 808 to confront the rebellion, dying at Tus in 809 before it was resolved. The episode shows both the reach of the system, which could assign a governor, receive reports, and mobilize the caliph himself, and its limits.

Everyday Life and Social History

Assessments, Petitions, and Records: How People Encountered the State

For most inhabitants of the caliphate, the Abbasid state was not a court or a capital but an official who appeared in the village at particular seasons. Assessors, often accompanied by local landholders, measured fields, estimated crops, and recorded what each village or holding owed, and in many regions these assessments were negotiated with the local headman or dehqān. Collectors returned at harvest or other fixed times to receive payments in grain, produce, or coin. Receipts and registers recorded what had been paid, and a cultivator who could produce a receipt had some protection against a second demand, though the surviving documents from the period are too few to say how consistently receipts were issued or honored.

Complaint and petition were part of this relationship. Writers on government, including Abu Yusuf in his treatise for Harun, urged rulers to hear the complaints of taxpayers, to prevent officials from beating or imprisoning those who could not pay, and to remove abusive collectors. The Abbasid caliphs, beginning with al-Mahdi according to later tradition, are reported to have held sessions to hear grievances against officials. That such advice and practices were recorded implies both that the problem existed and that the elite regarded access to a redress mechanism as important for legitimacy. Yet a village cultivator rarely had direct access to the caliph, and in practice a complaint usually went through a local notable, a judge, or a provincial official, whose own interests might conflict with the petitioner's.

Because most surviving sources were written by officials, jurists, and court historians, the voices of cultivators, small-town clerks, and local women and men rarely appear directly. Historians reconstruct their experience from the concerns that advice literature repeatedly raises, from legal rulings about what could be demanded, from the few documentary survivals from Egypt, Syria, Bactria, and Sogdiana, and from the archaeological record of settlements and irrigation. The result is a picture of a society in which the state's reach was substantial but uneven, and in which relations between officials and local communities were shaped by negotiation, obligation, and the constant risk of abuse.

Section 31.10

Baghdad and the Reorganization of the Imperial Center

In 762 al-Mansur began constructing a new capital on the western bank of the Tigris, in a region of canals and agricultural land north of the old Sasanian capital at Ctesiphon. The new city, called Madinat al-Salam, took the form of a circular enclosure, often called the Round City, with four gates named for the roads that led from them: those of Basra, Kufa, Syria, and Khurasan. At its center were the caliph's palace and the principal mosque. Construction continued until about 766. The traditions concerning the site and date, including the reported consultation of astrologers, may contain legendary elements, but the basic account of a planned administrative city is supported by the consistent testimony of later geographers and historians and by the subsequent growth of the metropolis.

For the purposes of this chapter, the significance of Baghdad is institutional. The caliphate had previously been governed from Damascus, where the Umayyads relied on the Syrian Arab garrisons. Al-Mansur's move to Iraq placed the capital in the heart of the former Sasanian territory, with access to the revenue of the Sawad and to the road leading to Iran. It also placed the caliph near the Khurasani soldiers who now formed his principal army, and the arrangement of the gates and quarters reflected the political geography of the empire. The dīwāns were housed near the center, and officials, soldiers, and merchants settled in the surrounding districts. Within a few decades, additional districts had developed on both banks of the Tigris, including the eastern district of Rusafa developed under al-Mahdi, and the commercial markets, moved out of the Round City during al-Mansur's reign, grew in the southern district of Karkh.

The shift had implications for the role of Iranian elites. Baghdad lay close enough to the former Sasanian heartland that Iranian secretaries, landholders, and merchants could easily reach it, and the road through the Zagros passes connected it directly with the Iranian plateau and, beyond it, Khurasan. Iranian and Iraqi administrative families, many of whom had served from the Sasanian and Umayyad periods, were well positioned to staff its dīwāns. At the same time, Baghdad was not an Iranian city. It was an Islamic imperial capital, populated by Arabs, Iraqis, Iranians, Christians, Jews, and others, in which Arabic was the language of government and of the growing scholarly and literary culture. Its establishment contributed to the redefinition of Iranian regions as provinces of a caliphate governed from the west.

The center did not stay fixed. Harun al-Rashid spent long periods at Raqqa on the Euphrates from about 796, governing from there while Baghdad remained the principal administrative city, and al-Maʾmun, as noted in 31.15, ruled from Merv in Khurasan for several years before returning to Baghdad. The movement of the court shows that the geography of imperial power was not settled, and that different rulers weighed the advantages of Iraq, northern Syria, and the east differently. The administrative role of Baghdad, with its dīwāns and its concentration of officials, nevertheless gave it a lasting importance. Later chapters will return to the relationship between Baghdad and the Iranian regions.

Section 31.11

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ and the Intellectual Responsibilities of Government

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ is the clearest example in this period of a secretary whose administrative experience shaped a body of political thought. Born with a Persian name, Ruzbih, in the region of Fars, he was the son of a tax official who, according to the tradition, had been punished by the Umayyad governor al-Hajjaj for financial irregularity, an episode reflected in the nickname that his son inherited. He served as a secretary under Umayyad governors, and then under Abbasid princes including Isa ibn Ali, an uncle of the caliph. According to later sources he entered Islam, taking the name Abdallah, during this period. The details of his conversion and his earlier religion are reported by writers who also accused him of religious unorthodoxy (zandaqa), an accusation whose reliability is hard to assess, and the broader question of religion and Iranian identity is the subject of Chapter 32.

His surviving work consists of translations, adaptations, and original compositions in Arabic prose. The best known is Kalīla wa-Dimna, an Arabic version of a collection of animal fables whose origins lie in Indian narrative literature and which reached him through a Middle Persian intermediary. Other works attributed to him include two treatises on conduct and manners, known as the Greater Adab and the Lesser Adab, and the epistle on the caliph's companions or entourage, Risāla fī l-Ṣaḥāba. The attribution to him of translations of Persian historical and royal literature, including works on kingship and governance, is widely repeated in later sources, but the exact list is uncertain and some attributions may be later. The literary significance of his prose, and of the translation movement more broadly, belongs to Chapter 33. The question here is narrower: what does his political writing reveal about the responsibilities of government as seen by a secretary?

The Risāla fī l-Ṣaḥāba is an advice memorandum, normally understood as addressed to al-Mansur. It surveys several matters of concern to a ruler: the selection and supervision of the caliph's associates and officials, the discipline and organization of the army, the administration of justice, and the conduct of finance. The treatise presents the caliph as the person who must impose order on a system that had been left disorganized by rapid expansion and by the circumstances of the revolution. Its most discussed proposal is that the caliph should issue authoritative rulings to resolve inconsistent legal judgments among judges in different regions. This suggestion shows a secretary's view of government: a rational administration, in which the ruler establishes clear rules and officials follow them, would be more just and more stable than one that depended on the personal judgment of regional officials. The advice implicitly places responsibility on the caliph and on the secretarial expertise that would implement his rulings.

The political context mattered. A secretary who offered advice to a caliph risked giving offense, and Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's career ended in violence. He was killed at Basra at the order of the governor Sufyan ibn Muʿawiya, in a year that the sources variously give between about 756 and 759. The sources offer several explanations. One concerns a letter of safe-conduct that he drafted for the rebellious Abdallah ibn Ali, which was written in terms that al-Mansur reportedly found offensive; another points to a personal quarrel between the secretary and the governor, who had been the target of his sarcasm; a third alleges religious heresy. Some combination is plausible, and the episode shows, at minimum, that a secretary's advice and writing could expose him to punishment from a caliph or powerful governor. It also illustrates that the secretarial elite had cultivated a tradition of political counsel, one in which administrators claimed a role in the moral and intellectual direction of the state, and that caliphs were not always willing to accept it.

Primary Source Window

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, Risāla fī l-Ṣaḥāba (Epistle on the Caliph's Entourage), mid-eighth century

Creator: Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, an Iranian-born secretary writing in Arabic · Date: composed probably in the 750s, in the reign of al-Mansur · Type: advice memorandum addressed to a ruler, an example of political counsel written by a secretary

Context: The epistle was written in the early years of Abbasid rule, when the dynasty was still establishing its army, its legal system, and its fiscal administration. The text survives in manuscript transmission and has been studied in modern editions and translations; its attribution to Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ is generally accepted, though scholars debate the identity of the intended recipient and the precise date. No verified translated wording is reproduced here, and the paraphrase below summarizes themes rather than quoting the text.

Paraphrase: The author advises the ruler to choose his associates and officials with care, to bring order and clear structure to the army and to the regular payment of soldiers, and to attend to the religious and political education of the newly dominant Khurasani troops so that they obey the caliph. He deplores the disagreement among judges, who give different rulings on similar cases in different cities, and proposes that the ruler establish a uniform body of judgments. He also discusses taxation, urging that assessments be set with attention to what the land can bear and that officials be restrained from oppressing the population.

Analysis: The text shows a secretary's practical concern with institutions: the discipline of the army, the consistency of justice, and the moderation of taxation. It reveals that by the 750s members of the secretarial class regarded themselves as having a right and a duty to advise the caliph on matters of state, and that they believed that written rules and a well-ordered administration could solve problems that the revolution had left unresolved. It also reflects a distinct political outlook: authority concentrated in the caliph and implemented by learned officials.

Limitations: The epistle cannot show that the caliph read or acted upon the advice, and legal unification of the kind it proposes did not follow in the form he envisioned. The text represents one author's view, and he was a secretary with interests of his own. It also cannot establish that Persian royal models shaped its recommendations, though its themes echo wider traditions of counsel, including those found in translated Middle Persian works.

Perspective: The text reflects the viewpoint of an educated administrator close to the court. It does not give the perspective of soldiers, cultivators, judges, or the Khurasani troops it describes.

Section 31.12

Khalid ibn Barmak and the Rise of the Barmakid Family

The Barmakids, known in Arabic as the Barāmika, were the most prominent family of Iranian-region origin in the early Abbasid administration. The name of the family derives from a title, Barmak, borne by an ancestor and associated in later sources with Balkh in Bactria. Later Arabic and Persian writers asserted that the family had been hereditary custodians of a temple known as the Nawbahar at Balkh, and some described it as Zoroastrian. Modern scholarship, including work on the Bactrian background of the family, has argued that the Nawbahar was a Buddhist monastery and that the title Barmak may derive from an Indian word for a leader of such an institution. The question remains debated, and the later Arabic and Persian accounts of the family's origins, which served the purposes of writers who either admired or criticized the Barmakids, should be read with caution.

Khalid ibn Barmak, the first of the family to hold high office under the Abbasids, appears in the sources as a participant in the revolutionary movement in Khurasan and as a financial administrator from an early date. Al-Mansur is reported to have used him in the dīwāns and as a provincial governor, including in Tabaristan after its conquest and in Fars, and under al-Mahdi he continued to hold important positions. He died in the early 780s. A famous anecdote reports that when al-Mansur considered demolishing the Sasanian palace at Ctesiphon, known as the Iwan of Khosrow, Khalid advised preserving it, and that the caliph, suspecting Persian sentiment, rejected the advice. The story cannot be verified and probably expresses a later literary view of the relation between Iranian officials and the Persian past. It does not demonstrate that Khalid acted from Persian nostalgia, and it should not be taken as evidence of what he thought.

The family's rise rested on the pattern described in 31.4 and 31.9. Khalid had local connections in the east, administrative experience, and the favor of Abbasid patrons, and he used his position to establish his son Yahya in the household of the young prince Harun, whom al-Mahdi designated for high office. Yahya became Harun's tutor and administrator of his affairs, and the relationship created a bond that later sources described in affectionate terms. Yahya's sons, al-Fadl and Jaʿfar, became companions of Harun and al-Maʾmun respectively, and according to tradition al-Fadl and Harun were foster brothers. The family thus built a network in which service, kinship, and personal affection reinforced one another.

Later literature credited the Barmakids with generous patronage of scholars, poets, translators, and physicians, including translators of Sanskrit, Greek, and Persian works. Their involvement in such activities is plausible given their wealth and their origins, and some of the evidence is contemporary, but much of it comes from retrospective sources that idealized them, especially after their fall, when a tradition of nostalgia grew up about the generosity of the household. Historians therefore need to separate the documented administrative and political careers of the family members from the later legend of Barmakid munificence.

Section 31.13

The Barmakids under Harun al-Rashid: Viziers, Patrons, and Power

When Harun became caliph in 786, he appointed Yahya ibn Khalid as his chief minister, and for the next seventeen years Yahya and his sons dominated the administration. The sources describe Yahya as effectively directing the empire's government, supervising the dīwāns, managing appointments, handling the caliph's correspondence and seal, and advising on policy. His authority was granted by the caliph and exercised on his behalf, and he is said to have referred to Harun with deference, while Harun is reported to have treated him as a father figure. This closeness was both the basis of his power and its limit.

Yahya's sons took on distinct roles. Al-Fadl ibn Yahya, an able administrator and soldier, governed Khurasan from about 794 to 796 and became known for improving security, building mosques and facilities, and recruiting troops. The sources report that he enlisted a large number of Khurasani soldiers, some of whom were brought to Baghdad, and that this force remained associated with the Barmakid connection after the family's fall; the numbers given are not trustworthy. Jaʿfar ibn Yahya, the younger and the most personally close to Harun, acted as the caliph's companion and as head of the chancery, and also held titular provincial offices. He is reported to have been charged with the education of al-Maʾmun, a link that mattered when al-Maʾmun later governed Khurasan. The three men thus controlled financial administration, correspondence, the Khurasan connection, and personal access to the caliph.

The Barmakid network operated through patronage. They placed relatives, clients, and associates in provincial and central offices, supported poets and scholars, and sponsored building and charitable projects. The patronage network included people of varied origins, Arab as well as Iranian, since patronage followed loyalty and usefulness rather than ethnicity. Their influence extended to appointments in the judiciary and in the provinces, and the sources associate them with major administrative initiatives such as the introduction of paper production in Baghdad. Their wealth was considerable, and later writers described their palaces and gifts in terms that reflect both admiration and, in some cases, criticism.

The nature of their office also needs care. The word wazīr was applied to Yahya, but the position of a chief minister did not yet have a fixed definition. Early ministers such as Abu Salama and Abu Ayyub had held informal or loosely defined roles, and the powers of a vizier varied with the ruler's preferences. The fully developed vizierate of the later ninth and tenth centuries, with its own bureaucratic apparatus, formal ceremonial, and sometimes real control of the caliphate's finances, cannot be read back into Yahya's time. In the late eighth century, the vizier was the caliph's principal agent, and his authority depended on delegation. The Barmakids' power was thus large in practice but legally insecure.

Historical Vocabulary
Wazīr

Definition: A chief minister or principal assistant to a ruler; in Abbasid usage, the highest administrative official under the caliph. The English forms "vizier" and "vizierate" derive from the Arabic.

Historical context: The Arabic root conveys the sense of bearing a burden or helping, and the Qur'an uses the word of Aaron as helper to Moses. In the early Abbasid period, the title was applied to figures such as Abu Salama, Abu Ayyub al-Muriyani, and Yahya ibn Khalid, though the office was not yet precisely defined. Some scholars connect it with the Sasanian office of chief minister, but any such link is debated.

Why it matters: The office of wazīr developed into the central institution of Abbasid and later Persianate government, and the careers of the early viziers show how administrative expertise and personal favor shaped it.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not equate the early wazīr with the powerful and institutionalized vizierate of later centuries, examined in connection with Nizam al-Mulk in Chapter 39.

Section 31.14

The Fall of the Barmakids and the Limits of Ministerial Authority

In January 803, while on his return from the pilgrimage to Mecca and staying at a place near al-Anbar on the Euphrates, Harun al-Rashid ordered the execution of Jaʿfar ibn Yahya. Yahya and al-Fadl were arrested, the family's property was confiscated, and other Barmakid relatives and clients were removed from office. Yahya died in confinement in 805 and al-Fadl in 808, both reportedly at Raqqa. These events are agreed upon by the sources. What is disputed, and has been since the ninth century, is why Harun acted.

Later writers offered a number of explanations. A popular story, widely repeated in literature, held that Jaʿfar had been secretly married to the caliph's sister Abbasa and that Harun discovered the relationship; modern historians generally regard it as a romantic embellishment, since it appears in later narratives with variations and does not fit the evidence of Harun's long reliance on Jaʿfar. Other accounts cite financial abuses, the accumulation of wealth, and the dominance of the family, so that the caliph came to resent their control of government. Another set of reports connects the fall with Jaʿfar's alleged leniency to an Alid rebel, Yahya ibn Abdallah, whom he is said to have allowed to escape. Others emphasize rivalries at court, and in particular the enmity of Fadl ibn al-Rabiʿ, who succeeded to the chief ministry. A further argument stresses Harun's desire to rule directly, and the changes in the succession arrangements, in which the Barmakid link with al-Maʾmun may have appeared threatening to partisans of al-Amin.

Historians usually treat these explanations as complementary rather than exclusive, and they stress the structural conditions that made the fall possible. The Barmakids held power by delegation from a caliph who could withdraw it, and their dominance of the administrative and military networks had created enemies among those who had been excluded. Harun, after seventeen years of reliance on them, apparently came to see their influence as too great. The suddenness and cruelty of the action, including the execution of a man who had been the caliph's close companion, reveals the vulnerability of men who had risen by royal favor and had no institutional protection. The fall was also fiscally significant, since confiscation brought wealth into the caliph's treasury.

The idea that the fall represented an Arab reaction against Iranian power is not supported by the evidence. Harun continued to employ officials from the eastern lands, such as Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan, a commander of Khurasani background to whom he entrusted Khurasan, and Fadl ibn al-Rabiʿ, who succeeded the Barmakids, was himself the son of a client of the caliphate and not of Arab descent in the tribal sense. The Barmakid fall ended the dominance of one family, not the participation of Iranians in the administration. Their memory, however, endured, and later poets and writers transformed them into symbols of lost generosity and of the instability of fortune.

Cause and Effect

How Administrative Expertise Became a Source of Political Influence

Long-term causes

The inheritance of a large agrarian empire that depended on written records and on local intermediaries; the existence of a trained scribal class from Sasanian, Umayyad, and other traditions; the relocation of the caliphate to Iraq, near the former Sasanian heartland.

Short-term causes

The need to govern after the revolution; the Abbasids' reliance on trusted officials rather than a hereditary aristocracy; the use of patronage to bring provincial officials to the center.

Immediate context

Reigns in which caliphs such as al-Mahdi and Harun relied on ministers to manage the dīwāns, provinces, and court, and placed trusted associates such as Yahya in positions of delegated authority.

Transformation

Secretaries and ministers moved from technical service to political leadership, controlling appointments, information, and access to the caliph, and thereby becoming major actors in court politics.

Immediate effects

Ministers accumulated wealth and patronage networks; secretarial families rose; rivals and critics emerged within the court and the army.

Long-term consequences

The development of the vizierate as a central institution; a recurring pattern of rise and fall for ministers; the establishment of a tradition of administrative and political counsel; a model of government in which expertise and royal favor were intertwined, later carried into Persianate states.

Historical complexity: Expertise alone did not produce political influence. It became powerful because caliphs needed it, because patrons placed it in positions of trust, and because the dynasty lacked other institutions for governing. The same conditions made it vulnerable, as the fates of Abu Ayyub, Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, and the Barmakids show.

Section 31.15

Al-Maʾmun, al-Fadl ibn Sahl, and the Renewed Importance of Khurasan

Harun al-Rashid's plans for the succession set the stage for the next phase. Around 791–792 he designated his son Muhammad, later known as al-Amin, as first heir. Al-Maʾmun was named second heir later, commonly dated to 799, and in 802, according to the sources, the arrangements were formalized in documents deposited at the Kaʿba in Mecca. The agreements assigned al-Maʾmun a large share of authority in Khurasan, and the east thus acquired a prince with an administrative base of his own. Al-Maʾmun's mother, Marajil, is reported in the sources to have been a concubine of Persian origin, and he had been educated under Barmakid influence. Whether or not such connections influenced him directly, the sources make them a recurring theme, and contemporary partisans on both sides are said to have invoked them.

When Harun died at Tus in March 809, the arrangements broke down. Al-Amin, who succeeded as caliph, moved to alter the order of succession in favor of his own son, and in the following years the two brothers went to war. The conflict is sometimes described as a struggle between Arabs and Persians, or between Iraq and Khurasan, and some later writers framed it in those terms. The evidence supports a more complicated picture. Al-Maʾmun's forces were led by Tahir ibn al-Husayn, a commander from the region of Herat, and they defeated al-Amin's army at Rayy in 811 before besieging Baghdad in 812–813. But Khurasani soldiers and descendants of the original revolutionary army were present on both sides, and the abnāʾ in Baghdad fought for al-Amin. The war was a dynastic struggle with regional dimensions, not an ethnic conflict. Al-Amin was killed when Baghdad fell in September 813.

Al-Maʾmun did not immediately go to Baghdad. For several years he governed the empire from Merv, and his principal adviser was al-Fadl ibn Sahl. He is known by the designation al-Sarakhsi, which associates him with Sarakhs in Khurasan, but accounts of his background differ. Some modern scholarship identifies his father as a Zoroastrian from the vicinity of Kufa who became attached to the Barmakids, so association with Sarakhs, place of residence, and religious background should be kept distinct rather than merged into one origin story. Al-Fadl himself is reported to have entered Islam and to have joined the service of al-Maʾmun through the Barmakid circle, though dates and details vary. He held the title Dhū l-Riyāsatayn, "holder of the two headships," understood to refer to command of both the army and the administration, and he was the dominant figure in al-Maʾmun's government during these years. His brother al-Hasan ibn Sahl was appointed to govern Iraq. Their accumulation of civil and military authority is evidence that the networks of Khurasani and Iranian secretaries, soldiers, and landholders remained central to the caliphate's politics.

Al-Fadl's ministry is also associated with a controversial initiative. In 817 al-Maʾmun designated the Alid Ali al-Rida as his heir, changed the dynastic color from the black of the Abbasids to green, and thus effectively altered the Abbasid succession. The reasons are debated: they may include an attempt to reconcile Alid supporters, a response to unrest, or an effort to strengthen al-Maʾmun's legitimacy against his Abbasid relatives in Baghdad. The reaction in Baghdad was hostile, and in 817 the Abbasids there proclaimed an anti-caliph, Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi. Al-Maʾmun had already begun his journey west from Merv when, in February 818, al-Fadl ibn Sahl was killed at Sarakhs, reportedly by assailants in a bath, and the sources divide over whether al-Maʾmun was complicit. Ali al-Rida died near Tus, commonly dated to September 818, and al-Maʾmun reached Baghdad in August 819.

The career of al-Fadl ibn Sahl resembles that of the Barmakids in its main features: a rise from a provincial background through administrative skill and patronage, concentrated authority exercised in the name of a ruler, and a violent end. It also differs. His power was exercised in a province, Khurasan, rather than at the capital, and it was connected to the political needs of a prince in a civil war. It demonstrated that in a time of crisis the eastern networks could move to the center of imperial politics. Al-Maʾmun's later appointment of Tahir ibn al-Husayn as governor of the east, around 821, began the process by which a family from Khurasan came to hold hereditary authority within the caliphate, a development examined in Chapter 34, which also discusses the broader question of the relationship between central and regional power.

Section 31.16

State, Society, and the Limits of Iranian Participation

It is now possible to assess what Iranian participation in the early Abbasid system amounted to. At the top, it is clear that Iranian-region soldiers, secretaries, and landholding families shaped the political life of the caliphate between 750 and 820. Khurasani commanders carried the revolution; secretaries such as Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ articulated ideas of rule; and families such as the Barmakids and the Banu Sahl exercised ministerial power. The caliphate's fiscal structure depended heavily on the agricultural revenues of Iraq and Iran and on the officials who knew how to assess and collect them. A reading of the period as one of Arab rule in which Iranians were merely employees does not account for this scale of influence.

At the same time, the limits of Iranian participation are equally clear. The caliphs remained Abbasids, claimants of authority through descent from the Prophet's uncle, who could and did dismiss and kill even the most powerful officials. The language of government was Arabic, and the religion of the state was Islam, even if many officials were of non-Muslim origin or had recently converted. The same individuals who rose could fall within a single reign, and there is no evidence of a lasting Iranian political institution within the caliphate that could protect them. Iranian participants were divided by region, religion, profession, and patronage, and they frequently competed with one another. The fall of the Barmakids, and the rise of men such as Fadl ibn al-Rabiʿ and Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan, demonstrate that no single Iranian faction controlled the state.

The experience of Iranian elites also differed sharply from that of other Iranians. A secretary who rose to ministerial rank, a landholder who became an official, or a commander who received an estate belonged to a small privileged group. The much larger population of cultivators, village artisans, laborers, and servants experienced the Abbasid state chiefly through taxation, military recruitment, and the demands of local officials, and the success of elite Iranians did not necessarily improve their circumstances. Indeed, the secretaries and governors who built careers by raising revenue often did so at the expense of cultivators, and the legal and advisory literature that warned against oppression suggests that abuses were common. Women appear in the sources largely as members of elite households, as mothers of caliphs and princes, and as concubines and servants, and their roles in networks of patronage are only partly visible. The experience of the great majority of the population is known only indirectly.

There was also regional variation within the Iranian lands. The Sawad and Khuzestan were closely tied to the center, Fars and the western plateau were governed through established provincial structures, Khurasan was both a major source of soldiers and a recurring site of rebellion, Tabaristan retained distinctive local traditions after its conquest, and Transoxiana remained governed through a mixture of local lords and caliphal governors. The result was an empire in which Iranian influence in the center rested on Iranian diversity in the provinces. These differences help explain why Chapter 34 will turn to regional dynasties emerging in the east rather than to a single Iranian political movement.

The pattern of institutional continuity and change can be summarized as follows. The Abbasids did not restore Sasanian rule, and the caliphate did not remain the Arab-led system of the Umayyad period. They built a system of dīwāns, secretaries, governors, and communications that drew on Sasanian, Umayyad, Byzantine, Aramaic, and Islamic sources, and operated in Arabic under a caliph claiming Islamic and dynastic legitimacy. Iranian elites were among the principal builders of this system, and its development created channels through which Iranian cultural and intellectual contributions entered the wider Islamic world, a process explored in Chapters 32 and 33.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Governing Institutions: Sasanian, Umayyad, and Abbasid

What Changed

  • Arabic replaced Middle Persian, Greek, and other languages in central records, though at different times in different regions
  • The capital moved from Damascus to Baghdad, and the dynasty's military base shifted from Syrian garrisons to Khurasani soldiers
  • The scale and specialization of the dīwāns increased, and offices such as the vizierate began to develop
  • The ruler's legitimacy rested on Islamic and dynastic claims rather than on the Sasanian royal ideology
  • Paper began to supplement papyrus and parchment as a medium for records

What Stayed the Same

  • Land revenue remained the principal source of state income, collected through local intermediaries
  • Trained secretaries continued to provide administrative expertise, many from families that had served earlier regimes
  • Local landholding elites such as the dehqāns continued to stand between the state and cultivators
  • Governors combined military, judicial, and fiscal responsibilities, and were supervised through couriers and informants
  • Rulers who relied on officials' expertise also feared their influence

Timing and experience: Change was gradual and uneven: Arabization came to Iraq decades before Khurasan, and the Abbasid expansion of the bureaus built on Umayyad groundwork. Secretaries, soldiers, and officials experienced the changes of 750 most directly in personnel and patronage, while rural cultivators experienced greater continuity in the demands made on them. Why the pattern matters: It explains why the Abbasid system can be described neither as a Sasanian restoration nor as a mere continuation of Umayyad practice.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Abbasid dynasty governed after the revolution described in Chapter 30, and how Iranian military leaders, landholding families, secretaries, and ministers participated in, and helped to transform, the resulting imperial system. Its central argument is that the early Abbasid state was a composite: it drew on Sasanian, Umayyad, Byzantine, Aramaic, and Islamic inheritances, operated in Arabic, and depended heavily on administrators and soldiers from Iranian lands, without being either a restored Persian monarchy or an unchanged Arab administration.

The first problem the Abbasids faced was the transition from revolutionary victory to government. The movement that had defeated the Umayyads was a coalition of Arab settlers, Iranian clients and notables, Alid sympathizers, and discontented villagers, and its components wanted different rewards. The first caliphs inherited a working administrative apparatus of coinage, registers, couriers, and secretaries, kept much of its personnel, and moved its center from Syria to Iraq. They also had to deal with rival claimants and with revolts, including the Alid rebellion of 762–763.

Al-Mansur consolidated the dynasty by defeating his uncle Abdallah ibn Ali, executing Abu Muslim in 755, suppressing revolts in the Iranian east, auditing and confiscating the wealth of officials, and founding Baghdad. The Khurasani soldiers who had carried the revolution became the dynasty's principal army, but "Khurasani" referred to origin and service, not to a single ethnicity, and their relationship with the court remained tense. Their descendants, the abnāʾ al-dawla, remained a political force in the capital region.

Beneath the courts and armies stood local notables, notably the dehqāns and other landholding families who mediated between peasants and the state. Evidence from Sogdian, Bactrian, and later Persian sources shows that these elites often operated in their own languages and networks. Their fortunes after 750 varied by region and family, and the movement of provincial officials into the central bureaus occurred through patronage and demonstrated competence.

The question of Sasanian inheritance can be answered only in qualified terms. Persian loanwords, Persian-speaking officials, translated royal literature, and some fiscal practices show real Sasanian contributions, but the Umayyads had already adapted this inheritance together with Byzantine and local traditions, and later writers idealized Sasanian precedent. The dīwāns, the secretarial profession, and the system of governors and couriers were products of that combination. The kuttāb were a professional class of varied origins, languages, and religions, whose skill with records, accounts, and drafting became a source of political influence and, equally, of personal vulnerability.

The fiscal basis of the system was agricultural revenue, assessed and collected through dīwāns, governors, and local intermediaries, and supported by the labor of cultivators, tenants, and sharecroppers. Legal and advisory writing shows concern about oppression of taxpayers, and the surviving evidence suggests that abuses were common. Baghdad, founded in 762, gave the caliphate an administrative capital near the revenues of Iraq and the road to Khurasan, though the court was not fixed there, as Harun's stays at Raqqa and al-Maʾmun's years at Merv demonstrate.

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ and the Barmakids illustrate two aspects of secretarial and ministerial influence. Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's epistle on the caliph's entourage shows how an Iranian-born secretary wrote about army, law, and taxation in terms of ordered, rule-bound government, and his violent death shows the dangers of such counsel. The Barmakids, whose background in Balkh is subject to debate, exercised wide authority under Harun al-Rashid through delegation and patronage, and their destruction in 803, for reasons that remain debated, showed that this authority had no institutional protection.

The civil war of 809–813 and al-Maʾmun's years in Merv brought Khurasan back to the center of imperial politics, and al-Fadl ibn Sahl's brief ministry showed the continuing influence of eastern networks. His killing in 818 repeated the pattern of sudden loss. The appointment of Tahir ibn al-Husayn to the east around 821, discussed in Chapter 34, opened a different relationship between center and region.

The limits of Iranian participation are as important as its extent. Caliphs remained Abbasids, who could dismiss and kill the most powerful officials; Arabic and Islam defined the language and legitimacy of the state; Iranian elites were divided by region, religion, and rivalry; and the elite's advancement differed greatly from the experience of cultivators and other ordinary people. The chapter thus supports a measured conclusion: Iranian participation was substantial, transformative, and fragile, and the Abbasid state was a new imperial system that Iranian and non-Iranian actors built together. The questions of religion and identity raised by these developments are the subject of Chapter 32.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Abbasid caliphate
The dynasty descended from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas that overthrew the Umayyads in 750 and ruled the Islamic empire, with changing real authority, for several centuries.
Khurasaniyya
The Khurasani army that carried the Abbasid revolution; it included Arab settlers and non-Arab clients and was defined by origin and service rather than by ethnicity.
Abnāʾ al-dawla
"Sons of the dynasty," the descendants of the Khurasani revolutionary soldiers, many of whom settled in the Baghdad region and formed a political and military group.
Mawlā (pl. mawālī)
A client attached to a patron or tribe; in this period, often a non-Arab Muslim convert or a freed person with a patron's protection.
Dehqān
A member of the landholding gentry in Iranian regions who served as a local intermediary between cultivators and the state.
Dīwān
A register and the bureau that kept it; the basic unit of caliphal administration, applied to financial, military, and correspondence offices.
Dīwān al-kharāj
The bureau that recorded land assessments and revenue receipts and supervised collection.
Dīwān al-jund
The register of soldiers and their stipends, determining who belonged to the army and what they were owed.
Dīwān al-rasāʾil
The chancery responsible for drafting and dispatching official correspondence.
Dīwān al-zimām
A controller bureau, attested by the later eighth century, that checked the accounts of other offices.
Kātib (pl. kuttāb)
A secretary or clerk; a member of the professional class that staffed the dīwāns, drafted correspondence, and kept accounts.
Dibīr
The Middle Persian term for a scribe, associated with the Sasanian scribal profession that preceded and partly overlapped the Abbasid kuttāb.
Wazīr
A chief minister or principal assistant to the ruler; in the early Abbasid period, an informal and delegated office, not yet the institutionalized vizierate of later centuries.
Kharāj
The land tax assessed on cultivated land or its produce, the principal source of caliphal revenue.
Jizya
The poll tax paid by non-Muslim protected subjects, which was not owed by Muslims.
Muqāsama
A system of assessment based on a proportional share of the harvest, reported in the Sawad of Iraq in the later eighth century in place of fixed assessments.
Muṣādara
The confiscation of the wealth of an official, used by caliphs as a fiscal and political instrument.
Barīd
The courier and post service, whose heads also reported on conditions and officials in the provinces.
Amīr / wālī
A governor appointed by the caliph with military, religious, and often fiscal responsibilities in a province.
ʿĀmil
An official, usually a tax collector or district administrator, acting on behalf of a governor or a bureau.
Sawad
The irrigated alluvial plain of southern and central Iraq, the main agricultural base of the early Abbasid state.
Round City
The circular enclosure of Madinat al-Salam, the core of Baghdad as founded by al-Mansur in 762, with four gates named for Basra, Kufa, Syria, and Khurasan.
Mirror for princes
A genre of advice literature addressed to rulers on conduct and government; Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's epistle is related to this tradition.
Patronage
A system in which a powerful person provided office, protection, or wealth to followers who returned loyalty and service, central to secretarial and ministerial careers.
Arabization (administrative)
The replacement of other languages by Arabic in official records and correspondence, a gradual and regionally uneven process.
Retrospective tradition
Accounts written long after the events they describe, shaped by later concerns; most narratives of the Barmakids are of this kind.
Dhū l-Riyāsatayn
"Holder of the two headships," the title of al-Fadl ibn Sahl, understood to refer to command of army and administration.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an empire is actually run, and how the people who run it can reshape it. The Abbasid revolution is often remembered as a dramatic military and religious movement, but its lasting effects depended on the quieter institutions that followed: registers, tax assessments, correspondence, governors, couriers, and the men who understood them. In these institutions, Iranian secretaries, landholders, and soldiers played a central role, and their participation helped to define what the early Abbasid caliphate became.

The chapter also reveals the conditions of that participation. Influence depended on patronage and royal favor, and the rise and fall of Abu Muslim, Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, the Barmakids, and al-Fadl ibn Sahl demonstrate how precarious it was. This precariousness is part of a larger pattern, one that recurs in later Persianate states, in which administrative expertise could provide real power without providing security. Chapter 39 will return to this problem in the context of Nizam al-Mulk and the Seljuk state.

The developments of this chapter also set the conditions for those that follow. The expansion of Arabic as the language of administration, and the participation of Iranian secretaries in its literary elaboration, led to the intellectual and linguistic developments of Chapter 33. The question of how people moved into Islam and how Iranian identity changed in a Muslim society, which the fiscal and political material in this chapter touches on, is the subject of Chapter 32. The continuing weight of Khurasan and the relationship between caliphal and regional authority prepare the study of the Tahirids and Saffarids in Chapter 34.

Finally, the chapter reflects the editorial commitments of this textbook. It treats Iranian history as part of a wider Islamic world without reducing either to the other, it distinguishes Persian, Iranian, Arab, Muslim, and Persianate categories, and it emphasizes both institutional change and long-term continuity. The early Abbasid system was a new creation, built by many hands, in which Iranian participation was real, consequential, and limited, and understanding it on those terms is part of what the rest of this volume is for.
