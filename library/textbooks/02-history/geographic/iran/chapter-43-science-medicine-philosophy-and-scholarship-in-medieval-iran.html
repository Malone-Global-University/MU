Science, Medicine, Philosophy, and Scholarship in Medieval Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between about 900 and 1256, scholars working in Bukhara, Rayy, Ghazna, Isfahan, Merv, and many other cities investigated the heavens, the human body, number, matter, and the nature of reasoning itself. They did so inside a web of patrons, teachers, books, instruments, and rivals that stretched far beyond any single dynasty or modern border.

Near the end of his life, the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina wrote an account of his own education. It survives in a form completed by his student al-Juzjani, and it describes a boy from a village near Bukhara who mastered Qurʾan, arithmetic, and legal reasoning, and who, as a young physician, gained access to the library of the Samanid ruler by treating the amir. The story contains some features that later admirers may have polished, and historians read it with that in mind. Still, it captures conditions that were real: a scholar needed teachers, books, a patron, and a city with enough wealth to support all three. When political order collapsed, he needed to be able to move.

That mobility defines much of this chapter. Ibn Sina left Bukhara for Khwarazm, wandered through northern Iran, and ended his career in Hamadan and Isfahan. His contemporary al-Biruni, a native of Khwarazm, worked at several courts before spending his last decades in the Ghaznavid capital. Omar Khayyam, born in Nishapur, worked under the Seljuk sultan Malik Shah. Their paths illustrate that the intellectual history of medieval Iran cannot be written as the history of one dynasty or one province. It belongs to a world of connected cities in which scholars read the same books, argued over the same problems, and competed for the same kinds of support.

This chapter is the principal history-of-knowledge chapter of Volume III. Chapter 42 examined how Iranian historical memory was preserved in poetry and narrative. The present chapter asks a different set of questions. What did scholars of this period actually investigate in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, nature, and philosophy? How did their arguments and methods work? How were knowledge and expertise financed, taught, tested, and handed on? And how did philosophy, religion, and political patronage interact as the political landscape changed from the Samanids and Buyids to the Ghaznavids, Seljuks, and their successors?

The chapter's title uses "science," "medicine," "philosophy," and "scholarship" as convenient modern headings, but medieval scholars organized knowledge differently. Philosophy included logic, natural science, mathematics, and metaphysics. Medicine was both a practical craft and a branch of natural philosophy. Astronomy, mathematics, and astrology overlapped, and religious scholarship shaped how all these fields were taught and judged. A reader who expects a straight line from medieval inquiry to modern laboratory science will misread both. The scholars discussed here were rigorous within their own frameworks. Those frameworks included humoral medicine, Aristotelian physics, astrology, and religious assumptions that modern science does not share.

The chapter also resists two opposite temptations. One is to describe a seamless "Golden Age" of unbroken progress. Intellectual life varied by region, discipline, patron, and political circumstance, and scholars were sometimes exiled, ruined, or killed. The other is to treat medieval scholars merely as couriers who preserved Greek learning until Europeans could reclaim it. The evidence shows critique, correction, and new work: new trigonometric methods, a classification of cubic equations, clinical writing that questioned ancient authority, large philosophical systems, and sustained debate over what could be known and by what means.

Historians reconstruct this world from manuscripts: treatises, commentaries, medical compendia, astronomical tables, autobiographical fragments, biographical dictionaries, and surviving instruments. Many works survive in copies made centuries after composition, some exist only in translation, and many are lost. Almost all were written in Arabic or Persian by educated men in urban settings. Patients, midwives, village healers, surveyors, and instrument makers appear mostly in passing, and this chapter tries to read the sources with those absences in view.

The chapter belongs to a division of labor within Volume III. The political histories of the Samanids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks belong to Chapters 35, 37, and 38. The Nizamiyya colleges and the administrative system of Nizam al-Mulk belong to Chapter 39, the institutional and doctrinal history of Sunni and Shiʿi communities, including al-Ghazali's spiritual career, to Chapter 40, the Nizari state to Chapter 41, and Persian poetry and the Shahnameh to Chapter 42. Chapter 44 turns to the Khwarazmian empire. The Mongol-era scholarly institutions, including Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's later career and the observatory at Maragha, belong to Volume IV.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Locate the principal centers of learning of the Iranian and Persianate world and explain why scholarly networks crossed dynastic and modern political boundaries.
  2. Identify the Greek, Indian, Iranian, and earlier Islamic traditions that medieval scholars inherited, and explain how they were critically examined rather than passively received.
  3. Explain how Arabic and New Persian, manuscripts, commentaries, and encyclopedic writing shaped the organization and survival of knowledge.
  4. Analyze the roles of courts, hospitals, observatories, libraries, madrasas, and private teaching in sustaining inquiry.
  5. Describe major developments in algebra, trigonometry, and the geometric treatment of cubic equations, distinguishing medieval methods from modern symbolic algebra.
  6. Assess al-Biruni's methods in astronomy, geography, chronology, and the study of India, including their limits.
  7. Evaluate astronomical observation, instrumentation, and the Jalali calendar reform of 1079.
  8. Compare the medical careers and principal works of al-Razi and Ibn Sina, and distinguish the Canon of Medicine from the philosophical Book of Healing.
  9. Interpret hospitals, pharmacology, and everyday healing as practical and social activities documented unevenly.
  10. Trace the development of philosophy from al-Farabi through Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali, and Suhrawardi, explaining what each argued.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of the relationship among religious scholarship, philosophy, and the natural sciences.
  12. Situate Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's pre-1256 career as a bridge to the Mongol period without anticipating Volume IV.

Historical Context

By 900 the Iranian world had been part of the Islamic polity for some two and a half centuries, a process traced in Chapters 28 through 33. Under the early Abbasids, Baghdad had become the center of an intensive effort to gather, translate, and study works of Greek, Syriac, Middle Persian, and Indian origin. Chapter 31 described how Iranian secretaries and families shaped the Abbasid administration, and the same Iranian elite helped sponsor translation and scholarship. This chapter assumes that background and concentrates on what followed. By the tenth century, scholars were no longer chiefly translating. They were commenting, correcting, systematizing, and producing original work.

The political framework changed repeatedly. The Samanids (Chapter 35) governed Khurasan and Transoxiana from Bukhara and patronized scholars and a royal library. The Buyids (Chapter 36) ruled Baghdad and western Iran from 945 and sponsored physicians, mathematicians, and astronomers. The Ghaznavids (Chapter 37) built a court at Ghazna that drew scholars by invitation and sometimes by compulsion. The Seljuks (Chapters 38 and 39) supported both colleges and astronomical work. None of these dynasties created intellectual life; each tapped, funded, or disrupted networks that already existed. When a court collapsed, scholars moved to another.

The language of scholarship was mixed. Arabic was the common language of religion, law, philosophy, mathematics, and medicine across the Islamic world, and the majority of the major works discussed in this chapter were written in it by authors of Iranian origin or residence. New Persian, whose literary rise Chapters 35 and 42 described, began to be used for scientific and philosophical prose in the tenth and eleventh centuries. Writing in Arabic does not remove a scholar from Iranian intellectual history, any more than writing in Latin removed a medieval French scholar from French history.

Religious institutions provided much of the framework. Chapter 40 described how mosques, teaching circles, endowments, and colleges organized religious learning. The same institutions hosted, or competed with, the study of the so-called "ancient sciences" of logic, mathematics, medicine, and natural philosophy. Some religious scholars valued these fields, others distrusted parts of them, and many held both attitudes at once. The relationship was neither a unified alliance nor a unified conflict, a point developed in §43.15 and in the second Historical Debate.

Finally, the geography of knowledge followed the geography of cities and routes, as Chapter 1 emphasized for the plateau as a whole. Scholars traveled along the Khurasan road, across the Zagros to Baghdad, through Khwarazm toward the steppe, and south to Ghazna and the Indus. Their world was not confined to the territory of the modern Islamic Republic, and this chapter uses the historical regions of Khurasan, Transoxiana, Khwarazm, and western Iran as the setting.

Section 43.1

The Intellectual Geography of Medieval Iran and the Persianate World

Scholarly life in this period was urban and clustered. Bukhara, the Samanid capital, offered a court library, wealthy patrons, and a long-established scholarly class. Khwarazm, with its capital at Gurganj (modern Urgench region), was a prosperous oasis on the Oxus, and for a time in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries its rulers sheltered a circle of scholars that included al-Biruni and Ibn Sina. Nishapur, the largest city of Khurasan, hosted hadith scholars, jurists, and Sufis, and later produced Omar Khayyam. Rayy, near modern Tehran, was a major city of the northern plateau where al-Razi worked and where Buyid viziers supported scholars. Isfahan became important under the Buyids and again under the Seljuks. Ghazna, in what is now Afghanistan, became a major court center under Mahmud and his successors. Baghdad, though not in Iran, was the largest scholarly market in the Islamic world and drew students from every direction.

The map of learning did not follow dynastic maps. A scholar born in Khwarazm might study in Bukhara, serve a Buyid vizier in Rayy, and die at a Ghaznavid court. Patronage created movement, but so did danger. When the Samanid state weakened around 1000, Bukhara's scholars scattered. When the Seljuks took power in the mid-eleventh century, some scholars lost patrons and others gained new ones. Persianate courts competed for famous names because a learned household added prestige, and because physicians, astronomers, and secretaries had practical uses.

The result was a set of overlapping networks. Teachers wrote letters of recommendation, students carried manuscripts home, and scholars sent questions to distant colleagues. The famous exchange of questions between Ibn Sina and al-Biruni, preserved in written form, shows two leading thinkers disputing points of natural philosophy across a political and geographic gap. Such exchanges depended on couriers, shared texts, and a common technical vocabulary more than on a shared ruler.

It follows that classifying medieval scholars by modern citizenship distorts the evidence. This chapter speaks of scholars of Iranian origin, or working in the Iranian and Persianate world, and identifies their regions of birth and work. Some of the figures it discusses had origins that are debated. Al-Farabi, for example, was born in Transoxiana, but sources disagree about whether his family was Turkic or Iranian, and he worked in Baghdad and Aleppo. What matters historically is the setting in which he learned and taught and the traditions on which he drew.

Section 43.2

Greek, Indian, Iranian, and Earlier Islamic Foundations of Scientific Learning

Medieval Iranian scholarship stood on several inherited traditions. From the Greek world came Aristotle's logic and natural philosophy, Euclid's Elements, Ptolemy's astronomy, and the medical writings of Hippocrates and especially Galen. From India came numerals and computational methods, astronomical parameters, and some medical learning. From the Iranian past came Middle Persian texts, including astronomical tables and medical and administrative knowledge, some of which were rendered into Arabic in the early Abbasid period. From the early Islamic centuries came the translations, summaries, and first critical reworkings produced in Baghdad and elsewhere.

The transmission was neither simple nor passive. Syriac-speaking Christian scholars, many of them physicians, mediated much Greek medicine and philosophy into Arabic. Persian-speaking officials commissioned and used translations. The famous medical school at Gundishapur in Khuzestan is often credited with preserving Greek medicine, but historians now emphasize that its role is documented unevenly and that later tradition probably inflated it. It is safer to say that a Sasanian-period medical community existed there and that its members contributed to early Abbasid medicine.

Translators did more than convert words. They chose which texts to render, created a technical vocabulary in Arabic, and often added explanations. Later scholars then criticized and extended the translations. Al-Razi challenged Galen on specific points, al-Biruni compared Greek, Indian, and Iranian astronomical methods, and Ibn Sina reorganized Aristotelian philosophy into a system with its own innovations. A fair description is that medieval scholars treated inherited authority as valuable but open to question, a stance developed in the first Historical Debate below.

Claims about origins need care. Algebra did not begin in medieval Iran, and neither did hospitals, trigonometric functions, or philosophical logic. Al-Khwarizmi, who came from Khwarazm and worked in Baghdad in the early ninth century, wrote an influential treatise on solving equations that is a foundation for the later tradition, but he built on earlier Babylonian, Greek, and Indian practices of calculation. What this chapter attributes to Iranian and Persianate scholars are specific developments within shared traditions.

Section 43.3

Languages, Manuscripts, Translation, and the Organization of Knowledge

Arabic was the main language of scholarly exchange across the Islamic world, and its prestige made it the natural language for works meant to reach readers in Baghdad, Cairo, and beyond. It had a rich technical vocabulary for logic, mathematics, and medicine, and a well-established culture of scribes, book production, and scholarly correspondence. Most of the writings of al-Razi, al-Biruni, and Ibn Sina are in Arabic. This does not make them less Iranian in origin or setting; it means that Iranian scholars contributed to a language and a library shared across a very large area.

New Persian entered scholarship gradually. Ibn Sina composed a philosophical encyclopedia, the Danishnama-yi ʿAlaʾi, in Persian for his patron at Isfahan. Al-Biruni wrote at least some works in Persian, though most that survive are in Arabic. Persian medical writers produced teaching and reference works for readers who did not wish, or were not able, to read Arabic, and in the early twelfth century Ismaʿil al-Jurjani began the Dhakhira-yi Khwarazmshahi after moving to Khwarazm around 1110. This large Persian medical encyclopedia demonstrated that Persian could carry detailed technical content. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's Akhlaq-i Nasiri (§43.19) later did the same for ethics. Persian's growth as a scientific language thus ran alongside, and did not replace, Arabic.

Knowledge circulated in manuscripts, copied by hand. A scholar's work might spread through copies made for patrons, sold in bookshops, or exchanged among students. Copyists introduced errors and variants, annotators added glosses, and later editors reordered texts. The great libraries, such as those described by Ibn Sina in Bukhara and the Buyid library at Shiraz, concentrated books, but libraries were vulnerable to fire, looting, and neglect. The destruction of libraries at Rayy under Mahmud's forces in 1029, noted in Chapter 40, and the later losses described in Chapter 41 at Alamut, show how fragile accumulations of books were. Historians reading medieval science therefore work from survivors, not from the full original record.

Authors used several genres. The compendium gathered a body of knowledge in organized form. The commentary (sharh) explained and criticized an earlier text, and much original thought appeared inside commentary. The summary condensed a large work for teaching. The treatise addressed a single problem. The correspondence and the question-and-answer format preserved disputes. Encyclopedic works, such as Ibn Sina's Book of Healing, claimed to cover whole disciplines.

Scholars also classified knowledge. Al-Farabi's Catalogue of the Sciences (§43.13) is one influential example, and Ibn Sina and others offered schemes of their own. These classifications distinguished, for example, the "ancient" or "rational" sciences from the religious sciences, and divided philosophy into theoretical and practical branches. Such schemes reveal how scholars thought about the unity and hierarchy of knowledge, though they should be read as arguments and not as neutral descriptions of what scholars did.

Historical Vocabulary
Falsafa, Hikma, and ʿIlm

Definition: Falsafa is the Arabic adaptation of the Greek word for philosophy and usually refers to the tradition of rational inquiry derived from Greek philosophy, especially Aristotelian. Hikma, "wisdom," is a broader term that could mean philosophy, practical wisdom, or in later Illuminationist writing a distinct philosophical system. ʿIlm means "knowledge" and covers both religious and worldly learning; the plural ʿulum ("sciences") was applied to fields of organized knowledge.

Historical context: Medieval Muslim authors used these terms with variations. A jurist might speak of ʿilm and mean religious learning. A physician might speak of hikma and mean medical philosophy. Philosophers of the Avicennan tradition spoke of falsafa and hikma as nearly interchangeable.

Why it matters: The vocabulary shows that categories did not match modern ones, and that the same word might carry different weight in different circles.

Potential misunderstanding: Falsafa did not name an opponent of religion. Many falasifa considered their work compatible with Islam, while some religious scholars disagreed.

Section 43.4

Scholars, Patrons, Institutions, and the Practice of Inquiry

Patronage was the main economic support for advanced scholarship. Samanid amirs gave Ibn Sina access to their library and, according to his own account, rewarded medical service. Buyid rulers, notably ʿAdud al-Dawla, supported physicians, mathematicians, and astronomers in Shiraz and Baghdad and founded a major hospital in Baghdad (Chapter 36). Ghaznavid rulers collected scholars at Ghazna, and al-Biruni's late career depended on that court. Seljuk sultans and viziers supported colleges, calendar reform, and observatory work. Patrons wanted prestige, practical skills such as medicine and horoscopes, and sometimes real intellectual companionship.

Dependence had costs. Scholars could lose their posts when a patron died or was deposed, and powerful rulers could command their presence. Tradition holds that Mahmud of Ghazna summoned Ibn Sina and that Ibn Sina avoided the summons, though the details are uncertain. Al-Biruni remained in Mahmud's world and wrote in it. The point is not that scholars were free or unfree but that they navigated a political environment that shaped their choices of residence, topic, and dedication. Many works are dedicated to patrons, and dedications tell us who paid.

Institutions beyond the court mattered. Hospitals, discussed in §43.11, combined care and training. Libraries held books and sometimes employed scholars. Private teaching circles, in which a master taught a few students in his house or a mosque, remained the basic form of advanced instruction (§43.17). Madrasas, described in Chapter 40, taught mainly religious law, and the extent to which they taught mathematics, medicine, or philosophy varied. Observatories, like those sponsored by Buyid and Seljuk rulers, were temporary or semi-permanent projects tied to patrons.

Scholarly practice combined several methods. Observation was central in astronomy and medicine: astronomers recorded positions of the sun, moon, and stars, and physicians recorded the course of illnesses. Calculation, whether arithmetic or trigonometric, turned observations into tables. Logical argument organized claims and tested consistency. Textual criticism compared manuscripts and translations. And inherited authority, whether Ptolemy, Galen, or Aristotle, provided starting points and targets. Medieval scholars did not distinguish "experiment" from "argument" as sharply as modern scientists do, and their standards of evidence differed by field. A mathematical proof was judged by deductive rigor, a medical claim by clinical experience and authority, and a metaphysical claim by argument.

It would be a mistake to confine inquiry to the court. Religious scholars taught arithmetic for inheritance law, physicians learned in apprenticeships, translators earned fees, instrument makers worked in workshops, merchants needed calendars and calculations, and scribes copied and sold books. The courts brought these people together and gave some of them a platform, but the base of knowledge was wider than the highest circles.

Division II · §§43.5–43.8 Mathematics, Astronomy, and the Measurement of the World
Section 43.5

Arithmetic, Algebra, and Mathematical Innovation

The medieval Islamic mathematical tradition began its algebra with al-Khwarizmi, whose early ninth-century book treated the solution of linear and quadratic equations through standard procedures and geometric justifications. The word "algebra" comes from al-jabr, one of the operations in his title, referring to restoring or completing by moving a subtracted quantity to the other side of an equation. His book was practical, aimed at problems of inheritance, trade, and land measurement, and it was written entirely in words.

Later mathematicians turned these procedures into a general theory. Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Karaji (d. c. 1029), who worked in Baghdad and whose origins in the Iranian world are reported in the sources though the exact place is debated, wrote on algebra in works such as al-Fakhri and al-Badiʿ. His contribution was to treat algebraic expressions as objects that can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided, including expressions made of powers of an unknown quantity. He described the successive powers of the unknown, set out rules for operating on polynomials, and worked on extracting roots. Historians also discuss his use of reasoning that resembles mathematical induction in some demonstrations, though how far it matches the modern principle is debated. His approach moved algebra from a toolkit for solving particular problems toward a body of operations that could be studied on its own terms.

Arithmetic also developed. Iranian and Persianate scholars used and taught Indian decimal numerals alongside the older sexagesimal methods used in astronomy and the finger-reckoning used in commerce. Works on arithmetic, written for scribes, merchants, and administrators, taught multiplication, division, fractions, and root extraction. Al-Biruni's study of Indian methods, discussed in §43.7, added to this base. The practical context mattered: much mathematical writing was motivated by inheritance law, taxation, surveying, and the computation of calendars.

Students should note what medieval algebra was not. It did not use the symbolic notation of modern textbooks. Equations were expressed in words, and problems were solved through rhetorical procedures or geometric constructions. Negative numbers were not accepted as solutions in the way they are today, and many equations were classified by type because each type required its own method. When this chapter uses modern symbols, as in the Mathematics Study in §43.6, they are an aid to students and are not the author's notation.

Section 43.6

Geometry, Trigonometry, and Omar Khayyam's Mathematical Work

Geometry was the prestige language of Greek mathematics, and medieval scholars continued to study Euclid, Archimedes, and Apollonius, often producing commentaries and extensions. Abu Sahl al-Quhi, a geometer of the tenth century associated with Buyid patronage, worked on problems involving conic sections. Such work formed the background for Khayyam's later geometric treatment of cubic equations.

Abu al-Wafa al-Buzjani (940–998), born at Buzjan in Khurasan and working in Baghdad, is a key figure in the development of trigonometry. Astronomers needed to compute positions of celestial bodies on a sphere, and they used chords and then sines, cosines, and tangents to relate angles to lengths. Abu al-Wafa and his contemporaries expanded the set of trigonometric functions in use, compiled accurate tables, and worked on spherical trigonometry for astronomical problems. He also wrote on practical geometry for artisans, indicating how mathematical knowledge served builders and craftsmen. As in many cases, attributions of "first" discoveries among the circle of Baghdad mathematicians are debated, and this chapter prefers to say that he was among those who developed and systematized these methods.

Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) deserves attention as a mathematician before he is remembered as a poet. Born in Nishapur and trained in Khurasan, he worked under Seljuk patronage at Isfahan and Merv. His Treatise on Demonstration of Problems of Algebra classified equations up to the third degree and set out solutions to cubic equations that had no general arithmetic method. He solved them geometrically by finding the intersection of conic sections, such as a parabola and a circle. He recognized that these were geometric rather than purely arithmetic solutions and expressed the hope that a numerical method might later be found. His treatment of cubics mostly considered positive roots, as the concept of negative solutions was not yet accepted. He also wrote on the difficulties of Euclid's parallel postulate, examining attempts to prove it, which belongs to the history of geometry more than of algebra.

Students should keep Khayyam the mathematician distinct from the Rubaʿiyat tradition. Quatrains attributed to him circulate widely, and their authenticity and attribution are heavily debated; many are probably by later writers. The scientific writings must be studied on their own evidence and not through the poetry or its nineteenth-century English translations.

Mathematics and Measurement Study

Solving a Cubic Equation Geometrically, in Khayyam's Manner

Khayyam treated an equation we would write as x³ + p²x = p²q as a problem about lengths. Modern symbols are used below to help students follow the reasoning. They are not Khayyam's notation: he wrote in words and spoke of cubes, squares, "things" (roots), and numbers.

Construction. Draw a parabola whose points satisfy x² = p·y. Draw a circle on a diameter of length q along the x-axis, whose points satisfy y² = x(q − x). Where the parabola meets the circle, both conditions hold at once.

From the parabola: y = x²/p
Substitute in the circle: (x²/p)² = x(q − x)
x⁴/p² = x(q − x), so x³/p² = q − x
Hence x³ = p²q − p²x, that is, x³ + p²x = p²q

The length x measured to the intersection point is therefore a root of the cubic. A worked case: take p = 2 and q = 4. The equation becomes x³ + 4x = 16. The parabola x² = 2y passes through (2, 2), and the circle y² = x(4 − x) gives y² = 4 at x = 2, so y = 2. The curves meet at x = 2, and indeed 8 + 8 = 16.

What the example shows: Khayyam's method gave a real solution without a formula for the root. It joined algebra and geometry, and it relied on a well-developed theory of conic sections. It was not modern analytic geometry, because Khayyam did not set equations of curves on coordinate axes in the way Descartes later did, and the use of symbols above is a convenience.

Section 43.7

Al-Biruni: Observation, Geography, Chronology, and the Study of India

Abu Rayhan al-Biruni (973–after 1050) was born in Kath in Khwarazm and trained in its scholarly circles. Political upheaval in Khwarazm in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries sent him from court to court, and after the Ghaznavid conquest of Khwarazm he came under Mahmud's authority and spent much of his later life at Ghazna and, according to his own writings, in the Indian borderlands during Ghaznavid campaigns. He wrote on a large range of subjects, and the list of his works he compiled himself is long.

His mathematical astronomy included the Masʿudic Canon, a major astronomical handbook dedicated to Mahmud's son Masʿud, and treatises on the astrolabe, on trigonometric methods, and on the determination of directions. In geography, he wrote on the determination of coordinates and on the measurement of the earth. One of his best-known measurements estimated the earth's radius using the height of a mountain and the angle of the horizon's dip. Historians have judged the result close to the modern value, though conversion of his units is uncertain, so claims of precise agreement should be treated with caution. The significance of the work lies in the method, which relied on trigonometry and observation.

His Chronology of Ancient Nations, completed around 1000, compared the calendars and eras of Persian, Greek, Jewish, Christian, and other communities, and discussed their astronomical and historical bases. It is a major source for pre-Islamic and non-Muslim calendar systems and for the history of Khwarazm. He compared testimony and calculations to resolve discrepancies and noted the sources he used.

His book on India, completed around 1030, is one of the most remarkable comparative works of the medieval world. Al-Biruni learned Sanskrit to some extent, consulted Indian informants and texts, and set out Indian religious ideas, philosophy, astronomy, mathematics, geography, customs, and law, often comparing them with Greek and Islamic parallels. He explained what he saw as obstacles to understanding: differences of language, religion, and custom, and the reluctance of some Indians to share learning with outsiders. He also explicitly criticized the exaggerations of earlier writers and the hostility created by Ghaznavid conquests.

His method and its limits should be described accurately. He compared texts, asked questions of learned informants, and tried to describe Indian ideas through their own sources, which was uncommon. But he worked within the assumptions of his time. He sometimes interpreted Indian thought through Greek or Islamic categories, depended on a small set of Brahmin informants, and wrote as a Muslim scholar attached to a conquering court. He was not an anthropologist in the modern sense, and the label should be avoided. He did not hold modern theories of cultural evolution or relativism.

Section 43.8

Astronomical Observations, Scientific Instruments, and Calendar Reform

Astronomy in this period combined observation, mathematical modeling, and the compilation of tables. The basic reference was the zij, a set of tables and instructions for computing planetary positions, eclipses, and calendars. Compilers revised Ptolemaic parameters with new observations. The tenth-century astronomer ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Sufi, who worked under Buyid patronage, revised and updated the catalogue of fixed stars in his Book of Fixed Stars, comparing Ptolemy's data with Arabic star names and his own observations. Al-Biruni's Masʿudic Canon was a broader astronomical encyclopedia: it provided mathematical and theoretical derivations as well as tables, unlike many works primarily organized as zij handbooks. In the twelfth century, al-Khazini at Merv, a scholar of Byzantine origin who was formerly enslaved according to biographical sources and who served the Seljuk ruler Sanjar, compiled the Sanjari Zij.

Instruments made observation and teaching possible. The astrolabe, a portable device for measuring the altitude of celestial bodies and solving problems of time and position, was widely produced and taught. Large sighting instruments, quadrants, armillary spheres, and gnomons were used in astronomical observation, though the availability and design of instruments varied across places and periods. Making them required skilled metalworkers, and instrument makers appear in the sources as a distinct professional group. The accuracy of observations depended on instrument size, graduation, and the stability of the setting.

Astronomy and astrology overlapped. Many astronomers also cast horoscopes, as courts valued predictions and calendars for timing decisions, and astronomy provided the data astrology needed. Some scholars criticized astrology: al-Biruni distinguished its assumptions from the mathematical astronomy he valued, and Ibn Sina rejected parts of it. Yet a historian cannot separate astronomy from astrology in the courts, and funding sometimes depended on the latter.

The most famous reform of the period was the Jalali calendar. In 1079 the Seljuk sultan Malik Shah sponsored a group of astronomers, including Omar Khayyam, to revise the solar calendar so that the beginning of the year would remain aligned with the vernal equinox. The calendar introduced a pattern of intercalation, adding leap days in a cycle, to keep the year close to the length of the tropical solar year. The reform was tied to the court's administrative needs, since taxation of agriculture depended on a calendar that matched the seasons. The sources attribute an observatory to Malik Shah's project, but its location and organization are not well documented, and claims about its size and equipment should be treated cautiously. Claims about the extraordinary accuracy of the Jalali calendar depend on how the cycle is modeled, and later Iranian calendars differ in their intercalation rules.

Cause and Effect

Why the Seljuk Court Reformed the Calendar in 1079

Long-term causes

The mismatch between the Islamic lunar calendar and the agricultural year; the use of older Iranian solar calendars for taxation and festivals; a mathematical astronomical tradition capable of precise computation.

Short-term causes

Malik Shah's interest in administrative order and prestige; the presence of skilled astronomers at court; drift in the older solar calendar's alignment with the seasons.

Immediate context

Sponsorship of an astronomical project at the Seljuk court in the 1070s, including Khayyam, under the broader patronage environment described in Chapter 39.

Event

Introduction of the Jalali calendar in 1079, aligning the year's start with the vernal equinox through a cycle of leap days.

Immediate effects

A solar calendar for administrative and agricultural use alongside the lunar religious calendar; enhanced prestige for the sultan and his astronomers.

Long-term consequences

The Jalali tradition influenced later Iranian solar calendars, though subsequent reforms altered details and the lineage should not be stated too simply.

Historical complexity: The reform combined state need, scholarly capability, and royal ambition. No one factor produced it, and its later reception shows that calendars evolve through repeated adaptation.

Section 43.9

Al-Razi and the Development of Clinical Medicine

Muhammad ibn Zakariyya al-Razi was born in Rayy, probably in the mid-ninth century, and died in the early tenth century; sources give dates between 925 and 935. He is reported to have studied philosophy and medicine, perhaps after an early career in other pursuits, and to have directed hospitals in Rayy and Baghdad, though the details of his appointments come from later biographical tradition. He wrote a very large number of works on medicine, natural philosophy, and alchemy.

His medical writing is distinguished by emphasis on observation and case records. His treatise on smallpox and measles, which survives and was widely translated, describes the two diseases, their symptoms, and the course of illness, and distinguishes them by clinical features. Historians treat it as an important early example of differential description, while cautioning that his diagnosis of the diseases in modern terms rests on interpretation of symptoms and not on laboratory confirmation. His Kitab al-Mansuri, written for a ruler of Rayy and Khurasan, is a concise medical handbook covering anatomy, physiology, hygiene, and treatment. It became a standard teaching text in Arabic and later in Latin.

His largest medical work, al-Hawi ("The Comprehensive Book"), is a vast compilation of excerpts from earlier authorities, combined with his own clinical notes and comments. It was assembled after his death from his papers, so the surviving form should not be treated as a book that he personally completed. Its value lies in preserving many passages and in recording his case experience.

Al-Razi also wrote a work questioning aspects of Galen's medical writings, usually known by a title that translates as "Doubts about Galen." In it he criticized Galen on points of theory and observation. This shows that medieval physicians could challenge a revered authority, though challenge took place within a shared framework. Al-Razi accepted humoral theory, the Galenic view that health depends on the balance of bodily humors, and used it to explain disease. He should not be portrayed as a modern empiricist. His practice emphasized diet, hygiene, and drugs, and he urged physicians to observe the patient carefully, to record what worked, and to be cautious about overconfident claims, including those of famous authorities.

Al-Razi was also a philosopher, and his philosophical views, some of them unorthodox, drew criticism from later thinkers. This chapter notes that his philosophical writing exists alongside the medical, and treats his religious and philosophical controversies only as far as they bear on his reputation. His career illustrates a general feature of the period: physicians were often philosophers, and philosophers practiced medicine.

Section 43.10

Ibn Sina and the Canon of Medicine

Ibn Sina (980–1037), born near Bukhara, gained a reputation as a physician in his youth. His own account says that treating the Samanid ruler opened the royal library to him. After the fall of Samanid power he moved through Khwarazm and northern Iran, served at the court of Buyid and related rulers at Rayy and Hamadan, was at times imprisoned or in flight, and spent his last years in Isfahan under a ruler of the Kakuyid dynasty. He died in 1037 at Hamadan. During these travels he wrote his major works.

His al-Qanun fi'l-tibb, the Canon of Medicine, is a systematic encyclopedia in five books. The first treats general principles: definitions of medicine, the elements and humors, the temperaments, anatomy, and the general causes and signs of health and disease. The second treats simple drugs, with descriptions of their properties. The third surveys diseases by affected organ, from head to foot. The fourth treats conditions not confined to one organ, such as fevers, and also tumors, poisons, and matters of cosmetics and surgery. The fifth treats compound medicines and their preparation. The organization made the work easy to teach and consult.

The Canon was a synthesis. It organized the humoral medicine of Galen and Hippocrates, the observations of earlier Arabic and Persian physicians including al-Razi and al-Majusi, and Ibn Sina's own reasoning and experience into a coherent whole. Its strengths included clear logical structure and comprehensive coverage. It reasoned about the correct methods for testing drugs, proposing conditions such as testing a drug on a simple disease, with consistency of effect, in the correct dose and time, and observing the action in the body. Such rules are interesting for the history of medical method, though students should not read them as modern clinical trials.

The work circulated widely. It was translated into Latin in the twelfth century by Gerard of Cremona and became a core text in European medical education for several centuries, and it was also commented on, abridged, and taught across the Islamic world. Its influence illustrates the way a single compendium could shape a field. The surviving manuscripts, which date from many centuries after composition, vary in their organization and marginal annotation, and historians study them to see how readers used the work.

Students must keep the Canon distinct from Ibn Sina's philosophical encyclopedia, the Book of Healing (al-Shifaʾ), discussed in §43.14. The title of the latter refers to the healing of the soul by knowledge and has no connection to clinical medicine despite its English translation. A further caution applies: humoral medicine was a systematic framework within its time, but its explanations of disease do not correspond to modern physiology. Admiring Ibn Sina's synthesis does not require endorsing its theoretical basis.

Section 43.11

Hospitals, Physicians, Pharmacology, and Everyday Medical Practice

The great medical books discussed in §43.9 and §43.10 were written for readers who were already physicians or students of medicine. Most sick people in medieval Iran never read them and never met a physician who had. Medical care was provided by a range of practitioners working in different settings: learned physicians (tabib, plural atibba) who had studied the textual tradition, often under a teacher and sometimes in a hospital; surgeons and bonesetters; oculists, who treated the eye diseases that were common in dry, dusty, and smoky environments; midwives and women healers; pharmacists and drug sellers; barbers, who performed bloodletting and cupping; and, beyond all of these, family members and neighbors who used home remedies, prayers, charms, and inherited practice. These categories overlapped, and the sources, written mostly by learned physicians, tend to mention the others only to criticize them.

The institution most often associated with institutional medicine in the medieval Islamic world is the bimaristan (Persian bimaristan, "place of the sick"), often shortened to maristan. Hospitals of this kind were established by caliphs, viceroys, ministers, and wealthy donors in cities of Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iran. The tradition of a major medical center at Gundeshapur (Jundishapur) in Khuzestan, discussed in earlier chapters in connection with late Sasanian learning, is a subject of continuing scholarly debate. Historians agree that Gundeshapur was a real city with Christian and other physicians and a place where Greek and Syriac medical learning was available, but they differ over whether it contained a formally organized hospital and school that served as the direct model for later institutions. This textbook treats it as one important site within a wider transmission, and does not describe it as the single source of the later bimaristan.

For the period of this chapter the evidence is clearer for Baghdad than for most Iranian cities. The Buyid ruler ʿAdud al-Dawla (r. 949–983) founded a major hospital in Baghdad in 981, known as the ʿAdudi hospital, that became a famous institution, and the sources also connect his patronage with medical activity in Shiraz. Physicians such as al-Razi are reported by later tradition to have directed hospitals in Rayy and Baghdad, though the details come from biographical dictionaries composed after his lifetime and cannot be fully verified. Some hospitals combined patient care with medical teaching, while evidence for systematic clinical recordkeeping varies; surviving accounts also describe salaried physicians, pharmacists, and attendants at major institutions. They were commonly supported by waqf, a charitable endowment in which property or its revenue was dedicated permanently to a stated purpose. Endowment provided a durable income that did not depend on the favor of a single ruler, although the quality of an institution could still decline if the endowed property was mismanaged or seized.

The sources describe hospitals as places where patients were examined, given diets and medicines, and treated by physicians who might specialize, for example in internal medicine, surgery, or eye disease. Because most descriptions come from admiring or promotional sources, such as biographies and foundation accounts, they tend to idealize the institutions. Historians have asked how many people actually used hospitals, who they were, and whether poor patients and travelers were served in practice as the foundation charters promised. The surviving evidence does not permit a numerical answer. The best conclusion is that hospitals were important but limited institutions, concentrated in larger cities and more accessible to urban residents than to villagers or pastoralists.

Historical Vocabulary
Zij, Bimaristan, and Waqf

Definition: A zij is a handbook of astronomical tables with instructions for using them to compute the positions of the sun, moon, and planets and to determine calendars and times. The word is of Persian origin and is related to the term for a warp thread, an image sometimes connected to the grid of the tables. A bimaristan is a hospital, a Persian word meaning "place of the sick." A waqf is an endowment: property or income dedicated permanently to a charitable or religious purpose, administered by a trustee according to the founder's conditions.

Historical context: The three terms come from different fields but describe the same larger system. Zijes served courts, astrologers, calendar-keepers, and astronomers. Hospitals and their equipment were supported by endowments, as were many libraries, colleges, and observatories. Together the terms show how learning depended on written reference works, trained specialists, and stable funding.

Why it matters: A student who understands these three terms can see that medieval science was an institutional activity involving texts, tables, buildings, and revenue, as well as individual genius.

Potential misunderstanding: The word "hospital" suggests a modern building organized around surgery and acute care. Medieval bimaristans also taught, kept books, and treated chronic and mental illness, but they were not identical to modern hospitals, and their scale varied widely.

Pharmacology was one of the fields in which Iranian scholars made especially visible contributions, partly because the drug trade connected Iran to India, Central Asia, China, and the Mediterranean. Medicines were made from plants, minerals, and animal products, and the knowledge of what each drug was, where it came from, and how to prepare it was practical as well as theoretical. Book Two of Ibn Sina's Canon treated simple drugs, and Book Five treated compound preparations. Abu Mansur al-Harawi composed a Persian materia medica, the Kitab al-abniya ʿan haqaʾiq al-adwiya, in the tenth century, which shows that Persian was used for specialist medical writing early in the Islamic period. Al-Biruni, in his old age, wrote the Kitab al-saydana (Book of Pharmacy), a very large work on drugs that lists their names in several languages and discusses the confusion caused when merchants, physicians, and herbalists used the same name for different substances. A twelfth-century Persian medical encyclopedia associated with al-Jurjani, composed for a Khwarazmian ruler, placed the Avicennan tradition in Persian for a broader readership. These works are evidence both of the vitality of Persian as a medical language and of the attention scholars paid to the problem of identifying drugs reliably.

Regulation of practice belonged to the same system. The muhtasib, the official who supervised markets and public morals, was in many places responsible for checking weights, measures, and the honesty of tradesmen, including in some places the sellers of drugs and, according to manuals of the office, those who claimed to practice medicine. Prescriptive texts describe examinations or tests of physicians and warn against fraud and incompetence. How consistently such rules were enforced in Iranian cities is not clear, and the manuals describe an ideal as much as a practice. Their existence shows that societies recognized the risks of unregulated medicine and that critics of fraudulent practitioners had institutional means to voice their concerns.

A reader should also remember the limits of medieval therapeutic knowledge. Humoral medicine offered a coherent theory of health and disease and recommended diet, regimen, drugs, bloodletting, cupping, and surgery, but it did not have a germ theory of infection, and many treatments were ineffective or harmful. Some, such as careful observation of fevers, the use of certain plant drugs, the setting of fractures, and the couching of cataracts, probably had real value. Medicine could also be shaped by religion, since the Prophetic tradition included sayings on health and healing that supported a distinct genre of "Prophetic medicine," and by astrology, which some physicians used to time treatment. Judging these practices requires attention both to the knowledge the practitioners had and to the world in which they worked.

Everyday Life and Social History

Healing, Learning, and Livelihoods Beyond the Famous Names

The scholars whose names fill this chapter were a small fraction of the people who worked with knowledge. A physician's household might include apprentices who learned by attending patients and reading with a teacher, a pharmacist who prepared and sold drugs, a copyist who reproduced texts, and family members who nursed the sick. The sources reveal these people mostly in passing: in a biographical dictionary's note that a physician trained a son, in a hospital's staff list, in a market inspector's manual, or in the colophon of a manuscript copyist.

Patients experienced medicine unevenly. A court official might summon a prominent physician to his house, while a villager might consult a local healer, a traveling oculist, or a religious figure, and a poor town resident might rely on a hospital or on charity. Women were prominent as midwives and as caregivers within households, and the sources sometimes mention women healers, though the evidence is much thinner than for men. Disease itself was an everyday presence: fevers, eye disease, intestinal illness, and the epidemics that periodically struck cities shaped what physicians wrote about and what families feared.

Learning also had its everyday side. Students traveled to find teachers, copied texts by hand, bought books from stationers, and attended informal gatherings where questions were debated. Paper, whose manufacture was established in Samarkand by the later eighth century according to tradition, made manuscript copying much cheaper than parchment had been, and a steady market for books supported copyists, stationers, binders, and papermakers whose labor underlies every text discussed in this chapter.

Section 43.12

Optics, Mechanics, Minerals, and the Natural Sciences

Natural philosophy in medieval Iran included inquiry into light, motion, weight, minerals, and the structure of the earth. These subjects were treated in the philosophical encyclopedias, in specialized treatises, and in the works of scholars who combined mathematical and experimental approaches. The evidence is uneven. Some fields, such as optics, are best known from authors who worked in Iraq and Egypt, while Iranian authors contributed distinctively to mineralogy, the measurement of specific weights, and the theory of mechanics. Students should therefore avoid the assumption that every field had an Iranian champion and should treat the Iranian world as part of a connected scientific community that extended from Central Asia to the Mediterranean.

The central figure in the history of optics in this period, Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), was born in Basra and worked mainly in Cairo, and he belongs to the Arabic-writing world outside Iran. His Kitab al-manazir (Book of Optics) developed an influential account of vision as the reception of light reflected from objects into the eye and combined geometrical analysis with experiment. Iranian scholars took part in the same discussion. Ibn Sina, in his philosophical works, rejected the theory that the eye emits rays and supported the view that vision depends on light coming from the object, and al-Biruni argued, in the course of his treatments of astronomy and geography, that light travels at a speed far greater than that of sound. A later Iranian optician, Kamal al-Din al-Farisi (died c. 1319), wrote an important commentary on Ibn al-Haytham and gave a sound explanation of the rainbow, but his career falls beyond the chronological limits of this chapter and belongs to a later period.

Al-Biruni's work on minerals and weights shows how careful measurement could be applied to natural substances. His Kitab al-jamahir fi maʿrifat al-jawahir (Book of the Most Comprehensive Knowledge of Precious Stones) describes gems and metals, their sources, properties, and uses, and reports measurements of their specific weights, using a method that compared the weight of an object in air with the weight of the water it displaced. The values he gave for many substances are reasonably close to modern measurements, a fact historians have taken as evidence of careful experimental practice, although the accuracy of individual measurements depends on the identification of the substance and on the manuscript tradition. This is a case in which a scholar's method can be inferred from a text, while the details of his apparatus remain partly uncertain.

The tradition of hydrostatic balances was continued by Abu al-Fath ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Khazini, who worked at Merv in the early twelfth century under the Seljuk ruler Sanjar. According to the sources, he was of Byzantine origin, was purchased as a slave, and received an education that made him a respected scholar at the court. His Kitab mizan al-hikma (Book of the Balance of Wisdom), completed in 1121, describes the design and use of balances for determining the specific weights of metals and gems, and incorporates tables, theoretical discussion of weight and the center of gravity, and the work of earlier authors, including al-Biruni and Archimedes through Arabic sources. He also contributed to the astronomical tradition by compiling a zij for Sanjar, known as the Zij al-Sanjari. Al-Khazini's career illustrates two features of the period: the movement of persons of varied origin into the scholarly world through slavery and patronage, and the importance of a provincial court in Khurasan as a site of scientific production.

Ibn Sina's natural philosophy treated motion, place, and time in the Aristotelian framework but modified it in some respects. In discussing projectile motion he argued that the moving object receives from its mover a quality, which he called mayl ("inclination"), that explains continued motion in the absence of further contact. Historians have discussed whether this idea contributed to the later medieval theory of impetus. The question involves chains of transmission that are not fully reconstructed, and the safest statement is that Ibn Sina's discussion was part of a long debate about motion among Greek, Arabic, and Latin authors. His treatment of minerals and meteorology in the Shifaʾ includes a discussion of mountain formation and of the origin of fossils that treated these as the products of natural processes over long time. He also rejected the claim of alchemists that a metal's species could be changed by art, holding that artisans could imitate but not transform the essential nature of a metal.

Alchemy deserves separate treatment because the sources mix several traditions. A large body of writings, the Jabirian corpus, was attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan, a figure of the eighth century whose historical identity and relation to the works are disputed. Modern scholarship generally holds that the corpus was written over several generations, probably from the later ninth to the tenth century, and that its authorship cannot be assigned to a single individual. Al-Razi wrote alchemical works that describe laboratory procedures and the classification of substances, and these are valuable evidence for the development of chemical practice, including distillation, sublimation, and the preparation of mineral acids and other substances. At the same time, such texts contain theoretical and ritual elements that do not fit modern chemistry. Students should treat alchemy as a body of practice and theory belonging to its own period, with contributions to technique and classification as well as aims, like transmutation, that experiment would later abandon.

Historical Debate

Preservers or Innovators? The Place of Medieval Iranian Science

The question: Did scholars writing in Arabic and Persian between the ninth and twelfth centuries mainly preserve and transmit Greek and Indian science, or did they produce new knowledge? How should historians describe their relationship to earlier traditions?

Interpretation A — Transmission and preservation: An older view held that the principal achievement of the medieval Islamic world was the translation and preservation of Greek learning, which was later passed to Europe. On this reading, original contributions were secondary and the main significance of the period was its role as a bridge between antiquity and the Renaissance.
Interpretation B — Creative development within a tradition: Many historians of science now emphasize that scholars criticized, corrected, and extended inherited material. They point to new mathematical methods in algebra and the geometric solution of cubic equations, revised astronomical parameters and instruments, the critical discussion of Ptolemy's models, the clinical analysis of diseases by al-Razi, the experimental approach in optics, and the new metaphysical arguments of Ibn Sina. On this reading, the period was a stage of active scientific development in its own right.

Evidence: The surviving works themselves, including al-Razi's doubts about Galen, Khayyam's treatment of cubic equations, al-Biruni's critical discussions of earlier authorities, and observational zijes, show both reliance on inherited sources and departures from them. Translation records and manuscript evidence show how texts moved among languages.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on the emphasis they give to continuity and novelty, on how to measure originality in a tradition where authors commonly worked through commentary and compilation, and on how to describe the relationship between Islamic-world science and later European science without implying a simple linear transmission. The disagreement is partly about interpretation and partly about the available evidence, since many texts remain unedited or unstudied.

Why it matters: If the period is understood only as preservation, its scholars appear as passive custodians and their own problems and achievements disappear. If it is understood only as innovation, the continuing dependence on earlier traditions and the real losses and limits are obscured. The most defensible view recognizes both dimensions, and recognizes that the question belongs to the history of science in general.

Section 43.13

Al-Farabi, Logic, Ethics, and Political Thought

The philosophical tradition that Ibn Sina brought to its fullest medieval form in Iran had a clear predecessor in Abu Nasr al-Farabi (c. 870–950). He was born in Farab, in Transoxiana, on the Syr Darya, and spent most of his working life in Baghdad and in the cities of Syria, where he served for a time at the court of the Hamdanid ruler Sayf al-Dawla at Aleppo, and he died in Damascus. Later tradition suggests a Turkic or Iranian background, but the evidence for his origins is limited and late, and what is secure is that he was a native of the eastern Iranian and Central Asian world who wrote in Arabic and worked mainly in the Arabic-speaking west. His case illustrates the point made in §43.1: the intellectual history of the Iranian world includes people who left their homeland, wrote in Arabic, and made their careers in other regions, and a narrow national definition would exclude them.

Al-Farabi was known in later tradition as the "Second Teacher," second only to Aristotle. His achievement lay in organizing and interpreting the Aristotelian logical corpus and in explaining the relationship between the philosophical sciences. In his Ihsaʾ al-ʿulum (Enumeration of the Sciences), he classified the fields of knowledge, including language, logic, mathematics, natural science, metaphysics, politics, jurisprudence, and theology, and so provided a map of learning that later scholars used. He wrote commentaries and treatises on the Organon and argued that logic is a universal tool whose rules are not tied to any particular language, a claim that drew critical responses from grammarians and theologians, who held that logic was specific to Greek. His writings on music, including a major treatise on the subject, treat theory and practice together and show a scholar who engaged with the arts as well as with logic and metaphysics.

His political philosophy is best known from al-Madina al-fadila (The Virtuous City), in which he compared a well-ordered city with a healthy body ruled by the heart, and described a ruler who combines philosophical knowledge with the capacity to lead. He treated religion and philosophy as two ways of expressing truth, the first through images and symbols suited to the many and the second through demonstration, available to the few. This argument was an important resource for later philosophers who wished to explain how revelation and reason could be reconciled. Whether it reflects a settled doctrine or a dialogue with Greek sources, and whether the ideal city was intended as a practical program or an intellectual model, are questions on which scholars hold different views.

A second strand of this tradition emphasized ethics. Miskawayh (also Ibn Miskawayh, c. 932–1030), who was born at Rayy and served as librarian and secretary to Buyid viziers, wrote the Tahdhib al-akhlaq (Refinement of Character), an influential treatise that combined Greek ethical philosophy with Islamic values and described the cultivation of virtue as a means of attaining human perfection. He also wrote the Tajarib al-umam (Experiences of the Nations), a history that treats political events in a pragmatic way, as sources of lessons for administrators, and that is a major source for Buyid history. His career shows that philosophy, ethics, and history were practiced by officials in the service of courts and were not confined to a separate philosophical class. Chapter 39 describes the administrative culture in which such secretaries worked, and the ethical tradition continued in works such as Tusi's Akhlaq-i Nasiri (§43.19).

Philosophy in this period was a social activity centered on small circles. In tenth-century Baghdad, a circle around the logician Abu Sulayman al-Sijistani met to discuss problems, and the Persian-born writer Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi recorded such discussions in works that give a vivid if literary picture. In Iran, circles formed around viziers and patrons at Rayy, Isfahan, and Nishapur. Philosophers faced suspicion from some jurists and theologians, and some wrote with caution about controversial doctrines. This contributed to the character of the literature, which uses technical language, commentary, and layered argument to address potentially sensitive questions, and which requires careful reading.

Section 43.14

Ibn Sina's Philosophy: The Shifaʾ, the Necessary Existent, and the Soul

Section 43.10 introduced Ibn Sina as a physician. His influence on later thought was at least as great as a philosopher, and the two roles should be kept distinct. His principal philosophical encyclopedia, Kitab al-shifaʾ (Book of Healing), treats logic, the natural sciences, mathematics, and metaphysics in a single large work, composed in stages during years of service to patrons in Hamadan and Isfahan. "Healing" here refers to the healing of the soul from ignorance, and the work has nothing to do with clinical medicine. He also produced an abridgment, the Najat (Deliverance); the Danishnama-yi ʿAlaʾi, a Persian summary written for his patron at Isfahan; and, late in life, the Isharat wa'l-tanbihat (Pointers and Reminders), a dense work that many later philosophers treated as his mature statement. His brief allegorical tales, such as Hayy ibn Yaqzan, present philosophical themes in symbolic form. Because the dating of individual works depends largely on his own autobiographical account and on his disciple al-Juzjani's continuation of it, chronology of the corpus involves some uncertainty.

Ibn Sina worked within the Aristotelian tradition developed by al-Farabi and others, while reworking it with considerable independence. Three of his arguments have attracted the most attention. The first is the distinction between essence and existence. He argued that for any thing other than God, what the thing is (its essence) is conceptually distinct from the fact that it is (its existence), and that existence is added to essence by a cause. This distinction became a major theme in later philosophy and theology, though historians debate how far he regarded it as a real distinction in things and not merely a distinction of thought.

The second argument concerns the Necessary Existent (wajib al-wujud). Ibn Sina reasoned that things whose existence depends on a cause are merely possible, and that a chain of possible things requires a being that exists necessarily by its own nature and not through another. This being is the source of the existence of everything else, and he identified it with God, described as one, simple, and without parts. The argument is metaphysical and does not depend on a beginning of the world in time. In his system, the world proceeds from God by emanation in an order of intellects and spheres, and the world was therefore eternal in the sense that it always depended on God without having begun at a moment. This feature of his teaching led to the most famous criticisms of his thought (§43.15).

The third concerns the soul. Ibn Sina held that the human soul is an immaterial substance, distinct from the body, and that it survives the body's death. His "floating man" thought experiment asks the reader to imagine a person created in a state of perfect suspension, without sensory input, who would still be aware of his own existence, and he used the argument to suggest that self-awareness does not depend on the body. Scholars discuss what the argument was meant to prove and how it relates to Aristotle's view of the soul as the form of the body. His account of prophecy, which treated the prophet as a person of extraordinary intellectual and imaginative faculties, was a serious attempt to explain revelation within a philosophical framework, and some religious scholars objected to it. As noted in §43.13, philosophers wrote under a degree of public scrutiny, and the interpretation of their statements about religion is a subject of continuing scholarly argument.

His logic and his psychology were also influential. In logic he reorganized the Aristotelian system, paying attention to the temporal and modal aspects of propositions, and his work became the basis for a long tradition of logical teaching. In psychology he described internal senses, such as common sense, imagination, and estimation, located in brain faculties, in terms that tied his medical and philosophical writings together. These features illustrate the way in which the philosophical and medical parts of his career, though separate in literary form, shared a conceptual vocabulary.

The reception of Ibn Sina's thought was complex and long. Some of his readers developed and defended it, some criticized it, and many reworked it. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, scholars such as Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (1149–1209), born in Rayy, wrote detailed commentaries and critiques that placed Avicennan philosophy in conversation with Ashʿari theology, and in the thirteenth century Nasir al-Din Tusi defended and clarified Ibn Sina's arguments against his critics, as discussed in §43.19. Avicennism thus became a framework within which philosophers and theologians argued with one another, a development whose later phases belong to subsequent volumes. Ibn Sina was also translated into Latin in the twelfth century and discussed by Christian scholastics, a transmission mentioned in §43.18.

Section 43.15

Al-Ghazali's Critique of the Philosophers

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111), born in Tus in Khurasan, was a jurist and theologian of the Shafiʿi and Ashʿari traditions who held a chair at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad from 1091. Chapter 40 describes his career, his departure from Baghdad in 1095, and his later turn toward Sufism, and this section examines only his engagement with philosophy. That engagement is often misdescribed. It is sometimes said that al-Ghazali "destroyed" philosophy or science in the Islamic world, or that his work ended a golden age. The evidence does not support those claims, as this section explains.

His principal works on the subject are two. The Maqasid al-falasifa (Intentions of the Philosophers) is a clear exposition of the philosophy of Ibn Sina and al-Farabi in logic, physics, and metaphysics, composed so that readers could understand the doctrines before assessing them. The Tahafut al-falasifa (Incoherence of the Philosophers), written around 1095, then argues against twenty doctrines of the philosophers. Of these, he judged three to be contrary to Islam to the point of unbelief: the claim that the world is eternal, the claim that God knows only universals and not particulars, and the denial of the bodily resurrection. On the remaining seventeen he held that the philosophers were wrong but not unbelievers. He directed much of the criticism against Ibn Sina and al-Farabi in particular, and the argument often proceeds by showing that the philosophers' own premises do not support their conclusions.

A significant part of the critique concerns causation. Al-Ghazali argued that observed connections between events, such as fire and the burning of cotton, do not prove that the fire necessarily produces the burning, and that God's will is the ultimate cause, which could in principle act otherwise, as in miracles. Historians discuss how far this argument was an attack on natural science and how far it was a defense of divine omnipotence that left ordinary scientific practice intact. He accepted that natural events normally follow regular patterns established by God's habit, and he did not forbid the study of those patterns.

Other works show that his position was more discriminating than a rejection. In the Miʿyar al-ʿilm and the Mihakk al-nazar he set out logic in an accessible form, and in the Mizan al-ʿamal he treated ethics, and in his autobiographical al-Munqidh min al-dalal (Deliverer from Error) he argued that logic and mathematics are not themselves opposed to religion, although he warned that students could be misled by the confidence of mathematicians into accepting their theology as well. His influence thus ran in two directions. He criticized specific philosophical doctrines, and he helped to bring logic into the education of jurists and theologians, so that later Sunni scholarship used Aristotelian logic far more than earlier Sunni scholarship had.

What followed was not the end of philosophy. In the twelfth century, Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and others continued to debate Ibn Sina; in Andalusia, Ibn Rushd (Averroes) wrote a reply titled Tahafut al-tahafut (Incoherence of the Incoherence) in the late twelfth century; and in Iran, as the next section shows, the thinker Suhrawardi developed a new philosophical system within the same century. Scientific work also continued. Khayyam and al-Khazini, discussed earlier, worked after al-Ghazali's critique, and the thirteenth-century activities of Tusi are further evidence. The decline-of-science narrative, which attributes the supposed weakening of science to a religious reaction, is not borne out by the record of scholarly activity, and historians have increasingly replaced it with accounts that emphasize institutional change, patronage, political instability, and shifts in the forms scholarship took.

Thematic paraphrase (not a quotation): The work is organized as a series of discussions, each taking one doctrine of the philosophers and arguing that their demonstrations do not establish it. In the discussion of causation he argues that the observed concomitance of events does not demonstrate necessary connection.

Analysis: The text shows a theologian engaging with philosophical arguments on their own terms and using philosophical methods to contest them. Its structure indicates that al-Ghazali regarded Ibn Sina's system as intellectually serious enough to require point-by-point reply. The charge of unbelief was a significant legal and religious step, and it demonstrates how contested the boundary between philosophy and religious doctrine was in the late eleventh century.

Limitations: The Tahafut is an argumentative work, not a neutral description, and its summary of the philosophers' positions should be checked against their own texts. The paraphrase above does not convey the detail of the arguments. The work cannot tell us how widely philosophers' ideas circulated, how many readers accepted al-Ghazali's conclusions, or how it affected scientific institutions. Later reception is a separate question.

Perspective: A leading Sunni jurist-theologian writing for scholarly readers. The views of the philosophers themselves, expressed in their own works and in replies such as Ibn Rushd's, are not represented here, nor are those of non-elite believers.

Section 43.16

Suhrawardi and the Philosophy of Illumination

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi was born about 1154 in Suhraward, a town in the Jibal region of northwestern Iran, near Zanjan. He studied at Maragha, where one of his teachers was Majd al-Din al-Jili, and at Isfahan, and he traveled through Anatolia and Syria, seeking patrons and teachers. He settled at Aleppo under the protection of the Ayyubid governor al-Malik al-Zahir, a son of Saladin. There his teaching and writing aroused the opposition of jurists, who accused him of heresy, and he was put to death in 1191, at about the age of thirty-six. Sources differ on the manner and exact circumstances of his execution, and the political context of a young governor under pressure from his father and from local religious authorities is part of the background. Because of his death, later tradition has often called him al-maqtul, "the killed," or "the martyr."

His principal work is the Hikmat al-ishraq (Philosophy of Illumination), completed in 1186. In it he criticized parts of the Peripatetic tradition, including Ibn Sina's definitions and his account of knowledge, and he proposed a philosophy in which light is the fundamental metaphor and, in his system, the fundamental reality. Beings differ in the intensity of their light, from the Light of Lights, the highest source, down through degrees of light and shadow. A central epistemological idea is ʿilm huduri, "knowledge by presence," according to which the soul knows itself and certain realities directly and immediately and not through definition or inference. For Suhrawardi, philosophy requires both rigorous reasoning and a direct experience, attained through spiritual discipline, of the realities reasoning describes.

The word ishraq means "illumination" or "the rising of the sun," and later authors have related it to an eastern orientation, though how that relation should be understood is debated. Suhrawardi presented his philosophy as a revival of an ancient wisdom shared by Greek and Persian sages, among whom he named figures he called the Khusrawani sages, a lineage of Iranian wise men. Historians disagree on how to read this claim. Some regard it as a genuine though idealized recollection of Iranian traditions that he connected to Zoroastrian ideas of light and angels. Others read it as a philosophical construction in which the ancient Persian legacy served to legitimate a new system. It is clear that he used Iranian names and angelic imagery, and that his texts are in conversation with Neoplatonic and Islamic mystical traditions, but there is no evidence that he had access to a continuous school of ancient Iranian philosophers.

Suhrawardi's thought needs to be kept separate from later developments. It is not identical to Sufism, though it has affinities with it and was later read by Sufi and philosophical writers together, and it is not simply a continuation of Ibn Sina. His writings influenced a long tradition of commentary in later centuries, which belongs to later volumes, and Illuminationist ideas became a significant part of Iranian philosophical teaching. For the present chapter the point is that Iranian philosophy in the twelfth century was neither extinguished by al-Ghazali's critique nor confined to commentary on Ibn Sina. A young scholar of provincial origin, educated in Iranian cities, produced a new philosophical system and paid for it with his life.

Historical Debate

Religion, Philosophy, and Science: Conflict, Cooperation, or Something Else?

The question: How did religious authority relate to philosophy and the natural sciences in medieval Iran? Was the relationship basically one of conflict, of harmony, or of changing negotiation?

Interpretation A — Conflict between faith and reason: Older narratives described a struggle in which rationalist philosophy and science were eventually suppressed by orthodox religion, with al-Ghazali's critique and the execution of Suhrawardi as symbols of a decisive turn.
Interpretation B — Integration and selective tension: Others stress that many scholars were simultaneously religious and scientific, that physicians, astronomers, jurists, and theologians often belonged to the same circles, and that tensions arose over specific doctrines, such as eternity of the world or divine knowledge of particulars, and not over science as such. On this reading, religious thinkers used philosophical logic, and philosophers framed their work within Islam.
Interpretation C — Institutional and political explanation: A third approach shifts attention from doctrine to institutions: patronage, political instability, the form of colleges, and the career paths of scholars shaped which fields flourished at which times, so that conflict between ideas explains less than changes in support.

Evidence: The evidence includes the philosophical and theological texts themselves, biographical dictionaries, endowment records of colleges and libraries, patronage patterns, and accounts of trials and executions such as that of Suhrawardi.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern interpretation and emphasis, and also evidence, because the surviving sources are unevenly distributed and most were written by scholars who had a stake in the argument. Historians differ on how general the occasional persecutions were and on how to weigh the persistence of scientific activity after the twelfth century.

Why it matters: If the relationship is read as conflict, later developments tend to be told as decline. If it is read as negotiation, historians look for the forms in which science and philosophy continued, including in madrasa curricula and commentary traditions. The choice affects how students understand both the period and the larger idea of a "Golden Age" followed by decline.

Division V · §§43.17–43.20 Institutions, Practice, Legacy, and Synthesis
Section 43.17

Learning Communities: Madrasas, Libraries, Observatories, and Courts

Knowledge in medieval Iran lived in particular places and relationships. The most important were the teaching circle, the college, the library, the hospital, the observatory, and the court. None of these functioned alone, and a scholar might pass through several in a career. The picture also changed over time. In the tenth century, much scientific and philosophical activity took place in courts and informal circles, as the Samanid, Buyid, and Ghaznavid patrons discussed in Chapters 35 and 36 competed to attract scholars. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the Seljuk-era colleges described in Chapters 39 and 40 gave religious learning a stronger institutional base, and the relationship of the sciences to these colleges is the central problem of this section.

The madrasa, a college endowed by waqf for the teaching of religious law and related subjects, is often treated as the standard institution of learning in the later medieval Islamic world. Its main purpose was the training of jurists, judges, preachers, and administrators. The Nizamiyya colleges promoted by Nizam al-Mulk, discussed in Chapter 39, were prominent examples. It would be a serious mistake to assume that every madrasa taught medicine, astronomy, or philosophy, and the evidence suggests that most did not. The mathematical, medical, and philosophical sciences were often taught in other settings: in private study with a master, in hospitals, in the households of patrons, and in informal circles. Some madrasas, however, included arithmetic, logic, or the "rational sciences" in their teaching, especially in later centuries, and the use of Aristotelian logic by jurists and theologians after al-Ghazali made some of this material more widely available. The relationship was thus partial and varied by place and period.

Libraries were another essential institution. Rulers, viziers, and wealthy scholars collected books, and some libraries were open to scholars. The tenth-century Buyid vizier al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad, whose court at Rayy was a center of literary activity, is reported to have possessed a very large library, and the sources describe collections at Nishapur, Rayy, Isfahan, and other cities. Merv, in particular, had libraries that the geographer Yaqut al-Hamawi, who visited in the early thirteenth century shortly before the Mongol invasion, described in terms that suggest a rich scholarly culture. Some of the sources describing library sizes give figures that cannot be trusted, and the descriptions are often rhetorical, but they show that book collecting was a valued activity. The destruction of libraries in the Mongol conquests, discussed in Volume IV, was therefore a major loss, as was the loss of the Alamut library described in Chapter 41.

Observatories formed a smaller category. The sultan Malik Shah's sponsorship of astronomical work in the 1070s, discussed in §43.8, is a prominent Iranian example, though its institutional form and duration are less well documented than those of later observatories. Courts provided salaries and prestige, and scholars competed for places. Patronage had advantages and drawbacks. It gave scholars resources and audiences, but it also made them dependent on rulers' favor and subject to the fate of dynasties, as Ibn Sina's wandering career and the dispersal of scholars after political upheavals show.

The ways in which knowledge was transmitted shaped what survived. A teacher might give a student an ijaza, a license certifying that the student had read a text with him and was authorized to transmit it. Students traveled long distances to study with particular masters, a practice sometimes called the "journey in search of knowledge." Copyists and stationers (warraqun) produced and sold books in city markets. Commentaries and abridgments allowed difficult texts to be taught, which helps explain why commentary became so central a genre. Women are less visible in the evidence, but the sources record women who transmitted religious texts and, in some cases, taught, particularly in the scholarly families of cities like Nishapur and Isfahan. The extent of women's participation in the scientific and philosophical fields is much less clear, and historians should not infer either wide participation or complete absence from the limited evidence.

Language shaped communities too. Arabic served as the main language of religious scholarship and of most scientific and philosophical writing, while New Persian developed as a language of literature, history, administration, and increasingly of science, as §43.3 explained. Some scholars wrote in both. Readers who knew only Persian could thus reach part of the learned tradition through works such as the Persian summaries of Ibn Sina, al-Biruni's Tafhim, and medical encyclopedias, while those who knew Arabic could read the full range of the technical literature. This bilingual situation was one of the distinctive features of Iranian scholarship.

Section 43.18

Knowledge in Practice: Calendars, Water, Land, Craft, and Prediction

Scientific knowledge was not only theoretical. Much of it was applied to the tasks of government, agriculture, building, trade, and ritual, and an account of Iranian science that stops at the great names omits this practical dimension. The evidence for it is spread across technical treatises, administrative manuals, and incidental references, and it is less complete than the evidence for theory, but it is enough to show that knowledge was embedded in everyday institutions.

Time-keeping and the calendar offer the clearest example. The Islamic religious calendar is lunar, with months beginning at the sighting of the new moon, and it does not track the seasons. Farmers, tax collectors, and estate managers needed a solar reckoning, and Iranian administration made use of older solar calendars of Sasanian origin and of local agricultural calendars. Astronomers and calendar specialists therefore had a continuing task: computing the times of prayer, the visibility of the new moon, the direction of Mecca, and the start of solar years. The Jalali reform of 1079 (§43.8) belongs to this context. Zijes were not simply scholarly products but tools for such uses.

Water management was a second field. On the Iranian plateau, agriculture depended heavily on irrigation, including the underground channels called qanats. The scholar al-Karaji (also called al-Karkhi) wrote, around the early eleventh century, a treatise on the extraction of hidden waters, Inbat al-miyah al-khafiya, which discusses the identification of groundwater, the digging of channels, and the surveying methods needed to give them a correct slope. The same author wrote on arithmetic and algebra, a combination that shows how mathematical and practical knowledge could belong together. Land measurement, the calculation of areas and volumes, and the arithmetic of taxation and inheritance were tasks for trained secretaries and surveyors, and the Persian and Arabic arithmetic manuals written for scribes show the extent of this practical mathematics. An encyclopedia for secretaries written in the late tenth century, the Mafatih al-ʿulum (Keys of the Sciences) of the Samanid-era author Muhammad al-Khwarizmi, who should not be confused with the mathematician of the ninth century, classified the vocabulary of both the religious and the foreign sciences for administrators who needed to understand them.

Craftsmen also used geometry. The tenth-century mathematician Abu al-Wafaʾ al-Buzjani, who was born in Buzjan in Khurasan and worked in Baghdad, is credited with a work on the geometrical constructions that artisans need, reflecting an exchange between mathematicians and practitioners. The geometric patterns in the architecture and decorative arts of Iran draw on a long tradition of practical geometry, though the relationship between written treatises and workshop practice is debated, since many craftsmen probably learned methods orally and by apprenticeship. Chapter 42 treats the arts and literature of the period more fully, and the point here is that mathematical and artistic knowledge overlapped.

Prediction was a field in which scientific and non-scientific concerns were deeply mixed. Astrology, the use of the positions of the celestial bodies to forecast events and advise on timing, was practiced at courts and in cities, and many astronomers also cast horoscopes, partly because patrons paid for them. Al-Biruni's Tafhim (Book of Instruction), composed in Arabic and Persian versions, introduces geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and astrology, and shows how closely the fields were joined in teaching. Opinions about astrology varied. Some scholars defended it as a legitimate application of astronomy, while others criticized its predictions as unsound or contrary to religion. A treatise attacking astrological judgments is transmitted under Ibn Sina’s name; its arguments question the reliability of prediction while retaining aspects of medieval celestial physics. A responsible account should neither treat astrology as a modern pseudo-science and ignore its role in patronage, nor present it as the accepted method of all astronomers.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iranian and Persianate Scholarship, c. 850–1256, and Its Afterlives

What Changed

  • The institutional setting: from courts and informal circles in the ninth and tenth centuries toward a heavier role for endowed colleges in the eleventh and twelfth
  • The languages of scholarship: from almost exclusive use of Arabic in the sciences toward significant Persian writing in medicine, philosophy, ethics, and astronomy
  • The philosophical agenda: from the reception of Greek philosophy, through Ibn Sina's synthesis, to criticism by al-Ghazali and new systems such as Suhrawardi's
  • The political environment, from the Samanid and Buyid courts through Ghaznavid and Seljuk rule to the Mongol threat

What Stayed the Same

  • The dependence of scholars on patronage, endowments, and personal networks
  • The use of manuscripts, teachers' licenses, and commentary as means of transmission
  • The close link between theoretical and applied knowledge in medicine, astronomy, agriculture, and administration
  • The participation of scholars from many regions and linguistic communities in a shared learned culture

Transmission and afterlives: Iranian-born authors influenced scholarship far beyond Iran. Ibn Sina's Canon was translated into Latin in the twelfth century and used in European medical teaching, as §43.10 noted, and Latin translators also rendered works by al-Razi and by al-Khwarizmi. In the Islamic world, Avicennan philosophy and Persian ethical and medical literature continued to be read and taught. These transfers did not move in one direction or by a single channel: texts and ideas passed through Spain, Sicily, Syria, and other routes, and later Iranian scholars worked in conversation with traditions from Iraq, Syria, and Egypt.

Pattern: Change was gradual and uneven, and different fields followed different timetables. Scholars and patrons experienced the political shifts most directly, while the techniques of surveying, irrigation, and arithmetic probably changed more slowly in practice.

Section 43.19

Nasir al-Din Tusi before 1256: A Scholar at the End of an Era

Nasir al-Din Tusi (1201–1274) is best known for work done after 1256, when he served the Mongol commander Hulagu and helped to organize the observatory at Maragha. That later career belongs to Volume IV and is not treated here. This section considers only his life up to the Mongol conquest of the Nizari fortresses, because that earlier part of his career shows the state of Iranian scholarship in the generation before the Mongol conquest and connects this chapter to Chapter 41.

Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Tusi was born in Tus in Khurasan in 1201, into a family of Twelver Shiʿi background. He received a conventional education in religious subjects and then pursued philosophy, mathematics, medicine, and logic with teachers in Tus and in Nishapur, which was still an important center of learning. He was a young man when the Mongol invasion of Khurasan in 1220 and 1221 devastated the cities of the region, an event discussed in Chapter 44. The disruption of the 1220s drove many scholars to seek patrons in safer places, and the surviving accounts of Tusi's life, which he told partly himself, say that he went to the Ismaili fortress districts of Quhistan and later to Alamut, where he lived for a period of some years. The exact dates and circumstances of his residence are disputed: he presented his stay as protection for a scholar in a dangerous time, while later hostile sources described it as captivity, and some modern scholars suggest a more mixed arrangement in which a scholar both benefited from patronage and had limited freedom to leave.

The works he composed in this period show the range of Iranian scholarship. The Akhlaq-i Nasiri (Nasirean Ethics), completed in 1235 and dedicated to the Ismaili governor of Quhistan, is a Persian treatise on ethics, household management, and political governance that draws heavily on the tradition of Miskawayh and al-Farabi (§43.13). It became one of the most widely read works of practical philosophy in the Persian-speaking world. He also wrote on Ismaili doctrine in the Rawda-yi taslim (Paradise of Submission), completed at Alamut in 1243; the degree of personal religious commitment reflected in these writings remains debated. He produced treatises on logic and philosophy and completed a commentary on Ibn Sina's Isharat around 1246, defending the Avicennan system against the criticisms of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi. Some other works remain difficult to date, so the full sequence of his intellectual development should not be made more precise than the evidence allows.

The example of Tusi shows several things. First, patronage in this period came from unexpected quarters: an Ismaili polity under frequent attack by Sunni rulers supported a Twelver Shiʿi scholar of philosophy and mathematics. Second, scholars were mobile and their careers followed political circumstances. Third, the intellectual tradition of Ibn Sina was alive and was being actively defended. Fourth, the boundaries among religious communities were more permeable for scholars than polemical literature suggests. He left the fortresses when the Mongols took them in 1256, and what followed is the subject of later chapters. The reasons for his departure, whether voluntary, compelled, or both, remain part of the same debate that surrounds his earlier residence.

Section 43.20

Synthesis and Review: Iranian Scholarship in the Medieval World

The chapter has argued that Iranian and Persianate scholars between roughly 850 and 1256 took part in a shared learned culture that extended across Islamic Eurasia, and that they contributed to it in many fields. They did so in Arabic and increasingly in Persian, in courts, colleges, hospitals, libraries, and observatories, and under patrons whose fortunes changed repeatedly. The record includes the algebra and geometry of al-Khwarizmi's tradition and Khayyam's treatment of cubic equations, the astronomical and geographical work of al-Biruni, the observatory and calendar of the Seljuk court, the clinical writing of al-Razi and the encyclopedic synthesis of Ibn Sina's Canon, the logical and political thought of al-Farabi, Ibn Sina's metaphysics, al-Ghazali's critique, and Suhrawardi's Illuminationism.

Several lessons follow from this record. Scholarship was social and institutional, dependent on patronage and endowment, and the periodization of Iranian science cannot be separated from political history. The sources are written mostly by and for an educated elite, and the experience of ordinary patients, craftsmen, students, and cultivators has to be inferred from indirect evidence. The relationship between religion and the sciences varied by field and period and cannot be summarized as harmony or conflict. And the idea of an uninterrupted Golden Age followed by sudden decline does not fit the evidence of continued achievement after al-Ghazali and into the thirteenth century.

Within the textbook, the chapter follows Chapter 42's treatment of Persian literary culture and prepares for Chapter 44, which treats the political crisis of the early thirteenth century, and for Volume IV, where the Mongol conquests and the later career of scholars such as Tusi are discussed. The scholars in this chapter were not preparing for any later outcome. They were responding to problems of their own times, and the interest of their work lies in those problems as well as in what later readers made of it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Iranian and Persianate scholars between roughly 850 and 1256 participated in, and contributed to, a shared learned culture that spanned Islamic Eurasia. They worked in Arabic and increasingly in Persian, in courts, colleges, hospitals, libraries, and observatories, and under patrons whose fortunes shifted with the dynasties discussed in earlier chapters. The chapter treats the Arabic-language scholarship of Iranian-born authors as part of Iranian intellectual history, while recognizing that the scholarly world was larger than any modern state.

The foundations were plural. Scholars inherited Greek philosophy and science through translation, Indian mathematical and astronomical methods, Sasanian and Iranian practical and administrative knowledge, and the new religious and legal learning of Islam. They were never simply passive recipients: they selected, corrected, and extended what they received. The movement from Arabic to the emerging New Persian shaped the way knowledge reached wider readerships, and manuscripts, paper, libraries, and licenses for transmission supported that movement.

In mathematics and astronomy, scholars developed algebra and its geometrical applications, trigonometric methods, and astronomical tables. Omar Khayyam classified cubic equations and solved them by intersecting conic sections, while al-Biruni combined astronomy, geography, chronology, and the study of India in works of great range, and the Seljuk calendar reform of 1079 shows state support for these skills. Observation and instruments improved the quality of zijes, though the institutional basis of observatories remained partial and uneven.

In medicine, al-Razi emphasized clinical observation and the critical assessment of earlier authorities, and Ibn Sina's Canon organized medical knowledge into a comprehensive system that influenced teaching in the Islamic world and in Latin Europe. Hospitals, pharmacies, and the work of physicians, surgeons, midwives, and healers made up a broader system of care that the great books only partly describe. Medicine remained humoral, and its achievements and limits should both be recognized.

In the natural sciences, Iranian scholars contributed to the measurement of specific weights, the study of minerals, and the theory of motion, and also practiced alchemy, which combined technique with aims that later experiment abandoned. Al-Biruni's mineralogy and al-Khazini's hydrostatic balance show careful measurement. The relation of medieval scholarship to inherited traditions is best described as creative development within them, and neither as passive preservation nor as wholesale innovation.

In philosophy, al-Farabi's logic and political thought, Miskawayh's ethics, and Ibn Sina's metaphysics, including the essence–existence distinction and the argument from the Necessary Existent, established a rigorous philosophical tradition. Al-Ghazali's Tahafut criticized specific doctrines of the philosophers, condemning three as unbelief, but also contributed to the integration of logic into religious learning, and it did not end science or philosophy. Suhrawardi's Illuminationism, with its metaphysics of light and its knowledge by presence, shows that Iranian philosophy was still generating new systems in the later twelfth century.

The institutions of learning were varied. Madrasas trained jurists and officials and did not uniformly teach the sciences, which were often transmitted in private circles, hospitals, and court settings. Libraries and booksellers supported the manuscript culture, and patronage, endowment, and personal networks determined which projects flourished. Practical knowledge in calendars, irrigation, surveying, craft geometry, and administration connected learned and everyday life, and astrology occupied a contested position between science and prediction.

The evidence for these developments comes largely from texts written by and for educated men, along with biographical dictionaries, instruments, and a limited archaeological record. The evidence is uneven, and many works remain unedited, which means that conclusions should be stated with appropriate caution. Several important traditions, such as the attribution of works to Jabir, the dating of Ibn Sina's writings, and the interpretation of Suhrawardi's Persian sources, remain disputed, and ordinary people appear only indirectly.

Nasir al-Din Tusi, who lived in the Nizari fortress districts before 1256, illustrates the end of an era. He wrote the Akhlaq-i Nasiri, defended Ibn Sina's philosophy, and worked in an Ismaili setting, a reminder that patronage and scholarly life crossed the boundaries of religious communities. His later career, with the Mongols, belongs to Volume IV.

The overall significance of the chapter lies in what it shows about the character of medieval Iranian intellectual life: plural in language and religion, dependent on institutions and patrons, mobile, and creative within inherited frameworks. It also challenges the narrative of a Golden Age followed by decline, and replaces it with a more detailed picture of continuing, uneven achievement in a changing political world.

Key Terms

Falsafa
The Arabic term for philosophy in the tradition derived from Greek, especially Aristotelian, thought.
Hikma
"Wisdom"; a broad term for philosophy, practical wisdom, or, for Suhrawardi, a system of illumination.
ʿIlm
Knowledge or learning, often religious knowledge but also scientific knowledge.
Peripatetic
Belonging to the Aristotelian tradition of philosophy, as developed by al-Farabi and Ibn Sina.
Zij
A handbook of astronomical tables and instructions for computing positions and calendars.
Jalali calendar
A solar calendar introduced under Malik Shah in 1079 to align the year with the vernal equinox.
Algebra
The mathematical discipline of solving equations by systematic operations, developed in this period from the work of al-Khwarizmi.
Cubic equation
An equation involving the third power of the unknown; Khayyam solved certain types geometrically by intersecting conic sections.
Conic section
A curve formed by cutting a cone with a plane, such as a parabola or circle.
Humoral theory
The medical theory that health depends on the balance of bodily fluids, or humors, inherited from Greek medicine.
Canon of Medicine
Ibn Sina's five-book medical encyclopedia, translated into Latin in the twelfth century.
Book of Healing (Shifaʾ)
Ibn Sina's philosophical encyclopedia, concerned with the healing of the soul and not with clinical medicine.
Bimaristan
A hospital, often endowed, that combined care, teaching, and recordkeeping.
Waqf
A permanent endowment of property or revenue for a charitable or religious purpose.
Muhtasib
An official who supervised markets, weights, and public conduct, sometimes including medical practice.
Materia medica
A reference work describing drugs, their sources, properties, and uses.
Specific weight
The weight of a substance relative to an equal volume of water, a measure used by al-Biruni and al-Khazini.
Mayl
"Inclination," Ibn Sina's term for a quality that explains the continued motion of a projectile.
Jabirian corpus
A large body of alchemical writings attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan, now generally regarded as the work of several authors.
Necessary Existent
Ibn Sina's term for the being that exists by its own nature and is the source of all other existence.
Essence and existence
Ibn Sina's distinction between what a thing is and the fact that it is.
Floating man
Ibn Sina's thought experiment suggesting that self-awareness does not depend on sensory experience of the body.
Emanation
The doctrine that the world proceeds from God by an ordered, eternal flow and not by a creation at a moment in time.
Tahafut al-falasifa
Al-Ghazali's "Incoherence of the Philosophers," a critique of twenty philosophical doctrines, written c. 1095.
Ishraq (Illumination)
Suhrawardi's philosophy in which light is the fundamental image and reality.
ʿIlm huduri
"Knowledge by presence," direct and immediate knowledge, central to Suhrawardi's epistemology.
Madrasa
An endowed college for teaching religious law and related subjects, not necessarily the sciences.
Ijaza
A teacher's license certifying that a student may transmit a text or teach a subject.
Warraq
A copyist and stationer who produced and sold manuscripts.
Qanat
An underground irrigation channel that brings groundwater to the surface by gravity.
Akhlaq-i Nasiri
Tusi's Persian ethical treatise of 1235, treating ethics, household, and government.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the intellectual life of medieval Iran was a major part of the region's history and not an ornament added to political narratives. Scholars working under the Samanids, Buyids, Ghaznavids, Seljuks, and their successors produced works that shaped medicine, mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, and ethics across Eurasia. At the same time, their work depended on the same dynasties, cities, and institutions that earlier chapters described, and its fortunes cannot be separated from them.

The chapter also reveals how historians know what they know. Much of the evidence is textual, produced by an educated elite, and sometimes dated or attributed uncertainly. The paraphrased source windows, the debate boxes, and the cautions about attributions are reminders that knowledge about the past is itself reconstructed and revisable. Questions about who counts as Iranian, whether a Golden Age ended, and how religion and science interacted have no single settled answer.

Its legacies were long. Ibn Sina's synthesis, Persian ethical and medical writing, Illuminationist philosophy, and the practices of calendar-making and irrigation survived in later Iranian and Persianate societies and in other parts of the world through channels that were multiple and not always direct. Their continued study belongs to later volumes.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for what follows. The political crisis of the early thirteenth century and the Mongol conquests, treated in Chapter 44 and Volume IV, interrupted many institutions and dispersed many scholars, but they did not end Iranian scholarship, and the careers of figures like Tusi show how learning adapted to new masters. Understanding the world that existed before that upheaval makes it possible to judge what was lost, what survived, and what changed.
