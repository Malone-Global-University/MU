The Safavid Sufi Order and the Rise of Shah Ismail

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Safavid dynasty did not begin with a conquest. It began, as far as the surviving evidence allows us to say, with a tomb, a lodge, and a succession of teachers whose followers believed that holiness could be inherited. Two centuries of religious institution-building lay behind the young man who led his supporters out of Gilan at the end of the fifteenth century, and the movement that carried him was shaped as much by kinship, pilgrimage, and devotion as by cavalry.

In the city of Ardabil, on a high plain beneath Mount Sabalan in the northwest of the Iranian plateau, stands a complex of buildings that grew up around the grave of a Sufi shaykh who died in 1334. The ensemble is today inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List, and its present form reflects centuries of additions, many of them made after the family of the shaykh's descendants had become kings. The tomb itself, and the community of disciples that gathered around it, are much older than the monarchy. They are the starting point of this chapter, which asks how a devotional community of the early fourteenth century became the organization through which a boy of about thirteen could, in 1500, set out to claim power from established rulers.

The answer unfolds across about two hundred years and across a set of historical processes that students should keep distinct. The first is institutional: the creation, under Shaykh Safi al-Din Ardabili (c. 1252–1334) and his descendants, of a Sufi order, the Safaviyya, with a lodge, a shrine, endowed property, and a hereditary leadership. The second is social and geographical: the extension of the order's following from Ardabil to Gilan, Azerbaijan, eastern Anatolia, and Syria, through deputies, travelers, and devotees who lived under many different governments. The third is religious: the changing language in which the order's leaders and followers expressed their devotion to the Prophet's family, to ʿAli, and to the spiritual guide himself, a change that scholars date and explain in different ways. The fourth is military and political: the increasing reliance, from the mid-fifteenth century, on armed followers, on dynastic marriage to a Turkmen ruling house, and on campaigns that brought the order into conflict with its neighbors.

The chapter's central argument is that Safavid state formation was preceded by approximately two centuries of religious institution-building, inherited spiritual authority, local patronage, regional recruitment, kinship alliances, and increasingly militant political organization. It does not follow that the outcome was inevitable. The Safaviyya could have remained what it was for much of the fourteenth century, a respected regional religious institution. Two of its leaders, Junayd and Haydar, died in campaigns against Caucasian rulers. Their descendants were imprisoned and hunted. A boy survived in the protection of a regional prince in Gilan. That the movement did not end with these setbacks reflects the durability of the structures its leaders had built, but also a series of contingent circumstances, among them the collapse of Aq Qoyunlu unity examined in Chapter 55.

A second theme concerns religious identity. A common narrative describes the Safavids as a Shiʿi dynasty and projects that description backward onto the founder of the order and his early followers. The evidence does not support so simple a picture. The earliest source for Safi al-Din's life, the Safvat al-Safa of Ibn Bazzaz, portrays a pious Sufi within the Sunni tradition of his region, and the version in which the text circulated later was revised under Safavid rule, in ways that affected the family's genealogy and religious profile. The official Twelver Shiʿism established after 1501, the subject of Chapter 57, was not identical to the devotional beliefs of fifteenth-century Safavid followers, many of whom were Turkmen pastoralists whose religion combined Sufi practice, reverence for ʿAli and the Prophet's family, and expectations about the extraordinary status of their guide. Students will need to hold several ideas at once: that devotion to ʿAli was widespread in the Islamic world and not confined to Shiʿis; that the Safaviyya did change over time; and that scholars still disagree about when and how.

A third theme concerns the people who made the movement possible. The leaders of the Safaviyya are better documented than their followers, but the order could not have functioned without deputies who traveled between Ardabil and distant communities, without the artisans, merchants, and cultivators who supplied the shrine, without the women of the family whose marriages and household management sustained it, and without the Turkmen horsemen who came to be known as Qizilbash, or "Red Heads." The Qizilbash were not a single tribe. They were a shifting coalition of groups, each with its own chiefs and interests, held together by loyalty to a spiritual guide. Understanding how such a coalition formed, and why it could be mobilized for war, is one of the chapter's principal tasks.

The evidence is distinctive. Much of what we know about the early order comes from hagiography, the literary genre that celebrates the virtues and miracles of a holy person, and from chronicles written under Safavid patronage after the dynasty had come to power. Those later works had reasons to present the family's history as orderly and destined. Against them historians set contemporary or near-contemporary evidence: Aq Qoyunlu court histories such as Abu Bakr Tihrani's Kitab-i Diyarbakriyya, Timurid and Ottoman narratives, the testimony of European and Mamluk observers, endowment deeds and decrees, coins, and the standing architecture of the Ardabil shrine. Each source class illuminates the movement from a particular angle, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose interests a given account served.

The chapter fits among its neighbors as follows. Chapter 55 examined the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu states, the political environment in which the Safaviyya operated, and it should be read alongside this chapter, which does not repeat the history of the Turkmen confederations. Chapter 57 will examine how Ismail established Safavid kingship, including the battle of Sharur, the entry into Tabriz in 1501, and the proclamation of sovereignty. This chapter therefore ends at the threshold of sovereignty rather than at its achievement. Its subject is the movement and the coalition that Ismail inherited and mobilized, a history that needs to be understood on its own terms before the dynasty can be properly understood.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Ardabil, and the Sufi lodge and shrine established there, could attract followers well beyond its immediate region in the post-Mongol period.
  2. Trace the development of the Safaviyya from the teaching of Zahed Gilani and Safi al-Din Ardabili through the hereditary leadership of Sadr al-Din Musa, Khwaja Ali, and Ibrahim.
  3. Distinguish a tariqa from a khanqah, and a murshid from a murid and a khalifa, and explain how these roles sustained a devotional network across political boundaries.
  4. Analyze the Safvat al-Safa as hagiography, explaining its value and limits as a source and the significance of its later revisions.
  5. Assess the contested succession of 1447–1448 and explain why later Safavid narratives obscure the dispute between Junayd and Jaʿfar.
  6. Explain how Junayd's recruitment among Anatolian and Syrian Turkmen, his use of the title sultan, and his alliance with Uzun Hasan changed the character of the order.
  7. Reconstruct Junayd's death in Shirvan in 1460 and Haydar's inheritance of leadership, correcting the common error that Haydar was born after his father's death.
  8. Evaluate competing interpretations of when and how the Safaviyya became Shiʿi, without projecting sixteenth-century Twelver Shiʿism onto fifteenth-century devotion.
  9. Describe the red headgear and the emergence of the Qizilbash, and explain why the Qizilbash must be understood as a shifting coalition and not as a single tribe.
  10. Analyze Haydar's Caucasian campaigns from the perspectives of his followers and of the communities he attacked, and explain why they led to his death in 1488.
  11. Trace the survival of Sultan Ali, Ibrahim, and Ismail through imprisonment, release, and refuge in Gilan, and the mobilization at Erzincan and in Shirvan up to the threshold of the events treated in Chapter 57.
  12. Synthesize the institutional, religious, kinship, and military processes that made Ismail's pursuit of sovereignty possible while explaining why the outcome was neither inevitable nor predetermined.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could religious allegiance connect people who did not live under a single government, and what made Ardabil a point of attachment for such a network?
  2. What is hagiography, and how can historians use a text built to celebrate a holy person as evidence for the life and community of that person?
  3. How did hereditary succession within a religious family strengthen the Safaviyya, and what tensions did it create?
  4. Why would a ruler of Azerbaijan take an interest in a dispute over leadership of a Sufi lodge?
  5. What changed in the Safaviyya under Junayd and Haydar, and how much of that change was religious, social, or political?
  6. What did it mean to be a follower of a Safavid shaykh in the fifteenth century, and how reliable are later labels such as "Shiʿi" or "Sunni" as descriptions of that devotion?
  7. How could a religious following assemble a formidable military coalition without possessing a state?
  8. Why did the Aq Qoyunlu rulers, who were kin to the Safavid family by marriage, turn against Haydar?
  9. How did the Safavid family survive the deaths of Junayd and Haydar and the persecution that followed, and what does survival reveal about the movement's structure?
  10. In what sense was Ismail's rise made possible by earlier developments, and in what sense was it not inevitable?

Historical Context

The Safaviyya took shape in the northwestern corner of the Iranian plateau, in the region of Azerbaijan, during and after the decline of the Ilkhanid state. Chapters 47 through 50 described the Ilkhans, who made Azerbaijan their political heartland and Tabriz their principal city, and Chapter 51 traced the fragmentation that followed the dynasty's collapse in the 1330s. Shaykh Safi al-Din lived most of his life under Ilkhanid rule and died in 1334, as that rule was weakening. His successors lived through the Jalayirids, the campaigns of Timur narrated in Chapter 52, the Timurid and Qara Qoyunlu governments of Azerbaijan, and the Aq Qoyunlu state examined in Chapter 55. The order's institutions therefore outlasted at least five successive regimes in the region, a fact that is central to understanding how it developed.

Religious life in this period was institutionally diverse. Jurists and scholars trained in the legal schools, among them the Shafiʿi school that was prominent in much of Azerbaijan and the Caspian region, staffed courts and colleges. Sufi lodges, known as khanqahs, served as centers of devotion, instruction, and hospitality, and many held endowed property. Shrines of saints, whether of Sufi shaykhs or of members of the Prophet's family, drew pilgrims and gave their custodians influence. Devotion to the Prophet's family, including ʿAli and the imams, was widespread among Sunnis, Sufis, and Shiʿis alike, as Chapter 55 noted in discussing Turkmen religion. The boundaries among these traditions were more porous than the confessional categories of the sixteenth century suggest, and this porosity is essential to understanding both the early Safaviyya and its later transformation.

Geography shaped the order's reach. Ardabil lay on a plain at the foot of Mount Sabalan, within reach of the pastures of the Mughan steppe, of the Caspian lowlands of Gilan across the Talysh mountains, of the cities of Arran and Shirvan to the north, and of Tabriz to the west. Routes from Ardabil linked these regions to eastern Anatolia and the upper Tigris. A religious center on such crossroads could receive pilgrims, merchants, and scholars from many directions, and its leaders could dispatch deputies along the same roads. The Turkmen groups who pastured herds across the region, whose seasonal movement and confederations were described in Chapter 55, formed an important part of the order's later following.

The political setting of the fifteenth century was one of competing regional powers. The Timurids held much of Iran and Central Asia and claimed lordship over the west; the Qara Qoyunlu under Jahan Shah controlled Azerbaijan and, for a time, much of western and central Iran; the Aq Qoyunlu under Uzun Hasan rose from their base on the upper Tigris to rule Iran from Tabriz; the Shirvanshahs governed a long-established principality in the eastern Caucasus; the Ottomans and Mamluks ruled to the west and southwest; and a mosaic of Georgian, Circassian, Armenian, Kurdish, and local Caspian authorities held fortresses, valleys, and passes. In this environment a religious family with a shrine, an endowment, and a network of devotees was a political asset to be courted, controlled, or feared. That is the background against which the Safavid leaders made their choices.

The chapter's period runs from the early fourteenth century, when Safi al-Din assumed leadership of his master's circle, to the eve of the Safavid state, with the emphasis falling on the transformation of the order between 1447 and 1500. It follows five phases: the foundation of the order and its institutions; the expansion of its network and the changes under Junayd; the alliance with the Aq Qoyunlu and the leadership of Haydar; the militarization of the following as the Qizilbash; and the persecution of the family, the refuge in Gilan, and the mobilization of Ismail. At its close the movement stood ready for the events that Chapter 57 will narrate.

Ardabil and the Foundations of the Safaviyya

The religious, geographic, and institutional origins of the movement before it became a military and political force.

Section 56.1

Ardabil, Azerbaijan, and the Religious Landscape of Post-Mongol Iran

Ardabil lies in the northwest of the Iranian plateau, on an upland plain beneath the volcanic massif of Mount Sabalan, in the historical region called Azerbaijan. The region should not be mapped onto the present-day republic of the same name, which occupies a part of what medieval writers called Arran, to the north of the Aras River. Azerbaijan in the fourteenth century meant the lands around Tabriz, Maragha, Urmia, and Ardabil, an area of mountain valleys, irrigated plains, and high pastures. Ardabil had long been a provincial town of some consequence, and it had suffered during the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century, but under the Ilkhans the region as a whole prospered, since the court wintered on the steppes nearby and Tabriz grew into one of the great commercial cities of western Asia.

What made Ardabil significant for a religious movement was less its size than its position. To the west, routes ran toward Tabriz and onward to Anatolia, the upper Tigris, and the Black Sea. To the north, the Mughan steppe and the lowlands of Arran led toward Shirvan and the Caucasus. To the east, mountain roads descended through the Talysh range to the Caspian coast and the districts of Gilan, with their rice fields, forests, and distinct local traditions. A shaykh at Ardabil could therefore be visited by merchants and pilgrims from several directions, and he could send representatives along the same roads. Geography did not make the Safaviyya a transregional movement, but it made such a movement possible.

The religious environment into which the order emerged was crowded and varied. After the Mongol conquests, Islamic institutions had survived and, in many places, flourished, supported by endowments, by the patronage of Mongol and Persian officials, and by the conversion of the Ilkhans to Islam under Ghazan at the end of the thirteenth century. Legal scholars of the Shafiʿi and Hanafi schools staffed courts and colleges. Sufi lodges, which provided hospitality, instruction, and a framework for personal devotion, multiplied. Shrines of saints and descendants of the Prophet drew visitors. Neither the Ilkhans nor their successors tried to impose a single confessional identity on this world, and the line between a Sufi, a pious jurist, and a devotee of the Prophet's family could be hard to draw. In the Caspian districts, Zaydi and other Shiʿi communities existed alongside Sunni populations, a point that matters for the later Safavid refuge in Gilan.

Networks of spiritual authority depended on ordinary people. Merchants carried news, donations, and requests for blessing along trade routes; craftsmen and bazaar traders supplied lodges and shrines and sometimes endowed them; village communities gave a tithe of produce or labor to a shaykh whom they revered; scholars lent a lodge their learning and legitimacy; and travelers, among them Sufis on pilgrimage or in search of a master, moved between centers. A shaykh's reputation was built through these exchanges. It was a social achievement, resting on the willingness of many people to treat his lodge as a place to take a problem, to seek advice, or to fulfill a vow.

Political change made such networks more, not less, valuable. In the decades after the Ilkhanid collapse, as Chapter 51 explained, Azerbaijan passed through the hands of Jalayirids, Chobanids, and others, and later through those of Timurids and Turkmen dynasties. Rulers changed; the roads, the pastures, and the pilgrims stayed. A religious institution that could maintain relationships across changing boundaries had a resilience that governments lacked, and the Safavid lodge would prove able to do so for a century and a half. Rulers, for their part, had reason to court a prestigious lodge: its blessing conferred legitimacy, its followers could be recruited, and its property could be confirmed or threatened.

This section sets the stage without rehearsing the history of the Turkmen confederations, which Chapter 55 has told. What students should take from it is a picture of Ardabil as a modest city at an important junction, embedded in a landscape where merchants, pastoralists, villagers, and scholars all sustained religious institutions and where political boundaries were porous. That picture explains why a religious center there could attract followers beyond its immediate surroundings, and it prepares the question to which the next section turns: who was the shaykh whose name the order bears?

Section 56.2

Shaykh Safi al-Din Ardabili and the Zahediyya Tradition

Safi al-Din Ishaq Ardabili, from whom the Safaviyya took its name, is traditionally said to have been born around 1252 and to have died in 1334. The date of birth rests on later tradition and should be treated as approximate. According to the principal source for his life, Ibn Bazzaz's Safvat al-Safa, he came from a family of the Ardabil district, lost his father while still young, and devoted himself early to religious study and ascetic practice. The text portrays a young man in search of a spiritual master, traveling in pursuit of instruction in the course of the later thirteenth century and finding no guide who satisfied him until he heard of Zahed Gilani.

Taj al-Din Ibrahim, known as Zahed Gilani (1216–1301), was a Sufi master based in the Caspian province of Gilan, in the region of Lahijan. His circle, known later as the Zahediyya, attracted disciples from Gilan and beyond, and his reputation for austerity and spiritual authority drew Safi al-Din from Ardabil across the mountains. According to the hagiographic tradition, Safi al-Din became Zahed's disciple and remained with him for many years, during which he underwent the discipline of retreat, fasting, and obedience, and eventually married Zahed's daughter. The tie was one of both spiritual instruction and kinship, a combination that recurs through the history of the order. Safi al-Din was not the only claimant to Zahed's legacy: Zahed had a son and other disciples, and the Zahediyya continued in Gilan in its own right. What happened at Zahed's death in 1301 was a succession, not the foundation of a wholly new tradition.

Safi al-Din took over leadership of the circle in 1301 and returned to Ardabil, where he established himself and his followers. Over the next three decades he built the community that would bear his name. The name Safaviyya is a later formation, derived from Safi in the same way that a family or order is named for its founder, and the sources of his own day more often describe followers of the shaykh than a branded organization. Students should therefore avoid imagining a fully formed "Safavid order" in 1301. What existed was a community of disciples gathered around a respected master who had inherited a teaching and a following, and who gradually built an institution.

Historical Vocabulary
Tariqa and Khanqah

Definition: A tariqa (literally "path") is a Sufi way of spiritual discipline, handed down through a chain of masters; in later usage the word also means the community or order that follows that path. A khanqah is the building or complex in which a Sufi community lives, prays, teaches, and welcomes guests.

Historical context: In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, lodges such as the one at Ardabil were centers of hospitality and instruction, often supported by endowed property and by donations. The path and the lodge were distinct. A path could be carried by a traveling deputy to places where no lodge existed, and a lodge could host visitors belonging to several paths.

Why it matters: The Safaviyya's history is the history of both. The tariqa gave the movement its devotional and spiritual structure; the khanqah and the shrine gave it a physical home, property, and a base for hospitality and recruitment.

Potential misunderstanding: A Sufi order in this period was not a secret society or a political party. Most were open communities devoted to worship and instruction, and nothing in the term itself implies militancy.

The practices associated with the early order were those of a devotional community. Ibn Bazzaz describes a regimen of prayer, fasting, retreat, and the ritual remembrance of God, known as dhikr, in which disciples repeated sacred phrases under the direction of their master. Instruction was personal: the disciple, or murid, committed himself to the shaykh, or murshid, and was guided in conduct and inner discipline. The lodge offered food and shelter to visitors, and charitable giving was a central expectation. Ibn Bazzaz also portrays the shaykh as observing the religious law as understood in his region, and modern scholarship, drawing on the oldest textual evidence, generally reads him as a Sufi within the Sunni, largely Shafiʿi, milieu of northwestern Iran. Cambridge scholarship on the period identifies the earliest Safaviyya as a recognizable Sufi community with practices described in the Safvat al-Safa, not as a political or sectarian organization.

The section must therefore resist two tempting errors. The first is to describe Safi al-Din as the founder of a militant, explicitly Twelver Shiʿi movement. The evidence of the oldest layer of the sources does not support this, and the claims of Alid descent and Shiʿi identity that appear in sixteenth-century Safavid works belong to a later stage of the family's history, as Section 56.6 will show. The second is to treat the early order as a purely private devotional circle without social weight. Safi al-Din's lodge attracted disciples from different social ranks, received gifts from the wealthy and the poor, and enjoyed the regard of regional notables, which gave the family a standing that its successors inherited. The shaykh of 1301 was a religious teacher with followers, not a prince in waiting.

Section 56.3

Sadr al-Din Musa, Khwaja Ali, and Ibrahim: The Hereditary Sufi Leadership

Safi al-Din died in 1334 and was succeeded by his son Sadr al-Din Musa, who led the community until his death in 1391–1392, a period of more than five decades. The succession of a son to the leadership of a lodge was not unusual in the Sufi world, but it was not automatic either. A shaykh's disciples might have chosen a senior companion, and many orders did. At Ardabil the choice of the founder's son established a pattern in which spiritual authority came to be understood as something that could be inherited, a pattern that would define the family's history. It also set the leadership within a household, with its women, children, and relatives, and in the course of the following generations the lodge and the family became inseparable.

Sadr al-Din Musa's long leadership coincided with the political upheavals described in Chapters 51 and 52: the end of Ilkhanid rule, the rise and fall of the Jalayirids, and the first invasions of Timur. The order survived these disruptions. Its survival depended in part on the endowed property and donations that supported the lodge, in part on the personal reputation of the shaykh, and in part on its ability to deal with whichever ruler governed Ardabil. The tradition preserved in the hagiographic literature stresses his piety and the miracles attributed to him, and it describes the continued arrival of visitors and gifts. Students should read such reports as evidence of how the community wished its leader to be remembered while recognizing that they also reflect a real institutional continuity.

Khwaja Ali, Sadr al-Din's son, led the order from the 1390s until his death around 1427, according to the usual reckoning, and his tenure spanned the years of Timur's invasions. A well-known tradition holds that Timur visited Ardabil in the early years of the fifteenth century, was impressed by the shaykh, and responded to Khwaja Ali's request by releasing captives taken in Anatolia and confirming gifts to the lodge. The story appears in Safavid-era works, and its details, including the number and fate of the captives, are not securely established. What can be said is that the tradition reflects two plausible historical facts: that conquering rulers, from Mongols to Timurids, had reason to show respect to a revered shaykh whose followers they wished to conciliate, and that the family's later narrative treated this recognition as a mark of its standing. Students should neither dismiss the story as invention nor accept its details as documented.

Khwaja Ali's son Ibrahim, sometimes called Shaykh Shah, followed him and led the order until 1447. His tenure falls within the long reign of Shah Rukh and the period when the Timurids and Qara Qoyunlu contested Azerbaijan, as Chapter 55 described. The family's relations with these rulers were varied. Timurid and Turkmen governments are reported to have confirmed the lodge's property and to have offered it protection, and the Safavid shaykhs maintained cordial relations with the powerful. Here again the documentary record is thinner than the tradition, and students should distinguish documented patronage from later claims that exaggerate the reverence of famous conquerors.

Hereditary leadership gave the order important advantages. It provided continuity across generations and across regime changes. It concentrated the spiritual prestige of Safi al-Din in a family that could claim a blood relationship to him, which made the family itself an object of devotion. It allowed the accumulation of endowed property under family custodianship. It also embedded the order within a network of marriages with local notables and with the families of other Sufi masters, a network that extended the order's reach. These benefits were real, but hereditary succession carried tensions. A family has more than one son, and more than one branch. A shaykh's brothers, nephews, and cousins might believe they had as good a claim to lead as his chosen heir. The property and prestige of the shrine were attractive to outside rulers, who could support one claimant against another to gain influence. And the combination of spiritual and household authority could blur the question of whether leadership belonged to the most qualified disciple or to the most closely related kinsman.

When Ibrahim died in 1447, these tensions came to the surface. He left a son, Junayd, who was young, and a brother, Jaʿfar, who was an adult and had supporters. The question of who should lead the order was no longer a matter that the family could settle quietly, because an outside ruler had his own interest in the answer. Section 56.7 returns to this dispute. Before that, it is necessary to consider the institution whose control was at stake: the shrine at Ardabil and the resources that came with it.

Section 56.4

The Shrine of Shaykh Safi: Endowments, Pilgrimage, and Institutional Resources

Safi al-Din's death in 1334 transformed the lodge into a shrine. His tomb became a place of visitation, ziyara, to which believers came to pray, to seek the saint's intercession, and to fulfill vows, and the community that had gathered around the living shaykh now gathered around his grave as well. The shrine added a second kind of authority to the first. A living shaykh could teach and guide, but a tomb endured, and the family that guarded it held a permanent focus of devotion. Over the following century the lodge-and-tomb complex grew in size and importance, and its leaders were, in effect, the custodians of a saint.

The institution depended on resources. Gifts of land, villages, shops, and cash came from rulers, officials, merchants, and ordinary believers, and many were organized as endowments, or waqfs, that dedicated the income of property to the shrine and its charitable and religious functions. Endowments gave the institution a stable income and, in principle, legal protection, since the property was dedicated to God and could not easily be sold or confiscated. They also created a body of managers and servants: stewards, accountants, cooks, caretakers, reciters, and others who lived from the shrine's income or worked in its service. The hereditary custodian of the shrine, usually the leading male of the Safavid family, controlled this apparatus and the patronage that came with it.

The shrine was woven into the economy of Ardabil. Pilgrims needed lodging, food, and goods, and the bazaar served them. Artisans and merchants found a clientele in the visitors who came for the shrine's feasts and in the shrine's own demand for supplies. Endowed properties, whether farmland in the countryside or commercial premises in the town, tied cultivators, tenants, and shopkeepers to the institution. The shrine also performed functions we would call social: it fed the hungry, sheltered travelers, and served as a place of arbitration and asylum. In a region of political instability such an institution offered a degree of stability, and its custodians could draw on the gratitude and obligation that this created.

Control of the shrine and its resources contributed directly to the family's standing. A family that owned and managed endowed property, that sheltered pilgrims and distributed food, and that guarded a saint's tomb held a position of local power that did not depend on any ruler's favor. At the same time, the resources were an obvious target. Rulers who wished to influence the order could confirm or withdraw the shrine's privileges, appoint or dismiss stewards, and favor one family branch over another. This helps explain why the succession of 1447 attracted the attention of the Qara Qoyunlu ruler of Azerbaijan.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: Pilgrims, Servants, and the Shrine Economy

The shrine's story is usually told through shaykhs and rulers, but most of the people connected with it were not leaders. A pilgrim might be a villager from the Mughan or from the Caspian coast who made the journey in fulfillment of a vow, hoping for a cure or for the resolution of a family trouble, and who brought a small gift of produce, livestock, or cash. A merchant on the road between Tabriz and the Caucasus might stop to pray and leave a donation, partly from piety and partly from the practical reasoning that a respected shrine was a safe place to rest and to hear news. The lodge fed such visitors, and the cost of feeding them was borne by the endowment.

Behind the lodge worked a staff of cooks, bakers, water-carriers, doorkeepers, reciters, stewards, and accountants, and the endowed fields and shops supported tenants and craftsmen whose lives were tied to the shrine's income. Women appear in the sources mainly as members of the founding family, whose marriages created alliances, and as pious donors; the women who cooked, cleaned, and cared for visitors, and the women who made their own pilgrimages, are almost invisible. The surviving texts, written by and for the leading men of the order, mention them rarely, and historians must infer their presence from the nature of the institution.

The shrine thus offered ordinary people a combination of religious comfort, material support, and social connection that no government provided in the same way. Allegiance to the family at Ardabil was, for many, a matter of gratitude and habit before it was a matter of politics. That layer of everyday devotion is easy to overlook, but it is part of the reason the order could later draw followers to its cause.

Section 56.5

Disciples, Khalifas, and the Expansion of Safavid Religious Networks

The governing question of this section is how religious allegiance could connect people who did not live under a single government. The Safaviyya's following was not confined to Ardabil or to any one state. By the fifteenth century the order had devotees in Azerbaijan, Gilan, eastern Anatolia, Syria, and neighboring regions, living under Jalayirid, Timurid, Qara Qoyunlu, Aq Qoyunlu, Ottoman, and Mamluk authority, and in places where no ruler paid any attention to them at all. Their common tie was a relationship, in different degrees of closeness, with the shaykh at Ardabil.

Historical Vocabulary
Murshid, Murid, and Khalifa

Definition: A murshid is a spiritual guide, a murid is a disciple who has committed himself or herself to that guide, and a khalifa (literally "deputy" or "successor") is a representative authorized by a shaykh to teach, admit disciples, and act on his behalf, often in a distant place.

Historical context: In Sufi orders the bond between guide and disciple was understood as one of obedience and trust. Deputies extended the guide's reach: a khalifa might preside over a local lodge, instruct new disciples, collect gifts and carry them to the center, and report back. The same word, khalifa, was used in other contexts for the successors of the Prophet, and students should read it according to its setting.

Why it matters: The deputy system allowed a family at one lodge to maintain communities in many regions. It is also the institutional channel through which, later, military mobilization could be organized, since the same men who gathered disciples and gifts could gather armed supporters.

Potential misunderstanding: In the early period khalifas were religious deputies, not military officers. The later Safavid use of the term for commanders in the Qizilbash coalition reflects a development, not the original function.

The relationship between master and disciple was personal and could be maintained at a distance. A man might travel to Ardabil, be received at the lodge, take an oath of allegiance to the shaykh, and return home with instructions for devotional practice and perhaps a token of the master's blessing. Thereafter he might correspond with the lodge, send donations, and receive visits from the shaykh's deputies. A khalifa in a district such as Anatolia or Gilan served as a local point of contact, a teacher to new disciples, and a collector of offerings. Through such people the shaykh's authority traveled.

Travel, hospitality, and personal recommendation were the mechanisms of expansion. Pilgrims returned from Ardabil with stories of the lodge's generosity and the shaykh's holiness and spread its reputation among their relatives and neighbors. Merchants who found hospitality at the lodge told others. Disciples who moved for work or in the course of military service carried their devotion with them and founded small circles of believers in new places. Gifts to the lodge from distant followers created a continuing connection. Because each link was personal, the network expanded by the accumulation of many small relationships and not by the plan of a central bureaucracy.

The followers occupied varied social positions. Urban supporters, including merchants, artisans, and members of the scholarly and official classes, valued the shrine as a center of piety and charity and as a social resource. Villagers and rural communities offered devotion and material support. Pastoral Turkmen groups, whose seasonal movements took them across the frontiers of states, found in the shaykh a figure of authority who was not tied to any single ruler. Soldiers and mercenaries, men whose lives were spent in the service of one commander after another, found in him a point of loyalty. Women also took part in devotional life, though the sources say little. These groups did not share the same relationship to the order: an urban notable who sent an annual gift stood in a different position from a pastoral household that regarded the shaykh as its spiritual protector.

Students should be wary of imagining the early Safaviyya as a tightly organized body. There is no evidence of a uniform bureaucracy, a registry of members, or a clandestine political organization in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. The network was loose, varied by region, and dependent on the energy and prestige of particular leaders and deputies. Its strength was not discipline but breadth, and its capacity to be mobilized for political purposes was a later development that required changes in leadership and in the character of the following.

Section 56.6

The Safvat al-Safa: Hagiography, Genealogy, and the Construction of Sacred Authority

Much of what is known about Safi al-Din and the early order comes from a single work, the Safvat al-Safa ("The Purity of Purity"), compiled by a follower named Tavakkul b. Ismaʿil, known as Ibn Bazzaz, in the fourteenth century, around 1358 by the usual reckoning. The date is approximate. The author lived within the community and wrote in the generation after the saint's death, when Sadr al-Din Musa was leading the order. The work records the saint's life, sayings, and miracles and the devotion of his followers, and it is the primary source for the early Safaviyya. It is also a work of a particular genre that shapes what it can tell us.

Hagiography is writing that celebrates the virtues, miraculous deeds, instruction, and authority of a revered religious figure. It is not invented from nothing: authors drew on memories, anecdotes, and the testimony of disciples. But it is shaped by devotional purpose. Its aim is to demonstrate holiness, to edify readers, and to strengthen the community's attachment to its master and his successors. Miracles are narrated as facts. Rivals appear as foils. The saint's character is idealized. A historian reading such a work must therefore ask what the text can establish despite its purpose, for instance the names of disciples, the pattern of visits, the kinds of practices described, and the regard in which the saint was held, and what it cannot establish, for instance the precise sequence or literal accuracy of the miracles.

The text's later history complicates its use. The Safvat al-Safa circulated in manuscripts of different dates, and scholars who have compared them have found that the work was revised under Safavid rule in ways that bear on genealogy and religious identity. Whereas the earliest textual tradition, as modern scholars read it, presents Safi al-Din as a pious Sufi without a developed claim to descent from the Prophet's family, later versions and Safavid-era genealogies traced his line back through a chain of ancestors to the seventh imam of the Twelvers, Musa al-Kazim, and thus to ʿAli and Fatima. The shift was not trivial. It converted a respected shaykh into a sayyid, a descendant of the Prophet, and provided the dynasty with a lineage that fit its Twelver Shiʿi profile after 1501. Scholars have long argued that the Alid genealogy was elaborated or consolidated at some point after the fourteenth century, and they have debated when, but the problem of the manuscripts is a central reason that sixteenth-century Safavid accounts cannot be read as transparent descriptions of the fourteenth century.

Section 56.7

Junayd's Contested Succession and Exile from Ardabil, 1447–1448

When Shaykh Ibrahim died in 1447, the leadership of the Safavid order passed into dispute. Two claimants stood forward: Ibrahim's son Junayd, who was young, and Ibrahim's brother Jaʿfar, who was older. Each could claim a connection to the family and its resources. A son could claim the inheritance that a father had held. A brother could claim that his greater maturity and his place in the family qualified him to guard the institution while a young heir grew up, or that his experience and standing suited him better. The sources that survive, most of them later, preserve the traces of this dispute only partially.

The dispute did not remain within the family because an outside ruler took an interest. Jahan Shah, the Qara Qoyunlu ruler of Azerbaijan whose reign Chapter 55 described, supported Jaʿfar. Scholarship such as Kathryn Babayan's study of Junayd in the Encyclopaedia Iranica documents this support and notes that later Safavid historiography suppressed the dispute. Why would the ruler of Azerbaijan care about the leadership of a lodge? There were several reasons. The shrine at Ardabil lay within his territory, near routes toward Shirvan and the Caucasus, and its resources and reputation made it a significant local power. Its disciples, scattered across the region, constituted a following whose loyalties a ruler would prefer to see attached to a friendly shaykh. A leader dependent on the ruler's goodwill was more useful than one who might ally with the ruler's rivals. Backing Jaʿfar was therefore an exercise in managing a religious institution that could help or hinder governance.

The dispute also shows the connection among control of the shrine, hereditary authority, and the political power of family branches. Possession of Ardabil gave a claimant the shrine's income, its stewards and servants, its prestige, and its devotees' loyalty. Hereditary authority gave him a claim that devotees could accept as legitimate. The patronage of a ruler gave him force to make his claim effective. Junayd, as a young son of the previous leader, had hereditary legitimacy and the sympathy of part of the following but lacked the backing of the ruling power. Jaʿfar, with Jahan Shah's support, could hold the lodge. The result was that Junayd left Ardabil, in 1447 or 1448, and began years of exile.

The exile, which might have ended his career, instead became the occasion of his transformation of the order.

Section 56.8

Junayd in Anatolia and Syria: Recruitment, Religious Messaging, and Political Ambition

Driven from Ardabil, Junayd traveled. The sources describe years of wandering in the Turkmen-inhabited regions of Anatolia and Syria, including, in accounts that scholars treat with caution as to detail, stays in the lands of the Karamanids in southern Anatolia and visits to Syria, in the territory of the Mamluk Sultanate. He moved among communities that did not owe allegiance to the rulers of Azerbaijan and who might be receptive to a holy man whose family had been driven out. In these regions he found followers. His success there is one of the reasons scholars regard the years of his exile as a decisive stage in the order's history.

Religious networks mattered precisely in areas the authorities at Ardabil did not control. The Turkmen groups of Anatolia and Syria lived in a world of shifting political loyalties, frontier raiding, and mobile pastoralism. Many of them practiced a popular Islam in which reverence for ʿAli and the Prophet's family, commemoration of the martyrdom at Karbala, devotion to charismatic saints, and expectations of religious deliverance were prominent. These were not the doctrines of a formal Shiʿi school; they were widely shared devotional traditions that crossed the lines between Sunni and Shiʿi identity. A holy man who embodied them, and who came from a respected family with a famous shrine, could win allegiance. Junayd's ancestry, his claim to spiritual authority, and his personal presence made him an attractive figure.

He attracted followers who were capable of fighting. Turkmen horsemen who had no fixed employer, and men displaced by the wars and political changes of the region, could find in his entourage a cause and a community. The sources describe his growing company and suggest that its members were not only disciples in the narrow sense but warriors who would follow him on campaign. This is a decisive development. A Sufi master with a following of devotees gains influence; a Sufi master with a following of armed men gains the ability to act.

Junayd's claims also extended beyond conventional spiritual guidance. He is reported to have adopted the title sultan, a designation of sovereign authority, and to have presented himself as a leader whose role was not confined to the lodge. The title suggests ambitions to temporal power, or at least a conception of his authority as encompassing the political as well as the spiritual. Later sources, some hostile and some friendly, also report that his followers attributed extraordinary spiritual status to him, and some accounts speak in language that critics regarded as exaggerated or heretical. These reports come mostly from sources written after the Safavids had come to power or from hostile observers, and their interpretation is debated. Students should therefore not assume that every adherent held an identical doctrine or that Junayd had a clearly articulated program of Twelver Shiʿi state formation. The evidence points to a variety of beliefs among his followers, united by loyalty to a charismatic guide.

Why does this represent a major development in the order's history? Before Junayd, the Safaviyya had been a respected Sufi institution whose leaders maintained good relations with rulers. Under Junayd, the leader of the order became an itinerant recruiter, built a following among mobile warriors, adopted a title of sovereignty, and sought military and political goals. Scholars disagree about exactly when and how this transformation occurred and about how sharp the break was. Some stress that the order had already acquired political weight before Junayd and that his career intensified existing tendencies; others emphasize the novelty of his militancy and his Alid and messianic appeal. The disagreement is not merely semantic, and Section 56.12 and the first historical debate return to it. For now, the essential point is that Junayd's exile broadened the order's following, gave it a military dimension, and set in motion the alliances and campaigns discussed in Division III.

Dynastic Alliances, Religious Transformation, and the Rise of Haydar

Section 56.9

Junayd, Uzun Hasan, and the Political Importance of Dynastic Marriage

In the mid-1450s Junayd found a patron. Uzun Hasan, whose rise from the fortress of Amid to leadership of the Aq Qoyunlu Chapter 55 traced, was in these years consolidating his position in the upper Tigris region and confronting the power of Jahan Shah. Junayd came to his court, and the sources describe a relationship of hospitality and alliance. The Safavid shaykh, who had been expelled from Ardabil with the backing of the Qara Qoyunlu, was a natural associate for the enemy of the Qara Qoyunlu.

Uzun Hasan's interest in supporting a Safavid religious figure was practical. Jahan Shah's backing of Jaʿfar had made the Qara Qoyunlu ruler the patron of the branch in possession of Ardabil; a rival branch in exile, headed by a charismatic figure with a growing following, offered Uzun Hasan a means of weakening that position and of drawing on the prestige and manpower of the order. A Safavid shaykh in his entourage added religious legitimacy to his rule, brought him into contact with Turkmen groups across Anatolia, and provided potential military support. The alliance served Uzun Hasan's purposes in a contest with a more powerful neighbor.

The bond was sealed by marriage. Junayd married Khadija Begum, Uzun Hasan's sister, no earlier than 1456 according to the chronology reconstructed by Kathryn Babayan. In a world where political loyalty was regularly secured through kinship, such a marriage connected the Safavid family to an increasingly powerful Turkmen ruling house, and it created obligations in both directions. Khadija Begum's household became a channel through which the interests of the two families were negotiated, and any child of the marriage would be at once the heir of the Safavid shaykhs and a nephew of the Aq Qoyunlu ruler. Dynastic marriage conferred hospitality, protection, access to military networks, and a share in the reputation of an ascending house.

Section 56.10

Junayd's Military Ambitions and Death in Shirvan, 1460

Under the shelter of the Aq Qoyunlu alliance, Junayd turned increasingly to armed action. His following of devotees and Turkmen horsemen gave him the means to undertake expeditions, and the sources describe campaigns directed at non-Muslim or frontier communities in the Caucasus and at neighbors whose territories lay on his path. A well-known tradition of the period associates him with attacks on Christian communities of the Black Sea and Caucasus regions, a form of raiding framed as ghaza, warfare on behalf of the faith. Such expeditions offered booty, prestige, and a reason to assemble armed followers. They also exposed him to the hostility of the local rulers across whose lands he traveled.

Shirvan is the key to the geography of his last campaign. The region of Shirvan, in the eastern Caucasus along the western shore of the Caspian Sea, had long been ruled by a dynasty of Shirvanshahs, with their capital at Shamakhi and their influence extending to the coastal city of Baku. To the north, the Caspian shore narrows between mountains and sea in the region of Derbent, whose fortified pass guarded the route between the steppe and the Iranian world, and beyond it lay Tabasaran and the mountains of what is now southern Dagestan. A force traveling from Ardabil toward the northern Caucasus would pass through or alongside Shirvan, whose rulers had their own interests and their own relations with Aq Qoyunlu, Qara Qoyunlu, Timurid, and Ottoman powers. Their principality was not a mere passage way. It was a state with fortresses, taxes, and a Muslim population of its own.

In 1460 Junayd marched into Shirvan with a following of armed supporters, in an expedition whose purpose the sources describe in different ways: some stress the pursuit of holy war against the infidels of the Caucasus, others the pursuit of political advantage. The Shirvanshah, Khalilullah I, who ruled a Muslim principality and regarded an armed Sufi in his lands as a threat, confronted him. Junayd was killed in the fighting in March 1460. His death ended the career of a leader who had spent a dozen years building the movement and left the community without its head.

Later Safavid narratives memorialized his death as a martyrdom and connected it to the subsequent conflicts of the dynasty, so that the Shirvanshahs became in the family's memory the killers of its ancestors and the target of vengeance. Students should distinguish this retrospective framing from the contemporary evidence. At the time, Junayd's death was the defeat of an ambitious shaykh by a regional ruler defending his territory, and its meaning was not yet fixed. The narratives that portray it as part of a predetermined dynastic destiny, culminating in the later avenging of the ancestors by Ismail, read the future back into the past.

Junayd's military turn changed the relationship between the Safaviyya, its disciples, and neighboring authorities. By leading armed followers across political boundaries, he made the order a force that rulers had to take seriously, and he created a connection between devotion and warfare that his successors would exploit. He also cost the movement dearly. The campaign was a gamble that failed, and it left his infant son as heir. Yet the movement lost a leader without losing its network. The devotees in Anatolia, Syria, Azerbaijan, and Gilan, the Aq Qoyunlu alliance, and the family's claim to Ardabil survived, and these resources would support the next generation. The death of a leader is not the destruction of the structure that produced him.

Section 56.11

Shaykh Haydar: Inherited Leadership, Aq Qoyunlu Kinship, and the Reconstruction of the Order

Haydar, the son of Junayd and Khadija Begum, was born around 1459. A common error should be corrected here. Haydar was not born after his father's death; he was an infant when Junayd was killed in 1460, and he grew up in the household of his Aq Qoyunlu relatives. The inheritance he received was thus a dynastic claim: the position of heir to a line of Safavid shaykhs, the backing of an uncle who was becoming the most powerful ruler in Iran, and the memory of a father who had died fighting. It was not yet leadership of a functioning movement.

The chronology of his rise is more complicated than later accounts suggest. Competing branches of the family continued to matter. The line of Jaʿfar, which had enjoyed Qara Qoyunlu favor, did not simply vanish when Jahan Shah fell in 1467, and the control of Ardabil and its shrine after Junayd's death involved several parties whose roles are imperfectly documented. Recent research, as summarized by Kioumars Ghereghlou in the Encyclopaedia Iranica, dates Haydar's effective leadership of the pro-Aq Qoyunlu branch to approximately 1471–1472. Haydar inherited a claim after 1460, but he became a working leader only as he matured and as Uzun Hasan's conquest of Azerbaijan in 1467–1469 changed the balance of power in the region. Under Uzun Hasan's protection he took charge of the Ardabil community.

His marriage tied the Safavid household to the Aq Qoyunlu dynasty in a second generation. Haydar married Halima Begum, also known as Alamshah Begum, a daughter of Uzun Hasan. Their children included Sultan Ali, Ibrahim, and, in 1487, Ismail. The marriage made Haydar both Uzun Hasan's nephew and his son-in-law, and made the Safavid heirs grandsons of an Aq Qoyunlu ruler on their mother's side. Their mother's mother, according to the usual accounts, was Theodora Komnene, the Greek Christian princess of Trebizond whose marriage to Uzun Hasan Chapter 55 described, a part of the family's ancestry that later Safavid tradition did not emphasize. The kinship offered Haydar protection and resources, and it also meant that when relations between the Safavids and the Aq Qoyunlu court deteriorated, as they would in the 1480s, the conflict was a family quarrel with political consequences.

The reconstruction of the order under Haydar had religious, social, and military dimensions. Religiously, he renewed the devotion of the following and extended its symbolic repertoire, as the next section shows. Socially, he reestablished contact with the network of deputies and disciples, in Azerbaijan, Gilan, and Anatolia, that Junayd had cultivated and that had survived his death. And militarily, he turned the following into an organized fighting force. These changes did not occur in isolation. They depended on the resources of the shrine, on the Aq Qoyunlu alliance, and on the willingness of the disciples to follow a young leader whose father they remembered. The documentary record also shows Haydar's concern with the institutional side of the family's inheritance. A deed of 1483 concerning the superintendence of the mausoleum of Zahed Gilani, reported by Ghereghlou, indicates the family's continuing interest in the shrine of the master from whom Safi al-Din had taken over the circle, and it is one of the few contemporary documents that illuminate the Safavid household in this period.

Section 56.12

From Sufi Devotion to Alid and Messianic Religious Authority

The religious ideas associated with the Safaviyya changed over the fifteenth century, and describing the change accurately is among the most difficult tasks in the chapter. The early order, as its oldest source portrays it, was a Sufi community with respect for the Prophet's family that was normal in the Sunni and Sufi world of its region. By the time of Haydar and his successors, the order's followers were marked by a more pronounced devotion to ʿAli and the Prophet's family, by reverence for the Twelve Imams, and by beliefs concerning the extraordinary spiritual status of the Safavid guide. These developments need to be understood in their own right and not as a simple shift from one confession to another.

Devotion to ʿAli, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, and to the family of the Prophet was widespread across the Islamic world. Sunnis honored ʿAli as the fourth caliph and revered the Prophet's descendants; Sufi orders traced their chains of spiritual authority back to ʿAli; and in the Turkmen lands of Anatolia and Azerbaijan popular religion included veneration of ʿAli, commemoration of the martyrdom of his son Husayn at Karbala, and stories of saints and warriors. A shaykh from a family that came to be regarded as descended from the Prophet could draw on this reservoir of sentiment. The movement's followers could see in him an embodiment of the holiness of the Prophet's house.

Related ideas centered on the guide himself. Sufi thought had long held that a perfect master might be a channel of divine light and a figure of special spiritual rank, and expectations of religious renewal, including hopes for a deliverer who would restore justice, circulated widely in a period of political turmoil and in the aftermath of the Mongol and Timurid conquests. Among followers of the Safavid guides some came to attribute to him powers and status that critics regarded as extravagant. The red headgear of twelve segments associated with Haydar, discussed in Section 56.13, expressed a symbolic link to the Twelve Imams. These beliefs fostered intense loyalty and made it possible for devotees to risk their lives in the guide's cause.

It is important to distinguish these devotional beliefs from Twelver Shiʿi jurisprudence. Twelver Shiʿism, as a legal and theological tradition, had its own scholars, texts, and institutions, which were centered in places such as Hilla and Najaf in Iraq and in Shiʿi scholarly communities elsewhere, and it recognized a defined line of twelve imams and a body of law. The beliefs of fifteenth-century Safavid followers, by contrast, were diverse and not systematized. They combined Alid devotion, Sufi ideas of spiritual authority, popular expectations, and loyalty to a family. The later establishment of Twelver Shiʿism as the official religion, treated in Chapter 57, involved importing jurists and creating institutions that the early movement did not possess. To say that the Safaviyya was "Shiʿi" in 1480 is therefore either to use the word in a loose devotional sense or to project a later system backward.

Religious identity interacted with kinship, military loyalty, and political ambition. The family's claim to prophetic descent supported its dynastic prospects. The followers' belief in the guide's special status sustained their loyalty in battle. The Alid sentiment of the following gave the movement a distinctive emotional register that set it apart from other Turkmen and Sufi groups. Yet the interaction was not smooth, and the transformation was not a single conversion. Recent scholarship challenges both the assumption that the Safaviyya was always explicitly Shiʿi and the claim that it changed completely and abruptly under Junayd. What emerges is a gradual and uneven development in which different followers held different beliefs, and the movement's religious profile took shape through the interplay of leaders' choices, followers' expectations, and political circumstances.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: When and How Did the Safaviyya Become Shiʿi?

The question: At what point, and through what process, did the Safaviyya change from a Sufi order of the Sunni milieu of northwestern Iran into the vehicle of a movement identified with Shiʿism?

Interpretation A — An early Sunni Sufi order, gradually transformed: Some scholars emphasize that the oldest evidence shows Safi al-Din and his early successors as Sunni Sufis, in the Shafiʿi milieu of the region, and that Alid devotion and Shiʿi identity developed gradually over later generations, accelerating in the fifteenth century as the order's following shifted toward Turkmen groups of Anatolia and as leaders sought new bases of authority.
Interpretation B — A decisive change under Junayd: Others locate a sharper shift in Junayd's career, when the leader built his following among Turkmen with Alid and messianic expectations, adopted the title sultan, and fashioned a militant movement. On this reading, Junayd's exile created a new kind of order.
Interpretation C — Standardization under the monarchy: A further view stresses that what we call Safavid Shiʿism was the work of the dynasty after 1501, which imposed Twelver Shiʿism and rewrote the family's past, including through revisions of the Safvat al-Safa and the construction of an Alid genealogy. On this view, the fifteenth-century movement should not be called Shiʿi in the later sense at all.

Evidence: The debate rests on the manuscript traditions of the Safvat al-Safa and their revisions; on Aq Qoyunlu, Ottoman, and other contemporary or near-contemporary descriptions of the followers; on the symbols and practices associated with Haydar; and on Safavid-era chronicles that reconstruct the family's past. Scholars such as Michel Mazzaoui, Kathryn Babayan, and Ayşe Baltacıoğlu-Brammer have each contributed to the study of the order's religious transformation, with differing emphases.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology, since scholars date the shift differently; terminology, since "Shiʿi" can mean devotional attachment to ʿAli or adherence to Twelver law; and the reliability of sources, since the later texts were shaped by the dynasty's interests. These are not mutually exclusive: a gradual development, a significant acceleration under Junayd, and later standardization may all be partly true.

Why it matters: The answer determines how the rise of the dynasty is understood. If the order was Shiʿi from the beginning, the Safavid state appears as the fulfillment of an inherited program. If the religious identity was fluid and later standardized, the state's Twelver Shiʿism appears as a decision made after conquest, with consequences examined in Chapter 57.

Student task: Using Sections 56.2, 56.6, 56.8, and this section, list the evidence for each interpretation. Then explain why devotion to ʿAli, in itself, does not establish adherence to Twelver Shiʿi law, and consider whether the three interpretations could all be partially correct.

Section 56.13

The Red Headgear: Safavid Symbols and the Emergence of the Qizilbash

The followers of the Safavid shaykhs came to be identified by what they wore on their heads. Specialist research credits Haydar with introducing a distinctive red cap, of twelve segments or gores, usually linked to the Twelve Imams, and by this headgear his followers acquired the name Qizilbash, Turkic for "Red Heads." The cap was commonly called the taj-i Haydari, the "crown of Haydar." Later sources differ about its exact shape, its materials, the reasons for its adoption, and the date of its introduction, and students should treat the detailed stories that explain it, including those that trace it to a vision or instruction, as traditions that require scrutiny. The broad association of the red headgear with Haydar's leadership, and with the period of the 1470s and 1480s, is generally accepted.

Historical Vocabulary
Qizilbash

Definition: A Turkic term meaning "Red Head," applied to the followers of the Safavid shaykhs who wore the red twelve-gored cap, and later to the tribal military elites of the Safavid state.

Historical context: The name began as a description of a visible sign of allegiance, adopted by followers and used by observers, sometimes hostilely. The groups it covered, Turkmen and others, had their own tribal names, chiefs, and histories, and the label did not erase them. Its meaning changed as the Safavid movement changed, and the Qizilbash of the 1480s were not identical to the Qizilbash of the sixteenth-century state.

Why it matters: The word describes a coalition formed around a religious guide. Understanding it that way helps explain how tribal groups with different interests could act together.

Potential misunderstanding: The Qizilbash were not a single tribe, a uniform ethnic population, or a modern disciplined army. They were a changing coalition whose members kept tribal affiliations, regional interests, and competing loyalties.

The meaning of a shared symbol in a movement of this kind is considerable. Clothing is a visible sign of belonging. To put on the cap was to declare allegiance to Haydar, to join a community of those who had done the same, and to mark oneself, for friend and enemy alike, as a member of that community. For a young Turkmen horseman whose normal loyalties were to his own tribe and chief, the cap offered an additional identity that cut across tribal lines. Men from different groups who might otherwise have regarded one another with suspicion could recognize one another by it, and the symbol reinforced solidarity among groups with different tribal backgrounds. The reference to the Twelve Imams, whatever the exact devotional content understood by each wearer, linked the cap to the reverence for the Prophet's family that animated the following.

A symbol that inspires devotion in some provokes hostility in others. For devotees, the red cap was an emblem of loyalty and sacred purpose. For opponents, it marked an armed and unwelcome presence. Rulers who regarded the Safavid following as a threat saw in it a visible sign of an organized force, and observers hostile to the movement used the name Qizilbash as a label for what they considered religious extremists. The same cloth could thus signify holiness or menace according to the viewer, and that duality was part of its power.

It is important not to read the later institutions of the Safavid monarchy back into this early symbolism. The ceremonies, titles, and court structures of the sixteenth-century state, treated in later chapters, belong to a different stage. What Haydar developed was a symbol of a devotional and military community led by a spiritual guide, and its use by a ruling dynasty came later.

Section 56.14

Qizilbash Tribal Networks, Khalifas, and Military Organization

The central question of this section is how a religious following could assemble a formidable military coalition without possessing an established state. The answer lies in the relationships among the Safavid spiritual leaders, their regional deputies, tribal chiefs, and armed followers, and in the way loyalty to a guide could connect groups that otherwise had different chiefs and interests.

The communities associated with early Qizilbash mobilization included the Rumlu, Shamlu, Ustajlu, Tekelu, and Dhuʾl-Qadr, and the lists in later sources often add others, among them the Afshar and Qajar. The names are those of Turkmen tribal groups, several with their origins in Anatolia (the name Rumlu refers to Rum, the Anatolian lands), Syria, and the frontier regions around them, and some with older histories in the confederations Chapter 55 described. The composition of such groups was never fixed. Lineages joined, split, and merged; chiefs changed sides; and the label of a tribe might cover a core of kinsmen and a periphery of attached followers. Later chroniclers who listed the Qizilbash tribes presented a tidier picture than the reality of the 1480s, and students should read the lists as a convention of the sixteenth-century sources and not as a census.

Recruitment in eastern and central Anatolia, Syria, Azerbaijan, and the neighboring frontier regions proceeded through the network that Junayd had cultivated and that Haydar rebuilt. Deputies, the khalifas of the order, visited communities, administered the guide's blessing, collected gifts, and kept alive the connection with Ardabil. When the guide called his followers to gather, the same deputies could summon them. The step from religious deputy to military organizer is a short one in a society where the same men who led a community in prayer often led it in the field. The term khalifa, which in the earlier period referred to a deputy for religious functions, acquired a military coloring, and in the sixteenth century the Safavid state used it for officials of the order and of the Qizilbash. Students should distinguish the early deputy from the later state office.

Loyalty to a murshid could connect groups that otherwise possessed different chiefs and political interests. A Turkmen chief had obligations to his kinsmen and followers; his horsemen might also have been Haydar's devotees. The guide's authority was of a different kind from the chief's, since it rested on spiritual allegiance and not on kinship or the distribution of booty. When the two coincided, the combined force was powerful. When they conflicted, tribal interests could pull the following apart. The Qizilbash coalition was therefore never simply a religious army: it was a negotiated relationship among a guide, his deputies, and the chiefs of groups who had their own aims, and its cohesion depended on continued success and on the guide's prestige.

Military service in this context meant principally mounted warfare. The Turkmen followers were horsemen, trained archers and lancers raised in a pastoral economy where riding and herding were daily activities. They fought in contingents under their own chiefs, and campaigns required the coordination of these contingents and the provision of supplies, mounts, and weapons. Kinship alliances reinforced the structure: marriages among the families of chiefs and between chiefs and the Safavid family linked the groups. Communication across the regional networks relied on deputies, messengers, and the movement of followers themselves, and it was slower and less reliable than a modern reader might assume. The Qizilbash were not a fully centralized modern army with a permanent staff and standard regulations. They were a flexible and, at times, unstable coalition that could be assembled for a campaign and could dissolve afterward.

Yet the coalition had distinctive strengths. It offered Turkmen horsemen, who might otherwise have served unpredictable masters, a cause and a leader who was not a rival chief. It combined devotional fervor with mounted skill. And it drew on the resources of an institution, the shrine and its endowments, that no tribal confederation possessed. These strengths explain how the Safavid following could become a force that rulers feared, and they set the stage for the campaigns in the Caucasus.

Section 56.15

Haydar's Caucasian Campaigns: Shirvan, Dagestan, and the Politics of Raiding

During the 1470s and 1480s Haydar led expeditions into the Caucasus. Kioumars Ghereghlou's study in the Encyclopaedia Iranica provides a detailed account of his military and organizational activities, and the following reconstruction rests on it and on the narrative sources it uses, while noting that chronology and detail vary among them. The expeditions were directed toward the lands north of the Safavid base at Ardabil: Shirvan, the Caspian coastal routes to Derbent, the region of Tabasaran, and the mountainous country of what is now southern Dagestan, and in some accounts further toward Circassia.

The geography shaped the campaigns. From Ardabil, a force could move north across the Mughan steppe toward the Kura and Aras river lowlands, then along the Caspian shore or through the foothills into Shirvan, with its capital at Shamakhi. North of Shirvan, the coastal strip narrowed toward Derbent, a fortified city whose walls once closed the route between the mountains and the sea. Beyond lay Tabasaran and the highlands of Dagestan, populated by mountain communities whose settlements, languages, and religious affiliations varied. Astara, on the Caspian coast to the southeast of Ardabil, was a route toward Gilan. These regions were not empty frontiers. They contained fortified towns, tax-paying villages, mountain communities, and rulers who had alliances and rivalries of their own.

Expeditions of this kind required practical preparation. A force of horsemen needed weapons, mounts, fodder, and food, and it needed to cross rivers and mountain passes. It required local knowledge, guides, and, if it was to avoid the hostility of the regions it traversed, alliances or at least acquiescence. The sources report that Haydar took a personal interest in the production of weapons and the organization of military resources, and Ghereghlou presents evidence for this activity. An order that had resources, a network of followers, and a leader who attended to logistics was able to sustain campaigns that a loose band of raiders could not.

The interests of the regional authorities threatened by Haydar's activities differed. The Shirvanshahs governed a long-established Muslim principality with its own capital, fortresses, and revenues, and an armed force passing through or raiding its territory challenged its sovereignty and its security. The mountain communities of Dagestan and the Caucasus, some Muslim and some not, faced seizure of property and the capture of people. The Aq Qoyunlu court, whose ruler was Haydar's uncle and father-in-law, had to decide how far to tolerate the activities of a kinsman whose power was growing. Some accounts suggest that the early expeditions enjoyed the acquiescence of the Aq Qoyunlu, which regarded the raids as directed against its enemies and as a means of channeling the energies of armed devotees away from its own lands, but the relationship was unstable.

These campaigns should not be romanticized. They were raids and wars, framed by the language of holy warfare, and they inflicted violence on people in the lands they crossed. Villagers lost grain, livestock, and goods; some were killed or carried off as captives; fortified towns were besieged and their hinterlands devastated. For the Safavid followers, the campaigns offered booty, honor, and the fulfillment of a religious duty as they understood it. For those attacked, they were incursions by armed bands from outside, whatever the religious justification. The perspectives differed sharply, and both must be part of the historical account. Neither a hagiographic celebration of holy war nor a purely hostile condemnation is adequate. Historians should explain why the campaigns happened, how the participants understood them, and what they meant for the people who suffered them.

Section 56.16

The Death of Haydar in 1488 and the Crisis of Safavid Leadership

Haydar's final campaign brought him into confrontation with the Shirvanshah Farrukh Yasar and the Aq Qoyunlu ruler Sultan Yaʿqub, and it ended in his death. The immediate cause was the continued escalation of his activities against Shirvan, whose ruler, with Yaʿqub's support, resolved to stop him. Yaʿqub, whose reign Chapter 55 described as relatively stable, had inherited a realm in which a kinsman commanded an independent armed following, and Haydar's increasing power gave the Aq Qoyunlu reason to see the Safavid order as a threat despite the earlier marriage alliances.

In July 1488, at Tabasaran near Derbent, Haydar's force met the combined forces of Shirvan and an Aq Qoyunlu contingent sent to support the Shirvanshah. Narrative sources, mostly later and shaped by different loyalties, describe a battle in which the Safavid army was defeated and Haydar was killed. The numbers of the contending forces and the details of the fighting vary among the accounts and should be treated with caution. What the sources establish is the outcome: Haydar died in battle, his following was scattered, and the Aq Qoyunlu state, which had been his kinsmen's government, was one of the parties that brought it about.

Why would a government regard the order as a threat despite its earlier kinship alliances? Marriage ties created obligations but did not remove rivalry. A shaykh with thousands of armed followers, a network of deputies reaching into the heart of Aq Qoyunlu territory, and a shrine from which to organize them possessed power that any ruler would find uncomfortable, and it was not only the Shirvanshah who was threatened by it. The movement's religious language, with its suggestion of a guide of extraordinary status, also raised the possibility that it might challenge the legitimacy of the ruler himself. Dynastic marriage had made the Safavid and Aq Qoyunlu houses kin. It had not made their interests identical.

The consequences for the Safaviyya were serious. The movement lost a leader who had rebuilt it and organized its military following. Its army had been defeated, and the hereditary leadership passed to young sons who were in danger. The unresolved succession facing Haydar's surviving family would dominate the following years. Yet the death of a charismatic leader is not the destruction of the organization he headed. The network of deputies, the shrine, the devotional loyalty of the followers, and the kinship ties of the family continued to exist. What the movement needed was a leader around whom they could be reassembled, and the family's survivors, whom Yaʿqub's government took into custody, were the only available candidates. The next division follows their fate.

Section 56.17

Sultan Ali, Imprisonment, and the Aq Qoyunlu Succession Crisis, 1488–1494

After Haydar's death in 1488 the leadership of the Safavid family fell to his eldest son, Sultan Ali, who was still a young man, with his younger brothers Ibrahim and Ismail, the latter an infant of about one year, in his care. The family stood in a precarious position. Its leader had died fighting a coalition that included forces of its own Aq Qoyunlu kin; its following was scattered; and the Aq Qoyunlu authorities, who saw the family as both a danger and a resource, intervened. The sources say that the brothers and their mother were taken into custody and confined in the fortress of Istakhr in Fars, far from Ardabil and from the networks that could have supported them. The chronology of the imprisonment is uncertain, with the sources placing the confinement in the early 1490s, around 1491 to 1493, and leaving some doubt about when the family was first detained.

The imprisonment shows how a regional government might seek to neutralize a movement by holding its hereditary leaders. A dynasty that depended on a family for its devotees' loyalty could be deprived of its head without being physically destroyed. It also shows the ambiguity of the Aq Qoyunlu position: the prisoners were the grandchildren of Uzun Hasan, and their mother was his daughter. They were treated as hostages and as potential political assets, and not as ordinary enemies.

The Aq Qoyunlu succession crisis, which Chapter 55 narrated, changed their situation. Yaʿqub died in December 1490, and the dynasty entered the decade of conflict among Baysunghur, Rustam, and other princes. In 1493, according to the usual accounts, Rustam, contending against Baysunghur, released the Safavid brothers and sought to exploit their supporters in his struggle for power. The calculation was straightforward. The Safavid following, which had been strong in Azerbaijan and Anatolia, could provide troops against his rival, and the young Safavid leaders were obliged to him for their freedom. Sultan Ali, with the Qizilbash who rallied to him, took part in the fighting in which Rustam prevailed over Baysunghur.

The relationship did not last. Rustam, having won, came to regard the Safavid leader and his armed followers as a danger, and relations deteriorated. In 1494, usually the date given, Sultan Ali was killed in the fighting that followed. The sources vary on the place and circumstances of his death, and students should treat the details with caution. His death left the family's survivors, among them his brothers, in flight again, and the Safavid movement without an adult leader.

This episode introduces the problem of succession within the Safavid family, and the sources are not in agreement. Some later chronicles present an uncomplicated transfer of authority to Ismail, as though the death of Sultan Ali immediately made the younger brother the head of the order. Other evidence suggests a more complex division of religious and political responsibilities, involving Ibrahim, the middle brother, and a period of uncertainty in which Ismail's primacy was not yet settled. Roger Savory, in his biography of Ismail for the Encyclopaedia Iranica, draws on manuscript evidence, including British Library Or. 3248, while also explaining conflicting traditions surrounding the succession. Students should encounter the disagreement rather than a simplified story of inevitable succession.

Section 56.18

Ismail's Childhood and Refuge in Gilan and Lahijan

Ismail was born in 1487, in the year before his father's death, so that he grew up knowing the Safavid family only as a persecuted house. After the death of Sultan Ali in 1494, the sources describe his removal to Gilan, the Caspian province from which Safi al-Din's teacher had come. The Gilan refuge is documented in the Encyclopaedia Iranica, although the precise chronology and details vary among the sources. The usual account places his stay in the years from about 1494 to 1499.

Protection was provided by the Karkiya ruler of Lahijan, Mirza Ali. Lahijan, the center of the eastern part of Gilan, was a principality ruled by a family of sayyids who enjoyed local legitimacy, and it lay beyond the reach of immediate Aq Qoyunlu control, shielded by the forests and mountains that separate the Caspian lowlands from the plateau. The link to the Safavid family had old roots: Lahijan lay in the district associated with Zahed Gilani, and the Safavid family's historical tie to the Zahediyya gave Gilan a place in its memory and its network. Lahijan thus offered a place where the family could survive.

Trusted followers and guardians played a part in his protection. The sources name men of the Qizilbash and the Safavid circle who accompanied and guarded the boy, among them a guardian and tutor called a lala, Husayn Beg Shamlu (also styled Husayn Beg Lala for that office), whose role is prominent in later accounts. They also report that communications between the Safavid leadership and communities of supporters were maintained, and that followers continued to look toward the boy as their future leader. The details of these arrangements, including the names and numbers of the companions, come largely from Safavid-era narratives, and students should treat them as traditions that require scrutiny while recognizing that some arrangement of guardianship and communication must have existed.

The religious environment of Gilan was distinctive. The Caspian districts had communities of Shiʿis, among them Zaydis, who follow a different line of imams from the Twelvers and traditionally hold that an imam must rise in arms against unjust rulers. The Karkiya rulers are associated with this tradition of Shiʿi sayyid leadership. It is important not to equate the Zaydi Shiʿism of Gilan with the Twelver institutions that the Safavids would establish after 1501. The Gilan refuge placed the young Ismail in a setting where devotion to the Prophet's family and Shiʿi sentiment were part of the local landscape, but whatever education he received there should not be assumed to have been a Twelver education, and the sources that describe his instruction are late and shaped by dynastic purposes.

Later stories about Ismail's concealment, his childhood education, and his miraculous preservation are historical traditions requiring scrutiny. They portray a child watched over by providence and destined for kingship. Such stories belong to the genre of dynastic legend and served to explain why a boy of such circumstances could become a king. The historian's task is to separate the plausible core, that a child of the Safavid house was sheltered in Lahijan by a local ruler and guarded by loyal followers, from the embellishment. The more solid conclusion is that Ismail's personal survival depended on a broader network of protection rather than on individual destiny: on a ruler who gave him shelter, on guardians who risked their lives, on followers who kept faith across years, and on the shrine and family connections that gave him a place worth protecting.

Section 56.19

Ismail's Emergence, the Qizilbash Gathering, and the First Campaigns, 1499–1501

Around 1499 Ismail left Gilan. By then he was about twelve years old, and the decision to bring him forth was the work of his senior followers and not of the boy himself. The sources describe his return to Ardabil, the family's ancestral seat, and the efforts of his supporters to assemble the dispersed Safavid following across Azerbaijan and Anatolia. The deputies and guardians who had maintained the network during the years of exile now put it to use. They sent word to the communities of devotees, summoned the Turkmen groups that had followed Haydar, and organized the gathering of those who were willing to commit themselves to a leader whom many had never seen.

The gathering at Erzincan around 1500 was the decisive step. Erzincan, in eastern Anatolia, lay in a frontier region that had been part of the Aq Qoyunlu sphere, where Chapter 55 located the battle of Otlukbeli, and it was close to the pastures and settlements of the Turkmen groups who made up the core of the following. Roger Savory's biography identifies the 1500 gathering at Erzincan and the subsequent Shirvan campaign as major steps preceding the establishment of Safavid rule. The participants included major Qizilbash groups, among them the Rumlu, Shamlu, Ustajlu, Tekelu, and Dhuʾl-Qadr, and later sources give a figure of about seven thousand, a number that should be treated as an approximation and not as a precise count. The organizational role of deputies, guardians, and tribal leaders was essential: it was they who maintained allegiance to the family and who could turn a scattered following into a campaign force.

Ismail then turned to Shirvan. The campaign against the Shirvanshah Farrukh Yasar had a clear symbolic and strategic logic. The Shirvanshahs, and Farrukh Yasar in particular, had been among those who confronted Haydar at Tabasaran in 1488, and the family's memory made Shirvan the natural first objective, a campaign of vengeance that also provided an opportunity to demonstrate the force of the assembled following. The sources describe the defeat of Farrukh Yasar and the occupation of the Shirvanshah's territory in 1500–1501, though they differ on the dates, on the names of the places where the fighting occurred, and on the fate of the ruler. The usual accounts report that Farrukh Yasar was defeated and killed. Students should read the campaign as an early demonstration of Ismail's military potential and of the capacity of the Qizilbash coalition. The subsequent history of Shirvan under Safavid influence belongs to Chapter 57.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Ismail Could Mobilize a Following in 1499–1500

Long-term causes

Two centuries of religious institution-building at Ardabil; hereditary spiritual authority; endowed property and pilgrimage; regional devotional networks reaching Anatolia, Syria, and Gilan; kinship ties to the Aq Qoyunlu house.

Short-term causes

The Qizilbash organization built by Haydar and sustained by khalifas after 1488; the memory of Junayd and Haydar as martyrs; the vacuum left by Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation after 1490.

Immediate trigger

The decision of senior followers, around 1499, to bring Ismail out of Gilan, and the call to the Turkmen groups to assemble in 1500.

Immediate effects

The gathering at Erzincan; the campaign against Shirvan and the defeat of Farrukh Yasar; the display of the coalition's military capability.

Long-term consequences

The challenge to the Aq Qoyunlu princes and the events of Chapter 57, including the establishment of Safavid kingship.

Historical complexity

Success was not predetermined. Three leaders had died or been destroyed; survival depended on protectors in Gilan, loyal guardians, and political conditions that could have been otherwise.

Why a single cause is insufficient: Neither Ismail's personal qualities, the Qizilbash's devotion, nor Aq Qoyunlu weakness alone accounts for the mobilization. The combination of inherited institutions, organized followers, and political opportunity made it possible.

Section 56.20

Historical Synthesis: How a Sufi Order Became a Movement for Sovereignty

The history of the Safaviyya from Safi al-Din's religious community to Ismail's mobilization is a history of accumulation. Over two centuries a lodge became a shrine, a shrine became the seat of a hereditary family, a family became the center of a transregional network, a network acquired a military arm, and a military arm found a leader and an opportunity. Each stage depended on the previous ones, but none was inevitable. The central argument of this chapter is that the interaction of hereditary spiritual leadership, control of the Ardabil shrine, devotional networks, regional recruitment, dynastic marriages, religious symbolism, and military organization made possible a form of political mobilization that few religious orders could have achieved.

The role of Junayd and Haydar in this process should be assessed with care. They altered the movement, giving it a following of armed men, a leader's claim to temporal authority, and a place in the politics of the Turkmen frontier. But it would be a mistake to assume that every earlier Safavid leader shared their political ambitions. Sadr al-Din Musa, Khwaja Ali, and Ibrahim were heads of a Sufi institution that dealt with rulers as a respected religious power, not as a candidate for rule. The transformation was the work of particular leaders in particular circumstances, and it was contested within the family, as the dispute between Junayd and Jaʿfar shows.

The contribution of the Qizilbash was distinctive, but it was not the whole story. They provided horsemen, devotion, and a willingness to fight, and they connected tribal coalitions to a hereditary spiritual leader. Yet the movement also depended on urban supporters who gave to the shrine, on religious deputies who maintained the network, on women of the family whose marriages created alliances and whose households sustained the children, and on regional protectors such as the Karkiya ruler of Lahijan. Historians who focus only on the cavalry underestimate the structure that made the cavalry's mobilization possible.

Students must also distinguish what the sources establish from the explanations advanced by later Safavid chroniclers. The chroniclers wrote after the dynasty had succeeded and they tended to present its history as a coherent story of destiny: an Alid genealogy, an orderly succession, martyrdom in battle, a miraculously preserved heir. The evidence suggests a more contingent story, in which succession was disputed, the religious character of the order changed unevenly, the alliance with the Aq Qoyunlu turned into hostility, and the family survived by a combination of protection, loyalty, and chance.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Religious Movement, Tribal Coalition, or Emerging State?

The question: What kind of organization was the Safaviyya on the eve of Ismail's mobilization: a religious movement, a coalition of Turkmen tribes, or the embryo of a state?

Interpretation A — A religious movement: Some accounts emphasize inherited spiritual charisma, devotional allegiance to the guide, the shrine, and the religious symbolism of the red cap. On this view the following obeyed Ismail because they regarded his family as holy.
Interpretation B — A tribal coalition: Other accounts stress military recruitment, the interests of Turkmen chiefs, dynastic marriage, the ambitions of particular leaders, and the political collapse of neighboring states. On this view the Qizilbash were Turkmen warriors who found in a Safavid guide an effective banner.
Interpretation C — An emerging state: A third view notes that the Safaviyya had a shrine with property, a hereditary dynasty, deputies who carried out functions of administration, and a claim to sovereignty, and so already possessed some of the features of a state in embryo.

Evidence: The question draws on the shrine's endowments, the organization of the khalifas, descriptions of the Qizilbash and their campaigns, the marriage alliances, and the chronicles of the Safavid and Aq Qoyunlu courts.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ about whether the Safaviyya's transformation is best understood as a change in theology, in social organization, in political strategy, or in some combination. They also differ about how to weigh evidence from sources that were written after the dynasty's success.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the dynasty's rise is explained. The evidence does not support the idea that the Safaviyya was simply a religious movement that abandoned religion upon acquiring an army. Religious devotion, tribal interest, and political calculation operated together, and the movement's strength lay in their combination.

The chapter's conclusion is that the establishment of Safavid kingship was possible because Ismail inherited an unusually durable religious and organizational structure, but that success was neither guaranteed nor predetermined. The structure had been built over two centuries, tested by defeats, and sustained by people whose names are mostly lost. Its leaders had changed it in ways that gave it military force and political ambition. Yet its leaders had also died in battle, its heirs had been imprisoned, and its survivor had been hidden in a mountain province. The political environment, shaped by the Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation that Chapter 55 described, offered a moment in which such a movement could succeed, and it was not certain that the moment would come.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: The Safaviyya from Safi al-Din to Ismail

What changed?

  • The following: from disciples of a regional shaykh to a transregional network including Turkmen horsemen
  • The leadership's aims: from spiritual guidance and hospitality to a claim to temporal authority
  • The religious language: from a Sufi community in the Sunni milieu of Azerbaijan toward Alid devotion and expectations of a guide of extraordinary status
  • The means: from endowments and pilgrimage to armed followers, symbolic dress, and campaigns

What remained continuous?

  • The shrine at Ardabil, its endowments, and the hereditary custodianship of the family
  • The bond of master and disciple and the use of deputies to maintain it at a distance
  • The importance of kinship and marriage in securing alliances
  • The ability to maintain relationships across changing political boundaries

When did change occur, and what was its character? Change was gradual in the fourteenth century and accelerated from 1447, uneven among regions and followers, contested within the family, and punctuated by defeats.

Who experienced change most directly? The leaders and their armed followers experienced the transformation most directly. Urban supporters, shrine servants, and many local pilgrims experienced greater continuity, since the shrine's daily functions persisted.

Why the pattern matters: Separating institutional continuity from strategic change avoids two errors: treating the Safavids as political from the start, and treating their rise as a sudden break with a purely religious past.

The final paragraphs of this chapter lead to Chapter 57. The movement's ambitions were about to confront the demands of actually ruling territories and diverse populations. A coalition that had been held together by devotion, shared symbols, and the promise of booty would have to be turned into a government with administrators, courts, revenue, and a religious policy. The cities of Azerbaijan, the Persianate bureaucrats who had served earlier regimes, the Sunni and Shiʿi scholars of the region, and the non-Qizilbash populations of the plateau would all have a say in what became of the Safavid project. Those developments, beginning with the battle of Sharur and the entry into Tabriz in 1501, are the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how a Sufi community centered on Ardabil developed over two centuries into a hereditary religious movement with extensive regional networks, acquired military and political ambitions under Junayd and Haydar, and provided the organization and following through which the young Ismail could challenge established rulers. Its central argument is that Safavid state formation was preceded by approximately two centuries of religious institution-building, inherited spiritual authority, local patronage, regional recruitment, kinship alliances, and increasingly militant political organization, and that the outcome was not inevitable.

The foundations were religious and institutional. Ardabil, on the northwestern plateau at a junction of routes toward Tabriz, Gilan, the Caucasus, and Anatolia, was a modest city whose position suited a center of devotion in the post-Mongol world. Safi al-Din Ardabili (c. 1252–1334) inherited the circle of his master Zahed Gilani in 1301 and built a community of disciples with a lodge, practices of prayer, fasting, and remembrance of God, and a reputation for piety and charity. The oldest evidence, in Ibn Bazzaz's Safvat al-Safa, presents him as a Sufi within the Sunni milieu of his region, not as the founder of a militant Twelver Shiʿi movement.

Hereditary leadership under Sadr al-Din Musa, Khwaja Ali, and Ibrahim gave the order continuity across the political upheavals of the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. The shrine of Safi al-Din, supported by endowments, pilgrimage, and the labor of servants and tradespeople, became an institution with resources and local power. The advantages of hereditary succession, namely continuity and concentrated prestige, were matched by tensions, since a family has more than one branch and an attractive institution invites outside interference. The surviving complex at Ardabil reflects centuries of building, and the fully developed Safavid shrine must not be projected backward onto the fourteenth century.

The order's following extended beyond Ardabil through disciples and deputies who maintained personal ties across political boundaries. The Safvat al-Safa, a hagiography compiled around 1358, is the principal source for the early community, but it survives in versions that were revised under Safavid rule, in ways that affected the family's genealogy and religious profile. The claim of descent from the Prophet through ʿAli and Fatima became important to dynastic legitimacy, and students must distinguish evidence about the early community from claims reshaped after 1501.

The succession dispute of 1447–1448 between Junayd and his uncle Jaʿfar, in which the Qara Qoyunlu ruler Jahan Shah supported Jaʿfar, drove Junayd into exile and began the order's transformation. Junayd recruited among Turkmen followers in Anatolia and Syria, adopted the title sultan, drew on widely shared devotion to ʿAli and the Prophet's family, and attracted men capable of fighting. His alliance with Uzun Hasan, sealed by marriage to Khadija Begum, connected the Safavid family to a rising Turkmen ruling house without making the Safaviyya a department of Aq Qoyunlu government. His death in Shirvan in March 1460 ended a gamble that failed but left the network intact.

Haydar, born around 1459, was an infant when his father died. His effective leadership dates to about 1471–1472, and his marriage to Halima (Alamshah) Begum, a daughter of Uzun Hasan, renewed the dynastic link. He rebuilt the order, introduced the twelve-gored red headgear that gave the Qizilbash their name, and turned a devotional following into an armed coalition. The religious ideas of his followers, including Alid devotion, reverence for the Twelve Imams, and belief in the guide's extraordinary status, were diverse and should not be equated with Twelver Shiʿi jurisprudence or with a sudden conversion. Scholars disagree about when and how the Safaviyya became Shiʿi, and the question cannot be answered by projecting the sixteenth-century state's religion backward.

The Qizilbash were a changing coalition of Turkmen groups, including Rumlu, Shamlu, Ustajlu, Tekelu, and Dhuʾl-Qadr, linked by loyalty to a murshid and organized through deputies, not a single tribe or a modern army. Haydar's campaigns in Shirvan, Derbent, Tabasaran, and Dagestan involved practical preparation, including weapons production, and brought violence to the communities attacked, whose perspectives are part of the historical record. His death at Tabasaran in July 1488, at the hands of forces of Shirvan and the Aq Qoyunlu, showed how a regional government might regard the order as a threat despite kinship, and left the family in crisis.

Sultan Ali and his brothers were imprisoned at Istakhr, released by the Aq Qoyunlu prince Rustam in 1493, and then pursued, and Sultan Ali was killed around 1494. The succession within the family was not simple, and later chronicles that present an orderly transfer to Ismail conflict with evidence of a more complex division of responsibility involving Ibrahim. Ismail, born in 1487, survived in Gilan under the protection of the Karkiya ruler of Lahijan and a network of guardians and followers, and his survival depended on that network and not on destiny alone.

Around 1499 Ismail emerged, and in 1500 the Qizilbash gathered at Erzincan. The campaign against the Shirvanshah Farrukh Yasar demonstrated the coalition's capacity and was made possible by the fragmentation of the Aq Qoyunlu state. The movement's success was not guaranteed: Junayd and Haydar had been killed, the family had been imprisoned and persecuted, and the outcome depended on contingent conditions. The chapter ends at the threshold of sovereignty. Chapter 57 narrates the battle of Sharur, the entry into Tabriz, the proclamation of kingship, and the establishment of Twelver Shiʿism as the official religion, when a movement built on devotion confronted the demands of governing.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it replaces a picture of sudden dynastic emergence with a picture of long institutional growth. The Safavid dynasty did not begin in 1501. Behind it lay a shrine, a hereditary family, a devotional network that crossed political boundaries, and a military following built by two leaders who died in battle. Seeing that history clarifies why the dynasty could appear so quickly and so forcefully, and why it could not have been built by a single campaign.

The chapter also reveals a larger process in Iranian and Islamic history: the capacity of religious institutions to organize people when states could not. Hospitality, pilgrimage, endowments, and personal allegiance created ties that outlasted dynasties, and Chapter 1's account of the plateau's varied peoples and routes helps explain why such ties mattered. The Safaviyya's history also shows how those ties could be turned to political and military use, and how a movement with deep religious roots could acquire interests of its own.

Several continuities extended beyond 1501. The Qizilbash tribal groups became a central element of the Safavid state's military and political elite. The shrine at Ardabil remained a symbol of the dynasty and a focus of its patronage. The tension between a hereditary spiritual leader and tribal commanders with their own interests persisted, and so did the problem of reconciling devotional loyalty with the practical demands of government. The religious ideas of the movement, diverse and fluid in the fifteenth century, would be redefined by the monarchy in ways that shaped Iranian religious life for centuries.

Finally, the chapter prepares the way for the next. Chapter 55 described the political world in which the Safavid movement grew, and this chapter has described the movement and the coalition that Ismail inherited. Chapter 57 now turns to the conquest of power and the creation of a state, where the movement's devotion and organization faced the test of governing diverse populations and where decisions about religion, administration, and the role of the Qizilbash determined what kind of dynasty the Safavids would become. Readers who carry forward the lessons of this chapter about contingency, memory, and the uses of sacred authority will read that history with more care.
