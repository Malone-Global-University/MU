The Ilkhanate: Mongol Rule Becomes an Iranian State

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Ilkhanate did not become an Iranian state by ceasing to be Mongol. It became one because a conquest army, a Chinggisid dynasty, and a very old tradition of Persianate government were obliged to work together in the same territory, and each changed in the process.

In the early 1260s, three kinds of places in northwestern Iran gave a partial picture of the new order that Mongol conquest had brought. On the grasslands of Azerbaijan and the Mughan steppe, the royal encampments of Hülegü, a grandson of Chinggis Khan, moved with the seasons, surrounded by the tents, herds, and commanders of an army that had come from the Mongolian steppe. At Maragha, according to the sources, work began around 1259 on an astronomical observatory under the direction of the scholar Nasir al-Din Tusi, financed from the revenues of religious endowments and the patronage of the conqueror; Chapter 43 introduces Tusi's career. And on an island in Lake Urmia, the Mongol rulers kept a fortified treasury in which, reportedly, plunder from the conquests was deposited. A steppe court, a scholarly institution that drew on Islamic traditions of learning, and a strongroom for wealth taken in war existed within a few hundred kilometers of one another. They belonged to the same political system, and the purpose of this chapter is to explain how that system worked and how it changed.

The previous two chapters described how the Mongols conquered Iran and what that conquest cost. Chapter 45 followed the campaigns from Chinggis Khan's invasion of the Khwarazmian lands to Hülegü's destruction of the Nizari fortresses and of the Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad. Chapter 46 examined the human, demographic, and economic consequences of war, and the difficult evidence by which historians try to measure them. This chapter asks a different question. Once the campaigns had slowed, what kind of government existed, who exercised authority within it, and how did a command established through conquest become a durable dynasty ruling Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, and neighboring regions for nearly eight decades?

The chapter's argument is that this transformation was neither an inevitable Mongol assimilation to Iranian ways nor the imposition of an entirely foreign system. Hülegü was dispatched in the 1250s as an imperial commander on behalf of his brother Möngke, the Great Khan. The territorial polity that bears the name Ilkhanate emerged gradually, and the decisive circumstances were largely external to Iran: the death of Möngke in 1259, the succession war that followed within the Mongol imperial family, and the growing separation of the western Mongol realm from the other Chinggisid khanates. The rulers who governed that realm after 1260 remained Chinggisids. They kept military organization, royal households, and political assumptions drawn from the steppe tradition. At the same time they depended on a bureaucracy, a fiscal apparatus, and local notables whose roots lay in the Seljuk and Khwarazmian periods and in the older Islamic and Persianate administrative traditions of Iran. The result was neither of the two older systems, and the way it was assembled is the subject of this chapter.

Our knowledge of this process depends on sources that were produced by participants with their own purposes. The Persian historian Ata-Malik Juvayni wrote while serving the conquerors. The Syriac Christian scholar Bar Hebraeus lived at Maragha and wrote a Syriac chronicle that covers the Ilkhanid court from the standpoint of a Christian churchman. Rashid al-Din, whose Jamiʿ al-Tawarikh is the most detailed surviving account of the Mongol dynasty in Iran, wrote in the early fourteenth century for a later ruler with an interest in how his own line was remembered. Mamluk, Armenian, Georgian, and Latin sources view the same events from the other side of the frontiers, while coins, decrees, and surviving buildings provide evidence that does not depend on any chronicler's account. Each of these sources is introduced where it matters, and three of them are examined directly in Source Windows.

The chapter also attends to the people who are easily lost in a history of dynasties. Royal women such as Doquz Khatun held significant political and religious influence. Ministers drawn from families of Persian administrators, such as the Juvaynis, supplied the expertise on which taxation and correspondence depended, and they paid for it in the volatile politics of the court. Christian, Buddhist, Muslim, and Jewish communities faced a government whose religious attitudes varied from one ruler to the next. The pastoral population that accompanied the Mongol army needed grazing land, and the settled farmers and townspeople of Iran needed protection from requisition. The government of the early Ilkhanate was a negotiation among all of these, and its fiscal and political strains help explain why the dynasty's later transformation under Ghazan Khan took the form it did.

The chapter's chronological frame runs from the Mongol occupation of western Iran before Hülegü's arrival, through his campaigns, the reigns of Abaqa, Ahmad Tegüder, Arghun, Gaykhatu, and Baydu, and ends with Ghazan Khan's accession in 1295. It is organized in five divisions of four sections each. The first traces the formation of the western Mongol state and its relationship to the Great Khan; the second examines Mongol kingship, military power, and territorial government; the third turns to Persianate bureaucracy, taxation, and law; the fourth follows Abaqa's reign, the wars with the Mamluks, religious politics, and diplomacy with the wider world; and the fifth follows the succession crises of 1282 to 1295. Ghazan's conversion, systematic reforms, and the institutionalization of Islam belong to Chapter 48 and are previewed here only to explain where this chapter stops. The term "Iranian state" in the title is a statement about the geographic and institutional center of gravity of the Ilkhanid government. It makes no claim that the Ilkhans became ethnically Iranian or ceased to regard themselves as Mongols.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why 1256 is a conventional starting date for the Ilkhanate and why effective state autonomy developed over several years rather than at a single moment.
  2. Describe the administrative and military situation in Iran before Hülegü's arrival, including the roles of earlier commanders and officials.
  3. Trace the effects of Möngke's death in 1259 and the Qubilai–Ariq Böke succession struggle on the western Mongol command.
  4. Analyze the relationship between the Ilkhans and the Great Khan, distinguishing formal allegiance from practical autonomy.
  5. Assess how royal camps, seasonal movement, and regional capitals structured Ilkhanid political geography.
  6. Explain the political roles of Mongol princes, commanders, and military households in sustaining and destabilizing the state.
  7. Compare the ways the Ilkhanate governed Fars, Kirman, Anatolia, Armenia, Georgia, and other regions through direct rule, tributary submission, and delegated authority.
  8. Evaluate how Persianate institutions, notably the diwan and its officials, operated under Mongol sovereignty, using the Juvayni family as a case study.
  9. Distinguish among the principal forms of taxation, requisition, and postal obligation that shaped the burden of Mongol rule.
  10. Interpret the wars with the Mamluks, rivalry with other Mongol khanates, and diplomacy with Latin Europe and Eastern Christian communities.
  11. Analyze the succession crises from Abaqa to Baydu, giving weight to religion, factional politics, finance, and Chinggisid dynastic rules.
  12. Evaluate chronicle, documentary, and numismatic evidence for the early Ilkhanate, identifying the perspective and limits of each.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a military command established under the authority of the Great Khan develop into a hereditary territorial dynasty, and who decided that it should?
  2. Why did the Ilkhans continue to acknowledge the Great Khan long after they ceased to be subject to his practical control?
  3. In what sense was the Ilkhanid government a "Persian bureaucracy with a Mongol ruler," and what does that formula leave out?
  4. How did the demands of Mongol armies, royal households, and princely appanages shape taxation and the position of local officials?
  5. Why did rivalries among Mongol ruling families constrain Ilkhanid policy on its northern, eastern, and western frontiers?
  6. What does the career of the Juvayni brothers reveal about the opportunities and dangers facing Iranian administrators under Mongol rule?
  7. How should historians interpret the religious politics of the early Ilkhanate when rulers, queens, ministers, and communities held varied religious positions?
  8. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct events that were recorded mostly in retrospective or partisan narratives?
  9. To what extent did the crises of 1291 to 1295 result from structural problems in the Ilkhanid state, and to what extent from contingent decisions?

Historical Context

The Mongol conquest of Iran was not a single campaign but a sequence of operations over several decades. Chinggis Khan's invasion of the Khwarazmian realm (examined in Chapter 45) destroyed the political order that had succeeded the Seljuks in Iran and Central Asia, whose final decades Chapter 44 describes. Mongol armies then pursued the last Khwarazmshah, Jalal al-Din, and stayed in the regions of western Iran, Azerbaijan, and the Caucasus. In the following years, Mongol commanders and civil officials established a system of occupation. Garrisons and field armies were maintained, local rulers who submitted retained their positions under obligations of tribute and military service, and officials from the conquered societies collected revenues and administered cities for the Mongol court. This arrangement was authorized from the imperial center in Mongolia, and its governors answered, at least formally, to the Great Khan.

The political landscape that the Mongols entered was already fragmented. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad retained religious prestige and some territorial authority in Iraq, but had long ceased to command a unified empire. The Seljuk sultanate of Rum controlled central Anatolia. Local dynasties held Fars, Kirman, Mosul, and several smaller territories, while the Nizari Ismailis held mountain fortresses in northern Iran and elsewhere (Chapter 41). Georgian and Armenian kingdoms occupied the Caucasus and parts of eastern Anatolia and Cilicia. The administrative traditions of this world were substantial. The Seljuk and Khwarazmian states had developed offices for chancery, finance, army administration, and the audit of revenues, staffed by trained secretaries who wrote in Arabic and, increasingly, in Persian, a profession whose ideals Chapter 39 examines through Nizam al-Mulk. Much of that personnel survived the conquest, often by moving into Mongol service.

The Mongols brought their own institutions. The empire was the property of the Chinggisid family, whose members held appanages, commanded armies, and had a claim to participate in the choice of a Great Khan at an assembly known as a quriltai. Military organization was decimal, units were commanded by hereditary or appointed commanders called noyans, and the army depended on pastoral resources, which meant that herds and grazing land were not incidental to government but part of its material foundation. Mongol practices of census, taxation, postal communication, and the issuing of written decrees were carried across Eurasia, and administrators from Chinese, Uighur, Central Asian, and Iranian backgrounds worked within them. The system had a unifying ideology, the claim that Heaven had granted universal rule to Chinggis Khan and his descendants, but it was also held together by family ties, and those ties were under strain by the 1250s.

Hülegü's expedition began within this framework. Möngke, who became Great Khan in 1251 after a contested succession that shifted power from the line of Ögedei and Güyük to the line of Chinggis's youngest son Tolui, sent his brother west to complete the reduction of the Nizari Ismailis, to bring the caliph to submission, and, in some accounts, to extend Mongol authority farther toward Syria and Egypt. The scope and duration of that mandate are debated, and how historians read it shapes how they understand the origin of the Ilkhanate. The chapter begins from this debate rather than resolving it in advance.

From Mongol Conquest to Ilkhanid State Formation

Sections 47.1–47.4
Section 47.1

Iran under Mongol Occupation before Hülegü, 1220–1255

When Hülegü's army crossed the Amu Darya at the beginning of 1256, it entered territory that had been subject to Mongol authority for a generation. The Ilkhanate is often described as beginning with Hülegü, but the Mongol presence in Iran was older than the dynasty, and understanding it is essential to understanding what Hülegü inherited. The first phase, the campaigns of Chinggis Khan and his generals against the Khwarazmian state in the 1220s, is the subject of Chapter 45. The next phase was an occupation of uneven intensity. Mongol commanders pursued the last Khwarazmshah, Jalal al-Din, across western Iran and the Caucasus until his death in 1231, and the general Chormaqan, dispatched to Iran by Great Khan Ögedei after his accession in 1229, extended Mongol control over Azerbaijan, Armenia, and Georgia during the 1230s. When Chormaqan was incapacitated by a paralytic illness around 1242, his deputy Baiju succeeded him and continued the campaigns. On 26 June 1243, Baiju's army defeated the Seljuk sultan of Rum at Köse Dağ in eastern Anatolia, which brought the Anatolian sultanate within the Mongol sphere as a tributary.

Military and civil authority were distinguished from the start. The commanders held armies, and their camps were concentrated in the grasslands of Azerbaijan and the Mughan steppe, where pasture could support the animals that a Mongol army required. Civil governors, by contrast, were appointed to administer the conquered settled territories, collect taxes, and arrange the provisioning of the army. In Khurasan and the eastern parts of the plateau, the early governor Chin Temür, who held authority in the 1230s, was followed by the Uighur secretary Körgüz, who administered Khurasan and adjacent regions for several years. Juvayni credits Körgüz with reorganizing tax assessments, curbing some arbitrary military exactions, and restoring cultivation, though this favorable portrait requires source criticism. Körgüz fell victim to rivalries at the imperial court and was executed in the early 1240s. His successor, Arghun Aqa, of the Oirat people, governed for more than three decades. Arghun Aqa was reconfirmed in the position by Möngke and, according to Juvayni, presided over censuses, the regularization of tax assessment, and attempts to restrain the arbitrary levies imposed by Mongol princes and envoys. He died in 1275, long after Hülegü's arrival, and some members of his family continued in high office under the Ilkhans.

Several features of this early system are important for what followed. First, power was divided. A senior Mongol commander controlled the troops, while a separate governor handled civil administration. The divided structure meant that disputes between soldiers and administrators were built into the system from the start, a theme this chapter will repeatedly encounter. Second, the Mongol administration relied heavily on non-Mongol secretaries, among them Uighurs, Persians, and others, who could read and write the languages of the settled populations. Third, local rulers who submitted retained their positions, so that the Mongol realm in the west was a patchwork rather than a uniformly governed province. Fourth, the system was imperial rather than regional. Governors received appointments, confirmations, and removals from the Great Khan's court in Mongolia, and they sent tribute and information there.

For these reasons, Mongol authority already existed across substantial parts of Iran before 1256 but did not yet constitute the later Ilkhanid dynasty. There was no resident royal house with a claim to the territory. The commanders and governors were appointees, not rulers in their own right, and there was no sustained attempt to create a single government for the western lands. What Hülegü's expedition added was a new Chinggisid prince with a large army, a mandate from the Great Khan, and, eventually, a household that would remain in the region after the campaigns ended. That distinction between an occupation administered by appointees and a dynasty is the central one for this division.

The evidence for the earlier period is unevenly distributed. Juvayni, who traveled in Mongol service and knew Arghun Aqa, is the best near-contemporary source for the civil governors of Khurasan, but he writes in the context of Mongol rule and his portraits of individual governors reflect both his experience and his political position. Juzjani, who wrote in India in 1260, adds an outside view. Armenian chroniclers and Georgian sources provide the perspective of the Caucasian kingdoms that were drawn into Mongol obligations, and Rashid al-Din's later account integrates these materials with Mongol sources that Juvayni could not use. Students should note that all of these were written after the fact, and all reflect the interests of those who wrote them.

Section 47.2

Hülegü, Möngke Khan, and the Creation of a Western Mongol Power, 1251–1260

Möngke's decision to send Hülegü west was part of a broader program to reorganize and extend the empire after he took power in 1251. His accession had required the removal of rivals in the Ögedeid and Chaghatayid lines, and it was followed by new censuses, a reform of taxation across the empire, and fresh campaigns, including Qubilai's expeditions in southern China and Hülegü's in southwest Asia. According to the later Persian chronicles, Hülegü was instructed to destroy the Nizari Ismailis of Iran, to bring the Abbasid caliph to obedience or end his rule if he refused, and to proceed against states farther west. Hülegü left Mongolia in the early 1250s, moved slowly, and required several years to bring together an army that drew contingents from across the empire, along with engineers and specialists in siege warfare, some of whom had Chinese backgrounds. He crossed the Amu Darya in early 1256, which is why 1256 is often counted as the beginning of his rule in Iran.

The campaigns themselves are narrated in Chapter 45 and need only be summarized here. The Nizari strongholds in the Alborz and in Quhistan were reduced in 1256, and their leader, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, surrendered; the great fortress of Alamut was given up late that year. In 1257 Hülegü's army advanced toward Iraq, and in early 1258 it besieged Baghdad. The city fell in February, and the Abbasid caliph al-Mustaʿsim was executed. In the following year, Mongol forces advanced into northern Syria, taking Aleppo and then Damascus in early 1260. Meanwhile, the Mongol army had accepted the submission of a number of regional rulers, among them the ruler of Mosul, and Georgian and Armenian rulers continued to provide troops. By 1260 the territory under Hülegü's authority or Mongol supervision extended from Khurasan and the Oxus frontier to Iraq, Azerbaijan, and parts of Syria.

Alongside the military campaign, Hülegü established a base in northwestern Iran. The sources describe his headquarters in Azerbaijan, where he built or restored palaces and fortresses, and the use of a treasury on an island in Lake Urmia. Maragha, a town with an established scholarly culture, became the site of an observatory built under Nasir al-Din Tusi, a scholar who had earlier served the Nizaris and had entered Hülegü's service after the conquest. Tabriz, a major commercial city of the region, grew in importance as the administrative and mercantile center of the realm. These choices are significant because they show an intention to remain. A commander who intends only to raid does not build observatories or treasuries.

Whether Möngke intended Hülegü to found a permanent hereditary realm is more difficult to establish. Later sources, especially those produced under the Ilkhans themselves, state that Möngke granted Hülegü the lands from the Amu Darya to Egypt, and this claim became part of the dynasty's account of its own legitimacy. Contemporary evidence for such an explicit grant is thinner. Many historians observe that the claim served the interests of the later dynasty and that Möngke may have intended to give his brother a command whose duration was left undefined, perhaps expecting to reassign or recall him once the expedition's objectives were achieved. In Mongol imperial practice, princes held appanages and commands, and it was common for a prince's descendants to inherit a territory, but the specific arrangement here was not necessarily settled in 1251. The Historical Debate in Section 47.4 examines this problem in more detail.

The key point for this section is that Hülegü was in 1256, and remained until 1259, a Mongol prince acting under the authority of his brother, the Great Khan. He sent reports and gifts and received orders. His decisions about the army, the treatment of local rulers, and the organization of administration were made within an imperial framework. The Ilkhanate of later history was a product of what happened next.

Section 47.3

The Emergence of an Iranian-Based Mongol State after 1260

In August 1259 Möngke died while campaigning in China. Hülegü, who was in Azerbaijan at the time, learned of the death some months later, and the consequences were immediate and long-lasting. Möngke's death opened a contest for the succession between two of Hülegü's brothers, Qubilai, who commanded in China, and Ariq Böke, who held the Mongol homeland. Each convened a quriltai and each was proclaimed Great Khan by his supporters in 1260. The war between them lasted until Ariq Böke's surrender in 1264, and its significance for Iran was that it removed the central authority that had supervised Hülegü's expedition. Hülegü could not simply wait for a new Great Khan to confirm his position, and he had to decide what to do with an army that was now in a region far from the center of power.

His first decision was strategic. Hülegü withdrew a large part of his forces from Syria and left a smaller army under his commander Kitbuqa to hold what had been gained. In September 1260 that army was defeated by the Mamluks at Ain Jalut in Palestine, and Kitbuqa was killed. The defeat ended Mongol control over Syria for the time being, and it marked the practical end of expansion toward Egypt. Chapter 45 gives the military details; the point here is that the battle fixed a frontier that Hülegü's successors would contest for decades without moving permanently. Whether Hülegü would have continued westward if he had not withdrawn is a counterfactual that historians have debated, and the answer depends partly on judgments about the size of his army and the grazing capacity of Syria, neither of which can be established with precision.

Hülegü's second decision was dynastic. In the succession struggle he sided with Qubilai, his full brother, and Qubilai, after his victory, recognized Hülegü's authority in the west. The exchange bound both parties. Hülegü acquired the legitimacy of an imperial confirmation, and Qubilai acquired a loyal western kinsman. At the same time, other branches of the Chinggisid family were hostile. Berke, ruler of the Golden Horde, had become a Muslim and was angered by the destruction of Baghdad and by Hülegü's presence in territories that the Jochid line claimed, particularly in the Caucasus. In 1261 and 1262 the quarrel developed into open fighting, as Section 47.8 explains. The conflict further separated Hülegü from the Mongol world to the north and left him dependent on his own resources and on the Great Khan far away in China.

A third process was the consolidation of governing structures. In 1263 Hülegü appointed Shams al-Din Muhammad Juvayni as sahib-i diwan, the head of the financial administration, an appointment that is treated in Section 47.10. His court acquired the apparatus of a territorial ruler, with a treasury, a chancery, and a body of officials. He fortified and provisioned strongholds, bound regional rulers to obedience through marriage and tribute, and distributed commands to his sons and commanders. When he died in February 1265, the succession of his son Abaqa proceeded according to Mongol custom, with a quriltai in which the princes and commanders gave their consent, and with a confirmation from Qubilai that the sources report.

1256 is thus a conventional starting point for the Ilkhanate, and an acceptable one if it is understood as the year in which Hülegü's army entered its decisive Iranian phase. But it does not mark the moment when an independent state came into being. The process that produced the Ilkhanid state was a sequence: an imperial expedition, a succession crisis that cut the expedition off from its commander in chief, a series of local decisions about territory and administration, and the hereditary succession of 1265. Each step was reversible at the time, and none was a founding act. Historians who describe the Ilkhanate as established in 1256, 1260, or 1265 are choosing different points in the same process.

Cause and Effect

Why a Mongol Command Became a Territorial Dynasty

Long-term causes

The Chinggisid practice of distributing appanages and commands to princes; the existence of an established Mongol occupation and Persianate administration in Iran before 1256; the large distances between Iran and the imperial center in Mongolia and China.

Short-term causes

Möngke's mandate to Hülegü, which left the duration of the command unclear; the need to maintain a large army with pasture and revenue in a region far from the center; Hülegü's decisions to build a base in Azerbaijan.

Immediate trigger

Möngke's death in 1259, the Qubilai–Ariq Böke succession war, and the Mamluk victory at Ain Jalut in 1260, which together isolated Hülegü and halted further expansion.

Transformation

Over the 1260s, Hülegü and his son Abaqa acquired a hereditary claim to the territory, recognized by Qubilai, and developed a separate government.

Immediate effects

Open conflict with the Golden Horde; the establishment of the diwan under a chief minister; hereditary succession in 1265.

Long-term consequences

A durable territorial state with its own interests and a distinct dynastic identity, still linked formally to the Great Khan.

Historical complexity: The outcome was not planned in 1251 and cannot be explained by any single decision. Contingent events in China and Mongolia, local choices in Iran, and the limited capacity of the imperial system to supervise distant princes combined to produce it. Nothing in the situation made a dynasty inevitable; Hülegü could have been recalled, replaced, or defeated.

Section 47.4

The Ilkhan, the Great Khan, and Chinggisid Political Legitimacy

The title "Ilkhan" is conventionally used for Hülegü and his successors, and its meaning has long been associated with subordination to a higher khan. The Turkic word il can mean "peace" or "submission," and many scholars therefore read il-khan as "subordinate khan" or "khan of the realm." Others note that the evidence for the title's usage in the early period is limited, that it appears in contemporary sources less consistently than the later literature assumes, and that its precise meaning may have been less important to contemporaries than its association with a distinct line. For this textbook "Ilkhan" is used as a conventional label for the Mongol rulers of Iran, with the understanding that it captures a relationship to the Great Khan without settling the question of how its holders understood their status.

Historical Vocabulary
Ilkhan and Ulus

Definition: Ilkhan (also il-khan) is the conventional title for the Mongol rulers of Iran descended from Hülegü. Ulus is a Mongol term for a people, realm, or the share of the imperial patrimony assigned to a branch of the Chinggisid family.

Historical context: The Mongol Empire was understood as the common possession of Chinggis Khan's descendants. Each branch held an ulus that could be governed with considerable independence, while the Great Khan, called qaʾan or khagan, held a superior claim. The word "Ilkhan" is usually interpreted as expressing the subordinate status of Hülegü's line, though the history of the title's usage and its exact meaning remain subjects of scholarly discussion.

Why it matters: These terms describe a political system in which sovereignty was both shared and ranked. Understanding them helps explain why the Ilkhans could be autonomous in practice yet continue to acknowledge the Great Khan in form.

Potential misunderstanding: "Ilkhanate" is a modern label. The dynasty did not necessarily use it of itself, and it should not be read as meaning a state that was "subordinate" in a practical sense after the early 1260s.

The Ilkhans were Chinggisids, and this identity was the foundation of their authority. In the Mongol political tradition, legitimate rule belonged to the descendants of Chinggis Khan, and others, however powerful, could not claim the supreme title. That principle shaped the Ilkhanid state in several ways. Succession was restricted to the royal line, and the Ilkhans' commanders and ministers, however influential, did not themselves become khans. Princes of the blood held appanages and commands, and the ruler's authority over them was limited by custom and by their own resources. And royal women, especially the principal wives, held political weight and managed their own households and estates, a point to which we return in Section 47.15.

Succession followed Mongol precedents more than the hereditary patterns of the Islamic and Persianate world. A new ruler was normally selected from among eligible princes by a quriltai at which the commanders and other princes gave their assent, and the choice was often contested. Primogeniture was not binding, and brothers, uncles, and nephews could all claim the throne. Throughout the Ilkhanid period this system generated competition and occasional violence, and several reigns discussed in Division V ended with the removal or killing of the ruler. A related practice was the avoidance of shedding royal blood, which led to executions by other means. These rules were not an Iranian inheritance but a Mongol one, and they continued to govern the dynasty even as its administration became more Persianate.

The relationship with the Great Khan combined formal deference and practical independence. The Ilkhans acknowledged the Great Khan in several ways. Their coinage, as the Coinage Study in Section 47.11 explains, sometimes carried his name or invoked the Mongol imperial authority. They sought confirmation of their accession from the court in China, and the sources report that envoys from Qubilai brought decrees confirming Abaqa's succession. They exchanged envoys, maintained representatives at one another's courts, and shared specialists, goods, and information. A figure like Bolad, a Mongol official who spent years in Iran as the Great Khan's representative and became an important source of information on Mongol history for Rashid al-Din, illustrates the connection.

Yet the Ilkhans did not follow the Great Khan's orders as an imperial province would. They made their own decisions about war and peace, taxation, appointments, and alliances, and they were not subject to the Great Khan's army or revenue officials. Distance made supervision impractical, and the Great Khan's own difficulties, including the competing claims of Ögedeid rulers in Central Asia and the Jochid line to the north, reduced his ability to intervene. The recognition by the Ilkhans of a nominal superior was useful to them as well. It strengthened their claim against rival Chinggisid branches, in particular the Golden Horde and the Chaghatayids, and it confirmed their standing within the family as legitimate holders of a territory. Formal allegiance was therefore a political resource, not merely a leftover of earlier subordination.

Historical Debate

Was the Ilkhanate Initially an Autonomous State or a Regional Branch of the Mongol Empire?

The question: Did Möngke intend Hülegü to found a permanent, hereditary realm in Iran, or did the Ilkhanate develop from a temporary imperial command? And once it existed, should it be understood as an independent state or as a province of a larger Mongol empire?

Interpretation A — An intended hereditary realm: Later Persian chronicles written under Ilkhanid patronage state that Möngke granted Hülegü the lands between the Amu Darya and Egypt for himself and his descendants. Historians who emphasize this evidence argue that the Ilkhanate's autonomy was foreseen from the beginning and that it should be read as a deliberate division of the empire into appanages.
Interpretation B — An autonomous state produced by the succession crisis: Other historians stress that contemporary evidence for such a grant is limited, that the later chronicles had reasons to emphasize the dynasty's legitimacy, and that the decisive developments occurred after Möngke's death in 1259. On this view, the Ilkhanate became autonomous through the breakdown of imperial unity rather than through a founding plan.
Interpretation C — A hybrid or evolving relationship: A third approach holds that the two questions should be separated. Hülegü may have been given a long-term command with ambiguous terms, and the dynasty's formal subordination to the Great Khan continued even as practical autonomy increased, so that the Ilkhanate was both a regional branch and a separate state at different times and in different respects.

Evidence: The sources include Juvayni, writing near the time, Rashid al-Din, writing decades later, and Mamluk, Armenian, and Chinese sources that describe Hülegü's expedition and the later relations of the Ilkhans with Qubilai. Coins and decrees show how the Ilkhans represented their authority.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the interpretation of Möngke's intentions, the reliability of later retrospective sources, and the definition of terms such as "state" and "autonomy" in a political system that did not distinguish sharply between family property and public authority.

Why it matters: If the Ilkhanate was planned as a separate realm, the Mongol Empire's fragmentation appears as policy. If it developed from a crisis, fragmentation appears as contingent and the dynasty's claims about its own origins appear as later legitimation.

Student task: Explain what kinds of additional evidence would help to decide between the interpretations, and assess whether the three positions are mutually exclusive.

Section 47.5

Royal Camps, Territorial Capitals, and the Political Geography of the Ilkhanate

An Ilkhanid capital was not a modern seat of government. The ruler moved with a court, an army, and a large pastoral following, and authority was exercised wherever the royal encampment, called an ordu, happened to be. The seasonal pattern was typical of steppe polities: winter quarters at lower elevations, such as the Mughan steppe in northwestern Iran and the plain of Baghdad, and summer pastures in the highlands, such as those near Alatagh in Armenia or in the region around Hamadan. Rulers might spend months in tents even when permanent palaces were available, and decisions on succession, war, and appointments were often made at camps in the grasslands. Cities existed alongside this system. They housed treasuries, chanceries, workshops, markets, and the mosques and colleges of the Muslim population, and many officials lived in them. The Ilkhans visited cities, built in them, and derived revenue from them, but they did not necessarily reside in them for long.

Azerbaijan was the political heartland. Its extensive pastures could support the animals of the Mongol army and households, its highland and lowland zones permitted seasonal movement, and its location gave access to the Caucasus, Anatolia, Iraq, and the Iranian plateau. Tabriz, a long-established commercial city, grew in importance as the principal administrative and financial center. Maragha, which Hülegü used as an early base, remained prominent as a scholarly center because of its observatory, library, and the presence of figures such as Nasir al-Din Tusi and the Syriac churchman Bar Hebraeus. Other places, including Ujan, Hamadan, and the fortress treasury in Lake Urmia, played roles in particular periods. Baghdad, now a provincial city under a Mongol governor, retained commercial and religious significance, and Khurasan, administered with considerable autonomy under governors, gave the dynasty a long eastern frontier against the Chaghatayids.

The mobile court mattered institutionally. Mongol practice made royal households, with their wives, retinues, and treasuries, important centers of administration. Each principal wife held her own camp, called an ordu, with its own revenues and personnel, and these households were politically influential, as the career of Doquz Khatun shows. Chanceries and treasuries traveled with the court or operated from fixed locations, and officials had to move between the two. This had practical consequences: petitioners followed the court, documents were issued from the camp, and access to the ruler often depended on influence with those who accompanied it. The relationship between mobile court and fixed city is therefore a key to understanding how a state dominated by a pastoral military elite could nevertheless rely on urban finance.

Everyday Life and Social History

Camps, Pastures, and Cities

The Mongol presence meant that tens of thousands of people and very large numbers of animals needed grazing land and water. Precise numbers are unknown and should not be asserted, but the demand was large enough to affect the use of farmland and pasture in Azerbaijan and the Mughan, and Persian sources complain of crops damaged, villages burdened by the provisioning of troops, and farmers or townspeople displaced when pasture was extended. For pastoral families attached to the army, seasonal movement was the pattern of life, and the household economy depended on herds, mares' milk, wool, felt, and the distribution of plunder and tribute.

Townspeople experienced the system differently. Merchants and artisans in Tabriz and Maragha supplied the court and army, and some prospered, but they also faced requisitions, taxes, and the arbitrary demands of envoys. The sources are written largely by officials and scholars and say little directly about the experience of rural cultivators, whose lives we reconstruct indirectly from complaints, tax records, and later reforms. Chapter 46 discusses the demographic evidence, and Chapter 50 follows the longer-term recovery of agriculture and trade.

Section 47.6

The Mongol Military Establishment: Noyans, Amirs, Garrisons, and Royal Households

The army was the foundation of Ilkhanid government, and its structure reflects the steppe origins of the dynasty. Mongol units were organized in theory on a decimal system, from tens to thousands to larger divisions often called tümens. The units were commanded by noyans, a term that Persian authors usually rendered as amir, and the commanders held positions that were frequently hereditary or that passed within families. Troops lived with their families and herds, which meant that army and population were not sharply separable, and a unit's grazing land and provisioning were obligations of the state. The Ilkhanid army also included contingents of Turkic and other steppe origin, local levies from subordinate rulers such as the Georgians and Armenians, and specialist troops such as siege engineers. By the time of Abaqa the army was no longer the force that had arrived with Hülegü, but a mixture of long-settled Mongol units and newer recruits.

The ruler's relationship with the military aristocracy was not a simple chain of command. The Ilkhan was the head of a family enterprise in which princes of the royal house, the commanders of the great units, and the senior officials of the household all held rights and expectations. Princes had their own followings and sometimes their own territories, and commanders who had served the dynasty for decades possessed considerable authority. The most senior commanders participated in choosing the ruler and in removing him, as Division V will show. A strong ruler such as Abaqa could manage this system through patronage, marriage alliances, and the distribution of offices. A weak or unpopular ruler faced the possibility of a coup.

The army's needs shaped the economy and administration. Provisioning an army required grain, fodder, horses, and transport, and Persian officials were responsible for arranging it. Grazing land had to be reserved for military use, and the allocation of pastures, the movement of herds, and the assignment of winter and summer quarters were matters of state policy. Soldiers were paid in various ways, including grants of revenue, shares of plunder, and the direct support of their families. The relationship between army and fiscal system was close and sometimes tense. Military commanders wanted resources, administrators wanted to control expenditure and protect the tax base, and the two interests came into open conflict at several points in this chapter.

Garrisons were placed in strategic locations, including the frontier areas of Khurasan, the Caucasus, Anatolia, and Iraq, and in the interior to maintain order. Mongol governors held both military and civil functions in some regions, particularly where an unsettled frontier or local resistance required it, and the officials known as darughachis (and, in some contexts, basqaqs) represented the central authority in subordinate territories, supervising tribute, taxation, and the conduct of local rulers. These officials could be oppressive, and local sources repeatedly describe their demands.

The army was thus both the foundation of the state and its principal source of instability. It made conquest and defense possible, but it also generated factional politics, and Mongol commanders could and did overthrow rulers. Arghun's rise through the support of commanders in 1284, the power of Buqa under Arghun, and the revolt against Gaykhatu in 1295 all reveal how dependent the ruler was on military backing. The Ilkhanate's rulers repeatedly attempted to balance that power by relying on administrators, favorites, or ministers, and these efforts produced the tension between military and bureaucratic power that Section 47.18 analyzes.

Section 47.7

Provincial Government, Local Dynasties, and Negotiated Sovereignty

The Ilkhanid realm was governed through a variety of arrangements that ranged from direct administration to nominal overlordship. The simplest model was direct rule, in which the Ilkhan appointed governors, tax officials, and military commanders. This was most common in the core regions of Azerbaijan, Iraq, and the central plateau, and in places such as Baghdad, where a Mongol-appointed governor such as Ata-Malik Juvayni held office. A second model was tributary submission, in which a local ruler retained authority in return for tribute, military contingents, and acceptance of Mongol officials. A third was delegated authority, in which an Ilkhanid prince or commander held a region as a quasi-hereditary charge. In practice these categories overlapped, and a single province could pass between them over a few decades.

Fars and Kirman illustrate the variation. In Fars, the local Salghurid atabegs had submitted to the Mongols and continued to rule under Mongol supervision. Their rule ended in the 1280s when the dynasty died out, and the territory came under more direct administration and under the influence of royal households and officials; Abish Khatun, a Salghurid heiress, married into the Ilkhanid family, a connection that tied the province to the dynasty. In Kirman, the Qutlughkhanid family, descendants of Qara Khitai refugees, held power under Mongol authority; one prominent figure, Qutlugh Terkan Khatun, ruled for a time and maintained relations with the Ilkhans. Women's rule in this case is not an anomaly but a reminder that Mongol political culture allowed prominent women to hold authority. The arrangement in these southern provinces depended on payments to the court and on political ties, and local rulers had to conduct a continuing negotiation with the central government.

Anatolia was a protectorate in which the Seljuk sultans of Rum remained on the throne but were closely supervised. After Köse Dağ in 1243, the Seljuk state paid tribute and its policies were constrained by Mongol military power, and the powerful vizier or pervane, Muʿin al-Din Sulayman, balanced between Mongol demands and local interests. Mongol troops and officials were stationed in the region. The arrangement was fragile, and Abaqa's execution of the pervane after the Mamluk victory at Elbistan in 1277 shows how quickly suspicion of divided loyalties could turn deadly. Over the following years Anatolia came under more direct Ilkhanid administration, though local Turkmen groups and frontier lords continued to complicate control.

In Armenia and Georgia, rulers who had submitted to the Mongols kept their kingdoms but were required to provide troops, pay tribute, and travel to the Mongol court. The Armenian kingdom of Cilicia, under King Het'um I, cooperated with the Mongols early and continued the connection, partly because it faced Mamluk pressure. Georgian kings supplied contingents for Ilkhanid campaigns, including the army defeated at Homs in 1281, and the Georgian nobility's relationship with the Mongols was often strained. Such regions show that the Ilkhanate could use allies as well as subjects, but that alliances imposed costs on the allies, who bore casualties and economic pressure.

Existing dynasties survived by accommodating Mongol sovereignty, and in doing so they changed it. They adopted Mongol titles and customs, sent envoys and hostages to the court, made marriage alliances, and aligned their own bureaucracies with Mongol demands. The Ilkhans benefited from a system that required fewer direct administrators and drew on local legitimacy. But they also faced risks. Local rulers might withhold revenue, side with a rival Mongol prince, or appeal to the Mamluks. The Ilkhanate's government was therefore a negotiated sovereignty, renewed at each accession and strained at each crisis, and its geographical extent depended on the success of those negotiations.

Section 47.8

The Ilkhanate and the Other Mongol Khanates

By the time of Hülegü's death the Mongol Empire was no longer a single polity under a single ruler. It had become a group of related realms, each ruled by a branch of the Chinggisid family and each with its own interests. The Great Khan, Qubilai, ruled China and the Mongolian homeland from the 1260s and in 1271 adopted the dynastic name Yuan. The Jochid line ruled the northwestern steppe, the realm usually called the Golden Horde. The Chaghatayid line ruled much of Central Asia, and the Ögedeid prince Kaidu, who rejected Qubilai's authority, built a rival power in Central Asia. The Ilkhans held the southwest. Nominal unity survived in the Chinggisid ideology, in exchanges of embassies and goods, and in the recognition of Qubilai by some branches, but the actual relationships were often hostile.

The conflict with the Golden Horde was the most serious. Berke, who had converted to Islam, resented Hülegü's destruction of Baghdad and his occupation of Azerbaijan, which the Jochids regarded as part of their sphere. The sources report an exchange of diplomatic accusations and, in 1261 to 1262, open warfare in the Caucasus. Hülegü's forces and those of Berke fought around Derbent and across the Terek River, with serious Ilkhanid reverses in 1262. Berke's cooperation with the Mamluks, formalized through exchanges of envoys, turned the Mongol conflict into part of a wider Eurasian alignment: the Mamluks and Golden Horde against the Ilkhanate. The Golden Horde and Ilkhanate continued to contest the Caucasus for decades. For the Ilkhans the northern frontier required the maintenance of garrisons and forced them to divert attention from Syria.

On the eastern frontier the principal rivals were the Chaghatayids and, from the 1260s, Kaidu and his allies. Chaghatayid rulers claimed lands in Khurasan and Afghanistan, and their incursions threatened Herat and the Khurasan frontier. The sources describe repeated raids and, in 1270, a major invasion by the Chaghatayid ruler Baraq, which Abaqa's army defeated near Herat, a victory treated in Section 47.13. A permanent settlement was not achieved, and Khurasan remained a militarized frontier in which governors had substantial authority, partly because of the need for rapid response.

The relationship with the Yuan was different. Qubilai and his successors were distant and supportive, and the Ilkhans maintained a diplomatic relationship in which Great Khan and Ilkhan exchanged envoys, goods, and expertise. Chinese and Iranian specialists in medicine, astronomy, printing, and administration traveled in both directions. The Ilkhans benefited from the legitimacy and occasional assistance of the Great Khan, and the Yuan benefited from an ally against the western Mongol khanates. The relationship lacked the capacity for practical command, because geography made coordinated military action nearly impossible.

The wars among Mongol ruling houses were a defining feature of the supposedly unified Mongol world. They shaped Ilkhanid strategy by dividing resources, limiting campaigns in Syria and Anatolia, and creating a permanent need for troops on the northern and eastern frontiers. They also gave opponents opportunities: the Mamluks exploited the Golden Horde connection, and local rulers sometimes sought help from rival Mongol princes. Historians therefore usually avoid treating the Mongol Empire after 1260 as a single state. It was a family of related states in uneasy cooperation, rivalry, and occasional war, and the Ilkhanate must be understood as one of them.

Section 47.9

The Diwan, Viziers, Secretaries, and the Continuity of Persianate Government

Under the Seljuks and the Khwarazmshahs, government in Iran was conducted through a set of offices collectively called the diwan, staffed by trained secretaries who wrote in Arabic and, increasingly, in Persian. A chief minister, the vizier, supervised the offices, and there were separate branches for the chancery, which drafted and sent official correspondence; for finance, which assessed and collected revenues and kept accounts; for the audit of expenditure; and for the army, which handled pay and assignments. The secretaries formed a profession with its own manuals, family traditions, and standards of style, a culture that Chapter 39 examines through the figure of Nizam al-Mulk. These men, not the rulers themselves, ran the daily operation of the state, and they passed their expertise within families and through networks of patronage.

Most of this personnel survived the Mongol conquest, though not without loss. Some secretaries were killed in the sieges of cities, some fled, and many entered Mongol service as the conquerors recognized that they could not collect revenues from agricultural and commercial societies without them. Families that had served the Seljuk and Khwarazmian courts took up posts under Mongol governors, often in the financial offices. The same families could serve successive rulers across several regimes, which gave Iranian administration a continuity that political change did not break. The Juvayni family of Khurasan, treated in the next section, was one such family.

Historical Vocabulary
Diwan and Sahib-i Diwan

Definition: Diwan means a government office, register, or the collective body of administrative departments. Sahib-i diwan ("master of the diwan") was the title of the official who headed the financial and administrative offices, often translated as chief minister or finance minister.

Historical context: The diwan was a feature of Islamic governance from the early caliphate, and Seljuk and Khwarazmian rulers continued it with their own modifications. Under Mongol rule the term continued to designate the civil and financial administration, but the office of sahib-i diwan acquired particular importance because it stood between the Mongol court and the taxpayers. The title and the vizierate overlapped, and the sources do not always distinguish them with precision.

Why it matters: The continuation of the diwan allowed the Mongols to extract revenue from settled societies and gave Persian administrators a route into the center of the state.

Potential misunderstanding: The same name did not mean the same function. Mongol commanders, royal households, and princes held rights that the diwan could not always override, so the office worked within different constraints than its Seljuk predecessor.

What these officials actually administered can be stated concretely. They assessed land revenue, which in much of Iran was based on a mixture of crop shares and fixed money payments, and they maintained registers of villages, irrigation arrangements, and landholders. They appointed and dismissed provincial officials, adjudicated disputes over revenues, issued receipts, and supervised the payment of the army and of pensions or endowments. They drafted royal orders and translated them between languages, and they handled the correspondence of the court with provincial rulers and foreign powers. They also negotiated conflicts between the royal treasury and the claims of princes, commanders, and queens, whose households held separate revenue rights. Without them the tax system would have been unmanageable.

Mongol practices were layered on top. Secretaries who wrote in the Uighur script, called bitikchis, drafted documents in Mongol. Mongol judges, sometimes called yarghuchis, administered customary law within the army and the royal household. Resident officials such as darughachis supervised subordinate rulers, and commercial taxes were collected by officials known as tamghachis. The imperial postal system, the yam, operated alongside local arrangements. Language in the chancery was mixed. Arabic remained the language of law, theology, and much scholarship, Persian grew as a language of administration and historical writing, and Mongol was used for the ruler's orders. The proportion of each in different contexts is difficult to measure from the surviving evidence, since many original documents are lost.

Section 47.10

The Juvayni Family and the Making of Ilkhanid Administration

The Juvayni family, from the town of Juvayn in Khurasan, illustrates how Persian administrators entered Mongol service and how precarious their positions were. The family had served the Seljuk and Khwarazmian courts, and its members served Mongol governors in Khurasan after the conquest. Two brothers rose to prominence under the Ilkhans, and they must be distinguished. Shams al-Din Muhammad Juvayni was the chief financial and administrative minister. Ata-Malik Juvayni was a governor and a historian. Each held distinct offices, and treating them as interchangeable would be an error.

Ata-Malik, who was born in the 1220s, accompanied Arghun Aqa and traveled to the Mongol court in Mongolia in the early 1250s. During this period he began to write the Tarikh-i Jahan-gushay, "History of the World Conqueror," a Persian account of the Mongol rise and of the Mongol conquest of Iran. After the capture of Baghdad he was appointed governor of Baghdad and Iraq, a post he held from about 1259 until his death in 1283. In that role he supervised the reconstruction of the city's finances, the restoration of agricultural land in Iraq, and the management of relations with the Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities of the city. The sources credit him with attention to irrigation works and with some effort to restore the city's institutions, though his accomplishments are known mostly through writers who favored him or his family.

Shams al-Din was appointed sahib-i diwan in 1263 under Hülegü and continued under Abaqa and, briefly, Tegüder. His authority extended across the financial administration of most of the Ilkhanid realm. He appointed provincial officials, many of them kinsmen or clients, and he presided over the revenue system through the years of consolidation. The sources describe his sons and relatives as holding governorships in several provinces, and they describe his patronage of poets and scholars, who dedicated works to him and praised his generosity. He also accumulated substantial wealth, estates, and endowments, which were a normal means of securing family position in this period and also a liability, since they could be used as evidence of corruption.

The brothers' careers show the opportunities available to Persian administrators. They gave Mongol rulers expertise in finance and communication, and they could defend local populations by shaping policies, restraining excessive demands, and negotiating exemptions. They could also enrich their families and place relatives in office. At the same time their power depended on the favor of the Mongol ruler, and they had enemies: Mongol commanders who resented civilian control over revenue, rival administrators who wanted office, and princes who wanted resources. Accusations of embezzlement and misconduct were a standard method of attack. In the final years of Abaqa's reign, Ata-Malik faced accusations of financial malfeasance and was subjected to investigation, from which he seems to have emerged with his position largely intact, and he died in 1283. Shams al-Din's downfall came after Abaqa's death, as Section 47.18 relates.

Ata-Malik Juvayni, Tarikh-i Jahan-gushay (History of the World Conqueror), c. 1252–1260 and later

Creator: Ata-Malik Juvayni, Persian administrator and later governor of Baghdad · Date: begun in the early 1250s and completed after the fall of Baghdad, with revisions · Type: Persian prose history of the Mongols and of the conquest of Iran, written in the service of the conquerors

Context: Juvayni wrote while in the service of the Mongol administration, at a time when the legitimacy of the new rulers was still being established among the Iranian population. He had witnessed events and relied on informants, written records, and conversations at the Mongol court. He wrote in an ornate Persian style intended to demonstrate the literary skills of a trained secretary.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The work presents the rise of Chinggis Khan as the working out of divine will, describes the destruction of earlier rulers and cities and the creation of a new order under Mongol sovereignty, praises certain Mongol practices of discipline and justice, and also records the abuses of Mongol governors and the suffering of the conquered. The author's tone shifts between admiration, apology, and lament.

Analysis: The Tarikh-i Jahan-gushay shows how an Iranian administrator explained Mongol authority to a Persian-reading audience. The idea that the Mongols ruled by divine permission allowed Muslim officials to accept their rule without abandoning their own religious framework. The book is also valuable for detail on governors, taxation, and the early structure of Mongol administration, and for the author's candor, within limits, about abuses.

Limitations: Juvayni was an employee of the regime and a member of a family whose fortunes depended on Mongol favor. His praise cannot be taken at face value, nor can his criticism, which had to be carefully phrased. He also wrote in literary conventions that shaped how he described violence and loyalty. His account of events he did not witness depends on what informants told him. This textbook paraphrases rather than quotes the work, since exact wording must be verified against a named scholarly translation.

Perspective: The text represents the viewpoint of an educated Persian official who served the conquerors. It says little directly about peasants, pastoralists, women, or the views of Muslim scholars who refused service.

Section 47.11

Revenue, Taxation, Appanages, and the Financial Demands of Mongol Rule

The financial system of the early Ilkhanate was a combination of inherited and imposed obligations, and the burden on taxpayers depended on which of them they owed. Land revenue, called kharaj in the Islamic tradition, was the principal tax on cultivators. It was collected in cash or kind according to local custom and the quality of land. Non-Muslims had traditionally paid a poll tax, and the Mongols retained some version of this, while exempting religious personnel of various faiths, a policy that Möngke had endorsed across the empire. A commercial tax, called tamgha, was levied on goods in trade. Alongside these taxes were Mongol levies. The qubchur was a tax whose original meaning seems to have been a levy on livestock and which was extended in Iran to broader populations. Its assessment varied across place and period. Extraordinary levies, often called avariz, were imposed for military needs and could be repeated. In addition, officials and travelers with authority had the right to requisition animals, food, and lodging, the ulagh.

Historical Vocabulary
Yarligh, Qubchur, and Yam

Definition: A yarligh was a written decree issued by a Mongol ruler. Qubchur was a Mongol tax, originally associated with livestock and later applied more widely. The yam was the Mongol postal and relay system, with stations that supplied horses, food, and lodging to authorized travelers.

Historical context: These institutions were common across the Mongol Empire, and administrators adapted them to local conditions. In Iran the yam was grafted onto older courier systems, and the qubchur was combined with existing taxes. The yarligh was the standard instrument by which the ruler granted privileges, issued appointments, and gave orders.

Why it matters: Taken together, they show how the Mongol state combined communication, taxation, and written command, and how each created opportunities for both administration and abuse.

Potential misunderstanding: The terms did not have identical meanings across regions or periods, and the sources sometimes use them loosely. Precise rates and procedures cannot always be reconstructed.

The postal system was both a tool and a burden. Stations were maintained at intervals along main routes, supplied by the population nearby, and the Mongol court issued tablets or passes authorizing travel. In principle this provided rapid communication across the realm. In practice, envoys, merchants, commanders, and others with real or claimed authority demanded horses and supplies at the expense of local communities, and Persian sources describe the strain on villages along the routes. Ghazan's later reform of the system is a subject for Chapter 48. In this chapter it is enough to note that the problem was recognized before his reign.

Revenue was claimed by competing parties. The ruler's own treasury needed funds for the court, the army, and gifts to the nobility. Princes, queens, and commanders held appanages and estates, sometimes including entire towns or districts, and drew revenues from them directly. The Ilkhanid crown also held lands of its own, sometimes called inju. Merchants known as ortoq operated with capital advanced by Mongol princes and received privileged treatment, a practice that blended commerce and politics. Tax farming, in which a person paid the state a fixed sum for the right to collect revenue from a district and kept the surplus, was widespread and produced overcollection. The arrangements overlapped, and disputes among those with claims were frequent. A farmer might owe land revenue to the diwan, a levy to a prince's agent, a requisition to a passing envoy, and a portion of the harvest to a local landlord.

The fiscal demands of the army and court were large, and they grew when wars required expeditions or when rulers spent lavishly. The diwan, led by figures like Shams al-Din, tried to regularize assessment, restrain irregular exactions, and maintain adequate revenue. These efforts had mixed success. The sources describe periods of relative order and periods of heavy extraction, and the quality of administration depended on the strength of the ruler and the stability of the court. It would be a mistake, however, to treat the later reforms of Ghazan as already accomplished in the 1260s or 1270s. The patterns of overlapping claims and irregular levies persisted until the crisis of the 1290s made reform an urgent matter.

Coinage and Documentary Evidence Study

Mongol and Persianate Symbols of Authority on Coins, Seals, and Royal Orders

Context: Coins are among the few forms of evidence that survive in large numbers from the Ilkhanid period and were produced directly by the state. Silver coins issued under Abaqa and Arghun can be used to examine how the dynasty represented its authority. Royal decrees and seals, where they survive, add evidence on written practice, though original Ilkhanid documents survive in far smaller numbers than coins, and many are known through later copying.

What students should look for: Using a catalogued example from a museum or numismatic collection, identify the mint, the date (typically in the Islamic calendar), the ruler named, the titles used, and the languages and scripts of the inscriptions. Some issues carry only Arabic inscriptions, including Islamic religious formulae. Some name the Great Khan, and some include Mongol inscriptions in the Uighur script. Practices vary by period, mint, and issue, and students should not assume that one formula applied everywhere.

Analysis: A single coin can combine a Mongol title, an Islamic formula, and the name of a distant Great Khan, and thus place the Ilkhan within several systems of legitimacy at once. The use of Islamic formulae on the coins of a ruler who was not a Muslim shows that the monetary system served a largely Muslim population and that rulers adapted to the expectations of merchants who handled the coins. The choice of mints shows the geography of administration.

Limitations: Coins show what rulers chose to display on a product intended for circulation, not what they believed. They do not directly reveal the details of tax policy or administration, and mint practices may have been delegated to officials. Surviving coins are a sample shaped by hoards and collecting.

Section 47.12

Law, Administration, and Local Society: Mongol Authority in an Iranian Setting

Several legal and normative systems operated at once. The Mongol rulers claimed authority through royal orders and through a body of customary and imperial norms often called the yasa, the exact content and codification of which are matters of scholarly dispute. Mongol military and household law governed the army and the royal family. At the same time, the settled Muslim population continued to use Islamic law, administered by judges (qadis), market inspectors, jurists, and other officials whose standing came from long tradition. Non-Muslim communities, including Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, maintained their own internal legal and religious arrangements. These systems overlapped, and the question of which applied in a given dispute could depend on who the parties were, who had authority in the district, and who had the ruler's ear.

The Mongols did not attempt to replace Islamic law across Iran, and they continued to grant exemptions and privileges to religious institutions. Endowments (awqaf), which supported mosques, colleges, shrines, and charitable foundations, remained a major part of urban life, though their revenues were sometimes diverted or placed under official supervision. Local notables, including judges, descendants of the Prophet, Sufi shaykhs, urban headmen, and landowners, continued to represent their communities to the authorities and to mediate between townspeople and officials. Their cooperation was necessary for collecting taxes and keeping order, and the Mongols recognized it with exemptions and appointments. Merchants likewise remained important, and the connection of some of them to Mongol princes through the ortoq system gave them access to power.

Authority was negotiated in practice, and the process had winners and losers. A ruler's decree could override local custom, and a commander's power could trump a judge's decision. A well-connected notable could secure tax relief or appointment, while a community without such connections might bear a heavier load. Complaints against officials, petitions to the ruler, and appeals to powerful ministers were routes by which local people pursued their interests. The state's reach was real, but it depended on cooperation, and the system could break down when the ruler was weak or when factional struggle at court disrupted the appointment of reliable officials.

The coexistence of competing legal and administrative arrangements could be peaceful or conflicting. When Mongol officials and Muslim judges disagreed over jurisdiction, or when requisitions conflicted with endowment rights, the outcome depended on the balance of power. Yet the persistence of Islamic institutions, the respect that many Mongol rulers showed to scholars and shaykhs, and the continuing employment of Iranian administrators meant that the Mongol state was not merely an occupation. It operated in an Iranian setting and was shaped by it.

Historical Debate

Did the Mongols Preserve Persianate Government, Fundamentally Transform It, or Do Both?

The question: To what extent did the early Ilkhanate continue the administrative traditions of the Seljuk and Khwarazmian states, and to what extent did Mongol rule change them?

Emphasis on continuity: Scholars emphasizing continuity point to the survival of the diwan, the reemployment of Iranian secretaries, the persistence of Islamic legal and religious institutions, and the use of Persian as an administrative and literary language. On this view the Mongols adopted an existing bureaucratic system because they needed it.
Emphasis on transformation: Others emphasize the destruction of the Abbasid caliphate, the dominance of Mongol military aristocracy, the introduction of new taxes and the postal system, the role of royal households, and the shift in the balance of power between bureaucrats and military elites. On this view the apparent continuity of institutions concealed a radical change in who held power and how revenues were claimed.
Emphasis on synthesis: A third position holds that continuity and transformation are not alternatives. Familiar offices and personnel were retained, but they operated within a new framework of Mongol sovereignty and new institutional layers, producing a hybrid form of government that was neither purely Persianate nor purely Mongol.

Evidence: Chronicles such as Juvayni's and Rashid al-Din's, administrative manuals, surviving decrees, coins, and descriptions by foreign observers inform the debate. Because many documents are lost, the evidence is uneven.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale (how much changed), terminology (what counts as continuity), and interpretation (whether surviving titles imply surviving function).

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand Mongol rule, whether as a rupture or as an episode within a longer Iranian administrative history, and how they view the later reforms of Ghazan.

Student task: Select one institution discussed in this division, such as the diwan or the yam, and argue for the interpretation that best fits the evidence for it.

Section 47.13

Abaqa Khan and the Consolidation of Ilkhanid Power, 1265–1282

Hülegü died on 8 February 1265, and his chief wife, Doquz Khatun, died four months later, on 16 June 1265. His son Abaqa was chosen as successor, apparently with the consent of the princes and commanders and the endorsement of Qubilai, whose confirmation was recorded in the sources. Abaqa had been governing in the east, and he began his reign by rewarding supporters, reorganizing commands, and confirming officials in their posts. He retained Shams al-Din Juvayni as sahib-i diwan, and with this continuity of administration he was able to avoid the disruptions that often accompanied a change of ruler. His reign of about seventeen years was one of the longest of the dynasty, and it was the period in which the dynasty's institutions were most stably established.

Abaqa maintained the Mongol style of kingship. He moved between winter and summer quarters, held quriltais, and relied on a circle of commanders drawn from the families that had come with Hülegü. He continued the policy of religious plurality, with Buddhist, Christian, and Muslim figures present at court, and his marriage to Maria Palaiologina, an illegitimate daughter of the Byzantine emperor Michael VIII and known as Despina Khatun, connected him to Constantinople and to Eastern Christian communities. Maria had originally been intended as a bride for Hülegü but married Abaqa after Hülegü’s death. In the government of settled territories he relied on the diwan, on regional governors, and on the Juvayni family. Within the royal house, he faced the ambitions of brothers and other princes, whom he managed through grants of territory and command.

The northern and eastern frontiers demanded attention. The Golden Horde remained hostile after Berke's death in the mid-1260s, and fighting in the Caucasus continued intermittently. In the east, the Chaghatayid ruler Baraq, who had come into conflict with Qubilai and Kaidu in Central Asia, turned toward Khurasan and invaded in 1270. Abaqa's forces defeated him near Herat in the summer of that year, and Baraq withdrew and died not long afterward. The victory secured the Khurasan frontier for a time, and it demonstrated the capacity of the Ilkhanid army to coordinate a rapid response. In 1272–1273, Abaqa’s forces raided Bukhara in Chaghatayid territory, illustrating that the eastern frontier remained contested even after Baraq’s defeat. Kaidu and his allies remained dangerous, and the eastern frontier required continuing garrison and attention.

Abaqa's reign is therefore best understood as one of consolidation more than expansion. He kept the borders largely where they stood, supervised governors and subordinate rulers, and sustained the diwan. The Syrian and Anatolian frontiers, discussed in the next section, produced both the most ambitious military operations and the most significant reverses. He died in April 1282 near Hamadan, reportedly after a period of heavy drinking, and the circumstances of his death later became part of the political conflict over the succession, since some suspected foul play. The details of his final illness are known mostly from later sources whose accounts of his habits may be shaped by the politics of the succession.

Section 47.14

The Ilkhanate, the Mamluks, and the Struggle over Syria and Anatolia

The Mamluk Sultanate, which ruled Egypt and, after Ain Jalut, most of Syria, was the Ilkhanate's principal western rival. The Mamluks were a military regime led by officers of slave origin, many of them Turkic, who used the memory of Ain Jalut to claim a role as defenders of Islam against the Mongols. Under Sultan Baybars, who ruled from 1260 until 1277, they built an effective army, a network of fortresses and intelligence, and a diplomatic system that included an alliance with the Golden Horde. The Euphrates formed a long and contested frontier. Mongol and Mamluk forces raided one another, the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia was attacked by the Mamluks for its alliance with the Mongols, and neither side was able to establish lasting dominance across northern Syria.

Ain Jalut remained symbolically important to both sides. For the Mamluks it was proof that the Mongols could be defeated, and for the Ilkhans it was a reverse that demanded revenge. Yet the Ilkhanid response was limited by competing commitments. The Golden Horde threatened the Caucasus, the Chaghatayids and Kaidu threatened Khurasan, and Syria lay at the end of long supply lines in country where grazing was less abundant than in Azerbaijan. Every campaign into Syria thus required the diversion of resources from other frontiers, and the Ilkhans were never able to commit their full force. This is the point that historians often make about Mongol-Mamluk rivalry: rivalry with other Mongol khanates, as well as logistics, constrained Ilkhanid operations.

Anatolia was the second theater. In 1277, Baybars led a Mamluk army into Anatolia and defeated a Mongol force at Elbistan in April. He advanced to Kayseri, but when Abaqa approached with reinforcements, Baybars withdrew. Abaqa's reaction was severe. Suspecting that the Seljuk vizier Muʿin al-Din Sulayman, the pervane, had been in contact with the Mamluks, he had him executed, and the Ilkhanid administration in Anatolia was reorganized with closer supervision. Baybars died later in 1277, and the Mamluk throne passed after a short interval to Qalawun, who ruled from 1279 and proved an able and cautious opponent.

The final major engagement of Abaqa's reign occurred in October 1281 near Homs in Syria. An Ilkhanid army commanded by Abaqa's brother Möngke Temür, with contingents from Georgia and Armenia, met Qalawun's forces and was defeated. Abaqa had planned to follow up the campaign with a larger effort, but he died the following spring. The defeat at Homs demonstrated that the Mamluks could stand against Ilkhanid power in open battle, and it strengthened the Mamluk position in Syria. Mamluk sources emphasize the victory, while Armenian and Persian sources describe the Mongol losses differently, and students should read these accounts with attention to their sources' purposes.

The wars did not end in decisive victory for either side. The frontier remained fluid until the early fourteenth century, and the cost of maintaining it affected both states. For the Ilkhanate, the conflict with the Mamluks shaped relations with Christian powers in Europe and the eastern Mediterranean, as the next sections show, and it contributed to the fiscal strain that culminated in the crisis of the 1290s.

Section 47.15

Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, and the Religious Politics of Early Mongol Iran

The religious position of the early Ilkhanid court was neither uniform nor simple. The Mongols had their own religious traditions, centered on the worship of the sky god Tengri, reverence for ancestors, and the activity of shamans and diviners. They also followed a long-standing steppe practice of respecting religious specialists of many faiths, who were often exempted from taxes and consulted for ritual or divination. Chinggis Khan and his descendants had interacted with Buddhist, Christian, Muslim, and Daoist leaders, and they used religion as part of imperial legitimacy without committing exclusively to any one faith. In Iran this practice meant that members of several religious communities held influence at court at various times.

Buddhism enjoyed patronage under Hülegü and Abaqa. Buddhist monks, often called bakhshis, served as scribes, physicians, and advisers, and the sources report that temples were built in parts of Azerbaijan and elsewhere. Eastern Christianity, especially the Church of the East, had a prominent place because of its presence among some Mongol and Turkic groups and because of the influence of Christian women in the royal household. The Muslim population remained the large majority of the settled inhabitants, and its scholars, judges, and officials continued to play important roles in administration. Jews also served as physicians and administrators, as the later example of Sa'd al-Dawla shows. The policies of individual rulers differed, and the general pattern is better described as changeable patronage than as a fixed policy of toleration.

Doquz Khatun's career illustrates the importance of royal women. She was a granddaughter of the Kereyid ruler Toghril, a leader of a steppe confederation that had included many Christians, and she became Hülegü's principal wife. Contemporary Christian sources and later chroniclers describe her as a patron of Christians who intervened on behalf of Christian communities, in particular at Baghdad after the conquest, where churches were protected and Christians were reportedly spared in the sack that struck much of the Muslim population. She also held authority within the royal household and participated in political decisions. Her death in 1265, shortly after Hülegü's, removed an influential advocate. Her case shows that royal women could play direct roles in high politics and religion, and that such roles were not merely derivative of their husbands' power.

Religion was a component of legitimacy. Rulers needed the support of the diverse communities they governed, and they used patronage, building projects, and ceremonial recognition to secure it. They also faced pressure. Muslim elites, who administered much of the realm, expected recognition of Islamic institutions, and Muslim opponents of the Mongols, including the Mamluks and, after his conversion, the Golden Horde, accused them of oppressing the faith. Christian leaders hoped to use Mongol power against Muslim rulers. The Ilkhans balanced these pressures in different ways, and the question of the ruler's own religion became increasingly political in the 1280s, as the next division describes.

Section 47.16

Diplomacy beyond Iran: The Great Khans, Latin Europe, and the Eastern Christian World

The Ilkhanate's rulers conducted diplomacy across Eurasia. With the Yuan court they exchanged envoys and goods. With the Golden Horde and Chaghatayids they exchanged threats and, occasionally, negotiations. With the Mamluks they exchanged raids and letters. And with the Christian powers of Latin Europe, Byzantium, Cilician Armenia, and Georgia they sought alliances that would support their struggle with the Mamluks. The motivation was strategic. The Ilkhans faced enemies on several fronts, and an alliance with the Latin Christian states that held coastal enclaves in Syria, or with European monarchs willing to send troops, could shift the balance in the west.

Overtures began under Hülegü, who corresponded with the French king Louis IX, and they continued under Abaqa. Abaqa sent letters and envoys to the papacy and to European rulers, and his representatives appeared at the Council of Lyon in 1274. In 1271 and 1272, when Prince Edward of England was on crusade in Syria, Mongol forces made a raid into northern Syria, and some European observers hoped that coordinated action would follow. In practice there was little coordination. Distance, slow communication, divergent interests, and the political divisions of Europe prevented a joint campaign. Abaqa also cultivated ties with Byzantium through his marriage and with the Armenian and Georgian kings, who contributed troops.

Arghun's reign produced the most elaborate effort. In 1287 he sent a delegation headed by Rabban Sauma, a Christian monk of Ongut Turkic origin who had traveled westward from China and had become a respected figure in the Church of the East. Rabban Sauma visited Constantinople, Italy, Paris, and Bordeaux, was received by the French king Philip IV and the English king Edward I in Gascony, and met with the cardinals and, in 1288, the newly elected Pope Nicholas IV in Rome. He carried proposals for combined military action against the Mamluks. The embassy was received with respect and curiosity, and he reportedly took part in religious services, but no European monarch committed troops. A follow-up embassy led by an Italian in Ilkhanid service, Buscarello de' Ghizolfi, carried further letters in 1289 and after. The fall of Acre to the Mamluks in 1291 ended the principal Latin Christian presence on the Syrian coast and removed one of the practical bases for cooperation.

The chief source for Rabban Sauma's journey is a Syriac text on the life of his companion and fellow monk Mar Yahballaha, who became head of the Church of the East in 1281. It exists in an abridged form that must be used with attention to its purposes, and its details cannot all be checked against European records. Latin records confirm the diplomatic contacts but say little of the specifics of Rabban Sauma's visit. The mission is best read as a serious attempt at international cooperation that reflects both the ambitions of Mongol diplomacy and its limited practical results. The Ilkhans and the European rulers shared a rival but not a plan, and neither side could provide what the other needed.

Section 47.17

Ahmad Tegüder, Conversion to Islam, and the Succession Crisis of 1282–1284

Abaqa died in April 1282, and the succession was contested between two branches of the family: his brother Tegüder, a son of Hülegü, and Abaqa's son Arghun, who held the eastern command in Khurasan. A quriltai, held in the spring, chose Tegüder, who was probably the senior prince present and had the support of influential members of the royal household and of senior commanders. Arghun was absent, and he did not initially challenge the decision. Tegüder took the Muslim name Ahmad, and some sources report that he had earlier been baptized as a Christian. His conversion to Islam was therefore a significant departure from the religious pattern of the court, and he adopted Islamic forms of kingship, including a title modeled on those of Muslim rulers, in his correspondence and coinage.

His reign lasted just over two years, and his policies reveal the aims of a ruler who sought a stable accommodation with his Muslim subjects and with the Mamluks. He sent envoys to Qalawun proposing peace and an end to the Mongol-Mamluk wars, and he gave support to Muslim institutions and scholars. The Mamluk response was cautious. Qalawun received the embassy and exchanged letters, but the Mamluks were not prepared to accept a settlement on Ilkhanid terms, and some Mamluk authors doubted the sincerity of the offer. Within the realm, Tegüder retained the Juvayni administration at first, though Ata-Malik Juvayni died in 1283. He faced the growing opposition of other princes and commanders, and he did not command the loyalty of the senior amirs who had held power under Abaqa.

Opposition was led by Arghun, who rejected Tegüder's legitimacy and gathered support in Khurasan, where he held the governorship. The sources report a conflict in which Tegüder's forces first prevailed, Arghun's position was temporarily weakened, and a settlement was attempted. But a faction of Mongol commanders, among them some who had been displaced or who feared a Muslim ruler's policies, turned against Tegüder and supported Arghun. In the summer of 1284 Tegüder was defeated and captured. He was put to death in August 1284 by a method that, in the Mongol tradition, avoided shedding the blood of a prince of the royal line. Arghun was proclaimed Ilkhan.

The extent to which religion explains the outcome is debated. Later Persian and Mamluk authors, writing with differing interests, sometimes present the conflict as a struggle between a Muslim ruler and Mongols loyal to older customs. There is evidence for such a dimension, since some Mongol commanders objected to his departures from Mongol practice, and Buddhist and Christian communities at court had reason to fear a reduction of their influence. But the conflict also reflected dynastic rivalry between two branches of the royal house, factional alignments among commanders, and Arghun's claim as son of the previous ruler. Tegüder's removal was thus a political crisis in which religion was one factor, and it cannot be reduced to a religious conflict alone.

Primary Source Window III

Rashid al-Din, Jamiʿ al-Tawarikh (Compendium of Chronicles), on the Succession of the 1280s

Creator: Rashid al-Din, physician, administrator, and historian in the service of the Ilkhans · Date: composed in the early fourteenth century, under the patronage of Ghazan and his successor · Type: Persian universal history with an extended section on the Mongols and the Ilkhans

Context: Rashid al-Din wrote some two decades or more after the events of 1282 to 1284. He had access to Mongol informants and to written materials, and he wrote for a ruler whose own claim to the throne descended from Arghun's line. Chapter 49 treats his career and method in detail.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The work narrates Tegüder's election, his adoption of Islam, the discontent of Mongol commanders, Arghun's resistance, and the eventual removal of Tegüder, and it presents Arghun as the rightful successor. It describes the conduct of key commanders and the political calculations of the court.

Analysis: The text is indispensable as the most detailed account of the succession and gives names, sequences of events, and the viewpoint of the dynasty's later historians. It shows how the Ilkhanid court remembered and justified its own history.

Limitations: Rashid al-Din was not an eyewitness to these events. His account was shaped by the interests of the dynasty that employed him, by the sources available, and by the need to present a coherent history to a ruler descended from the winning side. His treatment of Tegüder cannot automatically be treated as neutral. Mamluk and other external sources provide a check, although they have their own limits. This textbook paraphrases the text, since wording has not been verified against a named edition.

Perspective: The text reflects the viewpoint of a courtier-historian of the later Ilkhanate. The perspectives of Tegüder's supporters, and of those outside the court, are largely lost.

Section 47.18

Arghun Khan, Court Factions, and the Politics of Administration, 1284–1291

Arghun's accession brought to power a ruler who owed his throne to the support of commanders and who had to reward them. The most prominent was Buqa, a senior amir who became the dominant figure at court and held the highest military authority. Arghun also moved against the officials of the previous regime. In October 1284 Shams al-Din Juvayni, who had served as sahib-i diwan for more than two decades, was executed. The sources report that he was accused of having poisoned Abaqa and of embezzlement. The accusations drew on suspicion of his power and wealth, and on the hostility of rivals who wanted his offices. Whether the specific charges had any basis cannot be established from the surviving accounts, which come largely from those who profited from or lamented his fall. His death ended the Juvayni domination of the diwan, and members of his family lost their offices and property.

Arghun's administration was marked by the contest between military and bureaucratic power. Buqa's influence was substantial, and he used it to place allies in office. Arghun resented his dependence. In 1289 a plot against Buqa was uncovered or alleged, and Buqa was executed, which removed the most powerful commander but did not end factional struggle. The administration of finance was entrusted to Sa'd al-Dawla, a Jewish physician and administrator from Abhar who had risen through service in the diwan and who was appointed to senior office, including the vizierate, in 1289. His appointment reflected Arghun's need for a minister who would restore revenue and who had no independent military base, and Sa'd al-Dawla, who had served in Baghdad, had experience with accounts and with the Mongol system.

Sa'd al-Dawla's tenure was short. He is credited by some sources with efforts to increase revenue, restrain corruption, and strengthen the treasury, and he placed relatives and allies in office, as ministers did. His position, however, was exposed on several sides. Mongol commanders and princes resented a civilian who controlled access to funds, rival officials sought his place, and some Muslim authors regarded the appointment of a Jew to high office with hostility. These sources frequently rely on religious stereotypes, and historians should treat their accusations with caution and distinguish the political and fiscal grounds of opposition from sectarian polemic. When Arghun became seriously ill in early 1291, a campaign against the minister led to his arrest and execution in March, shortly before the ruler's own death. The persecution of Jews that followed his fall in some places shows the vulnerability of the community when a prominent member lost power.

Arghun's religious inclinations are difficult to characterize. The sources suggest that he favored Buddhist religious figures and was interested in the elixirs of Indian or Tibetan specialists, and that he supported Christians and diplomacy with European powers. He died in March 1291, probably of illness, though rumors of poisoning circulated, and the opinions of contemporaries were shaped by political allegiance. His reign had seen the elimination of two ministers of great power and the loss of the administrative continuity the Juvaynis had offered. Arghun's relations with the Yuan court were maintained by Bolad, the Great Khan's representative in Iran, and the dynasty continued to recognize the Great Khan in form.

The politics of Arghun's reign show a structural problem. The state needed administrators who could raise revenue, yet the Mongol military establishment regarded revenue as its own entitlement, and the ruler needed both. Ministers who succeeded in raising funds became rich and conspicuous, and the commanders who relied on that revenue could turn on them. The result was a repeating cycle in which ministers were appointed, enriched themselves and their allies, and were destroyed. This cycle sapped the state's administrative capacity and encouraged short-term extraction, and it set the stage for the fiscal crisis of the following reign.

Section 47.19

Gaykhatu, Paper Money, Baydu, and the Crisis of 1291–1295

After Arghun's death, a quriltai chose his brother Gaykhatu, who was in Anatolia at the time. Gaykhatu's reign, from 1291 to 1295, is described in hostile terms by many sources, which emphasize extravagance, generosity to favorites, and personal indulgence. These accounts were written after his overthrow and reflect the interests of those who removed him, so historians treat them with care. What the evidence shows more reliably is a state under financial strain. The expenses of the court, the army, and the dynasty's many households were high, the treasury was depleted by the demands of successive rulers, and Gaykhatu's grants to princes and commanders increased the pressure. Political factions competed for influence, and the chief minister, Sadr al-Din Zanjani, sought a way to restore the state's revenues.

The solution adopted was paper currency. In 1294, the Ilkhanid government attempted to introduce paper notes, called chao after the Chinese term, in Tabriz and other cities. The system followed the Yuan model, where paper money had been used for decades. According to the specialist reconstruction of the episode, the original proposal came from ʿIzz al-Din Muzaffar, an adviser to the vizier Sadr al-Din Zanjani; Bolad (also called Pulad), the Great Khan’s representative familiar with Yuan monetary practice, supported the project. The notes were to be issued by the government and accepted at fixed value in place of metal coin, and merchants and the public were to be required to use them. The rationale was financial: the state would obtain real goods in exchange for paper and thus relieve its shortage of precious metal.

The experiment failed within weeks. In Tabriz merchants closed their shops, goods disappeared from markets, and the population resisted, since the Ilkhanid state lacked the conditions under which paper money had worked elsewhere: a long tradition of paper use, administrative capacity to enforce acceptance, and public confidence. Iran's commercial economy depended on coin and on trust in the quality of metal, and the notes had no secure backing. The authorities withdrew the currency after a short period, and the failure damaged the credibility of the regime and its minister. The episode is useful for studying the limits of royal decrees: a government could issue an order, but it could not command acceptance in a market society that had its own means of valuing money. Its failure belongs to the history of state finance and not to a full monetary history of the Ilkhanate, which Chapters 48 and 50 take up further.

Gaykhatu was overthrown in the spring of 1295 by a group of commanders and princes who placed Baydu, a grandson of Hülegü, on the throne, and Gaykhatu was killed. Baydu's rule was brief. He did not have secure support, and the opponent who challenged him was Ghazan, Arghun's son and governor of Khurasan. Ghazan was backed by the powerful commander Nawruz, a son of Arghun Aqa, and the alliance reflected the long-standing prominence of Khurasan as a base. Ghazan advanced from the east, and during that campaign he adopted Islam, a decision examined in Chapter 48. Baydu was defeated and killed in the autumn of 1295, and Ghazan took the throne.

Section 47.20

Chapter Synthesis and Review: From Mongol Conquest to an Iranian-Based Dynastic State

Between 1256 and 1295 the Mongol presence in western Asia changed from an imperial expeditionary command into a territorial dynasty. The process had political, military, administrative, religious, and diplomatic dimensions, and each reveals a different aspect of the transformation. Politically, the western command became autonomous because the imperial center fractured after 1259, and Hülegü and Abaqa converted a delegated authority into a hereditary one with the formal agreement of the Great Khan. Militarily, the dynasty kept the structure of a steppe army and the political influence of its commanders, and it defended long frontiers against the Golden Horde, the Chaghatayids and Ögedeids, and the Mamluks. Administratively, it relied on Persianate institutions and personnel, which it transformed by placing them within a Mongol system of royal households, princely rights, and military privilege. Religiously, it governed a plural society through shifting patronage. Diplomatically, it sought allies across Eurasia, with limited practical results.

The central tension was between Chinggisid sovereignty and territorial state-building. A Chinggisid ruler governed by right of descent, shared the empire with kinsmen, and depended on commanders who held their own claims. A territorial state needed stable revenue, predictable administration, and the cooperation of its settled population. The two requirements were hard to reconcile. The army needed pastures and revenue, taxpayers needed protection, and ministers who mediated between them were exposed to the hostility of both. The succession system, which left the choice of ruler to a contest among princes and commanders, encouraged factional struggle. The crises of 1282 to 1295 were thus not accidents but expressions of structural features that earlier decades had managed with greater success.

The chapter's title should be read with these qualifications in mind. The Ilkhanate became an Iranian-based state in the sense that its political center was in Azerbaijan and Iran, its administration drew on Iranian institutions and personnel, and its policies responded to the economy and society of the plateau. It did not become Iranian in the sense that its rulers abandoned their Mongol identity, and the shift of its religion toward Islam, which Chapter 48 examines, is a later development. The relevant comparison is with other conquest dynasties in Iranian history, such as the Seljuks (Chapter 38), who also adapted to the administrative traditions of the lands they ruled while retaining their own military culture.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Mongol Occupation of the 1230s to the Territorial Ilkhanid State of 1295

What Changed

  • An administration run by appointees of a distant Great Khan became a hereditary dynasty with its own court, treasury, and succession disputes
  • The caliphate in Baghdad, a long-standing institution of the Islamic world, ceased to exist as a political entity
  • Government came to include royal households, princely appanages, and a more complex system of competing revenue claims
  • The Ilkhans developed a distinct political identity separate from the other Mongol khanates

What Stayed the Same

  • Chinggisid descent remained the basis of legitimacy, and the Great Khan continued to be acknowledged in form
  • The army remained steppe in structure and dependent on pasture, and its commanders retained political weight
  • Persianate secretaries, the diwan, and Islamic legal and religious institutions continued to operate
  • The pastoral heartland in Azerbaijan and the mobile court persisted as a pattern of rule

Timing and who experienced it: Change was gradual and uneven, with some rapid breaks, such as the fall of Baghdad. Military and administrative elites experienced it most directly, through shifts in rank and office. Rural cultivators experienced greater continuity in the form of obligations to remote authorities, though the weight of those obligations varied with circumstances.

Looking ahead, the crises that closed this period shaped the agenda of the following chapters. Chapter 48 examines how Ghazan, whose accession ends this chapter, combined a personal conversion to Islam with systematic reforms of taxation, landholding, and administration, in part as responses to the problems of overlapping claims, irregular exactions, and factional instability described here. Chapter 49 turns to Rashid al-Din, whose historical writing and administrative career belong to that reform period and whose work is a principal source for this one. Chapter 50 follows the longer-term recovery of cities, agriculture, and trade, and Chapter 51 later takes up the fragmentation that followed the dynasty's end.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a Mongol imperial command established through conquest developed between 1256 and 1295 into a durable territorial dynasty centered on Iran, and how the institutions of the conquered territories reshaped Mongol government. Its argument is that the Ilkhanate did not become an Iranian-based state by abandoning its Mongol origins. Its government emerged from the interaction of Chinggisid dynastic practice, a steppe military establishment, and the Persianate bureaucratic and religious institutions already present in Iran.

Mongol authority in Iran predated Hülegü. From the 1230s, commanders such as Chormaqan and Baiju held armies in Azerbaijan, while civil governors such as Chin Temür, Körgüz, and Arghun Aqa administered Khurasan and adjoining regions with the help of local secretaries. This occupation system, in which military and civil authority were divided and local rulers retained positions under tribute, was authorized from the imperial center. It was not yet a dynasty, and it is the background against which Hülegü's expedition must be understood.

Hülegü was sent by his brother Möngke to reduce the Nizaris, bring the caliph to submission, and extend Mongol power westward. His campaigns ended Nizari power and the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, and he established a base in Azerbaijan. Whether Möngke intended a permanent hereditary realm is disputed. The decisive circumstance was Möngke's death in 1259 and the succession war between Qubilai and Ariq Böke, which isolated Hülegü, and the defeat at Ain Jalut in 1260 fixed the western frontier. The year 1256 is thus a conventional starting date, and state autonomy emerged over several years, culminating in Abaqa's hereditary succession in 1265.

The Ilkhans continued to acknowledge the Great Khan while acting independently, and formal allegiance served as a political resource against rival Chinggisid branches. Legitimacy rested on descent from Chinggis Khan, and succession followed Mongol practice, with selection by a quriltai and a strong role for commanders, a system that generated recurring conflict. The title Ilkhan is conventionally read as expressing subordination, though its early usage and meaning are not fully settled.

Mongol kingship operated through mobile courts, seasonal movement between pastures, and royal households, including those of principal wives, alongside urban centers such as Tabriz and Maragha. The army, organized by noyans and amirs with their own followings, was the foundation of the state and a source of instability. Provinces were governed through a range of arrangements from direct rule to tributary submission and delegated authority, and local dynasties in Fars, Kirman, Anatolia, Armenia, and Georgia survived by accommodating Mongol sovereignty. The Ilkhanate's relations with the Golden Horde, the Chaghatayids and Ögedeids, and the Yuan show that the Mongol world was a family of states, often in conflict.

Persianate administration continued but was transformed. The diwan, viziers, and secretaries supplied the expertise to assess land revenue, draft correspondence, and manage accounts, while Mongol institutions such as the yam, the qubchur, resident officials, and royal appanages added new layers and competing claims. The Juvayni family, in which Shams al-Din served as sahib-i diwan and Ata-Malik as governor of Baghdad and historian, illustrates both the power and the vulnerability of Iranian administrators. Historians disagree about whether the result is best described as continuity, transformation, or synthesis.

Abaqa's reign was a period of consolidation. He defeated the Chaghatayid invasion near Herat in 1270, maintained the northern frontier, and struggled with the Mamluks over Syria and Anatolia, suffering defeats at Elbistan in 1277 and Homs in 1281. Rivalry with other Mongol khanates constrained these operations. The court's religious politics were plural and shifting, with Buddhist, Christian, Muslim, and Jewish figures holding influence at different times, and royal women such as Doquz Khatun held significant roles. Diplomacy with Latin Europe, Byzantium, and Eastern Christians, including Rabban Sauma's mission of 1287 to 1288, sought alliances against the Mamluks but produced no lasting military cooperation.

After Abaqa's death in 1282, the dynasty entered a series of succession crises. Ahmad Tegüder's conversion to Islam was significant but was only one factor in his overthrow by Arghun in 1284, which also reflected dynastic rivalry and factional politics. Arghun's reign saw the execution of Shams al-Din Juvayni, the dominance and fall of the commander Buqa, and the rise and execution of the minister Sa'd al-Dawla, revealing the tension between military and bureaucratic power. Gaykhatu's reign was marked by fiscal crisis and the failed paper-money experiment of 1294, which showed the limits of royal decrees in a market economy.

Gaykhatu was overthrown in 1295 and replaced briefly by Baydu, who was defeated by Ghazan, supported by commanders from Khurasan. Ghazan's accession closes the chapter. The crises of 1282 to 1295 expressed structural problems: competing claims on revenue, the influence of the military on appointments, and an unstable succession system. They set the agenda for Ghazan's reforms, treated in Chapter 48.

The evidence for these developments comes from Persian, Syriac, Arabic, Armenian, Latin, and other sources, together with coins and documents, and each has its own perspective. Juvayni wrote as a servant of the regime, Bar Hebraeus as a Christian churchman, and Rashid al-Din as a later court historian. Understanding the early Ilkhanate requires comparing these accounts, noticing their purposes, and recognizing what they leave out, especially the experience of rural and pastoral populations.

The chapter thus shows a conquest state becoming a regional dynasty through gradual and contingent processes, without losing its Mongol identity and without simply adopting Iranian forms. The resulting hybrid government governed a plural society, faced external rivals on several frontiers, and carried internal tensions that the following reigns would have to address.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how states are made in the aftermath of conquest. The Ilkhanate did not arise by design. It resulted from an imperial commission, a family crisis in a distant capital, and many local decisions about army, revenue, and administration. Seeing this helps students avoid reading the dynasty's later stability backward into its beginnings.

The chapter also reveals a broader pattern in Iranian history: conquerors who rule Iran must work with its administrative, religious, and social institutions, and in doing so they are changed. The Seljuks, examined earlier, adapted to Persianate government, and the Ilkhans did so under different conditions, with a more resistant military aristocracy and a more fragmented imperial family. The resulting hybrid was unstable but productive, and it shaped the administrative and cultural world that followed.

The developments of 1256 to 1295 shaped what came after. The problems of overlapping revenue claims, military dominance, and unstable succession framed the agenda of Ghazan's reforms, and the historical writing produced under his patronage, including the work of Rashid al-Din, is both a product of those reforms and a principal source for the earlier period. The links to the wider Mongol world, the Mamluks, and Europe continued to affect Iran into the fourteenth century.

Finally, the chapter reminds students that the history of this period must be read through sources with purposes of their own. The accounts of Juvayni, Bar Hebraeus, and Rashid al-Din are essential and partial, and the experiences of peasants, pastoralists, and many townspeople are recoverable only indirectly. The central skill the chapter builds is the ability to explain a political transformation while keeping evidence, perspective, and contingency in view.
