Revolutionary Consolidation, Political Repression, and the New State

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic · Principal scope 1979–1983

Chapter Opening

In June 1981 an elected president of the Islamic Republic of Iran was removed from office within roughly forty-eight hours of a parliamentary vote, by a decree from the Leader whose authority the revolution had created. The episode showed that the institutions of 1979 could operate, and that they operated within a balance of forces the constitution alone did not determine.

On 21 June 1981 the Majles voted overwhelmingly that President Abolhassan Bani-Sadr lacked the political competence required for his office. On 22 June Ruhollah Khomeini issued a decree dismissing him. Only seventeen months earlier Bani-Sadr had been elected with roughly three-quarters of the votes cast, a substantial electoral mandate. In the days before his removal, he had already lost the command of the armed forces that Khomeini had delegated to him, and his supporters had been assaulted in the streets or arrested. The president whose electoral legitimacy had seemed to rival that of any figure in the new state was, by midsummer, in hiding, and soon afterward in exile in France.

The episode is a useful point of entry because it displays the institutions described in Chapter 93 in actual operation. The constitution provided for an elected president, an elected parliament, a Council of Guardians, a judiciary, and a Leader who held ultimate religious and political authority. What the text could not settle was how these bodies would behave when they came into conflict. In 1981 that question was answered by a political struggle in which the Leader, the dominant party in the Majles, the revolutionary courts and security bodies, mass demonstrations, armed opposition, and the pressure of war with Iraq all played a part. This chapter asks how the answer came to be what it was.

The chapter's subject is the consolidation of the Islamic Republic between 1979 and 1983. It uses the word consolidation in a deliberately plural sense. The new order acquired political consolidation, as competing movements were marginalized and authority was concentrated. It acquired institutional consolidation, as improvised revolutionary organizations became permanent bodies. It acquired coercive consolidation through courts, committees, guards, and prisons, and economic consolidation through confiscated property, foundations, and welfare programs. And it pursued ideological consolidation through the remaking of universities, schools, and public life. These dimensions were intertwined, and the chapter treats them together rather than as separate narratives.

The chapter also treats consolidation and repression as related but not identical. The Islamic Republic built schools, clinics, roads, relief committees, and a mass base among rural and urban poor Iranians who had been poorly served by the monarchy, and it did so while detaining, torturing, and executing thousands of its opponents. Both facts belong in the account. A history that records only the second omits the social constituencies through which the new state sustained itself, while one that records only the first cannot explain why, or how, the political order that emerged from these years was so durable. Violence was also not one-sided: armed opposition organizations assassinated officials and bombed institutions, and those acts shaped the state's responses without justifying every one of them.

The evidence is uneven, and this is one of the chapter's principal lessons. Official publications, the revolutionary press, speeches by Khomeini and other leaders, and the text of the constitution document the state's own claims. Contemporary reporting by foreign journalists and diplomats, reports by Amnesty International and similar organizations, memoirs, and the publications of opposition groups document other aspects of events. Each category has limits. Official sources conceal what the state wished to conceal, opposition sources have their own purposes, and human-rights compilations record only the cases that could be verified. Casualty and execution figures in particular are partial and contested, and the chapter identifies source and limitation wherever numbers appear.

The chapter follows the Iranian Revolution's own sequence. Division I examines the fragmentation of the revolutionary coalition and the organizational advantages of Khomeini's supporters. Division II examines committees, guards, courts, and the economic foundations through which state power was built. Division III turns to Kurdistan and other regions, to religious minorities, to the universities, and to women and social regulation. Division IV examines the decisive political confrontations of 1981, and Division V the repression, the elimination of remaining organized opposition, and the institutional consolidation of 1982–1983. The chapter closes by connecting the consolidated state to the war with Iraq, whose origins and course are taken up in Chapters 96 and 97.

Three preceding chapters supply the setting. Chapter 92 followed the collapse of the monarchy and the emergence of competing centers of authority, Chapter 93 the construction of the constitutional order, and Chapter 94 the hostage crisis and the weakening of the provisional government and of liberal forces. Chapter 95 does not retell those events; it asks how the political system whose outlines they established came to be dominated by one group, and what that domination cost and enabled.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the revolutionary coalition of 1978–1979 fragmented after the fall of the monarchy and why the outcome was not predetermined.
  2. Describe the organizational foundations of the Islamic Republican Party and the clerical political network, distinguishing them from the wider clerical establishment.
  3. Compare the constitutional visions of liberal, nationalist, and religious opponents of concentrated clerical authority.
  4. Distinguish the major organizations of the revolutionary left and explain why their relations with the new government differed.
  5. Trace the establishment of revolutionary committees, the Revolutionary Guards, and the Basij and distinguish their roles from those of the regular armed forces and police.
  6. Assess the jurisdiction, procedures, and legal basis of the revolutionary courts and the gap between constitutional guarantees and judicial practice.
  7. Analyze how confiscation, revolutionary foundations, and reconstruction programs combined state-building with the creation of social constituencies.
  8. Evaluate the conflicts over Kurdistan and other regions, preserving the distinction between demands for autonomy and demands for independence.
  9. Explain the Cultural Revolution and the regulation of women's public life as institutional and ideological projects with differing social effects.
  10. Reconstruct the confrontation between Bani-Sadr and the Islamic Republican Party, distinguishing the Majles vote of 21 June 1981 from Khomeini's dismissal decree of 22 June.
  11. Interpret documentary evidence on political imprisonment and executions, identifying the limits of quantitative records.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of consolidation, legitimacy, and coercion in the early Islamic Republic.

Historical Context

The Pahlavi state fell in February 1979 with startling speed, but it left behind a society that had been mobilized for more than a year. Strikes, mass processions timed to religious commemorations, and street confrontations had drawn in groups with little else in common. Chapter 91 traced the formation of this coalition, and Chapter 92 its victory. The institutions of the old order, including the army, the police, the intelligence organization SAVAK, and much of the civil bureaucracy, were either dissolved, demoralized, or placed under suspicion. For some months the question of who actually governed was open.

Authority was divided among several centers. Khomeini, who returned to Tehran on 1 February 1979 and settled in Qom later that month, commanded a moral authority that no other figure matched. The Provisional Government headed by Mehdi Bazargan held the ministries and the formal task of administration. The Revolutionary Council, a body whose membership was largely not public, issued decrees. Local committees took over neighborhoods, factories, and offices; revolutionary courts began trying officials of the monarchy; and armed organizations of many persuasions held weapons seized from military depots in the final days. This diffusion of authority is the background against which the chapter's story of concentration must be read.

Iranians also held sharply different expectations of the revolution. For many clerics and their lay supporters it was the opportunity to build a state in which Islamic jurists would supervise law and public morality. For liberal and nationalist politicians it was the restoration of constitutional government and national independence, which they associated with the 1906 constitution and the movement of Mohammad Mossadegh. For the Marxist and Islamic-Marxist left it was the overthrow of a capitalist and imperial order and the beginning of a social transformation. For minorities in Kurdistan, Khuzestan, Baluchistan, Turkmen Sahra, and Azerbaijan it raised the hope of recognition after decades of centralization. These expectations overlapped in 1978 and conflicted in 1979.

The Pahlavi legacy shaped the field. The monarchy's repression of independent politics had left few mature political parties with national organizations, and the exceptions were mostly clandestine or exiled. Religious networks of mosques, seminaries, preachers, charitable associations, and bazaar guilds were the principal institutions to survive, in part because the state had tolerated them more than it tolerated secular opposition. This asymmetry gave those who could draw on religious networks a significant organizational advantage as open competition began. It was an advantage, not a destiny, and the chapter will show how it was converted into governing power.

Two events outside the main domestic story also shaped the period. The seizure of the American embassy in November 1979, examined in Chapter 94, ended the Provisional Government and strengthened those who claimed a more radical revolutionary mandate. The Iraqi invasion of September 1980, whose origins are the subject of Chapter 96, created a national emergency in which dissent could be portrayed as disloyalty. Neither event fully explains consolidation, but both altered its pace and its justifications. Readers should hold two propositions together: the war accelerated consolidation, and consolidation was already well advanced before the war began.

Division I · Sections 1–4

The Fragmentation of the Revolutionary Coalition

Why the alliance that overthrew the monarchy disintegrated, and how competing organizations struggled over the character of the new state.

Section 95.1

From Revolutionary Unity to Political Fragmentation

The movement that overthrew Mohammad Reza Shah was never a single organization. Over 1977 and 1978 it had been a convergence of groups that shared an adversary more than a program: bazaar merchants and guild members, clerics and seminary students, university students and teachers, Marxist and Islamic-socialist guerrilla organizations, liberal and nationalist politicians, oil workers and other strikers, the urban poor, and women of very different backgrounds. Khomeini's leadership gave the coalition a unifying symbol and a refusal to compromise with the monarchy. It did not give it agreement about what should follow.

Three disagreements ran through the coalition from the start. The first concerned the source of sovereignty. Liberal and nationalist figures understood the revolution as the restoration of constitutional, popularly sovereign government, with the 1906 constitution as their model. Khomeini and his supporters held that Islamic jurists should supervise the political order, an idea Chapter 93 examines as velayat-e faqih. The second concerned social and economic justice. Much of the left, and many Islamic radicals, understood the revolution as a redistribution of wealth and power, while the bazaar and propertied constituencies of the clerical movement wished to preserve private property and commerce. The third concerned the control of arms and institutions: who would command the weapons that had spread through the country, staff the ministries, and decide the cases of the former regime's officials.

The government formed in February 1979 expressed these tensions in its structure. Khomeini appointed Mehdi Bazargan prime minister on 5 February, before the monarchy had fallen, and Bazargan's cabinet held the formal ministries. Alongside it the Revolutionary Council issued decrees, and Khomeini's representatives, local committees, and revolutionary courts exercised authority that the cabinet neither controlled nor could easily contest. Bazargan later complained that he had been placed at the head of a government without the means to govern. His position was not weak because he lacked supporters; it was weak because the institutions that enforced decisions were answerable to other centers of power.

The question of legitimacy sharpened the divide. In the language of the time, authority could be claimed in at least three ways: from procedure, through elections, referenda, and constitutional rules; from popular mobilization, through the crowds that filled the streets; and from fidelity to the revolution as interpreted by its leader. The March 1979 referendum, in which voters answered a single question on whether Iran should become an Islamic Republic, was presented by supporters as an expression of popular will. Critics objected that it offered no alternative and no definition of the term. Khomeini himself described the revolution's legitimacy in terms of its Islamic character and the people's allegiance, and he treated criticism of the new order as obstruction of the revolution. This conception of revolutionary legitimacy, in which opposition could be portrayed as hostility to the revolution itself, gave ruling groups a powerful argument against organized dissent.

Violence and mobilization began to shape the competition at this early stage. Crowds of committed supporters, often described as hezbollahi, attacked meetings and newspapers of opponents. A small militant group called Forqan assassinated several prominent figures, including the cleric and theorist Morteza Motahhari in May 1979, and the death of Ayatollah Mahmud Taleghani in September removed a senior cleric who had been able to mediate between the clerical and lay factions. These events did not determine the outcome, but they narrowed the space for compromise, and they gave supporters of a harder line the arguments of security and revolutionary vigilance.

It is important not to treat the eventual outcome as inevitable. In February 1979 a different distribution of power was conceivable. Bazargan's government might have acquired control of the committees and courts, the senior clergy who differed with Khomeini might have mobilized their followers, the left or the Mojahedin might have created broader organizations, or regional movements might have secured recognized autonomy. That none did so reflects a combination of Khomeini's unmatched authority, the organizational advantages of his supporters, divisions among their rivals, and events beyond anyone's control, notably the hostage crisis and the war with Iraq. The distinction between revolutionary victory and political consolidation is therefore central: the first occurred in February 1979, and the second was the work of several years.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolutionary legitimacy

Definition: The claim that authority derives from loyalty to the aims of a revolution, rather than solely from legal procedure or election.

Historical context: In 1979 several rival claims to legitimacy coexisted in Iran. Ruling groups invoked the revolution to justify institutions, such as revolutionary courts, that did not fit established procedure, while their opponents invoked constitutional and electoral norms.

Why it matters: The concept explains why disputes over procedure were also disputes over who represented the revolution.

Potential misunderstanding: Revolutionary legitimacy is not simply a pretext. Millions of Iranians sincerely understood the revolution's leadership as the embodiment of the nation's will, and any account of consolidation must take that conviction seriously.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Anti-Monarchical Coalition to Competing Revolutionary Movements

What Changed

  • A shared objective, the removal of the monarchy, was replaced by contested objectives about sovereignty, economy, and the control of institutions.
  • Organizations that had cooperated in 1978 competed for ministries, committees, weapons, and the allegiance of workers and students.
  • Disagreements that had been deferred in the interest of unity became public and, increasingly, violent.

What Stayed the Same

  • Khomeini retained substantial revolutionary authority, although the distribution of public support cannot be measured precisely.
  • Religious networks of mosques, seminaries, and preachers continued to be the principal channel of mass mobilization.
  • The repertoire of mass demonstrations, strikes, and commemorations used against the monarchy continued to be used by every faction.

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested, and was experienced most directly by organizations excluded from state institutions. For many ordinary Iranians the continuity of revolutionary symbols and mobilization obscured how much the underlying coalition had changed.

  2. Why might shared symbols have masked the divisions within the coalition for as long as they did?

Section 95.2

Khomeini, the Islamic Republican Party, and the Clerical Political Network

Khomeini's supporters possessed what most rival movements lacked, an organization that already reached into every city and many villages. The Pahlavi state had curbed independent parties and unions, but it had left mosques, seminaries, and religious associations comparatively intact. During the revolution these institutions carried messages, raised funds, organized processions, and sheltered activists. The network of preachers and prayer leaders extended Khomeini's authority into daily life, and the bazaar, linked by credit, kinship, and religious patronage to the clerical establishment, supplied financing and a social base that other opponents could not easily match.

From this network emerged the Islamic Republican Party, announced in February 1979 by a group of clerics and lay activists close to Khomeini. Its principal organizers included Mohammad Beheshti, a cleric with a background in modernist religious education and long experience in Germany; Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, a cleric and organizer with extensive links to the bazaar and to the Tehran network; Ali Khamenei, then active in Khorasan and Tehran; Mohammad-Javad Bahonar; and Abdolkarim Mousavi-Ardebili. Many of them were associated with the Society of Combatant Clergy, which had been organized in the final years of the monarchy. Khomeini did not formally lead the party and did not claim to, and its leaders presented it as a vehicle for his line rather than as an independent political power.

The party's advantages were practical. It could mobilize the mosque network for elections and demonstrations, place its members in committees, courts, and ministries, and use revolutionary organizations to supply personnel and surveillance. Its daily newspaper reached a national audience, and its local branches coordinated Friday prayer leaders and activists. In the first Majles elections of 1980 it won a commanding position, assisted by a candidate-vetting process, the disqualification or intimidation of some rivals, and the abstention or boycott of several movements. These advantages were organizational rather than ideological: the party attracted people with different views on economic policy, and its internal factions later divided over land reform, foreign trade, and the role of the state in the economy.

It would be a mistake to equate the party with the clerical establishment as a whole, or the clerical establishment with the Shi'i tradition. Senior marjas held diverse positions. Kazem Shariatmadari in Qom favored a more limited role for jurists in government and was wary of the concentration of authority in a single leader. Hassan Tabataba'i-Qomi in Mashhad and other senior figures held reservations of their own, and the Najaf-based tradition associated with Abolqasem Khoei had long held that clerics should not govern directly. Mahmud Taleghani, a cleric of considerable popularity in Tehran, favored a more inclusive political order until his death in September 1979. The group that built the party and its allied institutions represented one current within a plural clergy, and its predominance was a political achievement as well as a religious one.

Khomeini's own role is also best understood in these terms. He possessed a unique authority, but he exercised it selectively, often by arbitrating among those around him rather than directly governing. His decisions, such as his approval of the prime ministers who succeeded Bazargan, his dismissal of Bani-Sadr, and his later interventions on the judiciary and the revolutionary bodies, generally favored the coalition of clerics and lay activists around the party, while preserving his position above any one faction. That position, standing above factions while ratifying the outcome of their conflicts, is a defining feature of the system that emerged.

Section 95.3

Liberal Constitutionalists, Nationalists, and Religious Opposition

The groups that favored constitutional and nationalist government had been indispensable to the revolution's victory and were among the first to lose their institutional position. Mehdi Bazargan, an engineer and long-time activist, led the Freedom Movement, which combined Islamic belief with commitment to constitutional liberties. Its supporters were drawn largely from the professional middle class and from devout intellectuals who believed that Islamic and democratic values were compatible. The National Front, the historic coalition of Mohammad Mossadegh's followers, appealed to secular nationalists, and its leaders, among them Karim Sanjabi, had endorsed the revolution but expected a constitutional order in which political parties would compete.

The Provisional Government's difficulties have been noted. Chapter 94 explains how the embassy seizure of 4 November 1979 and Khomeini's endorsement of it led Bazargan's government to resign on 6 November, and the present chapter does not repeat that narrative. What matters here is the pattern of the liberals' decline. They held offices but not the institutions that enforced decisions, they lacked a mass organization comparable to the party's, and they could be accused of insufficient revolutionary commitment, especially when they criticized revolutionary courts or committees. Their strongest arguments, about the rule of law and about the nation's right to choose its institutions, were not widely accepted by the crowds whose support mattered.

The liberal and nationalist camp did not disappear immediately. Bazargan remained a member of the first Majles and a critic of the government's treatment of prisoners and of the war's conduct, but his group's freedom of action shrank steadily. The National Front called for demonstrations and statements that the authorities treated as hostile, and in 1981 it was banned. Some of its leaders were arrested or forced into exile, while others withdrew from politics. What had been an influential current of the revolutionary coalition survived as a tolerated, sometimes harassed, critical voice and not as a competitor for power.

Dissent also came from within the clerical establishment. Kazem Shariatmadari, the leading marja among Azerbaijani Shi'is, objected to the provision of the draft constitution giving the supreme jurist broad powers, and his supporters in Tabriz founded the Muslim People's Republican Party in 1979. In December 1979 disturbances in Tabriz, which followed the referendum on the constitution, revealed that Khomeini's position was not unchallenged even among religious Azerbaijanis. The party was soon pressed to dissolve, and Shariatmadari's influence declined. His later treatment, discussed in Section 18, shows the narrowing space for religious criticism.

Liberal Islamic intellectuals formed a further stream. Figures associated with the writings of Ali Shariati, who had died in 1977 and had been popular among students, and with other reformist Muslim thinkers, advocated a vision of Islam as a liberating ideology against both clerical authority and Marxism. Many of them found themselves caught between a clerical establishment that distrusted lay interpreters of religion and leftist organizations that distrusted religious ideology. Some participated in the new institutions, particularly in education, while others were pushed to the margins.

These movements differed in how they understood constitutional sovereignty. For Bazargan, sovereignty belonged to the people acting through elected institutions within the limits of Islamic law. For the National Front, it was a secular constitutional principle. For Shariatmadari, the people's role was real, but the jurists' role should be advisory rather than supervisory. For the party and Khomeini's supporters, the jurist's guardianship was the structure within which popular participation took place. These disagreements, expressed in the Assembly of Experts of 1979 and in subsequent debates, were not trivial or merely tactical. They concerned the fundamental design of the state.

Section 95.4

The Revolutionary Left and the Struggle for Political Influence

Iran's revolutionary left was a collection of organizations with different histories, ideologies, and strategies, and it should never be treated as a single bloc. The two guerrilla organizations that had carried out armed struggle against the monarchy from the early 1970s, the Mojahedin-e Khalq and the Fedayan-e Khalq, had been decimated by SAVAK but retained a heroic image among young Iranians. Many of their members emerged from prison in the amnesties of late 1978 and early 1979. Their prestige and their young following made them important actors immediately after the revolution.

The Mojahedin-e Khalq, founded in 1965 by Muslim students of the Freedom Movement milieu, combined Islamic belief with a revolutionary reading of social conflict. After a violent internal split in the mid-1970s, a Marxist faction separated from the original organization, calling itself Peykar. The Islamic Mojahedin, led after 1979 by Massoud Rajavi, who had been released from prison, built a large student and urban youth following. It sought a role in the new state and condemned what it regarded as the monopolization of power by the clerical network. Section 15 follows its path to armed confrontation.

The Fedayan-e Khalq, a Marxist-Leninist guerrilla organization founded in 1971, emerged from the revolution with a large following among students and workers but divided over strategy. In 1980 it split into the Majority and the Minority. The Majority, led by Farrokh Negahdar, held that the Islamic Republic was an anti-imperialist and anti-capitalist regime that the left should support in order to push it toward socialism, and it moved toward cooperation with the government. The Minority regarded the regime as reactionary and continued to oppose it. The split illustrates the dilemma of the left: whether to resist an authoritarian movement that opposed the United States and rhetorically supported the poor, or to ally with it.

The Tudeh Party, Iran's oldest communist organization, had been banned since 1953 and had much of its leadership in exile. Returning from abroad in 1979, it followed a line that supported Khomeini as the leader of an anti-imperialist revolution, a policy reflecting Soviet foreign-policy interests and the party's long strategy of working with progressive clerical and nationalist forces. It endorsed the embassy seizure and the constitution, and it supported the government in several of its confrontations with the liberals. Its trade-union and press networks gave it some leverage, but it was an organization of long standing and few illusions about its fragility.

Other Marxist groups, including Peykar and many smaller organizations, and independent socialist intellectuals, pursued distinct paths. Some emphasized labor organization in factories, where workers' councils (shoras) had formed in the revolution's early months to manage or influence workplaces abandoned by owners. Student organizations were active on every campus, and their rallies, bookshops, and publications made the universities the principal arena of the left's public activity until the Cultural Revolution of 1980. The workers' councils, like many revolutionary bodies, were gradually placed under the oversight of government-appointed or Islamic associations. The left's difficulty was that its social base, students, some industrial workers, and urban intellectuals, was concentrated and visible, while the party's base reached into the countryside and the bazaar.

The left's decisions reflected its analysis of the revolution. Groups that concluded the new state was fundamentally progressive moved toward cooperation, and those that concluded it was reactionary moved toward opposition. Each judgment carried risks. Cooperation made the Tudeh and the Fedayan Majority vulnerable when the regime no longer needed them, as Section 18 shows. Opposition exposed the Mojahedin, the Minority, and Peykar to repression, and their relations with Kurdish and other regional movements further complicated their position. In both cases the left's inability to unify, and its reliance on organizations whose strategies were set partly by foreign or historical models, reduced its capacity to influence the new state.

How revolutionary organizations evolved into bodies able to exercise administrative, judicial, economic, and coercive authority.

Section 95.5

Revolutionary Committees and the Reconstruction of State Authority

The revolutionary committees, known in Persian as komitehs, were among the first institutions to fill the vacuum left by the monarchy's collapse. They had begun to form in neighborhoods and mosques during the final months of 1978, when local residents organized to distribute fuel, food, and kerosene during strikes, to guard districts against looting, and to coordinate demonstrations. In February 1979 they multiplied rapidly. Armed volunteers, many of them young men from the urban neighborhoods in which the revolution had been strongest, took over police stations and military depots, guarded public buildings, arrested former officials and suspected collaborators, and enforced order where the old police had vanished.

The committees were never a single organization at the beginning. Local bodies formed around mosque networks, factories, universities, and political groups, and they differed in leadership and style. Within weeks the clerical leadership sought to coordinate them. A central committee under the supervision of senior clerics, with Mohammad-Reza Mahdavi-Kani prominent among them, attempted to establish common rules, issue identification, and bring local units under a recognizable chain of authority. The committees were thereby partially converted from spontaneous bodies into a revolutionary security structure that overlapped with, and often superseded, the regular police and gendarmerie.

Their functions went well beyond patrol. They arrested persons accused of association with the monarchy, of drug dealing, prostitution, and gambling, and, increasingly, of opposition to the revolution. They enforced standards of public behavior, including restrictions on alcohol and mixed gatherings, long before such rules had been systematically legislated. They conducted searches of homes and offices, seized property, and brought detainees to the revolutionary courts, which in many cases accepted their evidence without independent verification. They also performed ordinary administrative tasks, such as distributing rationed goods, mediating neighborhood disputes, and registering residents, which gave them a role in everyday life that the older police had never had.

The committees' relations with other institutions were complicated. The Provisional Government considered them an obstacle to restoring the authority of its ministries, and Bazargan's government repeatedly sought their subordination to the Interior Ministry or the police. The clerical leadership considered them a necessary guarantee against counterrevolution and the army's possible resurgence. Mosques supplied their meeting places, their funds, and often their commanders, and clerical judges and prosecutors depended on their personnel. Because their members were typically devout young men from poor or lower-middle-class neighborhoods with a strong commitment to the revolution, they also served as a means of political integration, giving a group previously excluded from institutional power a stake in the new order.

The central problem of the committees was accountability. Their members were generally untrained, their authority was derived from revolutionary legitimacy rather than from statute, and their procedures were uneven. Complaints of arbitrary arrest, unauthorized searches, extortion, and violence reached the government, the press, and Khomeini himself, and they were a major theme of the Eight-Point Decree of December 1982, examined in Section 19. Efforts to rationalize the committees followed: some were merged with the police, some absorbed into the Revolutionary Guards, and in 1990 the committees, police, and gendarmerie were combined into a single law-enforcement force. That formal consolidation lay beyond this chapter's period, but it illustrates the movement from temporary revolutionary mobilization toward institutionalized security authority.

The historical significance of the committees thus lies in two things. They provided the new order with a coercive capacity that did not depend on the inherited state, and they gave it a decentralized presence in the daily life of every urban neighborhood and many villages. They also demonstrated the pattern by which revolutionary improvisations became permanent institutions: bodies that began as emergency measures acquired personnel, budgets, and constituencies with a stake in their continuation.

Section 95.6

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and the Basij

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, called in Persian the Sepah-e Pasdaran-e Enqelab-e Eslami or simply the Pasdaran, was created in spring 1979 by combining armed groups that had formed around the revolution's clerical leadership. Its establishment is generally dated to spring 1979, though the organization grew out of committees and armed cadres that predated the decree. Its founding members came from the Islamic currents of the revolution, including members of religious associations, former guerrilla fighters of Islamic orientation, and young volunteers from the committees. Its early commanders were appointed by the Revolutionary Council and approved by Khomeini.

The reason for creating a separate force lay in revolutionary distrust of the inherited military. The imperial army, air force, and navy had been closely tied to the monarchy, trained with American equipment and advisers, and commanded by officers who had sworn loyalty to the shah. The new leadership purged hundreds of senior officers and tried a number of them in the revolutionary courts, while the army's strength fell sharply through desertion and discharge. It also feared a coup, a fear reinforced by the memory of 1953 and by actual plots, notably the Nojeh conspiracy of July 1980, which led to the arrest and execution of many officers. A force with proven loyalty to the revolution, directly answerable to its leadership, seemed a necessary safeguard.

The coexistence of the Guards and the regular armed forces became a permanent structural feature. The regular army (Artesh) retained responsibility for territorial defense, while the Guards were charged with defending the revolution and its institutions. The constitution ratified in December 1979 recognized both, and its Article 150 confirmed the Guards' existence and their role in protecting the revolution and its achievements. The two forces had different recruitment, equipment, chains of command, and political reliability. Their later relationship, including competition for resources and strategy during the war, is taken up in Chapter 97, and the Guards' broader economic and political expansion in Chapter 105. For this chapter what matters is the fact of duplication and its political logic: the new state constructed an armed force parallel to the inherited one precisely because it did not entirely trust the latter.

The Guards were first used against domestic challenges. They took part in the suppression of regional rebellions, notably in Kurdistan in 1979, where they fought alongside army units and became identified in many Kurds' memory with the central government's coercion. They helped break the power of armed leftist organizations in the cities and countryside, and they provided personnel for the committees and for the protection of senior leaders. In the course of 1979 and 1980 the Guards developed a command structure and acquired public resources; separate ministerial arrangements followed later, and by the outbreak of the Iraq war they were a considerable force, though still smaller and less well equipped than the army.

The Basij was conceived as a still broader instrument of mobilization. Khomeini called in November 1979 for a popular force of twenty million, and the date is cited in Iranian sources as the origin of the Basij. The organization that came to bear that name was formed in stages. It developed in 1980 and 1981 as volunteers, many of them young men and teenagers from rural and poorer urban areas, were organized in mosque-based units under Guards supervision, and its legal and institutional framework was clarified in later years. The chronology therefore needs care: the call for mass mobilization came in 1979, but the structure usually associated with the wartime Basij was a product of the war and the early 1980s. The Basij's later role in battlefield mobilization belongs to Chapter 97.

The institutional significance of these bodies for domestic consolidation was twofold. First, they gave the new regime coercive capacity that was ideologically committed and answerable to revolutionary leaders, which reduced its dependence on a bureaucracy and army inherited from the monarchy. Second, they provided channels for political socialization and advancement. Participation in the Guards or Basij offered young men from modest backgrounds status, salary, and connections, and created a constituency of beneficiaries with a stake in the survival of the order. The changing relationship between political authority and coercive capacity, in which institutions created to defend the revolution acquired their own interests and influence, would be a defining feature of the Islamic Republic.

The legal consolidation of the two organizations proceeded at different speeds. The Revolutionary Council adopted legislation establishing the National Basij on 10 July 1980, after the initial call for mobilization in November 1979. Legislation in January 1981 incorporated the National Basij into the Guards. These measures distinguish a public appeal for collective defense from the administrative creation of a body with assigned responsibilities. The parliamentary statute of September 1982 further established the Guards as an organization with defined duties. Read the Basij incorporation legislation alongside the 1982 Guards statute. Neither document should be read as evidence that every local unit immediately operated in accordance with the new rules. In a revolutionary state, legislation could regularize existing organizations, assign new responsibilities, and resolve competing institutional claims at the same time. Article 150 of the original constitution recognized the Guards but left the delineation of their duties and relationship with other armed forces to legislation. The subsequent statute therefore matters as evidence of the conversion of revolutionary authority into an administrative organization, rather than merely as another founding date.

Section 95.7

Revolutionary Courts, Islamic Justice, and Legal Transformation

The revolutionary courts were established within days of the monarchy's fall to try officials of the old regime, and they became the most controversial institutions of the early republic. Their creation reflected a widespread demand, rooted in the decades of SAVAK torture and political killing, that those responsible for violence be held to account. The courts' judges were clerics and lay Islamic jurists appointed by revolutionary authority. They sat in prisons, schools, and other improvised venues, with armed guards and crowds outside, and they operated on the premise that the revolution itself created a special jurisdiction.

The first trials took place in February 1979 and were quick. In mid-February four senior generals, including the former head of SAVAK Nematollah Nassiri, were tried and executed within hours of the verdict on a rooftop in Tehran, and the photographs were published. Former prime minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda was tried and executed in April. Sadeq Khalkhali, a cleric with a reputation for severity, presided over many of these proceedings and became publicly identified with them. Later in 1979 the courts' jurisdiction broadened to include economic crimes, drug offenses, alleged corruption, and, increasingly, political opposition. By then several hundred officials had been executed, according to contemporary reports, though totals differ by source.

Defendants were generally charged under broad Islamic categories such as "corruption on earth" (efsad fi'l-arz) and "war against God" (moharebeh), which could be applied to acts ranging from torture and killing to alleged complicity in the monarchy's rule. Revolutionary interpretations of criminal responsibility treated association with the old regime's institutions as potentially punishable, and the courts' reasoning rested on a mixture of Islamic law, revolutionary decrees, and the claim that the revolution justified exceptional measures. Procedural safeguards were limited. Trials often lasted minutes, defendants frequently had no access to independent counsel, evidence could be accepted on the word of committee members or witnesses without cross-examination, and appeals were restricted or unavailable.

International observers noted these features early. Amnesty International and other organizations raised concerns in 1979 about summary trials, the denial of defense counsel, and the application of the death penalty, and some members of the Provisional Government, including Bazargan himself, protested the courts' conduct and sought to restrain them. Domestic objections also arose. Some clerics questioned whether the procedures conformed to Islamic law, which required demanding standards of evidence. The courts continued to operate, and Khomeini supported them as an expression of the revolution's justice while at times urging moderation in particular cases.

The relationship between revolutionary and regular courts was unsettled. The old judiciary had been disrupted by the dismissal of many judges and the abolition or reform of existing institutions, and Islamic jurists gradually assumed the leading roles. Mohammad Beheshti, as head of the Supreme Court, was central to this transformation. The 1979 constitution provided in principle for a judiciary guided by Islamic law, for the right to counsel, and for protections against arbitrary arrest and torture, among other guarantees. In practice the revolutionary courts continued to operate under their own rules for much of the early 1980s, and criminal law was progressively reshaped by legislation on retributive and discretionary punishments adopted by the Majles in the early 1980s. Distinguishing between the revolutionary courts and the entire judicial system is essential: most ordinary civil and criminal litigation continued in regular courts, but the revolutionary tribunals handled the politically sensitive cases that most affected the regime's opponents.

The courts' historical significance lies in how they combined punishment of real abuses with the creation of an apparatus that could be turned against new opponents. Many Iranians regarded the trials of SAVAK torturers as just, and the legal rationale for accountability for state crimes has parallels in many revolutions. But the courts' procedures, their openness to political direction, and their use against opponents other than members of the old regime weakened the distinction between punishment of the old order and suppression of the new opposition. The transition from one to the other, visible by 1980, was a key mechanism of coercive consolidation.

The original 1979 Constitution provides a concrete standard against which the courts can be assessed. Articles 32 and 34 address lawful arrest and access to courts; Article 35 provides for counsel; Article 36 places sentencing within the jurisdiction of competent courts acting under law; Article 37 establishes the presumption of innocence; Articles 38 and 39 prohibit coercion to obtain testimony and protect the dignity of detainees. These guarantees are not identical to a complete criminal procedure code, but they establish obligations that revolutionary enthusiasm could not by itself satisfy. Amnesty International examined revolutionary tribunal procedures in May 1980 and continued documenting failures of safeguards in subsequent reports. The historical question is not whether all defendants were innocent, nor whether officials of the former regime should have been immune from prosecution. It is whether individual responsibility was established through a procedure capable of testing allegations and protecting defendants against arbitrary punishment. The difference matters equally in cases involving former regime officials and opponents of the new government.

Section 95.8

Confiscation, Revolutionary Foundations, and Administrative Consolidation

The revolution transferred a vast quantity of property to new institutions. The monarchy's ruling circle had accumulated enormous holdings, held through the Pahlavi Foundation and through the personal wealth of the royal family, aristocratic families, industrialists, bankers, and officials. In 1979 the new authorities froze, confiscated, or placed under public control the assets of the former royal family and of many individuals associated with the old order, often by lists published by the Revolutionary Council. Article 49 of the constitution later provided the legal basis for confiscating wealth derived from illicit sources such as usury, bribery, and usurpation.

Confiscation must be distinguished from nationalization and from taxation. Nationalization meant the transfer of an economic sector to state ownership under a general law. In June and July 1979 the Revolutionary Council nationalized banks, insurance companies, and large industries, partly because many owners had fled and many enterprises were deeply indebted. Confiscation, by contrast, was the seizure of the property of named persons or categories of persons for reasons of alleged illegitimacy or political hostility. Both created a large public and quasi-public sector. The government's capacity to administer it was limited, however, and some property fell into the control of revolutionary foundations rather than ministries.

The foundations, or bonyads, were the characteristic institutions of this process. The Foundation of the Oppressed (Bonyad-e Mostazafan) was established in 1979 to take over the assets of the Pahlavi Foundation and other confiscated holdings, and it came to administer hundreds of enterprises, agricultural lands, and real estate. Other bodies followed: the Imam Khomeini Relief Committee, which provided assistance to poor families, the Housing Foundation, the Martyrs' Foundation, which cared for families of those killed in the revolution and later the war, and the Reconstruction Jihad (Jahad-e Sazandegi), created in 1979 to send volunteers to build roads, schools, clinics, and water systems in villages. Each was formally outside the regular ministries and answered to the leadership.

These institutions served several purposes. They gave the new state resources that could be used for welfare, patronage, and political mobilization. They extended the state into rural areas that the monarchy had largely neglected, where the Reconstruction Jihad's schools, electrification, and road-building won real support and created a generation of activists. They also employed large numbers of people, many of whom owed their positions to the revolution. Public-sector appointments, in the ministries and in the foundations alike, were used to place loyalists in office, and a system of ideological and political screening for employment, known as gozinesh, was applied to many positions.

The foundations' status raised unresolved questions. They were exempt from many regulations and taxes, their accounts were not fully transparent, and their relationship to the government was ambiguous. Critics within the revolution, including liberals and some bazaar traders who resented confiscations, argued that they created a parallel state outside legal control. Supporters answered that they delivered services the ministries could not. The tension between formal government and revolutionary institutions, which Bazargan had faced in the committees and courts, thus reappeared in the economy. Their later growth, including their role in the economy of the late twentieth century, is discussed in Chapter 105.

Economic redistribution and institutional legitimacy were closely linked. Welfare programs, rural development, subsidized goods, and housing programs distributed tangible benefits to millions, and the distinction between the beneficiaries of the revolution and its opponents was important to the new order's political base. At the same time, confiscation inflicted real losses on property owners, many of whom left Iran or lost their assets, and the political selection of those targeted raised concerns about arbitrariness. Land reform, which many radicals wished to extend, was blocked by objections from conservative jurists and the Council of Guardians on the ground that it violated property rights in Islamic law, a dispute that illustrates the internal limits of redistribution.

Article 49 supplied a constitutional basis for recovering wealth acquired through specified unlawful means. It did not establish that every asset formerly held by a supporter of the monarchy was automatically unlawful property. The legal question of ownership and the administrative question of who would manage confiscated assets were distinct. Foundations could receive assets without becoming ordinary ministries, and their operations did not necessarily follow the same budgetary or reporting arrangements as the civil service. Nationalization, in contrast, changed ownership of enterprises through public legislation and need not depend upon an individualized finding of unlawful acquisition. This distinction helps explain why the revolutionary economy combined conventional government departments, nationalized enterprises, private commerce and foundations with special institutional relationships to the leadership. The result was not a uniform command economy but an overlapping system of resource allocation.

Regional Resistance and the Transformation of Society

How the new political order confronted regional autonomy movements, religious diversity, educational institutions, and competing visions of everyday life.

Section 95.9

Kurdistan and the Conflict over Regional Autonomy

Iranian Kurdistan, a mountainous region in the northwest and west with a population speaking Kurdish dialects and including both Sunni and Shi'i communities, was the first region to confront the revolutionary government militarily. Kurdish political organization had a long history. The Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan, founded in 1945 and associated with the short-lived Republic of Mahabad in 1946, had survived decades of repression, and by 1979 its leader Abdul Rahman Ghassemlou, a scholar trained in Europe, had returned from exile. A rival organization, Komala, was Marxist and drew on younger and more radical activists. Kurdish clerics, tribal leaders, and local notables also held varying positions.

In the weeks after the monarchy's fall, Kurdish towns expelled or disarmed the old gendarmerie and established local councils and armed forces. Kurdish representatives put forward demands for recognition of Kurdish language and culture, local administration, a share of revenues, and representation in the national government. The Democratic Party's program spoke of autonomy within Iran, not independence, and Ghassemlou repeatedly stated that Kurds sought a democratic Iran with autonomous provinces. The distinction between autonomy and independence is crucial: the government nevertheless tended to treat the demands as separatist, and some Kurdish voices indeed went further than the party's stated position.

Negotiations began in early 1979 and failed. The revolutionary leadership held that the Islamic Republic was a unitary state whose unity was a religious as well as national obligation, and the draft constitution reserved no autonomous status for provinces. Kurdish representatives boycotted the March referendum in some places and later the elections to the Assembly of Experts, although Ghassemlou was elected to that body and did not take his seat. Clashes in Sanandaj, Mahabad, Paveh, and other towns in the spring and summer of 1979 escalated. In August, Khomeini declared the party's leaders enemies and ordered the army to act, and the Guards and army units, supported by helicopters and artillery, retook towns in the following months.

The revolutionary courts followed the troops. Khalkhali presided over a court that tried and executed Kurdish prisoners in a number of towns in late August 1979, and a photograph of one such execution became widely known abroad. The campaign's victims included combatants, but also civilians caught in shelling and reprisals, and there were reports of village destruction and displacement. Government accounts emphasized the defeat of separatists and counterrevolutionaries, while Kurdish sources emphasized the killing of civilians and the denial of rights. Reliable casualty figures are lacking, and estimates differ widely.

Fighting continued through 1980, when the outbreak of war with Iraq complicated the situation, since Baghdad sought to exploit Kurdish opposition. By 1981 the government had regained control of the major towns, and the Democratic Party and Komala withdrew into the mountains and neighboring countries, where they continued guerrilla opposition for years. Persistent Kurdish political opposition reflected the unresolved questions of recognition and rights, as well as the experience of repression. Ghassemlou himself would be assassinated in Vienna in 1989, an event that lies beyond this chapter.

Kurds were not politically homogeneous. Some, including many Shi'i Kurds in Kermanshah and Ilam, identified with the revolutionary government, and Kurdish clerics and notables in some areas cooperated with it. Sunni Kurds had distinct concerns about the new regime's Shi'i character. Tribal rivalries, class tensions between landlords and peasants, and differences between the Democratic Party and Komala further divided Kurdish society, and the government exploited some of these divisions. The conflict was therefore not simply between Kurds and Tehran, but a contest involving multiple Kurdish actors with different goals.

Section 95.10

Regional Movements, Religious Minorities, and National Integration

Kurdistan was the most violent regional conflict, but not the only one. Each region and community had a distinct relation to the new state, and understanding them requires separating ethnic identity, language, religion, regional autonomy, and political opposition, which overlapped but were not the same. A Turkmen could be Sunni and Persian-speaking in daily affairs, an Azerbaijani Shi'i might support Khomeini enthusiastically while resenting provincial neglect, and an Arab of Khuzestan might be Shi'i and a loyal citizen yet demand cultural recognition.

In Khuzestan, the oil-producing southwest, Arab communities, who form a significant share of the population, raised demands for cultural rights, local employment in the oil industry, and some degree of administrative autonomy. Their principal figure, the cleric Mohammad-Taher Shobeir Khaqani, led a movement in Khorramshahr and Ahvaz, and in May 1979 clashes occurred in which both Arab and non-Arab residents were killed. The government, concerned with the strategic importance of the oilfields and the border with Iraq, responded with force, with Guards and army units entering the province, and Khalkhali's court again made its appearance. These tensions later acquired a different meaning when Iraq invaded in September 1980 in the expectation, which proved largely mistaken, that Khuzestan's Arabs would welcome it.

In the northeast, the Turkmen of Turkmen Sahra, a Sunni, largely agricultural population near the Caspian and the border with the Soviet Union, formed local councils in early 1979 to reclaim lands and manage local affairs. Leftist activists were prominent in these councils, and in March 1979 violent clashes in Gonbad-e Kavus involving Turkmen councils, leftist groups, and the Guards left dead on all sides. The government regarded the councils as unauthorized and suppressed them, and many Turkmen leaders were arrested or killed. In Baluchistan, a poor and largely Sunni region in the southeast, tribal and religious leaders, including Sunni religious leaders, sought greater control over local affairs and protested the new Shi'i-oriented institutions, and fighting with government forces and Guards occurred in 1979 and 1980.

In Azerbaijan the situation was different. The Azerbaijani Turkish-speaking population, probably the largest minority, was closely integrated into Iran's religious and political life, and many Azerbaijanis had held high offices. The disturbances of December 1979 in Tabriz, linked to Shariatmadari and the Muslim People's Republican Party, were more a political and religious dispute over the constitution than a movement for regional autonomy, though regional grievances over language and neglect played a part. The government suppressed them, using the Guards and the party's supporters, and Shariatmadari's party was dissolved.

The fate of religious minorities followed different patterns again. The constitution recognized Zoroastrians, Jews, and Christians as protected religious minorities, with reserved parliamentary seats, and permitted them to follow their own personal-status laws. Nonetheless they faced pressure: the execution in May 1979 of the prominent Jewish businessman Habib Elghanian on charges that included contact with Israel alarmed the Jewish community and prompted emigration. Christians, including Armenians and Assyrians, retained schools and churches but faced restrictions on public activity and, in the case of converts, greater risk. Sunni Muslims, though not a minority in the constitution's terms, experienced the Shi'i character of the state, and some of their clerics and organizations voiced grievances, a subject that continued to shape politics.

The Baha'i community, the largest non-Muslim religious group in Iran, occupied a distinct and especially vulnerable position, since its faith is not recognized by the constitution and had long been denounced by many clerics as a heresy. Baha'is were dismissed from public employment and universities, their property was confiscated, their institutions were closed, and their cemeteries and holy places were seized. In August 1980 members of their national governing body disappeared, and members of its successor body were executed in December 1981. Documentation by human-rights organizations records executions, imprisonments, and discrimination in the early 1980s. Their persecution was based on religious identity rather than on political action, and should therefore be distinguished from the suppression of political opposition.

The persecution of the Baháʼí community illustrates the distinction between political opposition and religious identity. According to the Iran Human Rights Documentation Center’s study of Baháʼí persecution, members of the successor National Spiritual Assembly were arrested on 13 December 1981 and eight were executed on 27 December. The record of these events must be distinguished from the disappearance of the preceding assembly in August 1980. Article 13 recognized Zoroastrians, Jews and Christians as religious minorities; Baháʼís were not included. Sunni Muslims, by contrast, fell within the framework for Islamic schools of jurisprudence in Article 12. These legal distinctions shaped access to institutions but do not exhaust the history of discrimination or the diversity of political attitudes within each community.

Section 95.11

The Cultural Revolution and the Islamization of Education

Universities were the principal arena of the left and of the Mojahedin, and their transformation was among the central projects of the new regime. After 1979 the campuses were politically intense: student organizations held rallies and maintained offices and bookstores, and ideological debate was open and sometimes violent. Many students and teachers had been active in the revolution, and the leftist and Mojahedin groups regarded the universities as bases from which to contest the direction of the state. Supporters of Khomeini regarded this as a threat, and as proof that Iranian education remained dominated by secular, Western, or Marxist ideas.

Khomeini called for an "Islamic cultural revolution" in 1980, and the campus confrontations of April that year provided the occasion. Crowds of Islamist supporters, with the participation of members of the committees and Guards, attacked the campus offices of leftist and Mojahedin organizations in several cities in the days around 18 April, in some cases leading to deaths and many injuries. Shortly afterward the government announced that universities would close in order to be restructured. They remained closed for roughly two and a half years, with partial and phased reopenings beginning in 1982 and continuing into 1983.

A body called the Cultural Revolution Headquarters, formed in 1980, directed the process. Its members, whose composition changed, included clerics and Islamic intellectuals, among them Abdolkarim Soroush, who would later become one of the Islamic Republic's most prominent critics. The headquarters planned the purging of faculty, the revision of curricula, and the selection of students. This body should be distinguished from the Supreme Council of the Cultural Revolution, which was established under that name in 1984 as a permanent institution and which took over its functions. The earlier headquarters was an emergency body, while the later council was a standing component of the state's structure.

The purges affected faculty and students widely. Instructors regarded as secular, leftist, or associated with the monarchy were dismissed, retired, or excluded from reappointment, and many fled abroad. Students affiliated with opposition organizations were expelled or denied readmission, and admission criteria were revised to include ideological and political qualifications, such as commitment to the Islamic Republic, with preferences for the families of those killed in the revolution and the war. Estimates of the numbers dismissed or excluded vary widely, and available figures should be treated with caution. Curricula in the humanities and social sciences were reviewed, textbooks revised, and new subjects in Islamic thought introduced, while courses and fields seen as bearing Western ideological assumptions were reexamined.

The Cultural Revolution had several aims, some of which pointed in different directions. It was an ideological project to align education with a particular interpretation of Islam and to reduce Western and Marxist influence. It was a political project to remove organized opposition from campuses. And it was an institutional project to place universities under the supervision of the new state and its loyalists, replacing inherited autonomy and elite networks. Educational planners also hoped to expand access, and over time the number of students and institutions increased substantially, including opportunities for women and for young people from provincial and religious backgrounds who had been poorly represented under the monarchy.

The costs were substantial. The loss of experienced teachers and researchers, the interruption of study for many students, and the emigration of professionals reduced the universities' capacity for years, and they became an enduring subject of debate in Iran. Supporters argued that the changes opened universities to previously excluded social groups and freed education from foreign dependence. Critics argued that political screening damaged scholarship and academic freedom. Both views describe aspects of a process that transformed higher education and that illustrates the chapter's theme of ideological consolidation.

The chronology of educational restructuring also distinguishes closure from institutional redesign. In April 1980 the Revolutionary Council demanded the removal of political organizations from campuses. Khomeini established the Cultural Revolution Headquarters in June 1980; Encyclopaedia Iranica’s biography of Khomeini dates the decree to 12 June, while other chronologies use 13 June. Universities resumed instruction in stages in 1982–1983. The later reorganization in December 1984 is outside the principal period of this chapter. Screening staff and students, revising curricula and assigning administrative authority were separate tasks, and reopening did not restore the previous degree of political autonomy.

Section 95.12

Women, Social Regulation, and the Enforcement of Revolutionary Morality

Women took part in the revolution in great numbers and from every political and religious position. Many wore the chador in the mass processions of 1978, some as a religious statement and others as a sign of solidarity with the opposition, while secular and leftist women also marched, organized, and wrote. The revolution's leaders encouraged their participation, and many women understood the movement as a promise of dignity and justice. The Pahlavi state had given women the vote in 1963 and reformed family law in 1967 and 1975, but those reforms were associated with an authoritarian regime and criticized by many religious women as superficial or as elite measures.

The conflict over women's status began within weeks of the revolution's victory. Khomeini's statements in early March 1979 that women should observe the Islamic dress code in government offices were followed by demonstrations on International Women's Day, 8 March, in which thousands of women, with male supporters, protested in Tehran against the prospect of compulsory dress and the loss of rights. They were met with hostile crowds and some violence, and officials issued clarifications that the instruction was a recommendation. The demonstrations showed both the strength of opposition among a segment of urban women and the limits of its effect on the institutions that were being formed.

Legal change occurred in stages. The Family Protection Laws of 1967 and 1975, which had restricted polygamy, regulated divorce, and given women greater standing in family courts, were suspended in 1979 and replaced by interpretations based on traditional jurisprudence. Women were barred from serving as judges and dismissed from judicial posts, several fields of university study were restricted for women, and the minimum age of marriage was reduced. Compulsory hijab was likewise introduced gradually, not by a single February decree: it was required in government offices in 1980, enforced increasingly by committees, Guards, and moral-order patrols in public spaces, and given formal penal sanction in legislation adopted in 1983, which provided punishments for women appearing in public without proper covering.

The new regime also regulated cultural expression more broadly. Cinemas were closed or restricted, music was restricted, alcohol was banned, and the press was supervised. Public behavior, such as mixed gatherings, dress, and association between unrelated men and women, was subject to enforcement by committees and, later, by organized bodies. Censorship of books, films, and publications expanded, and the broadcast media became an instrument of ideological education. These policies reflected the view of many leaders that Iranian society had been corrupted by Western influence under the monarchy and required moral reconstruction.

Women's responses varied. Some, particularly among the religiously committed, accepted and supported the new order, and organizations such as the Society of Women of the Islamic Revolution, associated with Azam Taleghani, daughter of Ayatollah Taleghani, and the women who entered the Majles argued for a distinctive Islamic model of women's rights, emphasizing education, health, and political participation within a religious framework. Girls' literacy and school enrollment grew significantly in the following decades, especially in rural areas, where religious families were more willing to send daughters to a school under Islamic regulation. Other women, secular, leftist, and some religious, opposed the new restrictions, organized protests and publications, and in many cases were arrested, dismissed, or driven to emigrate. Many more adapted: accepting required dress while pressing for broader opportunities, finding spaces for education and professional work, and negotiating the boundaries of the permitted.

The chapter distinguishes between religious observance undertaken voluntarily and requirements enforced by state institutions. Millions of Iranian women wore the hijab out of belief or custom before and after the revolution, and compulsion changed the meaning of the practice for those who had not chosen it, and, for some, for those who had. Women who supported the Islamic Republic and those who opposed its social policies were both Iranian women with legitimate political voices, and their disagreement was part of the revolution's history rather than a departure from it.

The sequence of dress regulation illustrates the difference between exhortation, administrative enforcement and criminal legislation. The demonstrations of March 1979 followed public demands for Islamic dress; restrictions in government workplaces became more systematic in 1980, and public enforcement expanded thereafter. In 1983 penal legislation imposed penalties for appearing in public without Islamic hijab. The Encyclopaedia Iranica treatment of feminist movements provides historical context for these developments. Women were not merely the recipients of these policies: they also organized, debated, pursued education and employment, and participated in competing political and religious movements. The legal chronology cannot establish the uniformity of enforcement in every locality.

Section 95.13

Bani-Sadr and the Struggle Between Presidency and Parliament

Abolhassan Bani-Sadr, an economist and writer who had been close to Khomeini in Paris in 1978, won the presidential election of 25 January 1980 with roughly three-quarters of the votes cast. His victory reflected his prominence, his reputation as an independent Islamic thinker, and the support of voters who wished to counterbalance the clerical party, which had not endorsed him. Khomeini, who had initially been reluctant to see a layperson as president, gave him additional authority, including chairmanship of the Revolutionary Council and, in February 1980, the post of commander-in-chief of the armed forces. On paper he held the most powerful elected office in the republic.

His institutional base was weak, however. He had no party, and his supporters, many of them intellectuals, technocrats, and members of the liberal and Mojahedin milieu, held few positions in the committees, courts, Guards, or foundations. His rivals in the Islamic Republican Party controlled the judiciary through Beheshti, the party apparatus, and later the Majles. In the parliamentary elections held in two rounds in March and May 1980, the party and its allies won a dominant position, helped by organization, by the exclusion or withdrawal of some rivals, and by the disqualification and harassment of opposing candidates. The first Majles convened in late May 1980, with Rafsanjani as speaker, and it became the party's principal institutional platform.

The first major struggle concerned the cabinet. The constitution required the Majles to approve the prime minister and ministers, and the president wished to nominate people on whom he could rely. Several of his candidates were rejected, and the Majles favored Mohammad-Ali Rajai, a former schoolteacher and party activist who had served as minister of education. Khomeini supported the Majles's position and pressed Bani-Sadr to accept Rajai, who was confirmed as prime minister in August 1980; his cabinet was approved in early September after further disputes over ministers. Bani-Sadr, who regarded Rajai as inexperienced and sectarian, resented the outcome, and from then the president and the government worked in effective opposition.

The disagreement was institutional as well as personal. The constitution gave the president responsibility for implementing the constitution and for coordinating the executive, but it also created a prime minister responsible to the Majles, a Leader with ultimate authority, and a judiciary independent of the president. Chapter 93 explained that this design left unclear how far a president could direct a government whose head and ministers were chosen by parliament. Bani-Sadr argued that his electoral mandate entitled him to direct policy, while the party argued that the parliament, representing the revolutionary forces, was the proper source of government. Both could point to constitutional text, and the dispute therefore turned on political power.

The war complicated matters. As commander-in-chief, Bani-Sadr visited the front, advocated reliance on the regular army, and criticized the Guards, whose political loyalties and relative autonomy he distrusted. His opponents accused him of mismanaging the war, and a failed offensive in January 1981 gave them a target; Chapter 97 treats the military events. Bani-Sadr in turn accused the party's supporters of obstructing the army and monopolizing power, and he used his newspaper to denounce the revolutionary courts' abuses, the censorship of the press, and the violence of crowds against his supporters. Such criticism won him sympathy among liberals, leftists, and the Mojahedin, but it intensified the party's view that he was a threat.

Khomeini attempted to manage the conflict, as he did others. He urged both camps to end their public quarrels, established arbitration arrangements, and on several occasions rebuked the president and the party alike. But the balance of his interventions shifted. By early 1981, Khomeini's comments increasingly criticized Bani-Sadr's behavior and the alliances he had formed with opposition groups, which he regarded as dangerous in wartime. Bani-Sadr found himself isolated, with an electoral mandate but without institutions, supporters in the street whom the authorities could suppress, and a Leader who no longer shielded him. The conflict thus displayed several sources of authority, presidential, parliamentary, and revolutionary, and showed which of them counted most.

Section 95.14

The Impeachment and Removal of President Bani-Sadr

The crisis came to a head in June 1981. Chapter 95 distinguishes among four developments, which are sometimes compressed into a single event. First, in June 1981, Khomeini removed Bani-Sadr from his delegated position as commander-in-chief. Second, on 21 June the Majles voted that the president lacked the political competence required for the office. Third, on 22 June Khomeini issued a decree dismissing him. Fourth, after a period in hiding, Bani-Sadr left Iran in July for exile in France. The parliamentary determination and the dismissal decree are distinct constitutional actions, and the term impeachment, though widely used, does not describe either precisely.

The sequence had a political background. In the spring of 1981, tensions increased as Bani-Sadr appealed for support to the Mojahedin, to liberals, and to public opinion, and as the party's supporters attacked his rallies. Khomeini's statements grew more critical, and in early June he removed Bani-Sadr from command of the armed forces. Bani-Sadr, who had lost his principal institutional power, went into hiding. The Majles took up the question of his conduct, and the case against him focused on alleged violations of the constitution, defiance of the Leader, and political incompetence. The Majles assembled for the vote in a context of crowds and street mobilization, and a mass demonstration by the Mojahedin on 20 June, which security forces met with gunfire and mass arrests, set the stage.

On 21 June 1981 the Majles determined that Bani-Sadr lacked political competence: of 190 participants, 177 supported the finding, one opposed it and twelve cast neutral votes. The original 1979 Constitution, Article 110(5), empowered the Leader to dismiss the president following specified judicial or parliamentary findings. Parliament developed the procedure through legislation in June 1981. Article 89 as originally adopted principally concerned interpellation of ministers; the explicit presidential interpellation provisions of the amended 1989 text must not be read backward into this event. The parliamentary finding did not itself remove Bani-Sadr. On 22 June Khomeini issued the formal dismissal decree. Both the parliamentary notification and dismissal are reproduced by the Iran Data Portal. Rajai won the presidential election on 24 July.

Bani-Sadr remained in hiding for approximately five weeks, protected by supporters of the Mojahedin and other opponents. At the end of July he left Iran aboard an air force aircraft with Massoud Rajavi, an escape that was reported at the time as a considerable embarrassment to the government, and they arrived in France. In exile the two announced an opposition coalition, whose later history, and Bani-Sadr's eventual break with Rajavi, are beyond this chapter's scope. Bani-Sadr's name became attached, in the regime's rhetoric, to the labels of traitor and liberal, and in opposition circles to the claim that the revolution had been taken over by a faction.

The episode demonstrated the limits of independently exercised presidential authority. Bani-Sadr's mandate, formally the strongest in the republic, did not protect him once he lost the Leader's backing and the support of the institutions that mattered. His removal also showed how the system's pieces fitted together. The Majles supplied the constitutional procedure, the Leader the final decision, the party and its allied institutions the organizational force, and the street the demonstration of support. Later presidents in the Islamic Republic would operate in awareness of this precedent, and the question of the relationship between the elected president and unelected institutions would recur in Iranian politics.

Historians have differed on how to interpret the episode. Some emphasize Bani-Sadr's own political mistakes: his tendency to confront rivals, to rely on rhetoric over organization, and to associate with groups the regime was prepared to crush. Others emphasize the party's determination to monopolize power and its willingness to use extra-institutional pressure. A further view treats the episode as a conflict within the revolution's social base, between a technocratic and liberal Islamic current and a clerical and populist one. These interpretations are not exclusive, and each captures part of a process in which constitutional rules, political strategy, and coercive capacity operated together.

Section 95.15

The Mojahedin-e Khalq and the Turn Toward Armed Confrontation

The Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK), founded in 1965, had a distinctive ideology that combined a revolutionary interpretation of Islam with ideas of class struggle. Its members had fought the monarchy, suffered severe repression, and lost much of their leadership to executions in the 1970s, which gave the organization a martyr's prestige. Massoud Rajavi, who emerged from prison in January 1979, rebuilt it quickly as a mass organization with offices, a newspaper, and a following that reached hundreds of thousands, especially among students and urban youth.

The organization's relations with Khomeini's supporters were uneasy from the beginning. The Mojahedin declined to endorse the constitution in December 1979, objecting to the principle of clerical guardianship, and they were critical of the committees and courts. In the presidential election of January 1980, Rajavi was told that those who had not voted for the constitution could not stand, and he withdrew. In the parliamentary elections of 1980 the organization's candidates won no seats, and the group alleged fraud and intimidation. Its newspaper and offices were attacked by crowds, and its supporters were beaten and arrested. At the same time the organization continued to hold large rallies, and in 1981 it moved closer to Bani-Sadr, whose supporters it defended.

Khomeini and the party's leaders increasingly described the Mojahedin as hypocrites (monafeqin), a religious term meaning those who profess faith but act against it, and as counterrevolutionaries. The accusation drew on the organization's doctrinal heterodoxy, its ties to leftist circles, its refusal of clerical authority, and, during the war, its willingness to criticize the government. This language had practical consequences, since it placed members and supporters outside the legitimate community and made the use of force against them justifiable in the eyes of many supporters of the regime. A legal political opposition, with a large legal following, was thus being recast as an enemy of the revolution.

On 20 June 1981, the day before the Majles vote on Bani-Sadr, the Mojahedin called a large demonstration in Tehran. Security forces and Guards fired on the crowd, and reports at the time described dozens of deaths and many hundreds of arrests. Executions of arrested participants began within days. Rajavi declared that the organization would pursue armed resistance, and its operatives began a campaign of assassinations and bombings against officials and supporters of the regime. The relevant distinction is between legal political opposition and demonstrations, which the state restricted and sometimes met with lethal force, and the armed campaign that the organization chose after the events of June. Both occurred, and they should not be conflated.

The consequences for members and supporters were severe. Thousands of Mojahedin members and sympathizers were arrested in the second half of 1981 and the following years, and many were executed, often after brief trials. Among those detained or killed were teenagers and relatives of members who were not themselves active. At the same time, Mojahedin attacks killed senior officials, clerics, judges, and also civilians, including bystanders and young party supporters, and the organization's operations became a central justification for the state's security measures. Its leaders later moved to Paris and, in 1986, to Iraq, a decision that cost the organization much of its domestic credibility during the war and that is discussed in later chapters.

The narrative must therefore identify and assess violence committed by both state and opposition actors without treating their legal obligations or institutional powers as identical. A state is bound by legal and human-rights obligations that do not bind a clandestine organization in the same way, and it has far greater power to harm. Its use of lethal force against crowds, mass executions after summary trials, and torture of detainees cannot be justified by the opposition's violence. Equally, the Mojahedin's assassinations and bombings, which killed many people, cannot be justified by the state's repression. Both sets of actions drove an escalating cycle whose human cost fell heavily on those who were not leaders on either side.

Section 95.16

Political Violence, Leadership Succession, and the Consolidation of 1981

The summer of 1981 brought attacks that killed or injured prominent members of the regime’s leadership. Ali Khamenei was seriously wounded in June. The attack on the Islamic Republican Party on 28 June killed Mohammad Beheshti, the party’s secretary-general and head of the Supreme Court, along with other officials. It is remembered as the Hafte Tir bombing, after the date in the Iranian calendar. The deaths deprived the party and state institutions of experienced organizers at a moment of acute political confrontation.

A further attack on 30 August 1981 killed President Mohammad-Ali Rajai and Prime Minister Mohammad-Javad Bahonar. Other officials and clerics were assassinated during the period. The political consequence was the loss of several senior organizers and an intensified perception of danger, which the authorities invoked to justify emergency security measures. These events did not eliminate the need to establish individual responsibility for criminal acts or to distinguish peaceful opponents from perpetrators of violence.

Responsibility must be treated with care. The government attributed the attacks to the Mojahedin, and most historians accept that the organization's operatives carried out the campaign of assassinations and bombings in 1981, even though attribution of specific incidents rested on official statements, trials, and confessions that are difficult to verify independently, and the organization's own statements varied. The chapter therefore distinguishes documented organizational responsibility, where the evidence supports it, from the broader accusations that the state directed at a variety of opponents, including some who had no connection to the attacks. The section focuses on historical consequences and not on the operational details of the incidents.

The regime's institutional resilience was striking. Within hours of the Hafte Tir bombing, party and state organs began to function, Khomeini's authority was unaffected, and the system of succession set out in the constitution was followed. Mousavi-Ardebili took over the leadership of the judiciary; Majles leaders and the party reorganized; and the death of the president in August was followed by the formation of a provisional council of senior officials to oversee a new election. The party's organizational depth, its reserve of cadres, and the constitutional design that placed ultimate authority in a Leader who survived, all contributed to continuity. The assassinations did not decapitate the regime, but they did increase its dependence on security institutions.

The political response intensified repression and mobilization. Security forces, the committees, and the revolutionary courts expanded arrests and executions, and revolutionary prosecutors acquired greater discretion. Crowds were mobilized in funerals and rallies that expressed solidarity and condemned the Mojahedin, and the press carried accounts of the martyrs. The language of martyrdom, which had been prominent in the revolution and the war, was extended to the victims of the bombings, and the dead were commemorated as defenders of the revolution. Public mourning rituals thus supplemented coercion as a source of cohesion.

Presidential succession was handled swiftly. The party nominated Ali Khamenei, who had survived the June attack and had represented Khomeini in defense councils, and he was elected president on 2 October 1981 with the support of an overwhelming majority of those voting, before being sworn in on 13 October. Khamenei named Mir-Hossein Mousavi, who had been foreign minister and edited the party newspaper, as prime minister, and the Majles approved him later that month. The new leadership combined clerical and lay figures from the party, and its coalition, which still included the Majles speaker Rafsanjani, was recomposed. The events of 1981 thus produced a more consolidated ruling coalition, with the party's rivals removed, its own losses replaced, and its control of the major institutions strengthened.

The succession illustrates the difference between the survival of the ruling coalition and the absence of disagreement within it. Rajai was elected on 24 July, and Khamenei won the election of 2 October following Rajai’s death. The Majles did not simply endorse every presidential nomination: Khamenei’s initial candidate for prime minister failed to secure parliamentary approval, and Mir-Hossein Mousavi was approved on 29 October. Under the original constitution the president, prime minister and parliament had distinct roles in forming the government. The continuing contest over appointments is evidence that the elimination of independent opposition did not make the governing institutions politically interchangeable. For the sequence of events, see the Encyclopaedia Iranica chronology.

Section 95.17

Political Imprisonment, Revolutionary Executions, and Human Rights

Political imprisonment had been a central issue of Iranian politics before the revolution, and the new regime inherited the monarchy's prison system and much of its physical infrastructure. Evin Prison in northern Tehran, notorious for SAVAK's interrogations and torture, continued as the principal political prison, and it was joined by other detention centers in Tehran and provincial cities, among them facilities run by the committees, the Guards, and the revolutionary prosecutors. The same buildings that had held the revolution's activists now held its opponents, and a number of former prisoners of the monarchy found themselves imprisoned again.

The scale and character of imprisonment changed over time, and the distinction between categories of prisoners is essential. In 1979 and early 1980, most of those executed were officials of the monarchy, including security officers, military commanders, and politicians, together with people convicted of drug trafficking and offenses regarded as morally corrupt. After the confrontation of June 1981, according to contemporary human-rights reporting, the majority of those detained and executed were political opponents, most of them members or supporters of the Mojahedin, along with members of leftist organizations, Kurdish groups, and other opposition movements. A further category consisted of people persecuted for religious identity, especially Baha'is, and a further one of peaceful activists who had neither used nor advocated violence. Treating all these as a single group of armed militants, or as a single group of innocent victims, would distort the record.

Detention practices drew continuing criticism. Prisoners were commonly held without access to a lawyer, often for long periods before trial, and families frequently did not know where relatives were held. Former prisoners and human-rights organizations reported systematic mistreatment, including beatings, prolonged isolation, and torture used to compel statements, and televised confessions, in which prisoners denounced themselves and their organizations, became a regular feature of political trials. The authorities denied that torture was practiced and described it as incompatible with Islamic law, and Khomeini condemned it in principle, as the Eight-Point Decree shows. The historian's task is to weigh these denials and statements of principle against the volume and consistency of testimony.

Executions peaked after June 1981. Prisoners were commonly tried by revolutionary courts under the charges of moharebeh, "war against God," or of corruption on earth, and were sentenced and executed within a short time, often by firing squad or hanging. Contemporary reports referred to executions in prisons in Tehran and other cities on a nearly daily basis during parts of 1981 and 1982. The victims included young people, and human-rights organizations reported the execution of teenagers, and in some cases of detainees who were not known to have participated in violence. At the same time, prosecutors cited the assassinations and bombings as justification for urgency. The tension between these claims is central to evaluating the period.

The experience of prisoners' families shaped memory and politics. Families waited outside prisons for news, sometimes received personal belongings or notices of execution, were in some cases required to pay for the bullets used or were denied the bodies, and often faced discrimination in employment and education afterward. Mothers and wives of prisoners became in later years prominent witnesses and organizers of memorial activity, within Iran and in exile. The long-term social effects of imprisonment and execution, including emigration and the silencing of political activity, extended far beyond the individuals directly affected.

The documentary base for these events consists mainly of the reports of Amnesty International and other human-rights bodies, contemporary news coverage, testimonies of survivors collected in memoirs and later projects, the publications of opposition organizations, and official Iranian statements and press reports. None of these gives a complete account. Amnesty documented what it could verify, within the constraints of limited access to the country, while Iranian authorities published some executions and not others, and opposition groups issued figures that reflected their own interests. The following feature examines how to read such records.

Quantitative records document the acceleration of repression without providing a comprehensive national total. Amnesty International reported 709 known executions in Iran during 1980. Its October 1981 report recorded more than 1,600 known executions in the period following 20 June through the reporting cutoff in early October. By the end of 1983 it had recorded 5,447 executions since February 1979, including approximately 400 during 1983. These are counts of recorded executions generally, not a verified count restricted to political prisoners. The organization warned that unannounced executions made actual totals higher. Compare its 1980 figures, October 1981 report and report covering 1983. Different observation windows and retrospective revisions make simple subtraction or annual comparisons unreliable. The evidence supports a marked escalation after June 1981, but identifying the political status of each victim requires case-level records.

Section 95.18

The Suppression of Remaining Political Opposition, 1982–1983

After the crises of 1981, the regime turned to the remaining organized opposition, including some groups that had supported it. The campaign was gradual and selective, and its timing and targets depended on the political utility of each group. Understanding it requires attention to the different trajectories of the left, the nationalists, and the religious critics.

The Fedayan-e Khalq had already split in 1980, and by 1982 both wings were under severe pressure. The Minority, which opposed the regime, lost many members to arrests, executions, and flight, and its remnants withdrew to the mountains or exile. The Majority, which had cooperated with the government and defended its anti-imperialist stance, found that its cooperation did not protect it. In 1983 its leaders were arrested along with the leadership of the Tudeh Party, and the organization was suppressed. Peykar and other Marxist groups were largely eliminated in the preceding years, and the Mojahedin's domestic presence had been crushed.

The nationalists and liberals also lost the remaining space for organized activity. The National Front was banned in 1981, and its leaders were harassed, imprisoned, or confined to private life. The Freedom Movement, led by Bazargan, was allowed a limited and constrained existence, issuing criticisms and open letters, but its publications, gatherings, and electoral prospects were curtailed, and its members were subject to arrests and dismissals. These groups' position had been weakened by the hostage crisis and the war, and by their identification, in the eyes of the regime's supporters, with the policies of the monarchy's last years or with the Americans.

Religious independence was also circumscribed. Kazem Shariatmadari, the senior marja who had criticized the constitution and whose supporters had risen in Tabriz in 1979, was implicated in 1982 in an alleged plot connected to Sadeq Ghotbzadeh, a former foreign minister who was accused of conspiring against the government. Ghotbzadeh was executed in September 1982. Shariatmadari was not tried, but he was placed under restriction in his home in Qom, his institutions were closed, and a gathering of clerics declared that he was no longer qualified to hold the rank of marja. His case illustrates that the regime was prepared to restrict even a very senior cleric, and that religious authority did not protect critics. He remained under restriction until his death in 1986.

The Tudeh Party's fate is the clearest example of the limits of alliance with the regime. The party had supported Khomeini's leadership, defended the embassy seizure, endorsed the constitution, and defended the government's line in its press, and in the early 1980s it still operated openly. In early 1983 its first secretary Nureddin Kianuri and other leaders were arrested, and television broadcasts showed confessions in which leaders acknowledged what the authorities described as espionage for the Soviet Union and attempts to infiltrate the armed forces. In May 1983 the party was banned, and a number of Soviet diplomats were expelled. Large numbers of members and sympathizers were imprisoned, and some, including military officers, were executed in subsequent years. The coercive force that had been directed at opponents of the regime was now applied to a former ally.

Several explanations of the sequence have been offered. One stresses the regime's wartime security concerns and its fear of Soviet penetration, a concern made more acute by the party's links to Moscow. Another stresses its aim of eliminating every organized body independent of its control, regardless of its past stance. Both are plausible, and they are compatible. The lesson drawn by historians is that revolutionary alliances which appeared advantageous in 1979 did not guarantee survival, since the new ruling coalition judged each group by its usefulness and its autonomy.

Political disagreement did not vanish. Within the regime, factions continued to differ on economic policy, land reform, foreign trade, the role of the state, and the treatment of private property, and these disagreements played out in the Majles, the Council of Guardians, and the press. What the suppression of independent organizations did was to move conflict inside the governing system, where it was conducted among forces that accepted the constitution, and to restrict the range of positions that could be advocated in public. That feature of Iranian politics, limited competition among regime-affiliated factions, begins in these years.

The suppression of the Tudeh Party occurred in successive stages: major arrests in February and April 1983, televised statements by leaders at the end of April, and formal proscription on 4 May. The Wilson Center collection of archival materials on the Tudeh Party provides a means of comparing contemporary official allegations with records of international communist connections. Such links are historically relevant but do not establish the truth of every individual accusation. A confession made in detention cannot establish criminal responsibility without corroboration, particularly in a period for which coercive interrogations were documented. The party’s changing fortunes show why earlier ideological alignment and public support for the government offered no durable institutional protection.

Section 95.19

Institutionalizing Revolutionary Authority and the Problem of Legal Accountability

By 1982 the regime had defeated its principal domestic rivals, and some of its leaders judged that the revolutionary improvisations of the first years had become a liability. Committees, courts, and security bodies had acted with wide discretion, and complaints of abuse reached the government and Khomeini. Property had been seized without clear authority, homes had been searched without warrants, and individuals had been detained for long periods without trial. Some clerics and officials warned that such behavior damaged the republic's reputation and violated Islamic law, and the judiciary's heads sought to regularize procedures.

The reorganization of the judiciary was part of this process. In the early 1980s the constitution's framework for the judicial system, including a Supreme Judicial Council and a Supreme Court, was developed, and the Majles passed laws defining criminal offenses and penalties according to Islamic jurisprudence. The revolutionary courts, though their special status was criticized, were not abolished, and their jurisdiction over political and security cases remained. The tension between a system of regularized courts and procedures and a set of revolutionary institutions exercising discretionary power continued, and one aspect of political conflict within the regime concerned how far revolutionary necessity justified departures from established law.

On 15 December 1982 Khomeini issued the Eight-Point Decree, a statement addressed to the judiciary, officials, and security bodies. Its principal provisions, in summary, prohibited entering homes, shops, and offices without judicial authority, prohibited arrests and detention without lawful warrant, prohibited eavesdropping and invasion of private life, regulated the seizure and confiscation of property so that it required a judicial ruling, and warned officials and judges against abuses of power and the exceeding of their authority. The decree was presented as a reaffirmation of Islamic and constitutional protections and as an instruction to those who had acted outside them. The text is available through the Iran Data Portal.

The decree's significance has two dimensions, and they must be kept together. It was a notable statement of principle from the highest authority, acknowledging that violations had occurred and instructing officials to stop them, and it gave critics within the system a document to cite. It also had limitations. It did not abolish the revolutionary courts or the committees, did not create independent mechanisms for enforcing its provisions, and explicitly contained security-related exceptions in its seventh point, subject to stated legal and religious restrictions. Political repression continued after December 1982, including the suppression of the Tudeh in 1983 and many further prosecutions. The decree cannot be treated as evidence that abuses ended. Its importance lies partly in the problems it acknowledged and sought to address.

The gap between official directives and institutional practice is a theme that recurs in the history of the Islamic Republic. Leaders could issue instructions, but they depended on committees, prosecutors, and courts staffed by people with their own loyalties and ideas of revolutionary duty. Reforms were often incomplete, and conflicts between those who emphasized legal regularity and those who emphasized revolutionary vigilance persisted. Where hardline institutions had strong support among the Guards, the judiciary, or parts of the clergy, they could continue practices despite directives, and the leadership's willingness to enforce limits varied with political circumstances.

The deeper problem was legal accountability. In a system in which authority rested on revolutionary legitimacy and the Leader's guardianship, mechanisms for holding officials to account were weak, since the bodies that would review violations were often those that committed them or were closely allied with them. The constitution provided for judicial independence and for citizens' rights, but their enforcement depended on political will. This tension, between constitutional guarantees and the practice of security institutions, would be taken up again in later chapters on the end of the war, the reform movement, and the debates of the 1990s and 2000s.

The Eight-Point Decree also contained provisions beyond the protection of privacy. Its first three points addressed the priority of Islamic legislation, the qualifications of officials and judicial authority. Points four through six restricted unauthorized arrest, seizure and intrusion into private life. Point seven explicitly distinguished alleged plots and organizations seeking to overthrow the republic from ordinary private activity, while prescribing limits on officials. Point eight directed the creation of local complaint arrangements for misconduct. These arrangements were administrative mechanisms, not independent courts empowered to supervise the entire security apparatus. The decree therefore exemplifies an effort to regulate revolutionary institutions while preserving their capacity to confront perceived threats. Its effectiveness must be assessed through subsequent administrative records and testimony, not inferred from the existence of the directive alone.

Section 95.20

The Consolidated Islamic Republic and the Legacy of Revolutionary State-Building

By the end of 1983 the Islamic Republic had developed a governing order that rested on several interlocking supports. Constitutional institutions, the Leader, the presidency, the Majles, the Council of Guardians, and the judiciary, provided a legal framework and a vocabulary of legitimacy. Revolutionary political organizations, above all the Islamic Republican Party, supplied the cadres to staff them. Security and military bodies, the Guards, the Basij, the committees, and a regular army under supervision, supplied the capacity to enforce decisions. Judicial institutions, from the revolutionary courts to the regularized judiciary, supplied both punishment and an official account of legality.

Other pillars were social and economic. Religious and educational networks, the mosques, seminaries, reshaped schools, and universities, trained and mobilized the supporters of the order. Economic foundations and welfare programs, including the bonyads, relief committees, and Reconstruction Jihad, redistributed resources and created constituencies. Political mobilization, through Friday prayers, funerals, anniversaries, and rallies, reinforced loyalty, and restrictions on independent opposition limited the capacity of rivals to organize. These elements supported one another: the foundations financed the loyalist organizations, the courts and committees protected them, and the educational and cultural policies reproduced their personnel.

The chapter has used five dimensions to describe this process. Political consolidation marginalized competing movements and concentrated authority. Institutional consolidation turned improvised bodies into permanent ones. Coercive consolidation created the courts, prisons, and security forces that enforced decisions. Economic consolidation placed resources in the hands of foundations and state-linked bodies. Ideological consolidation reshaped education, culture, and public life. Consolidation should be distinguished from unanimity. The order that emerged by 1983 drew genuine support from parts of Iranian society, including beneficiaries of its programs and people who identified with its religious and political objectives, while also relying on the coerced silence or exile of others. Estimating the proportions is difficult, because the absence of free elections and independent polling leaves the historian reliant on indirect evidence, such as turnout in referenda, the size of rallies, and the testimony of participants. It is clear, however, that the regime's survival owed something to real support as well as to force, and that both elements need to be explained.

Factional competition continued within the order. The government of the 1980s contained advocates of a state-directed economy and defenders of private enterprise, clerics who wished to expand the Islamic law's reach and those who favored caution, and supporters of an assertive foreign policy and of more pragmatic approaches. These divisions, reflected in disputes over land reform, the nationalized sector, and foreign trade, would structure the politics of later decades. The elimination of independent parties meant that such disagreements occurred within a restricted circle, and the Leader's role as an arbiter among factions became still more important.

The consolidated state faced the war with Iraq, which was by 1983 in its third year and had become a prolonged conflict of attrition. Chapter 96 examines the origins of that war and Chapter 97 its conduct and effects on Iranian society. The domestic order described in this chapter provided the political structure within which wartime mobilization took place, and the war in turn reinforced the legitimacy of that order and the importance of its security institutions. The relationships between domestic consolidation and war would shape the later history of the Islamic Republic, including the events of 1988 and 1989 discussed in Chapter 98.

Historical Debate

Revolutionary State-Building, Political Legitimacy, and Coercive Consolidation

The question: How should historians weigh popular support, institutional design, wartime emergency, and coercion in explaining the consolidation of the Islamic Republic by 1983?

Interpretation A — consolidation as the product of mass mobilization and institution-building: Emphasizes Khomeini's charisma, the wide support of religious and lower-income Iranians, the benefits of foundations and rural programs, and the organizational strengths of the party and clerical network. Repression is seen as a response to armed opposition and war.
Interpretation B — consolidation as coercive monopolization: Emphasizes the early use of courts and committees against rivals, the exclusion of opposition from elections, the use of crowds and violence against dissent, and the elimination of allies as well as opponents. War and opposition violence are seen as pretexts or accelerants for a pattern of monopolization already underway.
Interpretation C — an interaction among several processes: Treats legitimacy, organization, ideology, opposition violence, war, and coercion as mutually reinforcing, and argues that none can be isolated as the sole cause.

Evidence: Participants in the debate draw on the same body of evidence: the constitution, official decrees, the press, contemporary reporting, human-rights investigations, opposition accounts, memoirs, and the record of elections and referenda. They differ on how to weigh the evidence of support against that of coercion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the diverse coalition that overthrew the Pahlavi monarchy became, between 1979 and 1983, a political order dominated by Khomeini and his institutional allies. Its central argument is that consolidation was a contested and uneven process in which constitutional institutions, revolutionary organizations, ideological mobilization, political alliances, and coercive authority combined. The war with Iraq accelerated the transformation but did not independently determine its outcome.

The revolutionary coalition fragmented because its members shared an adversary but not a program. They disagreed about the source of sovereignty, about the meaning of social justice, and about who would control the weapons, ministries, and courts. The distinction between revolutionary victory in February 1979 and political consolidation, which took several years, structured the chapter's account, and the outcome was not predetermined.

Khomeini's supporters held organizational advantages: mosque and seminary networks, bazaar connections, and the Islamic Republican Party, whose leaders included Beheshti, Rafsanjani, Khamenei, and Bahonar. These advantages did not make the clergy a unified body, and the chapter showed that senior clerics such as Shariatmadari held different views. Liberal constitutionalists and nationalists, who held office in the provisional government, lacked comparable institutions and lost influence, while the revolutionary left divided over whether to support or oppose the regime.

Revolutionary committees, the Guards and Basij, and the revolutionary courts were the principal instruments of state power. Each began as an improvisation and became permanent. The committees gave the new order a decentralized coercive presence, the Guards a loyal armed force parallel to the regular army, and the courts a means of punishing officials of the monarchy that was also turned against new opponents. Their procedures, which provided limited safeguards for defendants, diverged from the guarantees of the constitution.

Confiscation, nationalization, and the creation of foundations such as the Foundation of the Oppressed gave the state resources and constituencies. Welfare programs and the Reconstruction Jihad extended its presence in rural areas, while employment screening placed loyalists in public positions. These institutions generated administrative capacity and social support, and they also created parallel structures whose accountability was limited.

The new state faced regional and religious diversity with force and with exclusion. In Kurdistan, demands for autonomy were treated as separatism, and a military campaign and revolutionary courts followed. Other regional movements in Khuzestan, Turkmen Sahra, Baluchistan, and Azerbaijan were suppressed, and religious minorities experienced different degrees of recognition and pressure, with the Baha'is persecuted specifically for their faith. The Cultural Revolution closed and restructured universities, and women's legal status and public life were regulated, in the face of protest, adaptation, and support.

The confrontations of 1981 were decisive. Bani-Sadr, with a large electoral mandate but few institutions, was stripped of his command, found politically incompetent by the Majles on 21 June, and dismissed by Khomeini on 22 June. The Mojahedin, excluded from legal politics, turned to armed struggle after the events of 20 June, and a campaign of assassinations and bombings killed Beheshti, Rajai, Bahonar, and many others. The regime's institutions proved resilient, and Khamenei and Mousavi assumed the presidency and prime ministry. Repression, including mass executions, intensified.

Documentary records of imprisonment and executions are partial and contested, and the chapter showed how to evaluate them by identifying the source, period, population, and limitations. It distinguished former officials, armed opponents, peaceful activists, and people persecuted for religious identity. In 1982 and 1983 the regime suppressed remaining independent organizations, including the Fedayan and the Tudeh Party, which had supported it, and restricted Shariatmadari and the nationalists.

The Eight-Point Decree of December 1982 acknowledged abuses and sought to regulate revolutionary bodies, but it did not end repression, and it illustrates the gap between directives and practice. By 1983 the Islamic Republic rested on constitutional institutions, a ruling party, security bodies, courts, religious and educational networks, economic foundations, and mobilization, together with restrictions on independent opposition. Consolidation was not unanimity, and factional competition continued within the order.

The chapter's contribution lies in explaining how power was organized, institutionalized, legitimized, financed, exercised, contested, and enforced. Its account connects to the war with Iraq, which Chapters 96 and 97 examine, and to the later history of the Islamic Republic, including the end of the war and the succession discussed in Chapter 98 and the institutional development of the Revolutionary Guards addressed in Chapter 105.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the institutions and habits formed between 1979 and 1983 shaped the Islamic Republic for decades. The relationship between the Leader and elected offices, the coexistence of regular and revolutionary institutions, the role of foundations in the economy, and the pattern of limited factional competition within a restricted political field all took form in these years.

It also shows how a revolution's legitimacy and its coercion can operate together. The same institutions that delivered services and recognition to previously neglected groups also imprisoned and executed many Iranians, and understanding the period requires holding both facts in view without reducing one to the other. The chapter's emphasis on evidence, and on the limits of figures and official statements, is part of that discipline.

The chapter connects Chapter 92's revolutionary victory, Chapter 93's constitutional design, and Chapter 94's political realignment to the war chapters that follow. It also anticipates later themes, among them the Guards' institutional growth, the role of foundations, and the continuing debate over legal accountability. Students should carry forward the question of how new states are made, who bears the cost, and how historians can speak responsibly about events whose records are incomplete.
