Chapter Opening

Under the Ilkhans, merchants, courts, and builders made some Iranian cities busier and richer than they had been for a generation, while cultivators, herders, and townspeople bore the costs of taxation, requisition, and political insecurity. Prosperity was real, but it was neither uniform nor automatically shared.

In 1294, according to the narrative sources, the Ilkhan Gaykhatu's government in Tabriz tried an experiment unknown in Iranian monetary history: it issued paper notes, modeled on the paper money of Mongol-ruled China, and ordered that they circulate in place of metal coin. Chroniclers such as Rashid al-Din and Wassaf record the sequel. Shopkeepers closed their stalls rather than accept the notes, the supply of ordinary goods in the city was disrupted, merchants and townspeople protested, and the government abandoned the scheme within a short time. The episode is usually told as a story about a failed financial policy. For the purposes of this chapter it is more useful as evidence about a society. It illustrates a serious breakdown of trust in a particular monetary instrument and the difficulty of imposing a fiscal innovation on a trading city. The chronicles do not establish how every group of inhabitants responded.

That market was one part of a larger economic world that this chapter examines. The Ilkhanate, ruled from the 1250s into the 1330s by descendants of Hülegü, governed a territory whose economic geography ran from the pastures of Azerbaijan and Arran, across the irrigated plains and oasis cities of the plateau, to the Persian Gulf coast, and westward into Mesopotamia and eastern Anatolia. Within that space lay densely cultivated river valleys, marginal rain-fed grain lands, engineered underground water channels, mountain pastures, caravan towns, and ports. These zones did not respond to Mongol rule in the same way, and no single description, whether of ruin or of revival, applies to all of them.

Chapter 46 examined the violence, displacement, and demographic damage of the Mongol invasions and the difficulty of measuring them, and Chapters 47 and 48 explained how the Ilkhanate was organized and what Ghazan Khan ordered in his program of fiscal and administrative regulation. This chapter asks a different question. Given those conditions and those decrees, how did people actually produce, exchange, and pay for things? How did fields, orchards, pastures, workshops, bazaars, caravans, and ports operate, who controlled their revenues, and what can historians legitimately conclude about the extent, geography, and durability of recovery?

The answer requires attention to several kinds of economic actor at once. The Mongol court and its army were enormous consumers whose seasonal movements concentrated purchasing power in particular places. Cultivators and pastoralists produced the grain, meat, wool, and hides on which both towns and courts depended. Artisans turned those materials, together with imported goods, into textiles, metalwork, ceramics, and buildings. Merchants, some of them financed by princes and by Mongol aristocratic women, carried goods across the Ilkhanate and beyond it. Officials, landholders, and endowment administrators claimed shares of what the others produced. A history of recovery that follows only one of these actors, particularly the merchants and officials who wrote or were written about, will misdescribe the rest.

The evidence also demands care. Much of what historians know comes from court-connected authors: Rashid al-Din, a vizier and reformer who had reasons to present reconstruction as successful; Wassaf, an ornate prose stylist whose work contains valuable financial detail alongside rhetorical exaggeration; and Hamdallah Mustawfi, a financial official who composed his geographical work around 1340, after the Ilkhanate's unified rule had ended. To these must be added al-Umari, writing in Mamluk Syria from informants' reports; European merchant and traveler accounts; endowment deeds and accounting manuals; coins; and the buildings and ceramics recovered by architectural and archaeological study. Each source illuminates certain places and social groups, and each is silent about others.

This chapter is therefore organized as a sequence of economic landscapes: the inheritance and regional geography of recovery, the rural base of agriculture and irrigation, the cities and their artisans, the merchant networks that tied them to a wider Eurasian and Indian Ocean world, and finally the question of who benefited and how durable the gains proved. Its central question is how far Ilkhanid rule enabled reconstruction and commercial expansion in Iran, and why the benefits and durability of recovery differed so sharply among regions, institutions, and social groups. The chapter's closing section prepares, without narrating, the transition to Chapter 51, in which the political unity that had sustained many of these arrangements broke apart.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the economic conditions the Ilkhans inherited and why those conditions differed from region to region rather than being uniformly devastated.
  2. Compare the economic trajectories of Azerbaijan, Arran, Khurasan, Fars, Iraq-i Ajam, and Kerman, identifying the factors that shaped each.
  3. Analyze how the mobile Ilkhanid court, army, and pastoral economy generated demand and imposed provisioning burdens.
  4. Describe the principal crops, irrigation systems, and forms of landholding and taxation that structured the rural economy, and distinguish among different groups of cultivators.
  5. Distinguish official measures of agricultural restoration from demonstrated results, using the limits of the surviving evidence.
  6. Assess Tabriz and Sultaniyya as contrasting urban projects and explain why neither can stand for the Ilkhanate as a whole.
  7. Trace the principal caravan, Anatolian, Caucasian, and Gulf routes and explain the costs, risks, and infrastructure of long-distance trade.
  8. Evaluate the role of merchant partnerships, credit, and the ortoq system, including the economic agency of elite Mongol women.
  9. Interpret the strengths and limits of Rashid al-Din, Wassaf, Mustawfi, al-Umari, fiscal manuals, endowment deeds, and coins as evidence for economic life.
  10. Contrast how wealthy merchants, powerful households, artisans, cultivators, pastoralists, and urban workers experienced Ilkhanid economic conditions.
  11. Evaluate competing historical arguments about whether recovery was broad-based and what drove it.
  12. Connect the durability of Ilkhanid economic arrangements to the political fragmentation examined in Chapter 51.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. To what extent did Ilkhanid rule enable economic reconstruction, and how can historians tell reconstruction from recovery that was only claimed?
  2. Why can evidence of prosperity in a city such as Tabriz not establish the condition of the whole Ilkhanate?
  3. How did the mobility of the royal court and army shape markets, pastures, and provisioning in different places?
  4. Why did the recovery of the countryside depend on irrigation, labor, tenure, and taxation rather than on royal decrees alone?
  5. In what ways did merchants, officials, and elite women use state power and private capital to build commercial wealth?
  6. Why might commercial expansion and rural hardship have coexisted in the same decades?
  7. What different indicators, such as trade, building, cultivated land, revenue, and household welfare, can be used to measure recovery, and why are they not interchangeable?
  8. How did the political settlement that supported Ilkhanid economic life affect the durability of recovery once it weakened?

Historical Context

The Ilkhanate emerged in the 1250s from the western campaigns of Hülegü, the brother of the Great Khan Möngke, and it remained formally linked to the wider Mongol imperial world in its early decades through its relationship with the Yuan court in East Asia. Its rulers, called ilkhans, governed Iran and neighboring regions from bases in Azerbaijan and the Caucasus borderlands, in rivalry with the Golden Horde to the north and the Mamluk sultanate to the southwest. The political history of these decades is the subject of Chapter 47. What matters here is that the Ilkhans presided over an economy that was already old, regionally varied, and highly dependent on water, on seasonal pastoral movement, and on caravan routes that predated the Mongols by many centuries.

The Iranian plateau, as Chapter 1 explained, has never possessed a single continuously fertile agricultural heartland comparable to the Nile valley. Settlement and production clustered in river valleys, foothill zones, and oases, linked by routes that skirted the interior deserts. Qanats, canals, wells, and surface irrigation made cultivation possible in places where rainfall alone would not suffice, and these systems required continuous maintenance by communities, landholders, and states. The Mongol invasions of the 1220s and the campaigns that followed disrupted many of these arrangements unevenly: some districts suffered severe destruction, depopulation, or flight, while others negotiated surrender, were spared, or recovered relatively quickly. This chapter assumes that background, which Chapter 46 treats in detail, and begins from the problem of reconstruction rather than from the destruction itself.

Mongol rule changed the economic environment in several ways beyond destruction. It introduced a ruling elite of pastoralists whose wealth and military power depended on herds and on pasture, and it created courts whose seasonal movements were as economically significant as their political decisions. It brought new claimants to the revenue of the land, including royal estates, military assignees, and Mongol princes and princesses. It introduced or expanded levies such as the qubchur, the tamgha, and requisitions for post-station horses and provisions, some of which fell heavily on settled communities. It also placed under one overarching authority regions that had been politically divided, from Anatolia's frontier to the Gulf coast, which could reduce certain frontier barriers to trade even where it created others through new rivalries.

Iranian and Muslim officials did not disappear under these conditions. The Ilkhans continued to depend on literate administrators, accountants, and viziers, many of them from established Iranian families, to collect revenue and keep records. Local notables, religious families, and merchants retained influence in their cities, and Mongol rulers cooperated with them for reasons of both necessity and self-interest. Economic life under the Ilkhans was therefore shaped by a negotiated and uneven relationship among Mongol military elites, Iranian administrators, local landholders, merchants, and producers, with outcomes that varied by period and by place. Understanding that negotiation is the key to reading the sources discussed throughout this chapter.

Division I · Economic Landscapes and the Conditions of Recovery · Section 50.1

The Economic Inheritance of Mongol Conquest

The Ilkhans did not govern a blank slate, and they did not govern a uniform ruin. The economic world they inherited had been shaped by two generations of Mongol campaigning, by the political settlements that followed, and by older structures of agriculture, trade, and landholding that conquest modified more often than it erased. To understand what recovery could mean, it is necessary first to identify what had been disrupted, what had survived, and what had changed in the ownership and taxation of resources. This section establishes those conditions without retelling the history of destruction and demographic loss, which belongs to Chapter 46.

The first inheritance was an uneven pattern of survival. Cities that submitted to the Mongols early, often by paying tribute or accepting a Mongol overseer, frequently kept their markets, their elites, and much of their built fabric. Tabriz, which bought off Mongol forces in the 1220s, is the best-known example of a northwestern city whose commercial life was never interrupted as completely as that of some eastern centers. Cities in the south, including Shiraz under the local Salghurid dynasty and Kerman under the Qutlughkhanids, also entered the Mongol order through negotiated submission and retained local rulers who paid tribute. By contrast, major urban centers of Khurasan and Transoxiana, which had resisted, experienced severe violence, and their recovery was slower, more partial, and in some districts doubtful. The differences matter because they meant that Ilkhanid economic reconstruction began from different starting points in different regions.

The second inheritance was disruption in agricultural production, which was less visible in the sources than the fall of cities but arguably more consequential. Warfare, flight, and requisition removed laborers, draft animals, and seed stock from some districts. The abandonment of villages could interrupt the cooperative maintenance on which irrigation depended, a problem examined in Section 50.6. Because cultivation in much of the plateau relied on water systems that required continuous attention, a period of neglect could reduce output well beyond the immediate years of fighting. Historians nonetheless cannot reconstruct a uniform curve of agricultural decline. Evidence from narrative sources, fiscal records, and archaeological survey varies by district, and some regions, especially those with abundant water or distant from the main campaign routes, appear to have maintained production with comparatively little interruption.

The third inheritance was a changed distribution of property and revenue claims. Mongol conquest created new categories of landholding and new claimants to the surplus of the land. Estates came under royal or princely control, a category examined under the term inju in Section 50.7. Military commanders and soldiers received assignments of revenue. Established Muslim landholding families, some of them religious notables, sought to protect their wealth by converting property into endowments, whose revenues supported mosques, madrasas, or shrines and whose status gave them a degree of protection from seizure. These shifts redistributed who benefited from agricultural production without necessarily changing how much was produced, and they left the countryside with overlapping claimants whose demands could conflict.

The fourth inheritance was a weakened and improvised system of revenue collection. Early Mongol governance of Iran relied on a combination of lump-sum exactions, census-based levies, and the continued use of existing administrators and accounting practices. Tribute and special levies fell unpredictably on towns and districts, and the officials who collected them often had personal as well as institutional incentives to extract more than the stated amounts. Scholars such as Ann K. S. Lambton have stressed that the machinery of taxation in the early Ilkhanate was unstable and prone to abuse, and that the regularization of taxation was an ongoing project rather than a completed fact. That instability is a central condition for understanding why restoration depended on administrators and policy as well as on the environment.

Finally, the Ilkhans inherited surviving commercial networks. The routes linking Iranian cities to Anatolia, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and the Persian Gulf existed before the Mongols, and merchants, caravan operators, and local brokers continued to operate within and across political boundaries. Mongol rule altered the environment of this trade by placing large regions under a single overarching authority, by creating demand from new courts, and by making some merchants partners of the ruling family, a development examined in Section 50.14. It did not create commerce from nothing. Recovery, in this sense, often meant the reactivation and reorientation of existing networks, and the chapter's later sections treat the commercial economy as an inherited institution that the Ilkhans exploited and reshaped.

Section 50.2

Regional Economies and the Uneven Geography of Recovery

The Ilkhanate was not an economic unit. It was a political umbrella over regions that differed in climate, water supply, settlement history, distance from the court, and relationship to Mongol military power. A student who generalizes from the commercial vitality of Tabriz to the condition of the empire, or from the ruin of an eastern city to the plateau as a whole, will misread the evidence. This section compares the principal regions and identifies the factors that shaped their economic outcomes.

Azerbaijan, the northwestern region centered on Tabriz and Lake Urmia, enjoyed several advantages. It lay at the heart of Ilkhanid political life, close to the pastures where the court and army spent much of the year, and it possessed a major commercial city with routes toward Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Black Sea coast. Court demand, administrative employment, and patronage all concentrated there. At Maragha, Hülegü's patronage of an astronomical observatory beginning around 1259, supported by endowed revenues, shows how political favor could direct resources into scholarly and institutional projects in the region. The same concentration of favor created a risk of dependence: the region's prosperity was tied to the continued presence and spending of the court.

Arran and the Mughan plain, to the north across the Aras river, served as winter quarters for Ilkhanid armies and herds, with abundant pasture supported by river irrigation. The economy of the region combined pastoralism, irrigated cultivation, and the heavy presence of military households, so that agricultural land and grazing land existed in a relationship that could be complementary or competitive, depending on how pasture was managed. The border with the Golden Horde to the north made the region strategically exposed, and military campaigning there affected local production and security.

Khurasan, the northeastern region described in Chapter 1 as larger than its modern namesake, presents a different picture. Its great cities, including Merv and Nishapur, had suffered severely, and its distance from the Ilkhanid centers in the northwest placed it on a contested frontier with Mongol powers of Central Asia. Raiding and campaigning continued intermittently, and the security required for agricultural and commercial recovery was repeatedly interrupted. Parts of the region recovered under local dynasties that accepted Mongol overlordship, such as the Kartids at Herat, but the evidence suggests slower and more localized recovery than in the northwest. The weight of Khurasan in Chapters 30 through 38 makes its relative decline in this period historically significant.

Fars in the south illustrates how political arrangement shaped economic outcome. Shiraz had been spared through submission under the Salghurid atabegs, and the province long retained a degree of local rule. Over time, direct Mongol administration and heavy demands for revenue replaced earlier arrangements, and the province's relative prosperity became tied to the extraction of tribute, a pattern that later sources describe in terms of both wealth and burden. Kerman, under the Qutlughkhanid dynasty, enjoyed a similar semi-autonomous status within Ilkhanid overlordship, and its position on routes toward the Gulf and Khurasan gave it commercial importance. In both regions, local dynasties and elites mediated between Mongol demand and local production, and their policies shaped regional outcomes.

Iraq-i Ajam, the central and western plateau region including Hamadan, Isfahan, Qum, and Kashan, was highly varied. Some cities had suffered destruction in the first conquests while others recovered, and the region's agricultural districts lay along important routes between the Ilkhanid northwest and the south. Kashan's ceramic and tile production, discussed in Section 50.12, shows that specialized craft production could flourish in a region whose agricultural condition was mixed. In Iraq-i Arab, centered on Baghdad, the Ilkhans maintained an administrative presence, and the canal-fed agriculture of Mesopotamia depended on water works whose maintenance was an ongoing concern for governors.

Several factors explain these differences. Environmental conditions determined whether a region could recover quickly: water-rich districts with durable infrastructure could rebound faster than zones dependent on fragile qanats. Settlement history mattered, because places that had suffered severe depopulation had fewer people to rebuild. Political stability and proximity to the court affected security and demand. Transport routes determined access to markets. And local institutions, including dynasties, notable families, and religious foundations, shaped how Mongol demands were negotiated. These variables interacted differently in each region, so recovery must be assessed regionally rather than for the Ilkhanate as a whole.

Section 50.3

The Mobile Royal Court, Pastoralism, and Economic Demand

The Ilkhans were rulers of cities and farmland, but they remained, in large part, rulers whose households and armies moved. The royal camp, called the ordo, followed a seasonal rhythm adapted from steppe practice: winter quarters in lower, warmer pastureland such as the Mughan plain and, at times, the lowlands near Baghdad; summer quarters in cooler highland pastures of Azerbaijan and the northwestern mountains. This mobility, which Chapter 47 treats as a feature of Ilkhanid political life, was also an economic institution. It determined where large concentrations of people, animals, and purchasing power would gather and when.

The scale of demand generated by the court and army is difficult to quantify, and no reliable figures survive for the numbers of people or animals involved at any given time. What the sources permit is a qualitative picture. A royal encampment required food for people and fodder for animals, felt and textile products for shelter and clothing, saddlery and weapons, transport animals, and a steady supply of luxury goods for the ruler's household and for gifts. Military households required horses, and the Mongol cavalry's dependence on remounts made horses and pasture central military resources. Livestock moved in large numbers, and the pastoral economy that supplied meat, wool, hides, and dairy products was therefore tied directly to the court's needs.

That demand could stimulate local economies. Where the court spent its seasons, nearby towns and markets could expect increased trade, craftsmen could expect commissions, and merchants could expect buyers for imported luxuries. Ilkhanid rulers also made gifts and payments that circulated purchasing power. The seasonal concentration of consumers could draw merchants, brokers, and artisans toward the court's usual sites, and it contributed to the commercial importance of Tabriz and, later, Sultaniyya. For merchants who could secure access to the court, the opportunities were substantial.

The same movements imposed burdens that fell on settled and pastoral communities along the way and at the sites of encampment. Provisioning an army or a court from the surrounding districts could involve requisitions of food and fodder, the billeting of soldiers, and obligations to supply post-station horses and provisions under the system of relay posts known as the yam. The associated requisitions, called ulaq in the sources, could weigh heavily on villages near the routes. Narrative sources, including Rashid al-Din's account of Ghazan's measures, record complaints about abuses by officials and travelers who used the post system to extort goods and labor, and the existence of attempts at regulation suggests that the abuses were real, though the effectiveness of regulation is a separate question.

Pastoral land use created a further source of tension. The grazing of large herds required pasture, and in zones where pastureland and cultivated land adjoined or overlapped, such as parts of Arran and Azerbaijan, herds could damage crops and strain irrigation systems. Pastoralists themselves, whether Mongol and Turkic military households or longer-established groups of herders, were not a homogeneous population; some were closely tied to the court and army, while others were communities with their own obligations and vulnerabilities. The relationship between pastoralists and cultivators was therefore partly complementary, since each supplied goods the other needed, and partly competitive, since both claimed land and water. Recognizing this ambivalence prevents the chapter from treating the pastoral and settled economies as separate worlds.

Royal mobility also altered the calendar of exchange. A merchant who expected to sell horses, textiles, or grain to the court had to know not only what it needed but where its principal households would spend the next season. The traders who could arrange pack animals, provisions, and credit along expected routes possessed an advantage over producers unable to follow the encampment. Court spending thus could benefit towns linked to recurring itineraries without spreading evenly into surrounding villages. Conversely, a delayed or diverted movement might strand supplies assembled for expected purchasers. The sources seldom preserve the accounts of these smaller traders, so this consequence follows from the logistical requirements of mobile consumption rather than from an independently measured seasonal price series.

Section 50.4

Towns, Villages, and the Integration of Economic Life

Cities in the Ilkhanate lived on their hinterlands. A town of any size depended on surrounding villages for grain, on pastoral communities for meat, wool, and hides, and on the larger regional economy for the raw materials of craft production. Conversely, villages and herders depended on towns for markets, manufactured goods, credit, and the services of officials and religious institutions. This section examines the flows that tied these zones together, because recovery in one depended on recovery in the other.

Grain was the essential commodity. The feeding of a large city such as Tabriz required a reliable supply from irrigated and dry-farmed districts within a practical transport range, and shortages could produce rapid price increases and social unrest. The paper currency episode of 1294, mentioned in the chapter opening, illustrates the sensitivity of urban food supply: according to the narrative sources, the closing of shops and the refusal of sellers to accept paper threatened the provisioning of the city and contributed to the policy's rapid abandonment. Hinterland production, urban demand, and monetary confidence were thus bound together in a single system.

Livestock and its products moved along parallel routes. Herders brought animals to market towns and to the court's encampments, and wool, hides, and animal fiber supplied workshops that produced textiles, leather goods, and other products. Intermediaries, including brokers, dealers, and moneylenders, connected producers to consumers and extended credit to cultivators and herders who needed seed, animals, or cash to meet tax payments. The sources rarely describe these intermediaries in detail, but fiscal and legal materials from the period show that debt and brokerage were pervasive features of rural-urban relations, and later sections discuss indebtedness as a factor in rural hardship.

Financial resources also circulated between town and countryside. Tax collections moved from villages toward administrative centers; revenues from endowed property supported urban institutions; and urban landholders, including officials and merchants, invested in rural estates. Wealthy officials and merchants who purchased or received land became absentee landlords, drawing income from villages they rarely visited. This arrangement could provide capital for agricultural improvement, but it could also direct rural surplus toward urban consumption and investment, leaving cultivators with limited resources.

The integration of towns and villages carries an important implication for the study of recovery. Urban building and commercial prosperity were not independent of the rural economy, but neither were they automatically evidence of its health. A city could prosper from tribute, from trade passing through it, or from the spending of a court, while its hinterland remained constrained by taxation and abandoned land. The remainder of this chapter returns repeatedly to this distinction, beginning in Division II with the countryside itself.

The relation between a rural surplus and a city's growth was mediated by institutions as well as by simple distance. A grain seller might face fees at a market gate, rent for storage, and a demand from an official or creditor before reaching an ordinary buyer. Local landholders and urban investors could finance agricultural activity, while merchants sometimes advanced funds against an expected crop. Such arrangements helped move products from field to market, but they also made producers vulnerable when harvests failed or officials made unexpected demands. A town with richly stocked bazaars therefore might draw its abundance from unequal bargaining power in its hinterland; the ability to purchase food in town cannot establish that food producers shared the resulting prosperity.

Agricultural Production and the Rural Economy

The agricultural economy of the Ilkhanate varied with ecology. On the plateau and in highland valleys, the basic staples were wheat and barley, grown through a combination of rain-fed cultivation in areas with sufficient winter precipitation and irrigated cultivation where water could be brought to the fields. Along the Caspian littoral, discussed in Chapter 1 as ecologically distinct, rice and other crops suited to heavy rainfall predominated, though much of that region lay at the margins of direct Ilkhanid control. In the south and in Khuzestan and Mesopotamia, irrigated cultivation supported dates, cotton, and, according to later descriptions, sugar cane. Orchards produced fruit and nuts in many districts, and viticulture supplied grapes and raisins, with wine production persisting in some communities despite Islamic restrictions.

Production depended on a bundle of resources beyond land and water. Plowing required draft animals, usually oxen, and the loss of animals to requisition, warfare, or disease could limit the area a household could cultivate. Seed grain had to be retained or borrowed, and in years of poor harvest, households that consumed their seed were forced into debt. Labor was seasonal and concentrated at planting and harvest, so that the loss of working-age men through military service or flight could reduce output. These interdependencies explain why a decree encouraging cultivation could not by itself produce a harvest; households needed oxen, seed, and security.

Equally important is the recognition that the rural population was not a single peasant class. Documentary and narrative sources point to several overlapping categories: smallholders who owned their land; tenants and sharecroppers who worked land owned by others and surrendered a share of the crop, a share that in later documented practice varied according to which party supplied water, seed, animals, and labor; hired agricultural laborers; village headmen and local notables who mediated between communities and officials; and pastoral and semi-pastoral groups whose relationship to cultivation varied. Status, access to resources, and vulnerability to taxation differed sharply among these groups, which is one reason the effects of the same policy could be so uneven.

The sources for agricultural production are limited in ways that matter. Fiscal handbooks, narrative chronicles, and geographical works record revenues and products, but they tend to describe the state's perspective and rarely provide systematic figures for yields, cultivated acreage, or rural population. Mustawfi's geographical work, written around 1340, lists products and revenue assessments for many districts, which historians have used with caution since the figures were compiled for fiscal purposes and cannot be treated as direct measures of production. This evidentiary problem returns in Section 50.19.

Crop choices reflected both ecology and access to buyers. Irrigated garden land could support fruits, vegetables, and other comparatively valuable produce, but maintaining such plots demanded dependable water and labor. Grain cultivation supplied a basic food reserve and tax base, while animal husbandry converted pasture into meat, wool, transport power, and marketable animals. These activities were not mutually exclusive: rural households might combine a small cultivated holding with seasonal grazing, or depend on access to communal and privately controlled water. To reconstruct the rural economy, historians must consider combinations of activity rather than impose a rigid separation between cultivator and herder.

Nor should references to agricultural wealth be read as quantitative surveys. Descriptions of fertile orchards or substantial rents often record an administrator's assessment of taxable potential, or a writer's praise of a city, rather than a count of harvests actually collected in successive years. Comparing districts requires attention to changes in assessed tax, collection practices, local prices, and the extent of the cultivated area. Where these indicators do not survive together, economic history must remain a set of bounded regional conclusions rather than an empire-wide growth estimate.

Section 50.6

Irrigation, Water Management, and Agricultural Infrastructure

Water, as Chapter 1 emphasized, was the most persistent constraint on settlement across the plateau. In the Ilkhanid period, as in earlier and later centuries, agriculture in much of the interior depended on engineered systems for moving water to fields: surface canals drawn from rivers, wells, and the underground channels known as qanats. The condition of this infrastructure was central to the possibility of agricultural recovery, and its maintenance was a collective and institutional task, not simply a technical one.

Historical Vocabulary
Qanat (kariz)

Definition: A qanat is an engineered underground channel, usually gently sloping, that carries groundwater from an aquifer at the foot of a mountain or hillside to the surface at a lower elevation, where it supplies irrigation and drinking water. A line of vertical shafts gives access for construction and cleaning.

Historical context: Qanats were used across the Iranian plateau and beyond for many centuries before the Ilkhanid period. A qanat required skilled specialists to dig, substantial labor and capital over a period before it produced reliable water, and continuous cleaning to prevent collapse and silting. Rights to its water were typically divided among shareholders, often by units of time, and disputes over shares and maintenance were common subjects of local custom and law.

Why it matters: Because a qanat's productivity depended on regular maintenance, neglect or depopulation could cause a system to fail even if it had not been deliberately destroyed. Restoring a failed qanat could demand more capital and labor than repairing a surface canal, which helps explain why agricultural recovery was slow and uneven.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the existence of qanats guaranteed prosperity, or that Mongol conquest destroyed the plateau's irrigation systems wholesale. Systems failed, survived, or were restored district by district, and the surviving evidence rarely allows confident estimates of how many were affected.

Operating an irrigation system required several things at once. It required capital, because digging or repairing a qanat or canal was expensive and the returns were delayed. It required expertise, because the specialists who built and maintained qanats and the officials who managed canal systems possessed skills that were not easily replaced. It required labor, often organized collectively by villages or through corvée obligations. It required agreements over access, since water shares were valuable property whose allocation was a frequent source of conflict. And it required continuity, because intermittent maintenance produced decline. Any interruption in these conditions, whether through depopulation, loss of expertise, collapse of the local elite that managed the system, or the diversion of labor to military or other service, could reduce a system's output.

The Mongol invasions and the unsettled decades that followed affected these systems unevenly. In districts where populations fled or were killed, maintenance could lapse, and canals silted up or channels collapsed. In districts that retained their communities, systems could continue to function. Mongol military use of land could also damage irrigation, as when herds trampled channels or when pastoral use displaced cultivation. Chapter 46 examines the evidence for destruction of irrigation and settlement; the present task is to assess the conditions of restoration.

Narrative sources credit some Ilkhanid rulers, viziers, and governors with irrigation works. Accounts report that officials had canals dug or repaired and that new estates were developed with water supplied by new channels; for example, narratives about the administration of Mesopotamia credit the historian and governor of Baghdad ʿAṭā-Malik Juvayni with the construction of a canal from the Anbar region toward Kufa and Najaf. Rashid al-Din and other wealthy officials are also said to have developed estates and suburbs supplied with new water works near Tabriz. Such reports show that irrigation development was an active field of investment for the powerful. They do not, however, establish how widespread restoration was. Many of the reports are brief, derive from authors who admired the patrons they described, and do not provide independent evidence of the area irrigated or the duration of the improvement.

A careful assessment therefore distinguishes three kinds of claim. Some irrigation works are documented in more than one source or are supported by surviving physical evidence. Others are reported in a single narrative source whose author had reasons to praise the patron. And some general statements that restoration was widespread rest on rhetorical rather than documentary foundations. The best-supported conclusion is that irrigation was restored in specific places, often those favored by powerful patrons or located near the court and major cities, while evidence for comparable restoration in peripheral or less-favored districts is thin.

A canal project in lowland Mesopotamia posed different technical problems from a qanat on the Iranian plateau. River water demanded embankments and regulation of flow, with possible damage from flooding or channel movement; an underground qanat required the excavation and clearing of shafts and tunnels at a workable gradient. Both systems depended on sustained authority over labor and water rights. The construction credited to the historian and governor ʿAta-Malik Juvayni along the Euphrates, toward the Kufa–Najaf region, is consequently valuable as a particular reported intervention in water management, not as evidence that the irrigation systems of Iraq generally returned to their earlier capacity.

Section 50.7

Landownership, Taxation, and the Position of Cultivators

Cultivators in the Ilkhanate stood at the lower end of a chain of claims on the surplus of the land. Understanding their position requires identifying the categories of land and the types of claimant, and then asking what the combination of rents, taxes, and obligations meant for rural security and incentives. This section concentrates on the economic consequences for producers, while the content and rationale of Ghazan's decrees remain the subject of Chapter 48.

Historical Vocabulary
Inju

Definition: In Ilkhanid sources, inju refers to estates and revenue-producing property belonging to the ruling Chinggisid family, including the personal property of the ruler, his household, and other members of the dynasty, as distinct from land belonging to the state treasury.

Historical context: Inju property was administered through its own officials and accounting structures and was distinct from the diwani land whose revenue went to the state treasury. Its extent grew through grants, confiscations, and purchases. Rashid al-Din's own career included responsibility for royal estates, which gives his writings particular importance for this subject.

Why it matters: The growth of inju property meant that large areas of agricultural land and their cultivators fell under a distinct administrative regime, with its own demands and protections. Whether cultivators on inju land fared better or worse than others varied with local management.

Potential misunderstanding: Inju should not be confused with private ownership in the ordinary sense, nor with state land. Its status derived from the ruling family's claim, and the boundaries among inju, state, endowed, and private land were sometimes contested.

Several categories of land coexisted. Diwani land, whose revenue went to the state treasury, was administered by the bureaucracy. Inju land belonged to the Chinggisid family. Iqtaʿ assignments gave military men the right to collect revenue from specified districts in lieu of salary, a system Chapter 48 discusses in the context of Ghazan's reform. Waqf property was endowed to support religious, educational, or charitable purposes, and mulk was private property. A given district could contain several of these categories, with different claimants collecting from different villages or even from the same village, and the layering of claims is a recurring feature of the sources.

Cultivators faced several types of demand. Land taxes, often called kharaj in older terminology, took a share of the crop or a fixed amount in cash or kind. Mongol-era taxes included the qubchur, a levy whose form and incidence varied over time and place, and which in some contexts was assessed on livestock and in others extended to settled populations. The tamgha was a tax on commercial transactions. Extraordinary levies, called ʿavariz, could be imposed for special purposes. Requisitions for the post system, for army provisioning, and for the entertainment of officials added further burdens. And tenants owed rents to landlords, whether private owners, military assignees, or endowment administrators.

The cumulative effect depended on how much of these claims was collected, how predictably, and by whom. Predictable taxation, even if substantial, allowed households to plan and invest. Arbitrary, repeated, and overlapping demands discouraged investment and encouraged flight, borrowing, and the concealment of resources. Indebtedness to moneylenders and landlords tied many cultivators to creditors, and tax collectors who farmed or advanced revenue had incentives to extract as much as possible. Narrative sources from the period, including those favorable to the regime, record complaints about the extortion of tax collectors and the flight of peasants from heavily burdened districts. Historians such as I. P. Petrushevsky, whose studies shaped later interpretations, emphasized the severity of exploitation, while later scholars have cautioned that his conclusions rely heavily on narrative evidence and fit a broader interpretive framework that requires scrutiny.

For cultivators, then, the economic meaning of Ilkhanid rule was determined less by the total claims of the state than by the mix of claimants, the regularity of demands, and the security of tenure. A village on well-managed inju land near the court might fare differently from a village burdened by multiple military assignees and unpredictable levies, and the surviving sources cannot be generalized across the countryside without caution. What they do establish is that rural security was a central condition of recovery, and that attempts at regulation were responses to real problems.

These claims could be layered. A cultivator might work land whose nominal ownership belonged to one household, whose revenue had been assigned to another, and whose produce was assessed by officials of a central treasury. In practice the legal categories found in fiscal writing did not always map neatly onto effective control. A tax assignment gave its beneficiary a claim to income; it did not necessarily transfer the land itself or guarantee that the assignee could maintain irrigation and settle disputes. An assessment of rural security therefore has to ask who could enforce demands, whether several demands overlapped, and what recourse a cultivating family had when a powerful collector disregarded orders.

Section 50.8

Reclamation of Abandoned Lands and Agricultural Restoration

The Ilkhanid state, particularly under Ghazan and his ministers, undertook or at least announced measures to restore cultivation. The narrative sources, above all Rashid al-Din's account in the Tarikh-i Ghazani, describe an effort to identify uncultivated land, to classify land by its condition, to encourage settlement and production, and to provide cultivators with resources. The question this section asks is how much of this is demonstrated and how much is reported intention.

The reported measures fall into several groups. One concerned information: officials were to determine what land was cultivated, what was abandoned, and what was in what condition, a precondition for assessing taxes and allocating resources. A second concerned inputs: cultivators were to be assisted with seed, animals, and implements, so that they could resume or expand production. A third concerned land: abandoned or uncultivated lands could be assigned to individuals or groups who would bring them under cultivation, with relief from some taxes for a period. A fourth concerned security: officials and soldiers were to be restrained from seizing cultivators' property and animals, and peasants who had fled were to be encouraged to return.

These measures, if effectively carried out, would address several of the constraints identified earlier in this chapter: the shortage of draft animals and seed, the loss of cultivators, the insecurity of tenure, and the uncertainty of taxation. Their logic was coherent. The Ilkhanid state had a fiscal interest in cultivation, since abandoned land produced no revenue, and the reform-minded administrators who authored or implemented these policies understood that cultivators could not be taxed if they could not produce. That convergence of state interest and cultivator need is one reason historians take the policies seriously.

The evidence for results, however, is far weaker than the evidence for intentions. The principal sources are narrative accounts and decrees reported by the officials who designed and implemented the policies, and they present the program in a favorable light. Independent evidence, such as detailed cadastral records, tax registers showing rising acreage, or archaeological survey data demonstrating renewed settlement, is scarce and uneven. Ann K. S. Lambton, whose analysis of the period remains foundational, noted that the sources report attempts at reclamation but do not allow historians to determine how fully they were implemented or how successful they were. Provisioning cultivators with seed and animals was costly, local officials and landholders had interests that conflicted with the program, and the political authority to enforce it was strongest near the center.

A cautious conclusion distinguishes among propositions. It is well supported that the Ilkhanid government, at least for a time, adopted a policy aimed at restoring cultivation and protecting producers. It is plausible that this policy contributed to local recovery in places where officials enforced it and where the physical and human conditions for cultivation survived. It is not established that the policy produced broad recovery of agricultural output across the Ilkhanate, nor that its effects endured after the political conditions that sustained it weakened. Those limits shape the evaluation of recovery in the final division of this chapter.

There was also a timing problem at the heart of reclamation. Seed and draft animals had to be available before sowing, whereas taxes and rents ordinarily depended on a later crop. If assistance came too late, cultivators could not use it; if officials demanded repayment before a productive holding was established, a program intended to restore cultivation could reproduce indebtedness. The distinction matters because a description of available loans or land grants says little about whether the recipients could bear their terms. Rashid al-Din's account is evidence that the government recognized this problem, but it does not supply the local account books needed to trace the results for households.

Tabriz: Political Center and Commercial Metropolis

Tabriz is the principal urban case study of this chapter because the sources are richest for it and because it combined political and commercial roles more fully than any other Ilkhanid city. It lay in Azerbaijan, within reach of the summer pastures and winter quarters used by the court, and it stood at the junction of routes running west toward Anatolia and the Black Sea, north toward the Caucasus, east toward Khurasan and Central Asia, and south toward the central plateau and the Gulf. Under the Ilkhans, and especially from the later thirteenth century, it became the main administrative and commercial center of the realm. That importance should be understood as the result of several overlapping causes, not as a natural destiny of the city's location.

Its political functions generated economic activity. The presence of the court for part of each year, the offices of the central administration, the mints, and the households of viziers, commanders, and princes created demand for goods and services and employment for scribes, accountants, artisans, and servants. Ilkhanid rulers and their ministers invested in the city. Ghazan is reported to have built an extensive suburb, known as Sham-i Ghazan, that included a tomb complex and a range of endowed institutions, and Rashid al-Din established a large endowed quarter that included residences, a mosque, and facilities for scholars and the poor, though the manuscript-production activities of this complex are treated in Chapter 49. Other high officials also sponsored mosques and public works. These projects drew labor, materials, and craftsmen into the city and gave its built landscape a recognizable character as a place of both political display and economic activity. Only a limited portion of the medieval fabric survives, and the extensively documented historic bazaar complex of Tabriz is largely the product of later centuries, built upon the commercial role the city held in this period; students should therefore be cautious about using surviving structures as direct evidence for Ilkhanid markets.

Its commercial functions were equally important. European merchants, particularly those from the Italian maritime cities, are recorded in Tabriz from the late thirteenth century, and merchant handbooks of the early fourteenth century describe the route from the Black Sea port of Trebizond to the city. Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, and other merchants also traded there, and the city served as a point of exchange between goods from the Mediterranean, the Black Sea, Central Asia, the Gulf, and beyond. Textiles, silk, spices, gems, and other goods are among the commodities associated with its markets in the sources, and the existence of an administrative and fiscal apparatus capable of taxing this commerce through levies such as the tamgha tied the city's commercial success to the state's revenue.

The city's economy also depended on its surroundings. The demands of a large urban population for grain, meat, fodder, firewood, and building materials meant that Tabriz relied on the agricultural and pastoral production of Azerbaijan, a relationship examined in Section 50.4. Wealthy residents invested in nearby estates and gardens, linking urban capital to rural production. The prosperity of Tabriz was therefore bound up with that of a hinterland whose own condition varied, and with the continuing presence of the court and the stability of the political order that sustained it.

The city's economic prominence was not created by the Ilkhans alone. Tabriz had long served as a regional center, and its position linking the plateau, Caucasus, and routes toward Anatolia enabled merchants and institutions to adjust as political power shifted. Court patronage strengthened that advantage by bringing officials and consumers into proximity with established bazaars. Yet the geographic advantage was conditional: peace along a route, availability of transport animals, and the policies of neighboring rulers could affect whether caravans actually used it. A historian who attributes every sign of urban prosperity to a single khan therefore overlooks the inherited commercial infrastructure on which Ilkhanid patronage depended.

Cause and Effect

Why Tabriz Became the Commercial Hub of the Ilkhanate

Long-term causes

A position at the junction of routes toward Anatolia, the Black Sea, the Caucasus, Khurasan, and the plateau; a productive Azerbaijani hinterland; a commercial tradition that predated the Mongols; and survival of its markets through early submission to Mongol forces.

Short-term causes

The location of Ilkhanid political life in the northwest, the concentration of the central administration and mints in the city, and the investment of rulers and ministers in building and endowments.

Effects

The city drew merchants, artisans, officials, and scholars; attracted European and regional commercial networks; and became a principal source of tax and customs revenue. Its prosperity encouraged contemporary writers to describe it in superlatives.

Long-term consequences and complexity

The city's wealth rested partly on court presence and political favor, so it was exposed to political change. Its prosperity also cannot be taken as a measure of conditions in the Ilkhanate generally, since much of it derived from tribute, trade passing through, and spending by elites rather than from broad growth in production.

Historical complexity: No single cause made Tabriz prosper. Geography made it a possible hub; political decisions, merchant networks, and state investment made it an actual one.

Al-Umari, writing from reports supplied by others, emphasizes the city's commercial importance and the wealth of its merchants and rulers.

Analysis: Read together, the two sources show that Tabriz held a reputation as a leading commercial and political center, both inside the Ilkhanid realm and in neighboring regions such as Mamluk Syria. They also reveal different ways of knowing a city: Mustawfi as a specialist in revenue and place with direct administrative familiarity, al-Umari as a compiler of reports for an audience in another state. The agreement of two independent traditions on the city's prominence strengthens the case that its status was real.

Limitations: Both are retrospective or secondhand. Neither provides reliable population figures, and statements of size and wealth reflect literary praise as well as observation. Fiscal figures attributed to districts represent assessments, not measured production or household income. Both authors wrote for audiences with particular interests, and neither can be used to claim prosperity for the countryside or for other cities.

Perspective: Both represent elite, administrative, and scholarly viewpoints. They say little about artisans, laborers, cultivators, or the poor of Tabriz, and nothing that would show how the city's prosperity was distributed.

Section 50.10

Sultaniyya and the Creation of a New Ilkhanid Capital

Sultaniyya offers a contrast with Tabriz. Where Tabriz grew through long commercial and political accumulation, Sultaniyya was a planned royal center in the northwestern plateau, on a plain long used for pasture and seasonal encampment. Arghun began developing the settlement around 1290; Öljeitü greatly enlarged and monumentalized it, principally between 1305 and 1313. The site lay on routes connecting Azerbaijan to the central plateau and Mesopotamia, and its meadows made it suitable for a court that still moved with its herds. Founding a monumental capital on a pastoral landscape expresses the ambiguity of Ilkhanid rulership: a dynasty with steppe habits investing heavily in permanent architecture.

The building program was substantial. Öljeitü's mausoleum, the most famous surviving structure, is crowned by a large dome and illustrates the technical capacity and resources that royal patronage could mobilize. Surrounding it were walls, mosques, markets, and residences, and officials and notables were encouraged or compelled to build in the new city. Narrative sources report that leading figures, including high ministers, sponsored construction, and that population was drawn to the city, sometimes from elsewhere, though reports of the scale and the extent of coercion are not reliably quantified. A royal foundation of this kind generated demand for builders, masons, tile makers, carpenters, laborers, and suppliers of materials, and it required food and fuel from the surrounding countryside.

The relationship between the new city and its productive hinterland is a key question that the sources answer only partially. A city built beside royal pastures could draw on livestock and the products of herding, but sustaining a large permanent population required agricultural supply and water. The development of estates and irrigation to support such a foundation is plausible and is suggested by general reports of royal and ministerial investment, but evidence on the specifics is limited. The long-term viability of the city depended on whether the court and administration continued to reside there; a capital sustained largely by royal presence is vulnerable when the court moves or the dynasty weakens.

The coexistence of Sultaniyya with continued seasonal movement illustrates the point of this division. Even after the city was founded, the royal government continued to move through its seasonal cycle, so Sultaniyya functioned as one node in a mobile political geography, not as a fixed seat that replaced Tabriz or the encampments. Tabriz remained a major commercial center, and rulers and ministers invested in both. The Ilkhanate thus had a plural urban system in which different cities fulfilled different political and economic roles, and in which royal favor could elevate a site quickly but could not guarantee its long-term economic independence.

Arghun's earlier development of a settlement at Sultaniyya is important because it separates the selection of a promising pastoral site from the later decision to fashion it into a monumental capital. Öljeitü did not build upon vacant ground without precedent. The major expansion of the city mobilized architects, brickmakers, transport workers, and suppliers, generating demand that could outlast the immediate building campaign if permanent markets and institutions remained active. The great mausoleum is exceptionally visible evidence of this expenditure, but its survival is not a statistical record of wages paid, food prices, or the number of people who settled permanently near it.

Section 50.11

Regional Cities: Shiraz, Isfahan, Yazd, Kerman, and Beyond

Beyond the northwestern capitals, the cities of the plateau and south followed their own trajectories, shaped by provincial politics, local elites, religious institutions, and economic specialization. Treating them as copies of Tabriz would obscure these differences.

Shiraz, the principal city of Fars, had avoided the worst destruction through the submission of its Salghurid rulers and remained a center of religious learning, literary life, and regional administration. Its economy combined the administration of a populous province with trade along routes toward the Gulf and the central plateau. As direct Mongol administration replaced Salghurid rule, the city and province became subject to heavier revenue demands, and the local elite navigated a succession of changing political arrangements. The sources for Shiraz are richer for literary and religious life than for commerce, which limits what can be said about its markets.

Isfahan, in central Iran, had a long history as a regional commercial and administrative center, and its recovery in the Ilkhanid period was gradual and uneven, shaped by the experience of earlier conquest and by the fortunes of its local notable families. It lay on routes connecting the northwest with the south and east, which supported its role in regional trade. The surviving evidence from the period is thinner than for Tabriz, and the city's greater prominence in later centuries should not be projected backward.

Yazd developed in a desert-margin environment that depended on carefully managed water and on textile production and regional commerce. Reports, including those associated with European travelers, mention its cloth production and trade, although the reliability of such accounts must be weighed. Under local dynasties that accepted Mongol overlordship, Yazd enjoyed relative security. Kerman, governed by the Qutlughkhanid dynasty, linked the interior to Gulf trade and to Khurasan, and its local rulers, including powerful women of the dynasty, patronized building and administration under Mongol suzerainty. Both cities show how local dynasties and elites could preserve stability and economic activity under distant Ilkhanid authority.

Several smaller centers illustrate further variation. Kashan produced ceramics and tiles for patrons within and beyond the region. Hamadan, Qum, and other towns of Iraq-i Ajam served as regional markets and religious centers. Maragha had a distinct role as the site of the observatory founded under Hülegü, supported by endowed revenues. Baghdad remained a major city in Mesopotamia under Ilkhanid governors, with a commercial and administrative role that differed from that of the Iranian highland cities.

Differences among these cities reflect provincial political conditions, from direct Ilkhanid administration to semi-autonomous local dynasties, and the nature of their hinterlands and routes. They also reflect the unevenness of the evidence: because the sources favor the court and its cities, historians know much more about Tabriz and the capital sites than about regional centers. The relatively thin evidence for provincial cities does not prove that they were poor, nor that they were prosperous; it means that conclusions about them must be limited and provisional.

A comparison of these cities must also distinguish the date of a source from the period it describes. A geographical author writing after 1335 might present a city whose tax base had already changed, while a traveler could report impressions formed during a brief visit. Local dynasties and religious families might leave better documentary traces than artisans or wage earners. The appearance of a city in a famous account can thus reflect the survival of a text and the interests of its author as much as its relative economic scale. The absence of detailed reporting for a town should never be treated automatically as proof that it lacked productive life.

Section 50.12

Artisans, Workshops, Marketplaces, and Urban Livelihoods

Cities existed to make and exchange goods as well as to administer territory. The artisans and traders whose work filled the bazaars are poorly documented as individuals, but material evidence and scattered textual references allow historians to describe the principal branches of production and the kinds of demand that sustained them.

Textile production was probably the most widespread and commercially significant craft. Weavers, dyers, and finishers produced cotton, wool, linen, and silk cloth for local households and for trade. Luxury textiles, including gold-woven brocades known in the sources as nasij, were prized by Mongol elites and appear in descriptions of courtly display and in lists of gifts. The demand of courts for rich cloth for clothing, tents, and diplomatic presentation was a significant stimulus to specialized production, and workshops associated with the court are reported in some sources. The scale of this production, and the relationship between court-linked and independent workshops, cannot be reconstructed in detail.

Metalwork flourished in this period, and surviving inlaid brass and other metal objects demonstrate sophisticated technique and elite patronage. Ceramics provide stronger material evidence. The production of lusterware and tilework at Kashan, and of other decorated wares associated with sites in the central plateau, continued and developed, supplying architectural tilework for royal and religious buildings as well as vessels for domestic and commercial use. The excavated Ilkhanid palace complex at Takht-i Sulayman in Azerbaijan, associated with Abaqa and his successors, contained decorated tiles and illustrates how the architectural demands of rulers could sustain specialized ceramic production. Construction trades, including masons, bricklayers, carpenters, plasterers, and tile setters, were in constant demand for royal and endowed buildings, and the great domed structures of the period depended on their skills.

The crafts connected with books and writing, including paper-making, binding, calligraphy, and illumination, belonged to a related world of skilled labor, whose manuscript-production dimension Chapter 49 treats. For this chapter the point is economic: such crafts relied on patrons, including courts, viziers, and endowed institutions, and on supplies of paper, pigments, and other materials that moved through commercial channels.

Artisans worked in markets and workshops, often grouped by trade in particular streets or sections of the bazaar, a pattern well documented for later periods and likely, though not always directly attested, for this one. Masters, journeymen, apprentices, and unskilled laborers occupied different positions, and merchants who supplied raw materials and marketed finished goods could exert considerable control over producers. Shopkeepers, brokers, porters, and others who made the bazaar function formed a broad urban working population. The sources provide little reliable information on wages, working hours, or the size of occupational groups, and historians should not infer such details from the general descriptions available.

Demand came from several quarters. Courts and the households of the elite purchased luxury goods and commissioned buildings. Religious foundations required construction, furnishings, books, and lamps. Local households purchased cloth, vessels, tools, and food. Distant markets purchased export goods, including textiles and ceramics. The diversity of demand helps explain how specialized production could be sustained in cities, and it also meant that artisans were exposed to changes in patronage: the withdrawal of a court or the loss of a patron could rapidly reduce the market for luxury crafts.

A workshop's connection to wealthy patrons could be beneficial and constraining at the same time. Commissioned tiles or textiles required skilled labor, raw materials, and predictable payment; patrons could enable expensive production that ordinary buyers could not finance. Yet the withdrawal of court orders might leave skilled artisans with few alternative customers, and a ruler's preference for work in another city could shift demand abruptly. Surviving decorated objects are strongest as evidence of technical skill, style, and elite consumption. They are less direct evidence of the income of individual workers or the volume of ordinary household production, much of which used perishable materials and has not survived.

Everyday Life and Social History

Livelihoods in Town and Countryside

The documents of the period concentrate on rulers, ministers, and merchants, but the economic world they describe rested on ordinary labor. A cultivator in an irrigated village rose to the rhythm of water turns and planting seasons, depended on a small number of animals, and owed shares to landlords, assignees, and tax collectors. A pastoral family moved between winter and summer pasture and sold animals, wool, and dairy products to towns and encampments. An artisan in an urban workshop, perhaps a weaver or a tile maker, depended on suppliers of raw material and on patrons or merchants who purchased finished work. A porter or caravan hand carried goods along routes whose danger and expense fell partly on those who did the carrying.

Women's economic roles were also significant, though poorly recorded outside elite settings. In rural households, women's labor in agriculture, animal care, textile spinning, and food processing was essential to household income. In the Mongol aristocracy, royal women controlled estates and invested in commerce, as Section 50.14 shows. For most social groups, however, direct evidence of women's work is thin. Historians therefore cannot assume that the experiences of elite or court-linked individuals represent those of the population, and must supplement narrative sources with legal, archaeological, and material evidence where possible. They also cannot assume that a prosperous bazaar meant comfort for those who worked in it.

Caravan Routes, Transport, and Commercial Infrastructure

Trade across the Ilkhanate moved along routes shaped by geography and by older patterns of settlement. Moving goods over land was expensive and risky, and the routes that carried commerce were determined by water, pasture, mountain passes, and the location of cities and markets. This section reconstructs the principal routes and the infrastructure that supported them.

Westward routes from Tabriz ran through the northwest toward Anatolia and the Black Sea, with Trebizond serving as a principal port for goods moving to and from the Mediterranean and Black Sea commercial worlds, and with another outlet at Ayas on the Cilician coast of the eastern Mediterranean. Northward routes crossed the Caucasus toward the Caspian and the Black Sea steppe, though the rivalry between the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde made these routes politically contested. Southwestward routes led to Mesopotamia and Baghdad. Eastward and southeastward routes crossed the plateau through cities such as Sultaniyya, Rayy, Kashan, Isfahan, and Yazd toward Khurasan, Central Asia, Kerman, and the Persian Gulf. These routes tended to follow the mountain margins and oasis chains rather than crossing the interior deserts directly, a pattern discussed in Chapter 1.

Infrastructure supported movement along these routes. Roads required maintenance, bridges and fords had to be kept passable, and caravanserais provided lodging, storage, and security for merchants and animals. Relatively few caravanserais securely dated to the Ilkhanid period survive in Iran, and historians therefore rely on a combination of textual references, later structures, and comparison with better-preserved earlier and later examples in neighboring regions. Rulers, officials, and wealthy individuals sponsored such facilities, sometimes as endowments, and the yam relay system provided communication and travel facilities primarily for officials and authorized travelers, with the burdens of provisioning falling on local communities.

Moving goods required pack animals, especially camels and mules, and the labor of caravan drivers, porters, and guides. Costs included the hire or purchase and feeding of animals, payment of guards and guides, tolls and customs duties, and losses from theft or accident. Customs stations and tolls, including the tamgha, imposed charges along the way, and the multiplicity of authorities could make long journeys costly. Security depended on local conditions, the presence of troops, and arrangements with powerful tribes or local rulers along the route.

The phrase Pax Mongolica, often used for the period, can mislead if taken literally. Mongol imperial power did reduce certain political barriers and enabled merchants and envoys to travel across large distances under a degree of protection, and merchants with official protection could travel more safely. But the Mongol world was divided among rival successor states whose conflicts closed or endangered important routes, and the Ilkhanate's wars with the Golden Horde and the Mamluks repeatedly disrupted trade. Banditry, local rebellion, and fiscal exactions also persisted. Commercial security was real in some places and periods and absent in others, and it was never uniform.

Transport costs created strong differences between what could move profitably over long distances and what usually circulated locally. High-value goods could justify extended caravan journeys, escort expenses, tolls, and the risk of loss, whereas bulky grain normally depended more heavily on nearby productive districts unless particular conditions made distant supply worthwhile. This did not mean routes carried luxuries alone: animals, metals, textiles, and food moved within overlapping regional networks. It means that the apparent sophistication of long-distance commerce should not be mistaken for an integrated market in which prices and supplies were the same throughout the Ilkhanate.

Section 50.14

Merchants, Partnerships, Credit, and Commercial Finance

Commerce on this scale required capital, trust, and ways of sharing risk. Merchants worked through partnerships, agents, and credit arrangements that extended across cities and regions, and in the Mongol world these arrangements were closely tied to the households of rulers and aristocrats.

Historical Vocabulary
Ortoq (urtaq)

Definition: An ortoq was a merchant partner of a Mongol ruler, prince, or princess, who received capital or silver from the patron and traded with it, returning a portion of the profit and enjoying the patron's protection and privileges.

Historical context: The ortoq relationship appears widely in sources for the Mongol empire. It linked the commercial skills and networks of merchants, who were often Muslim, Uyghur, or from other groups, to the wealth and political authority of the Chinggisid family. Elite women, including khatuns, participated as patrons and investors, and their economic activities are an important subject of recent scholarship.

Why it matters: The system shows that ruling households did not simply consume tribute and taxation but invested in trade through intermediaries, which tied commerce to political power and could give merchants privileged access to protection, transport, and exemptions.

Potential misunderstanding: Ortoq merchants should not be seen as mere servants or as fully independent traders. The relationship involved mutual obligations and varied with the patron, the period, and the region, and it could expose merchants to risk when patrons fell from power.

Merchants combined capital through partnerships in which one party supplied funds and another traveled and traded, dividing profits according to agreed terms. Agents represented principals in distant markets, and letters and accounts recorded transactions. Credit was pervasive: merchants bought goods on credit, advanced funds to producers, and lent to officials and landholders. Moneylenders and financial intermediaries played an important role, and the need to move funds across distances encouraged practices of remittance and written instruments, though the details for this period are only partly documented.

The ortoq relationship connected private commercial wealth to elite households. Princes and princesses provided capital and, in return, gained a share of profits and access to goods and information. Research, notably by Bruno De Nicola on Mongol women and the economy, shows that elite women controlled substantial resources, owned or managed estates, and invested through merchant intermediaries, and that they were significant economic actors rather than passive recipients of royal wealth. Royal women maintained their own households, called ordos, with revenues and staff, and their patronage extended to building and religious endowments. The evidence for specific amounts is limited, but the pattern of female economic agency is well established.

The links between merchants and political elites also created risk. Merchants who relied on a patron's protection could suffer when the patron died or lost favor. Officials and wealthy merchants who rose through fiscal service were vulnerable to confiscation, and the fates of several prominent ministers illustrate this vulnerability. The failed paper currency of 1294, discussed in Section 50.4, also shows how state fiscal needs could clash with merchants' interests and with the practical requirements of exchange. Metal coin, rather than paper, remained the medium that merchants and townspeople trusted, and later reforms in coinage, studied in Chapter 48 and in the source window below on coins and fiscal manuals, aimed at restoring confidence in a standard currency.

The term ortoq, often rendered as merchant partner, identifies a relationship whose institutional shape varied across the Mongol imperial world. A commercial intermediary could work with capital supplied by a ruler or a high-ranking household, benefit from privileged protection, and repay an agreed return or share the proceeds. Such networks connected political access with enterprise, but the surviving evidence does not justify treating every merchant as an ortoq or assuming that all agreements followed a single standardized contract. Merchant agency remained considerable: traders chose routes, evaluated goods, maintained correspondents, and negotiated uncertain market conditions.

Credit relations created another layer of interdependence. Merchant capital might finance a journey long before sale proceeds returned, while a producer needed an advance at the start of a growing or manufacturing cycle. Trust could rest on kinship, reputation, repeated transactions, religious or community networks, guarantees, and the capacity to enforce agreements. The sources reveal some elaborate long-distance connections but give much less detail about the everyday bargaining of local borrowers. Economic historians therefore reconstruct the functions of credit with caution, avoiding claims that all transactions relied on a single banking institution or universally used financial instrument.

Elite women's participation requires equally careful attention to evidence and social differentiation. Bruno De Nicola demonstrates that khatuns could possess substantial movable wealth, estates, gifts, and revenue rights and could direct economic resources through their own households and commercial agents. Their influence did not mean that ordinary women enjoyed comparable opportunities, nor that political power automatically translated into independent management of every transaction. An estate controlled by a royal woman might support religious patronage or commercial activity while also generating taxes and labor demands for producers. The same case can therefore illuminate elite female agency and the unequal distribution of resources within Mongol Iran.

Political protection itself had a price. A patron could offer a merchant information, permission to travel, or help in recovering debts, but also expect favored supplies, financial advances, or extraordinary contributions. When a patron fell, the trader might lose access to markets or find assets exposed to seizure. Court-connected merchants therefore occupied a position of opportunity and vulnerability. The richer the evidence for their success, the more important it is to ask whose transactions have been preserved and whether lesser merchants outside high political networks could operate on similar terms.

Section 50.15

Eurasian Commerce and the Exchange of Goods

Ilkhanid Iran lay between several major commercial zones: China and Central Asia to the east, Anatolia, the Black Sea, and the Mediterranean to the west, the Caucasus and steppe to the north, and the Indian Ocean to the south. Goods, merchants, envoys, and ideas moved through these connections, and Mongol imperial networks facilitated some of them.

Textiles and silk were among the most important commodities. Silk, raw and woven, moved from Iranian and Caucasian production areas and from further east toward Mediterranean markets, and Italian merchants purchased it in quantity. Precious metals, gems, and spices also passed through Iranian markets. Horses were a major commodity, bred on the pastures of Iran and Central Asia and exported to India in particular; luxury manufactures, including Chinese porcelain and silk, reached Iranian elites, and Rashid al-Din's writings reflect awareness of Chinese goods and knowledge. Iranian products, including textiles and ceramics, circulated in return.

It is important to distinguish among different kinds of exchange. Diplomatic exchanges involved gifts and embassies between rulers, including the Ilkhans, the Yuan court, the Mamluks, and European powers; these transferred valuable goods but did not constitute commercial trade. Merchant commerce was driven by profit and carried by private traders, sometimes in partnership with elites. State-supported transportation, including the use of the yam by privileged travelers, facilitated the movement of envoys and certain goods. And cultural transmission, such as the movement of artistic motifs, scientific knowledge, and technologies, accompanied these exchanges but is a distinct historical process, whose intellectual dimensions appear in other chapters.

Mongol networks helped some exchanges by reducing barriers, protecting favored merchants, and creating shared political authority over large spaces. They did not eliminate limits. The Ilkhanate's political rivalries with the Golden Horde and the Mamluks interrupted trade at various times, with the 1323 peace with the Mamluks reducing but not ending tension. Taxation, including the tamgha and customs duties, took a share of profits. Insecurity, local conflicts, and the burdens of requisition added costs. European merchants, particularly Italians, operated through agreements with the Ilkhans and in colonies or trading posts on the Black Sea and eastern Mediterranean, and their records, such as merchant handbooks of the early fourteenth century, provide valuable but partial evidence about prices, routes, and the commodities that interested them.

Consequently, Eurasian commerce was real and important for the economies of Tabriz and other trading centers, but it should not be assumed to have benefited all regions or all social groups equally. Its volume cannot be measured with confidence. Its benefits concentrated in cities and among merchants and elites with access to the networks, while rural producers and most urban workers were largely at its margins.

The term 'Pax Mongolica' is useful only with qualifications. A traveler moving between friendly Mongol jurisdictions could encounter overlapping systems of diplomatic protection and commercial privilege, but movement across the borders of rival khanates was vulnerable to war and changing alliances. The Ilkhans' relationships with the Yuan court did not abolish the separate interests of the Golden Horde or the Chaghatayid rulers. Maritime commerce likewise involved authorities outside Mongol territory. Instead of treating Eurasian connectivity as the consequence of a single blanket peace, the chapter should identify the specific political corridors, intermediaries, and periods in which merchants could put wider connections to use.

Section 50.16

Persian Gulf Ports and Indian Ocean Trade

The southern face of Ilkhanid Iran looked toward the Persian Gulf and the Indian Ocean. Overland routes from Fars and Kerman led to ports on the Gulf coast and islands, where goods moved by sea to Arabia, India, East Africa, and farther east. This maritime dimension, introduced in Chapter 1 as a permanent feature of Iranian history, was an important part of the Ilkhanid economy.

The principal commercial centers of the period included the island of Qays (Kish) and the port of Hormuz on the southern coast, the latter a southern Iranian entrepôt whose role would be reshaped by the movement of its commercial center offshore. Merchants based in these ports and in Fars and Kerman carried goods to India and beyond. Horses were exported from Iran and Arabia to southern India, a trade reported by both Persian and European sources, and imports included spices, textiles, pearls, and other goods. Wassaf also describes merchant-administrators operating at Qays under Ilkhanid authority. Such accounts illustrate the intersection of commerce and political office, but need to be read with attention to their rhetorical character.

Provincial authorities and local rulers played a major role in this trade. The governors and dynasts of Fars and Kerman, and the rulers of the island and coastal polities, taxed and protected trade, controlled access to harbors, and sometimes participated directly. Local intermediaries, including shipowners, brokers, and merchants familiar with languages and markets across the Indian Ocean, were essential, since Ilkhanid officials in the northwest had limited direct knowledge of maritime conditions. Inland routes linked the ports to the interior and, through Kerman and Yazd, to the chain of oasis cities discussed earlier.

It is useful to separate long-distance maritime commerce from regional coastal trade. Long-distance voyages across the Arabian Sea required large capital, specialized ships and crews, and knowledge of monsoon winds, and they concentrated wealth in a small number of ports and merchant houses. Regional coastal trade in food, fish, dates, and local goods supported smaller communities and was less visible in the sources. The relative weight of each is difficult to measure. What can be said is that the Gulf economy possessed its own dynamics, partly independent of the northwest, and that its prosperity depended on local political arrangements and on the security of both sea and land routes.

The relocation of Hormuz illustrates commercial adaptation to military insecurity. Under Baha al-Din Ayaz, who took power in 1296, the town shifted from the mainland site near Minab to Jarun Island around 1300. The new island town came to bear the name Hormuz and drew trade partly because an island position offered different defensive and maritime advantages. That did not remove dependence on food, water, shipping, and caravan connections with the mainland. A successful port needed more than a defensible harbor: it required predictable relations with inland suppliers and rulers who could keep approaches usable.

Qays provides a complementary case of commercial authority entwined with provincial administration. Jamal al-Din Ibrahim al-Tibi, who died in 1306, appears in the sources as a merchant-administrator whose influence stretched through the Gulf trading world and whose position was connected to Ilkhanid government in Fars. His prominence demonstrates that port wealth was not wholly separate from fiscal power. Accounts of remarkable revenues or fleets, however, are reports transmitted by named chroniclers rather than verified shipping ledgers. The point of the example is the political organization of commerce and the opportunities created by a connected maritime market, not any apparently precise measure of its volume.

Trade with Indian Ocean ports also depended on the specialized movement of horses. Horses sourced from Iran and other western Asian regions could command demand in South Asian markets where military elites purchased imported animals, but the route involved difficult sea transport, losses, and intermediary costs. Spices, precious stones, textiles, and pearls moved in other directions, passing through multiple commercial hands before reaching inland consumers. Since many carriers and purchasers left little written record, reconstructing a commodity chain requires combining travelers' descriptions, locally produced documents, and evidence from the wider Indian Ocean rather than relying only on a Persian court chronicle.

Restoration Projects, Endowments, and Economic Investment

Much of what survives of Ilkhanid investment took the form of buildings and endowments. Royal households, wealthy administrators, merchants, and religious families mobilized resources for mosques, madrasas, shrines, hospitals, bridges, caravanserais, water systems, and urban amenities. Understanding the economic functioning of these projects shows how wealth was converted into durable institutions and who controlled it.

The central instrument was the waqf, or endowment. A founder dedicated property, such as agricultural land, shops, baths, or caravanserais, to a stated purpose, and the revenue paid for the upkeep and operation of a mosque, school, hospital, or charitable service. The founder usually appointed an administrator, often a family member, and laid out in a deed the property, the beneficiaries, and the rules of management. The endowment thus created an economic enterprise: it owned productive assets, collected rents and revenues, and paid salaries to teachers, custodians, physicians, and others. Endowed institutions employed people, stimulated local markets, and anchored neighborhoods.

Endowment also served the interests of founders. Property endowed to a family-administered waqf was relatively protected from confiscation and could provide for descendants, which made endowment attractive to officials and wealthy families operating in an environment of political risk. Rashid al-Din's elaborate endowment deed, which survives, shows how a leading minister dedicated extensive properties to support an urban quarter with a range of institutions, and it is a rare example of detailed documentary evidence for the scale and organization of such an enterprise. Its manuscript-related dimensions belong to Chapter 49; here its value is as evidence for how revenue-producing property was assembled and managed. Yet the later execution of Rashid al-Din and the reported seizure of property show that endowment did not guarantee security when a patron fell politically.

Royal patronage worked on a larger scale. Ghazan's suburb at Tabriz, Öljeitü's Sultaniyya, and the buildings sponsored by ministers and princesses mobilized labor, materials, and revenue on a scale beyond the capacity of private individuals. Such projects could stimulate employment and local trade, and endowed institutions could provide services, such as medical care and education, that benefited urban populations. They also concentrated resources in a few places, usually cities favored by the court, and the revenues that supported them often came from rural estates, drawing wealth from villages toward urban institutions.

Investment in infrastructure outside the capitals is less well documented. Reports of bridges, caravanserais, and water works exist, and some surviving buildings can be dated to the period, but a systematic account is not possible. Maintenance posed a continuing problem: an endowment depended on competent administrators and on the stability of the properties that funded it, and political crisis or mismanagement could diminish its capacity. The durability of Ilkhanid investment thus depended on the continuing authority of the institutions and families that managed it.

An endowment converted selected private or royal resources into a legally regulated stream of support for named functions, but its financial strength depended on the underlying assets. A school, mosque, hospital, or water facility could be granted rents from shops, farmland, or orchards; if those properties failed to yield income, the institution could struggle despite its impressive deed. Endowments also could reflect competition for legitimacy and the desire to safeguard elite wealth. Historians must therefore distinguish the existence of an endowed institution from evidence that it delivered services continuously to a broad local population.

Historical Debate

What Drove Recovery: Administrative Reform or Commercial Opportunity?

The question: Where recovery occurred, was it driven mainly by the state's administrative reforms, especially under Ghazan, or by commercial opportunity created by court demand, existing markets, and Eurasian trade networks? Historians differ in emphasis, and the question involves causation and interpretation of limited evidence.

Interpretation A — Reform as the main driver: Emphasizes the policies attributed to Ghazan and his ministers, including regulation of taxation, protection of cultivators, and monetary and administrative standardization, as conditions that allowed cultivation and trade to revive. This view draws heavily on the narrative sources produced by officials involved in the reforms, such as Rashid al-Din.
Interpretation B — Commercial and structural conditions as the main driver: Emphasizes the demand generated by mobile courts and armies, the resilience of existing cities and routes, the participation of merchants and elites in trade, and the connection of Iran to wider Eurasian networks. On this view, much commercial and urban expansion would have occurred without specific reforms, and reforms mattered chiefly where they protected existing activity.
Interpretation C — Interaction, with regional variation: Argues that reform and commerce reinforced each other in some places, while in others neither succeeded, and that explanation must be local rather than general.

Evidence: The reform account rests mainly on official narrative and administrative texts. The commercial account draws on European merchant records, geographical works, coins, building evidence, and the fiscal importance of trade. Archaeological and documentary evidence that could separate the two causes is limited.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation of evidence, and also scale, since the extent of reform implementation is uncertain.

Why it matters: If reform was decisive, recovery depended on political will and on individuals whose power could vanish. If commercial structures were decisive, recovery rested on broader foundations that might survive political change but benefited mainly cities and merchants.

Section 50.18

The Social Distribution of Prosperity and Economic Hardship

Economic change affected social groups differently, and a society can grow richer in aggregate while many of its members do not. This section contrasts the circumstances of different groups, recognizing that evidence is uneven and that generalizations require caution.

Wealthy merchants with access to elite patrons and long-distance networks were among the clearest beneficiaries of commercial expansion. They could profit from trade in luxury goods, participate in ortoq partnerships, and invest in property and endowments. Powerful households, including those of Mongol princes and princesses, high officials, and military commanders, controlled estates, collected revenue, and commanded labor and goods. Elite women who controlled households and investments were part of this group. Their wealth, however, was exposed to political fortune, and officials in particular could be ruined suddenly.

Artisans occupied an intermediate position. Those producing for elite patrons or export could benefit from demand, but they depended on patrons and merchants who controlled markets and materials, and they were vulnerable to changes in patronage. Urban workers such as porters, laborers, and servants had few resources and little protection, and the sources scarcely record their circumstances. For them, urban prosperity might bring employment but not security.

Cultivators bore much of the weight of taxation and landlord claims. Their situation depended on local conditions, but the sources record complaints of heavy and arbitrary exactions, indebtedness, and flight. Where security improved and taxation became more predictable, cultivators could benefit, but the evidence for sustained improvement is limited. Pastoralists varied. Those tied to the court and army could prosper from demand for animals and from military status, while others faced taxation on herds, pasture disputes, and the effects of seasonal requisitions. Marginal populations, including displaced people and the poor, appear mostly through charity and endowments rather than through their own voices.

These differences mean that commercial expansion could coexist with rural hardship and local insecurity. Revenue collected from the countryside supported urban building and court expenditure, and the profits of trade accrued mainly to those already positioned to benefit. The unequal distribution of gains is a central reason that the evidence of prosperity cannot be translated into a claim about general welfare.

Prosperity must first be separated into income, security, and bargaining power. A merchant trading under royal protection might benefit from wider routes and high demand but remain exposed to confiscation if his patron fell. A cultivator might harvest more grain while retaining less of it because several authorities claimed rent and tax. A craft worker might gain employment through a grand building project and then lose that market when the court moved. Even within a single city, the fortunes of property holders, market intermediaries, day laborers, and households dependent on charity could move in different directions. A claim that an urban economy grew is therefore incomplete until its social distribution is addressed.

The source base also imposes a gender and status imbalance. Royal women and powerful administrators appear because their estates, gifts, and institutions attracted chroniclers; the work of women in cultivation, household production, textile preparation, and local marketing is much harder to recover. Enslaved people, migrants, and displaced families likewise enter the record more often when officials saw them as taxable, dependent, or subject to control. Absence from narrative histories is not absence from the economy. Rather than inventing household budgets that the evidence does not preserve, students can identify which productive tasks the documented markets and elite projects required and then state clearly whose experiences remain unknown.

Section 50.19

Measuring Economic Recovery: Evidence, Regional Variation, and Debate

The preceding sections have repeatedly noted that the sources limit what can be concluded. This section evaluates the principal evidence directly and asks what can reasonably be inferred about recovery.

Narrative sources dominate. Rashid al-Din, writing from the center of power, provides detailed information on administrative measures but has reasons to present them favorably. Wassaf offers financial and administrative detail within a highly ornate rhetorical style that makes literal interpretation hazardous. Mustawfi's geographical work contains district-by-district information, including fiscal assessments, but was composed around 1340 and reflects fiscal categories rather than measured output. Al-Umari preserves reports gathered from informants in another state. Administrative manuals and accounting handbooks, including recently studied examples from the early Ilkhanid period, offer insight into how revenue was calculated and recorded, though they describe procedures and model cases more than actual collections. Endowment deeds document properties and institutions. Coins reveal mints, dates, and standards. Archaeological evidence, including excavated palace sites, ceramics, and buildings, provides physical evidence but is unevenly distributed and does not directly measure production or welfare.

Several indicators of recovery must be kept distinct. Growth in commerce, shown by trade routes, merchant activity, and the market importance of cities, is not the same as an increase in cultivated acreage. Urban building, visible in monuments, reflects the resources that elites could mobilize, not the condition of the population. Public revenue can rise because of heavier taxation, better collection, or greater production, and the sources rarely allow these causes to be separated. Household welfare, which would require evidence about nutrition, income, and security, is the least documented of all. Evidence for one indicator cannot be substituted for another.

Historical Debate

Was Ilkhanid Economic Recovery Genuinely Widespread?

The question: Did the Ilkhanate experience broad-based economic recovery, or was recovery concentrated in particular regions, cities, and social groups, or too poorly documented to quantify?

Interpretation A — Recovery was limited by agricultural decline and fiscal burden: Emphasizes evidence of abandoned land, heavy taxation, indebtedness, and rural flight, together with the uncertain implementation of reclamation measures. Influential studies of the socio-economic condition of the period, such as that of I. P. Petrushevsky, stressed the severity of exploitation, though later scholars have questioned some of the underlying framework.
Interpretation B — Commercial and urban resilience was real and significant: Emphasizes the vitality of cities such as Tabriz, the growth of long-distance trade, building programs, and the evidence of foreign merchants and contemporary observers.
Interpretation C — Recovery was regionally differentiated and imperfectly measurable: Holds that both patterns occurred in different places and that the evidence is too uneven to support a single trajectory for the whole Ilkhanate.

Evidence: Each position draws on narrative, fiscal, material, and merchant sources, but they weigh the evidence differently and rely on sources with different biases.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation, scale, and evidence: how much weight to give literary praise, how to treat fiscal figures, and whether urban evidence can stand for wider conditions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the effects of Mongol rule, the legacy of Ghazan's reforms, and the economic position of Iran on the eve of political fragmentation.

Student task: Evaluate the three interpretations using Sections 50.2, 50.8, 50.9, and 50.18, and identify which kinds of evidence would most improve the debate.

A careful answer is that recovery was real in specific places and sectors but cannot be shown to have been broad-based. The evidence supports commercial resilience in leading cities, building and endowment activity concentrated around the courts, and some local agricultural restoration. It does not support claims of general restoration of cultivation or rising household welfare across the Ilkhanate. The honest conclusion is partly one of limits: the surviving sources favor the center, the elite, and the cities, and the condition of most rural producers and workers remains the least well documented part of the picture.

A useful approach is to construct a separate question for each type of evidence. Coins demonstrate mint activity and official monetary messages, but changes in the number of surviving specimens reflect both medieval production and modern preservation or collecting. Fiscal manuals describe procedures, yet a worked example is not a receipt proving payment by a particular village. Geographical descriptions identify routes, settlements, and assessed revenues, but an assessment can rise because collection became more effective even if agricultural output remained unchanged. Monumental buildings demonstrate concentrated expenditure and skilled craftsmanship, not necessarily high average incomes. Comparing sources becomes meaningful only when the historian keeps the question matched to the kind of evidence available.

This is particularly important when terms such as 'recovery' and 'prosperity' appear in modern accounts. Recovery requires comparison with a baseline; the baseline for a district devastated in the 1220s may be entirely unlike that for a city that negotiated submission. Prosperity may refer to large merchant fortunes, rising public revenue, or a flourishing urban craft, none of which is a direct measurement of household welfare. Durability is yet a third question: a short building boom can be documented without proving that its institutions persisted through a change of ruler. Medieval evidence is rarely sufficient to build a consistent empire-wide series for any of these measures.

The most defensible historical method is comparative and explicit about uncertainty. Tabriz may be studied through reports of commercial centrality, endowments, and court investment, while a different district is examined through land assessments, irrigation projects, and settlement histories. Agreement among independent sources increases confidence only when those sources do not merely repeat a common earlier account. Disagreement should prompt questions about dates, genres, administrative categories, and geography before it is resolved into a general judgment. Historians can make strong claims about the coexistence of urban investment and uneven rural conditions while acknowledging that the exact magnitude of overall change is not recoverable.

Section 50.20

Historical Synthesis: Prosperity, Reconstruction, and the Limits of Ilkhanid Recovery

The preceding sections describe an economy in which agriculture, pastoralism, cities, merchants, and institutions were closely interdependent. Cultivators and herders produced the grain, animals, and raw materials that fed cities and courts. The court and army, with their seasonal movements, generated demand and imposed provisioning burdens. Cities concentrated administration, craft production, and commerce, and they depended on hinterlands that varied in condition. Merchants, many tied to elite patrons, connected local markets to Eurasian and Indian Ocean networks. Endowments and royal projects converted accumulated wealth into durable institutions. Political authority held these elements together by taxing, protecting, regulating, and sometimes disrupting them.

Within this system, some forms of economic activity recovered more clearly than others. Commerce and urban life, especially in Tabriz and in cities favored by the court, showed resilience and growth, supported by existing networks, court demand, and the participation of merchants and elites. Craft production for elite and export markets, including textiles, ceramics, and building trades, found patrons. Agricultural restoration was attempted and in some places probably achieved, but the evidence for broad recovery is weak, and the security of cultivators remained precarious. Pastoral activity continued to be central to the economy, with benefits and burdens unevenly distributed.

Those who benefited most were merchants with elite connections, powerful households and officials, and elite women who controlled resources. Cultivators, many pastoralists, and urban workers bore heavier burdens and had less protection. Regions closer to the court and on major routes fared better than those on contested frontiers or far from centers of power. Constraints remained: unpredictable taxation, overlapping claimants, indebtedness, uncertain security, dependence on court presence and political favor, and the vulnerability of the wealth of ministers and merchants to political reversals.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Iranian Economy under the Ilkhans

What Changed

  • A new ruling elite of pastoralist origin, with courts and armies whose movements shaped demand
  • New categories of landholding and new taxes, including inju estates and levies such as the qubchur
  • The rise of Tabriz as an administrative and commercial center and the founding of Sultaniyya
  • Closer commercial contact with Eurasian networks, and the participation of elite households and women in merchant partnerships

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on irrigation, seasonal rainfall, and scarce water, with qanats and canals requiring continuous maintenance
  • The importance of Iranian administrators, local notables, and merchants in running the economy
  • The caravan routes and city networks that predated Mongol rule
  • The central role of endowments, bazaars, and craft traditions in urban life

Pattern of change: Change was uneven and regional, concentrated in court-connected cities and among elites, and was most abrupt in landholding, taxation, and the ruling elite's demands, while production methods and trade routes changed more gradually. Those closest to the court and its commerce experienced the greatest change; many rural producers experienced greater continuity of labor with new claimants on its product. Why it matters: This pattern explains why neither narratives of devastation nor of renaissance describe the whole experience.

These limits mattered when Ilkhanid political unity weakened after 1335. Much of the commercial and institutional prosperity depended on conditions that a unified state helped sustain: security on major routes, the protection of merchants, the maintenance of endowments, the regularity of taxation, and the concentration of demand around courts. The reforms that had aimed to stabilize the countryside depended on officials and rulers whose authority was about to fragment. Where recovery rested on local productive capacity and enduring commercial networks, parts of it could survive political change; where it depended on royal favor and central administration, it was more vulnerable. Chapter 51 turns to the successor regimes and the regional struggles that followed, and Chapter 52 to the invasions of Timur, which tested the durability of Iran's economic arrangements again.

The historical significance of this economic pattern extends beyond the final decade of unified Ilkhanid rule. Institutions embedded in local agriculture, craft skill, religious endowments, and established routes could persist when their patrons changed, whereas offices dependent on a particular court and commercial privileges attached to a particular ruler were more fragile. The critical distinction is between the continuation of productive capacities and the continuation of the political arrangements that concentrated rewards. Chapter 51 will examine successor regimes; this chapter ends by establishing what they inherited, not by assuming that the end of central unity meant the immediate disappearance of every earlier economic achievement.

Chapter Summary

This chapter asked how far Ilkhanid rule enabled economic reconstruction and commercial expansion in Iran, and why the benefits and durability of recovery differed among regions, institutions, and social groups. Its central answer is that recovery was real but selective: concentrated in particular cities, sectors, and social groups, dependent on political conditions that proved fragile, and difficult to measure because the surviving evidence favors the court, the city, and the elite.

The Ilkhans inherited an economy marked by uneven survival. Cities that submitted early often preserved their markets, while some eastern regions suffered severe disruption. Agricultural production had been interrupted unevenly, property had been redistributed into new categories, revenue systems were unstable, and commercial networks survived and could be reactivated. Recovery therefore began from different starting points in different regions, and the Ilkhanate was a collection of regional economies, not a single economic unit.

The mobile court and army were central to economic life. Their seasonal movements created concentrations of demand for food, animals, textiles, weapons, and luxury goods, stimulating trade where they stayed and imposing burdens of provisioning and requisition on communities along their routes. Pastoralism was integral to the economy, and its relationship with cultivation was both complementary and competitive. Towns and villages were bound together by flows of grain, livestock, goods, and money, so urban prosperity could not be separated from the condition of the hinterland.

In the countryside, production depended on ecology, labor, draft animals, seed, and especially water. Qanats and canals required continuous capital, expertise, and cooperation, and neglect could disable them. Cultivators held varied positions and faced layered claims from the state, royal estates, military assignees, endowments, and landlords, alongside taxes and requisitions. Attempts at reclamation and protection of cultivators are well documented as intentions, but the evidence for their results is limited, and the available sources do not establish broad agricultural recovery.

Cities showed the clearest signs of prosperity. Tabriz became a political and commercial center connected to Anatolia, the Black Sea, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and the Gulf, while Sultaniyya was a planned capital built within a still-mobile political order. Regional cities followed their own paths, shaped by local dynasties, trade routes, and craft specialization. Artisans produced textiles, metalwork, ceramics, and buildings for courts, religious foundations, and distant markets, but depended on patrons and merchants and are poorly documented as individuals.

Commerce linked Iran to wider worlds. Caravan routes, caravanserais, pack animals, and customs stations supported long-distance trade, which was costly and risky, and the so-called Pax Mongolica was neither uniform nor uninterrupted. Merchants worked through partnerships and credit, often as ortoq partners of Mongol princes and princesses, and elite women were significant economic actors. Trade in silk, textiles, horses, and luxury goods connected Iran to China, Anatolia, the Black Sea, the Mediterranean, and the Indian Ocean, with Gulf ports and local intermediaries playing a major role in the south.

Endowments and building projects converted wealth into durable institutions, employing people and providing services, but they concentrated resources in favored places and drew revenue from rural estates. The benefits of prosperity were distributed unequally: wealthy merchants, powerful households, and elite women gained the most, while cultivators, many pastoralists, and urban workers bore heavier burdens. Commercial expansion could therefore coexist with rural hardship.

Measuring recovery proved difficult. Narrative sources reflect the perspectives of officials and authors with reasons to praise, retrospective sources such as Mustawfi's date from after the Ilkhanate's unified rule, and administrative, numismatic, and archaeological evidence measure particular things but not welfare. Growth in commerce, building, cultivated land, revenue, and household welfare are distinct indicators. The best-supported conclusion is that recovery was regionally differentiated and partial, with the greatest documentary support for commercial and urban resilience.

Finally, the durability of recovery depended on political conditions. Security on routes, protection of merchants, maintenance of endowments, regular taxation, and concentrated court demand all relied on a unified state. When that unity weakened after 1335, the parts of the economy that rested on royal favor and central administration were most exposed, while those anchored in local production and enduring networks had better prospects of surviving. This distinction prepares the study of post-Ilkhanid fragmentation in Chapter 51.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shifts attention from rulers and campaigns to the systems that fed, clothed, taxed, and employed people. The Ilkhanid period is often described as either catastrophe or renaissance. The economic evidence supports neither description in simple form. It shows an economy in which water, pasture, roads, markets, and institutions shaped what rulers could achieve, and in which the same political order produced prosperous cities and burdened villages.

It also reveals a larger process: the interpenetration of steppe and settled economies. Mongol rulers brought pastoral habits, mobile courts, and new claims on land and revenue, and they depended on Iranian administrators, merchants, and cultivators to sustain them. Out of that interdependence came new forms of landholding, new partnerships between elites and merchants, and new patterns of urban investment, even as long-standing realities of irrigation, caravan routes, and endowment persisted.

Many of these arrangements shaped what followed. The weakness of institutions that depended on a single dynasty, the vulnerability of ministerial wealth, and the unequal burden on cultivators reappear in the political struggles of the successor regimes, and the commercial networks centered on Tabriz and the Gulf continued to matter long after the Ilkhanate. The pastoral and urban elites whose relationship this chapter has traced remain central actors in the chapters on Turkmen confederations and the Safavid rise.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the larger textbook a methodological lesson. Students should leave it able to ask of any claim about prosperity, whose evidence is this, what does it measure, and what does it leave out? The difficulty of measuring recovery in the Ilkhanate is not a defect of the period alone; it is a feature of the historian's craft, and recognizing it prepares students for the contested evidence they will meet in every later chapter.
