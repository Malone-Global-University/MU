Iran between Russia and Britain: The Great Game and Imperial Competition

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In 1857 the Qajar government held a city far to the northeast, Herat, and watched British forces occupy ports in the far south. The two facts were connected. Together they show how the defense of British India, Afghan politics, Iranian territorial ambition, and the geography of the Persian Gulf had become one diplomatic problem.

In October 1856 an Iranian army entered Herat, the walled city on the western edge of the Afghan lands, after local politics there had turned against the ruler who had previously resisted Qajar influence. Iranian officials regarded this as the recovery of a place long connected to the political world of Khurasan. British officials in India and London regarded it as a violation of a diplomatic undertaking made in 1853 and as a threat to the approaches to India. Britain declared war in November 1856. Instead of marching on the distant city of Herat, British forces occupied Kharg Island and Bushehr in December. Diplomats signed a peace treaty in Paris on 4 March 1857; because news traveled slowly, British forces subsequently attacked Mohammerah in late March and moved toward Ahvaz in early April, after the treaty had already been signed. Under it Iran withdrew from Herat and renounced claims to sovereignty over Herat and Afghanistan.

This chapter begins with that episode because it exposes the structure of the entire period. Iran, between the 1828 Treaty of Turkmenchay and about 1890, lay between two expanding empires. The Russian Empire pressed from the north, through the Caspian Sea, the Caucasus, and eventually Central Asia. The British Empire pressed from the south and east, through the Persian Gulf, Baluchistan, and the approaches to Afghanistan and India. Each power watched the other and regarded Iran as a strategic space in which the rival must not gain a decisive advantage. Neither was always unified in its own policy, and neither could act without regard to local conditions.

Historians have often summarized this competition as the "Great Game." The phrase is convenient, but it can mislead. It suggests a single contest between two chess players with the peoples of Iran, Afghanistan, and Central Asia as pieces. This chapter treats that image as a historical construction rather than a description. British and Russian policy was made by many offices, governors, envoys, and commanders who frequently disagreed with each other. Qajar kings, their ministers, provincial princes, Afghan rulers, Baluch chiefs, Arab sheikhs of the Gulf, Turkmen leaders, and merchants all pursued their own interests. Their choices sometimes confirmed imperial plans, sometimes disrupted them, and sometimes created the very crises that the empires then tried to manage.

The chapter's governing argument is that nineteenth-century Iran was neither a neutral buffer nor a passive victim. Qajar rulers preserved formal political independence through negotiation, accommodation, and selective resistance. They also lost freedom of action. They surrendered claims in Afghanistan, accepted British arbitration of eastern boundaries, acknowledged a Russian-dominated northeastern frontier, and agreed to communications arrangements that served imperial interests. Independence therefore did not mean equal bargaining power. A state could keep its monarch, army, and capital and still find that its decisions about war, frontiers, and commerce were conditioned by foreign naval power, military conquest, and diplomatic leverage.

The chapter rests on several kinds of evidence. Treaty texts, such as the 1857 Treaty of Paris and the 1881 boundary agreement with Russia, record formal obligations but not the pressures that produced them. British and Russian diplomatic and military correspondence is abundant, much of it preserved in India Office and other archives, and often written by officials with strategic anxieties and incomplete local knowledge. Iranian chronicles, court records, and the accounts of provincial officials supply Qajar viewpoints, but they too reflect elite concerns and royal self-presentation. Travel narratives, consular reports, and trade statistics add detail about commerce and daily life. Voices from Herat, Sistan, Baluchistan, the Turkmen steppe, and Gulf ports survive less directly and usually through the writing of outsiders, which is a limitation the chapter keeps in view.

The chapter is arranged in five divisions of four sections each. Division I establishes the strategic setting and interests of the two empires. Division II examines Herat and Afghanistan, culminating in the war and treaty of 1856–1857. Division III turns to the Persian Gulf and the southeastern frontier. Division IV follows Russian expansion in Central Asia and the Turkmen frontier. Division V considers telegraphs, commerce, diplomacy, ordinary lives, and the limits of Iranian autonomy. Chapter 71 examined the Caucasian settlements in detail, and later chapters examine reform (Chapter 73), religious movements (Chapter 74), and concessions and foreign capital (Chapter 75). This chapter concentrates on strategy, frontiers, and diplomacy.

  5. Analyze the Treaty of Paris of 1857 as a foreign-policy settlement, including its provisions on Herat and Afghanistan.
  6. Describe the Persian Gulf as a connected commercial and political region and assess the meaning and limits of British maritime treaties with coastal rulers.

  8. Differentiate annexation, protectorate, and continuing local rule in the Russian conquest of Central Asia.
  9. Assess the political position of the Turkmen, including raiding, enslavement, resistance, and the violence of Russian conquest, without reducing Turkmen society to a single image.
  10. Analyze how intermediaries, merchants, consuls, telegraphs, and trade routes extended imperial influence.
  11. Interpret the consequences of imperial rivalry for merchants, workers, migrants, frontier households, and minorities.

Historical Context

The chapter begins after the Treaty of Turkmenchay of 1828, which Chapter 71 examined in detail. Iran had lost its remaining Caucasian territories north of the Aras River, accepted an indemnity, and agreed to commercial and consular arrangements that favored Russia. The Qajar dynasty, founded by Agha Mohammad Khan (Chapter 69) and led after him by Fath Ali Shah (Chapter 70), remained in control of the plateau. But the military experiments of Abbas Mirza had not prevented defeat, and the monarchy's finances, provincial control, and international credibility were damaged.

Iran's internal structure shaped its foreign policy. The Qajar state governed through a patchwork of royal princes serving as provincial governors, local notables, tribal leaders, landowners, merchants, and religious scholars. Revenue came mainly from land taxes, customs, and payments from provinces, often collected through intermediaries and distributed through court patronage. Tribal and regional forces supplied much of the army's manpower, and an army raised through provincial levies could be assembled slowly and sustained with difficulty, especially at great distances from Tehran. Distant frontiers such as Khurasan, Sistan, and the Gulf coast therefore depended heavily on local governors and powerful local families, whose interests did not always match those of the court.

Britain's interests in the region were shaped by India. Before 1858, British authority in India was exercised through the East India Company under the supervision of the British government; after the uprising of 1857, the Crown took direct responsibility. Throughout the period, British officials worried that Russia, if it dominated Iran or Afghanistan, could threaten India or encourage unrest within it. They also valued the Persian Gulf as a maritime route linking India to the Middle East, and they acted to secure maritime trade and communication there. Russia, for its part, sought to consolidate the Caucasus, protect and expand trade through the Caspian, and eventually extend its frontier into Central Asia, where military commanders and governors often acted with considerable autonomy.

These two systems met on terrain that neither controlled. Between Iran and India lay the Afghan lands, politically divided after the breakup of the Durrani empire. Between Russia and Iran lay the Caspian Sea, the Turkmen steppe, and the khanates of Central Asia. South of the plateau lay a Gulf coast dotted with ports and small ruling families, some tied to Qajar authority and others to Oman or independent Arab dynasties. Each of these places had its own politics. The chapter therefore follows what happened when imperial strategy encountered them.

Section 72.1

Iran after Turkmenchay: Independence, Vulnerability, and the Search for Security

After 1828 Iran's position was paradoxical. The state had lost a war and territory, and Russia held a strong military and diplomatic position along the northern frontier. Yet the Qajar monarchy continued to govern the plateau from Tehran, collect taxes, appoint governors, conduct diplomacy, and make war and peace. Neither Russia nor Britain wished to see the dynasty collapse. Russia valued a compliant monarchy that would honor the treaty and keep the Caucasian frontier quiet. Britain valued an Iran strong enough to resist Russian domination yet not so strong as to threaten British interests in the Gulf or Afghanistan. These preferences gave Qajar rulers room for maneuver even when their military position was weak.

The royal succession illustrated both the room and its limits. Abbas Mirza, the crown prince and architect of Iran's military reforms, died in 1833, and Fath Ali Shah died in 1834. Mohammad Shah, Abbas Mirza's son, came to the throne with diplomatic and some military support from both Russia and Britain, each hoping to secure an Iran friendly to its interests. The new king and his chief minister, Haji Mirza Aqasi, inherited a treasury strained by the Russian indemnity, a provincial army of uneven quality, and rival princes whose loyalty required management. These constraints shaped later decisions. A king who wanted to demonstrate that the dynasty could still win victories had good reasons to look toward the east, where the Qajar claim to Khurasan and to Herat could be presented as the recovery of historic territory rather than as an aggression against a European power.

Naser al-Din Shah came to the throne in 1848 under different circumstances. He was young, and the early years of his reign were dominated by the reforming minister Amir Kabir, whose program Chapter 73 examines. By the early 1850s the king and his chief minister Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri again confronted the old problems: how to hold the frontiers with limited revenue, and how to respond to British and Russian representatives who offered support and also demanded concessions. Foreign policy was therefore never conducted in isolation from fiscal capacity, provincial administration, and court politics. A governor in Khurasan, a prince in Fars, or an official in Tabriz could pursue policies that complicated those of the capital.

The chapter's first analytical point is that Iran retained real choices after Turkmenchay. Qajar rulers could reorient diplomacy between London and St. Petersburg, choose when to press claims in the east, accept or resist demands for concessions, and decide whom to appoint to key posts. These were not trivial powers. But the choices were made under constraints that tightened with time: Russian military expansion, British naval and financial leverage, and the increasing dependence of Iranian commerce and communications on foreign networks. Understanding the period requires holding both facts together.

The practical business of diplomacy began in provinces. Royal governors monitored coastal trade, managed frontier alliances, sent reports to Tehran, and sometimes pursued their own agreements with neighboring rulers. They could make promises faster than the court could verify them, particularly during distant emergencies. The shah's position therefore depended on a difficult balance: delegating sufficient authority for local defense while limiting the independence of powerful officeholders. Foreign emissaries who encountered conflicting messages from provincial governors and the center sometimes interpreted the differences as deliberate deception; in reality they could reveal the limits of communications and the varied interests inside the Qajar government. These conditions should be considered alongside military power when assessing why Iran could assert sovereignty over a district yet struggle to enforce a decision there.

Section 72.2

The Great Game: Imperial Strategy, Geopolitical Language, and Historical Interpretation

The phrase "Great Game" is associated in popular memory with the rivalry between Britain and Russia in Central Asia. It entered English-language discussion in the nineteenth century through the British officer Arthur Conolly, who is reported to have used it in correspondence, and it acquired far wider currency after Rudyard Kipling used it in his novel Kim in 1901. The phrase thus belonged to the language of British observers, and it carried the imprint of their sense of adventure and anxiety. Its later popularity, reinforced by narrative histories emphasizing explorers, spies, and secret missions, produced an image of a long, coordinated contest. Specialists have since stressed that the rivalry was real but looser, less centralized, and more dependent on local conditions than that image suggests.

What can be said with confidence is that, across the nineteenth century, British and Russian officials repeatedly perceived each other's actions in Iran, Afghanistan, and Central Asia as threats. British authorities feared that Russian influence over Iran or Afghanistan could create a base from which India might be pressured. Russian officials, in turn, feared British commercial and political penetration of Central Asia and the Caspian region. These anxieties were genuine, but they were also frequently exaggerated by the officials who voiced them, because dramatic warnings helped secure resources and attention from superiors. The records of both governments contain more speculation about the rival's intentions than evidence of coordinated invasion plans.

The two empires also did not decide policy in a single place. In Britain, the cabinet and Foreign Office in London weighed European diplomacy, trade, and domestic politics; the Government of India, based in Calcutta and, in summer, in the hills, often took a more alarmed and forward-leaning view of the northwest frontier. Disagreements between "forward" policy advocates, who favored active intervention beyond India's frontier, and advocates of "masterly inactivity," who preferred restraint, shaped events in Afghanistan. In Russia, the Foreign Ministry sought to avoid costly conflict with Britain, while the War Ministry, governors-general, and ambitious commanders on the frontier sometimes advanced faster than St. Petersburg wished and presented the government with accomplished facts. Treating each empire as a single actor therefore misrepresents how decisions emerged.

Equally important, Iranians, Afghans, Turkmen, and other regional actors had their own agendas. Qajar rulers sought territory, revenue, and security, and used the foreign powers when useful. Afghan rulers sought recognition, subsidies, and protection against both neighbors and rivals. Turkmen leaders sought autonomy, trade, and security for their communities. The rivalry between London and St. Petersburg was one condition of their decisions, not the only one. A history of the "Great Game" told solely from the viewpoint of British and Russian archives risks erasing these actors altogether.

The 'game' metaphor is also misleading about chronology. British fears of Russia preceded the later nineteenth-century conquest of Turkestan, while Russian expansion into Central Asia accelerated unevenly in the 1860s and 1870s. The Herat crises in the 1830s and 1850s cannot be explained as identical episodes to the Panjdeh alarm of 1885. Institutions and technologies changed, as did the balance between London and British India. Historians use the phrase as a convenient label for a broad field of relationships, but rigorous accounts need dates, identified officials, actual military capabilities, and evidence of the threat perceived by contemporary decision-makers. A dramatic travel narrative may illuminate contemporary fears without providing proof of a coordinated long-term imperial plan.

Historical Vocabulary
Great Game, Buffer State, and Sphere of Influence

Definition: The Great Game is a modern shorthand for the strategic rivalry between Britain and Russia across Central Asia and neighboring regions, especially concerning the approaches to British India. A buffer state is a state whose territory lies between rival powers and which each is said to wish to preserve as an independent barrier. A sphere of influence is a region in which one power claims preferential political or commercial standing without formal annexation.

Historical context: The terms were not used in these senses by every actor of the period. British writers and officials spoke of Persia and Afghanistan as barriers or intermediate states, and later diplomatic arrangements, such as the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 (discussed only as later context here), would formalize spheres of influence. During the period examined in this chapter the language was less settled.

Why it matters: These categories describe imperial viewpoints. They can obscure the fact that Iran and Afghanistan had governments, interests, and populations of their own. A "buffer" is defined by its position between others, not by its own history.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the Great Game was a named, organized campaign, that spies were its principal instruments, or that Iran and Afghanistan simply accepted the roles assigned to them by European observers.

Section 72.3

British India and the Iranian Plateau: Routes, Security, and Strategic Anxiety

British interest in Iran flowed first from India. Seen from Calcutta, the northwestern approaches to the subcontinent ran along several routes: through the Persian Gulf to the Indian coast; across Baluchistan toward the Indus basin; and through Herat, Kandahar, and Kabul to the Khyber and other passes. The mountainous terrain of Afghanistan, the deserts of Baluchistan, and the aridity of the Iranian plateau made large-scale military movement difficult. British authorities nevertheless worried that a hostile or Russian-influenced government in Iran or Afghanistan could make such movement easier, supply local allies, or stir unrest among Indian rulers and populations. Whether a Russian invasion of India was ever a realistic military prospect is doubtful; the anxiety existed independently of that assessment.

British officials responded in several ways. They sought information, through officers, envoys, and travelers who surveyed routes and reported on rulers and armies. They sought influence at courts, through diplomatic missions and subsidies. They intervened in local politics when they believed it necessary, as the Anglo-Afghan war would show. And they pursued maritime security in the Persian Gulf, where British naval power was strongest. The relative weight of these approaches shifted over time and differed among institutions.

Institutional divisions matter. Before 1858, the East India Company governed British India under the supervision of the British government. After the uprising of 1857 the Crown assumed direct rule, and the Government of India operated under the Secretary of State for India in London. Responsibility for the Tehran mission and for the Gulf residencies was divided between the Foreign Office and the Indian authorities in ways that changed over time, so envoys and Residents sometimes reported to different superiors who held different priorities. Officials in India tended to see Iran as an approach to their frontier. Officials in London, more concerned with the balance of power in Europe and the Ottoman Empire, often treated Iran as one theater among many. The resulting disagreements help explain why British policy toward Iran could appear alternately conciliatory and coercive.

Routes mattered differently to military columns and trading caravans. A pass used by a handful of mounted scouts did not necessarily provide water and supplies for thousands of infantrymen, artillery teams, and accompanying animals. Indian officials commissioned surveys in part to distinguish geographic possibility from operational feasibility. Reports of Russian agents moving toward Afghanistan could therefore inspire alarm without establishing that a Russian army was capable of invading India through the same corridor. For local people these roads were not hypothetical military lines. They were paths to pasture, pilgrimage, seasonal markets, and family connections. An imperial map emphasizing invasion could omit precisely the human uses that made the route economically valuable and politically difficult to control.

Section 72.4

Russia's Southern Strategy: The Caspian, the Caucasus, and Khurasan

Russian interests in Iran after Turkmenchay combined commercial, military, and strategic aims. Securing the Caucasus was the immediate priority: the new frontier had to be defended and the newly annexed populations governed, tasks that absorbed the attention of the Caucasus command for decades. Commerce followed. The treaty settlement gave Russian merchants favorable terms in Iran, and the Caspian Sea, on which Russia held a dominant naval position, linked the Volga and the Caucasus to Iran's northern provinces. Ports such as Anzali and Astarabad Bay mattered as gateways for trade and as points where Russian influence could be exerted. Russian presence at Ashuradeh, near Astarabad, extended this maritime reach, though the details of the arrangements are a matter for the specialist literature.

The northeast gradually drew Russian attention. Khurasan, with its capital at Mashhad, a major pilgrimage center, lay across caravan routes toward Bukhara, Khiva, and Herat. Russian consuls and agents observed the region, and commercial and political interests developed together. In the late 1830s Russian envoy Count Simonich encouraged Mohammad Shah's eastern ambitions, as the next division will show, though the Russian government in St. Petersburg did not control events at Herat. Another episode illustrates the limits of Russian capability: an expedition under General Perovsky marched against Khiva in the winter of 1839–1840 and failed, largely because of severe cold and logistical collapse. Russian expansion into Central Asia was not an unstoppable advance, and its pace depended on resources, the personalities of commanders, and calculations in St. Petersburg.

The mid-century turn toward Central Asia reflected pressures within the Russian state as well as its rivalry with Britain. After the Crimean War (1853–1856), in which Russia confronted Britain, France, and the Ottoman Empire, Russian officials looked for ways to restore prestige and secure frontiers. The steppe frontier offered opportunities, since local khanates were militarily weaker, and commanders won promotion and glory there. Foreign Minister Alexander Gorchakov, in a famous circular of 1864, justified advance as a response to the disorder of frontier societies. By then the Qajar government faced a new neighbor not only to its northwest across the Aras but increasingly to its northeast, where Russian power was approaching Turkmen lands that had long pressed on Khurasan.

It would be a mistake to see this as a seamless advance toward India. Russian decision-making involved the Foreign Ministry, the War Ministry, the Emperor, and local governors who made initiatives of their own. Sometimes St. Petersburg restrained its commanders; sometimes it endorsed their accomplished facts. Russian commercial interests, particularly in the Caspian trade and textiles, did not always coincide with military ambitions. For Iran the practical consequence was the same: a powerful neighbor with a long frontier, growing presence in the east, and the capacity to affect the security of Khurasan and the Caspian provinces.

Russian trade policy introduced additional complexity. The Caspian connected Astrakhan and Baku to Iranian ports, while roads through Azerbaijan and the northern plateau moved goods toward Tabriz and Tehran. Russian officials sought advantages for traders and the protection of nationals, but merchants' behavior could not be commanded completely from St. Petersburg. Iranian manufacturers and wholesalers adjusted prices and commercial partnerships according to demand and credit conditions. Governments that considered a port strategically valuable might disagree with merchants who preferred predictable customs rules and safe transport above territorial expansion. The relationship between military power and trade was thus reciprocal: commercial interests could encourage official intervention, while military control might reshape the routes by which commerce moved.

Division II

Afghanistan, Herat, and the Anglo-Persian Confrontation, 1830s–1857

Explaining why Herat became a major diplomatic and military issue, how Afghan political agency influenced the conflict, and why Britain intervened against Iran.

Section 72.5

Herat and Afghanistan: Dynastic Claims, Regional Sovereignty, and Strategic Geography

Herat stood on a fertile plain watered by the Hari Rud, on routes linking Khurasan, Kandahar, Kabul, and Central Asia. For centuries it had been a major city of the eastern Iranian world, with rich agricultural land and a famous place in Persianate culture (Chapter 54). In the mid-eighteenth century it became part of the Durrani empire, founded by Ahmad Shah Durrani. After that empire fragmented in the early nineteenth century, Afghanistan did not exist as a single unified state. Kabul, Kandahar, Peshawar, and Herat came under different rulers, often from rival branches of the Durrani Pashtun elite. The Sadozai line, descendants of Ahmad Shah, retained Herat, while Barakzai leaders, among them Dost Mohammad Khan, rose to power in Kabul and Kandahar.

Herat's rulers in the 1830s were Kamran Mirza, a Sadozai prince, and his powerful vizier Yar Mohammad Khan Alakozai, who held the real military and administrative authority and later ruled in his own name. They governed a polity of modest size with a mixed population of Persian-speaking townspeople, Pashtun and other tribal groups, and communities of Shi'a, many of whom had ties to Khurasan. Herat's rulers dealt with the problems of any small state: extracting revenue, keeping the loyalty of tribal chiefs, and defending themselves against larger neighbors. They sometimes sought Qajar protection and sometimes resisted Qajar claims. Their raiding and the slave-taking of Turkmen allies and others on the Khurasan frontier also angered Iranian authorities, who regarded the capture of Shi'a Iranians as an intolerable injury.

Qajar rulers regarded Herat as historically connected to the political world of Khurasan. Safavid and Afsharid rulers had held it (Chapters 57 and 68), and Qajar monarchs claimed the inheritance of those earlier states. Iranian officials argued that Herat's rulers had at times acknowledged Iranian overlordship, and from the Qajar perspective the claim was not an invented pretext. It was also, however, a claim to rule people who had their own leaders. Herat's elites, whatever their relations with Tehran, were not in general eager to be absorbed into a Qajar administration, and they repeatedly looked for alternatives, whether in Kandahar, Kabul, or, later, British support.

The chapter therefore treats Herat as a functioning regional polity. British and Iranian official writing often framed the city as an object of rival ambitions, but Herati rulers made decisions based on their own survival: they bargained with Tehran, with Kandahar, with Kabul, and with British envoys. Their decisions had consequences. The siege of 1837–1838 and the crisis of 1856 both grew from local political developments as well as from imperial calculation.

Herat's political geography complicates modern assumptions about the 'Afghan frontier.' A ruler of Kabul did not necessarily command Herat, and a Herati leader's recognition of a Qajar claim might serve a temporary purpose rather than imply lasting incorporation into an Iranian province. The city's defenses, agricultural hinterland, caravan links toward Mashhad and Kandahar, and local religious and commercial institutions gave its leaders resources for independent negotiation. Its inhabitants were divided by occupation, lineage, property, and access to patronage. When a prince sought help from one neighbor against another, the calculation could be framed in contemporary language of loyalty or protection while serving the immediate need to survive a local rivalry. Historians need to reconstruct these circumstances before applying broad labels such as 'reconquest' or 'foreign aggression.'

Section 72.6

Mohammad Shah's Siege of Herat, 1837–1838: Iranian Ambition and Foreign Intervention

Mohammad Shah's campaign against Herat began in the autumn of 1837. Iranian forces marched east through Khurasan and invested the city, and the siege continued into 1838. The shah's objectives combined the recovery of a city regarded as historically connected to Qajar territory, the punishment of Herati raiding, and a demonstration that the dynasty could still win success in war. Haji Mirza Aqasi, the chief minister, supported the enterprise. Contemporaries and later historians have also debated how much the campaign reflected Mohammad Shah's own ambitions and how much it reflected the influence of advisers.

Foreign diplomats were closely involved. The Russian envoy, Count Ivan Simonich, accompanied the Iranian expedition for part of its course and encouraged the shah, and he arranged assistance, including support linked to Russian deserters serving in the Iranian army. At the same time a Russian agent, Jan Vitkevich, was in Kabul in 1837–1838 seeking to establish relations with Dost Mohammad Khan, who was simultaneously negotiating with British envoy Alexander Burnes. For British officials, such contacts suggested coordinated Russian action. The Russian government, which later disavowed aspects of Simonich's and Vitkevich's conduct, appears not to have planned a campaign against India, but its envoys' activities fed British suspicions. The record is thus one of Russian diplomatic encouragement and local initiative, not of a Russian operation conducted through Iranian troops.

Inside Herat, the defense was led by Yar Mohammad Khan and Kamran Mirza. A young British officer, Eldred Pottinger, who had arrived in Herat before the siege, advised the defenders and helped organize the fortifications. British writers later gave Pottinger great prominence, and he did contribute to the defense. Historians now caution that the city's resistance depended primarily on its own fortifications, its garrison, its supplies, and the determination of its leaders, and that Pottinger's role was important but not decisive in the way romantic narratives claimed. The Iranian force, operating at a long distance from its bases with limited siege equipment and difficulties of supply and discipline, was unable to break the defenses.

Britain, meanwhile, was moving to intervene. John McNeill, the British envoy in Tehran, protested the siege and, in 1838, traveled to the Iranian camp to demand its end. His efforts failed at first. The British Government of India then ordered a naval expedition to the Persian Gulf, and in mid-1838 British forces occupied Kharg Island, off the Iranian coast. A British ultimatum conveyed the threat of further action unless Iranian forces left Herat. A last Iranian assault in June 1838 was repulsed, and in September the shah lifted the siege. The relative weight of the failed assault, the British ultimatum, supply difficulties, and Russian reluctance to support an extended confrontation with Britain is a matter that sources and historians weigh differently. Diplomacy and military pressure interacted, and neither alone provides a full explanation.

Britain's occupation of Kharg lasted until the settlement of its differences with Iran, and the island was evacuated in 1842. The episode showed that Britain could use naval power against the Iranian coast to influence events deep in the interior. It also demonstrated the vulnerability of Iranian ports to a maritime power, a theme examined in Division III.

A siege is also a social event, not merely a contest between commanders. Defensive preparations can consume grain, compel construction labor, redirect trade, and force residents to shelter during bombardment. In Herat, authority over the gates and access to food mattered for the bargaining position of rulers and inhabitants alike. British observers frequently reported the steadfastness of the defenders, but their accounts were shaped by the political value of portraying resistance to Qajar forces. Iranian chroniclers, in turn, emphasized the shah's rights and military determination. The historian must compare such sources while acknowledging that the experience of ordinary residents is often mediated through foreign military and diplomatic writing. Failure to capture the city tells us about logistics and diplomacy, but not by itself how everyone inside the walls judged the crisis.

Section 72.7

Afghanistan's Political Response and the Unstable Eastern Frontier, 1839–1855

The siege's failure did not settle Herat's status, and events in Afghanistan soon altered the regional balance. In 1839 Britain, acting from India, invaded Afghanistan, deposed Dost Mohammad Khan, and installed Shah Shuja, a Sadozai claimant, in Kabul. British officials justified the invasion partly by the fear of Russian and Iranian influence. The occupation provoked resistance, and in the winter of 1841–1842 British forces and their followers were destroyed during their retreat from Kabul, an event that damaged British prestige in the region. Britain later withdrew, and Dost Mohammad returned to power.

For Iran the war carried two lessons. It showed that Britain was willing to intervene militarily in Afghanistan and could be defeated there, and it suggested that Britain's capacity to dictate Afghan outcomes was limited. In Herat, the aftermath strengthened Yar Mohammad Khan, who removed Kamran Mirza in 1842 and ruled the city in his own name until his death in 1851. Yar Mohammad Khan's pragmatic policy balanced the neighbors: he negotiated with Britain, kept on terms with Tehran when convenient, and expanded his authority into surrounding territory. His son Sa'id Mohammad Khan succeeded him but was unable to maintain stability, and Herat's politics became increasingly factional.

Qajar policy in these years alternated between caution and assertion. Iranian governors on the Khurasan frontier remained concerned by raids and by the instability beyond Herat, and the court considered renewed intervention when the city's rulers faced rivals. British representatives, convinced that Iranian expansion would serve Russian interests, repeatedly warned against it. In January 1853 Iran concluded an agreement with Britain by which it undertook not to send troops to Herat except in the event of a foreign attack on the city, and to withdraw them if they were sent, and to refrain from interference in Herat's internal affairs. The agreement was a significant constraint, and Iranian officials accepted it partly because the war in the Crimea was approaching and the court wished to avoid antagonizing Britain, and partly because the Qajar government had not abandoned its claims and expected to retain the right to act in a crisis.

Herat's own politics set the next crisis in motion. After the death of Yar Mohammad Khan, rivalry within the city's elite and pressure from Dost Mohammad Khan in Kandahar and Kabul created repeated openings for outside intervention. In 1855 Dost Mohammad took Kandahar, a step toward consolidating Barakzai rule in the south of Afghanistan, and Herat's leaders, anxious about the same Kabul ruler, sought Iranian support. British officials meanwhile cultivated relations with Dost Mohammad, concluding an agreement with him in 1855 and a subsidy arrangement in 1857. This section's central point is that the Herat question was driven by Afghan politics as much as by Qajar ambition: factions in Herat, rivalry between Sadozai and Barakzai, and the growing power of Dost Mohammad created the conditions in which Iranian forces were invited, or provoked, to move.

The 1853 undertaking should be placed within a rapidly changing diplomatic environment. Britain feared renewed Iranian intervention in Herat but had to weigh relations with the Ottoman Empire, Russia, and British India; the Qajar government considered both eastern claims and the risks of another maritime confrontation. The agreement could therefore be read differently by each side. Officials might regard an exception for responding to a foreign threat as a safeguard, while their counterparts could interpret almost any military involvement as prohibited interference. Local Herati leaders had no guarantee that their political interests would coincide with either capital's interpretation. These ambiguities mattered after 1855, when changing conditions in Kandahar and Herat raised the question of whether the earlier promises remained adequate.

Section 72.8

The Capture of Herat, the Anglo-Persian War, and the Treaty of Paris, 1856–1857

By 1856 relations between Britain and Iran were poor. A dispute in 1855 involving the British minister in Tehran, Charles Murray, and the court, over protection of a person under British patronage and related diplomatic offenses, led to a break in relations. Naser al-Din Shah and Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri were alarmed by British hostility but were also confronted with events in Herat. A new faction in the city, supported by Iranian backing, sought Qajar assistance, and the court decided to act despite the 1853 undertaking. An Iranian army under Sultan Murad Mirza, a Qajar prince, marched east and entered Herat in October 1856.

Britain responded with war. The decision was taken in London and India in the autumn of 1856, with the Government of India taking the lead in military preparations. As the previous division showed, British forces could not easily reach Herat. They instead used sea power. A force from Bombay occupied Kharg Island in December 1856 and took Bushehr shortly after. In February 1857 British forces defeated Iranian troops at Khushab, in the Bushehr hinterland. After the peace had been signed on 4 March but before the news reached the expeditionary command, British forces under Sir James Outram, accompanied by officers including Henry Havelock, attacked Mohammerah on 26 March and advanced toward Ahvaz in early April. Section 72.11 examines these coastal operations and their social consequences.

Diplomacy ran in parallel. Iranian representatives in Paris, notably Farrokh Khan Ghaffari, negotiated with British representatives under the mediation of French officials, and the Treaty of Paris was signed on 4 March 1857. The legal settlement preceded the British attack on Mohammerah later that month. The treaty provided for the restoration of peace and the withdrawal of British forces from Iranian territory. Its most consequential articles concerned Herat. Under Article V the shah undertook to withdraw Iranian troops from Herat and Afghan territory within the specified period. Article VI separately required Iran to relinquish claims to sovereignty over Herat and the countries of Afghanistan, recognize their independence, refrain from interference, and submit future disputes to the good offices of the British government before resorting to arms. Article VII concerned obligations relating to frontier disturbances. The specific provisions should be read separately rather than compressed into a single clause. Students should consult a published text for the exact wording (see the Source Window below).

The treaty was a substantial foreign-policy settlement, not simply the end of a short campaign. It converted British objections into a formal Iranian renunciation, and it created a standing mechanism, British mediation, through which Iranian disputes on its eastern frontier would pass. It also did not give Britain sovereignty over Afghanistan. Herat remained independent of Iran after the Iranian withdrawal, and it was subsequently incorporated into the Afghan state: Dost Mohammad Khan took the city in 1863, shortly before his death. From the Afghan point of view the treaty represented not so much a favor as a coincidence of British and Afghan interests.

The phrase 'ended the war' requires careful chronological use. Representatives signed the Treaty of Paris on 4 March 1857; fighting and British operations continued before news of the settlement reached the forces in the Gulf. The British attack at Mohammerah on 26 March and the advance to Ahvaz in early April occurred after the signature, not as the final battles that persuaded the negotiators to sign. Ratification, withdrawal, and the restoration of diplomatic relations were further processes. This distinction is more than a point of dating: it prevents a misleading cause-and-effect account of negotiations and shows how a government could commit itself to peace without possessing the communications needed to halt its distant army immediately. The text's legal provisions must be read against that delay.

Historical Debate

Did the Herat Conflict Reflect Iranian Reconquest, Afghan Sovereignty, or British Imperial Security?

The question: The Herat crises can be told as a story of Iranian efforts to recover a historic territory, of Afghan resistance to outside control, or of British determination to protect India. Historians weigh these emphases differently.

Interpretation A — Iranian reconquest: Emphasizes Qajar claims grounded in Safavid and Afsharid precedent, the provocation of raids, and the legitimacy of a state seeking territory it considered rightfully connected to Khurasan. It treats British intervention as an external limit on a regional ambition.
Interpretation B — Afghan sovereignty: Emphasizes Herat's own rulers and the larger process of Afghan state formation under Dost Mohammad Khan. It treats Iranian and British actions as episodes in which a local polity pursued survival, and it sees the 1863 incorporation of Herat as the outcome of Afghan politics rather than a British gift.
Interpretation C — British imperial security: Emphasizes the Indian government's fears of Russian influence and the use of naval power to restrain Iran. It treats the Treaty of Paris as an instrument of British frontier policy.

Evidence: Diplomatic correspondence, military reports, chronicles, and treaty texts support each reading differently. British archives are abundant and shaped by strategic concerns. Iranian court sources emphasize royal rights. Afghan and Herati sources are less accessible and often come through outsiders.

Points of disagreement: The interpretations disagree mainly about causation and emphasis: which actor's motives best explain the crisis, and how much weight to give Russian involvement. They also differ in terminology, such as whether Iranian claims are described as "reconquest," "aggression," or "assertion of suzerainty."

Why it matters: Each reading assigns different agency to Iranians, Afghans, and Britons, and each implies a different understanding of what it meant for Iran to renounce claims in 1857.

Division III

The Persian Gulf, Maritime Power, and Iran's Southeastern Frontiers, 1820s–1870s

Coastal commerce, British maritime agreements, military coercion, and the negotiated borderlands of Makran and Sistan.

Section 72.9

The Persian Gulf as a Political and Commercial Frontier

From the deck of a vessel approaching Bushehr, the Persian Gulf might appear to have been a single maritime corridor between India and the Ottoman world. Its actual politics were more intricate. The coast linked ports, islands, date-growing settlements, pearl banks, and caravan routes whose commercial life depended on movement between shores. Bushehr connected southern Iran with Bombay and Basra; Bandar Abbas faced the islands and the entrance to the Strait of Hormuz; Lingeh served merchants with ties to both Iranian towns and Arab trading families; Muscat was an Omani center with influence stretching across the sea. Bahrain's pearling economy drew labor and credit from beyond the islands. These settlements were neither isolated nor necessarily obedient to one central government. Merchants moved goods and money across jurisdictions, and rulers depended on taxing, protecting, or bargaining with them.

Iranian sovereignty along the northern shore was exercised through governors whose practical authority differed from town to town. Qajar appointments mattered in Bushehr and Fars, but powerful local families collected revenues, maintained armed retainers, and negotiated with merchants. In Khuzestan the sheikhs of Mohammerah, situated near the Shatt al-Arab and Karun river system, gained leverage from their geography and their relationships with the Ottoman as well as Iranian authorities. Across the water, the al-Bu Sa'id rulers of Oman, the Al Khalifa dynasty of Bahrain, the Qawasim of Sharjah and Ras al-Khaimah, and the rulers of Abu Dhabi and Dubai occupied distinctive positions. Some agreements linked rulers to Britain; others involved tribute, occasional Qajar claims, or settlements with neighbors. None should be treated as a homogeneous 'Arab coast' without political agency.

The goods carried through these networks reveal why European officials valued the Gulf. Dates, pearls, horses, textiles, coffee, grain, timber, and Indian manufactures moved through its ports. Traders connected the region to western Indian Ocean markets and to inland Persian caravan towns. Shipping also facilitated the movement of enslaved people, a history that cannot be excluded from commercial accounts. Sailors and port laborers sustained commerce under uncertain working conditions; fluctuating harvests, credit, monsoon schedules, and rulers' customs policies affected their earnings. Many merchants worked through familial or communal networks extending beyond formal political borders. An agreement that disrupted one harbor could redirect commerce to another without ending the network.

For the East India Company and later British India, Gulf access mattered because communications, provisioning, and commerce were tied to imperial rule in the subcontinent. British officials worried that rival fleets, maritime raiding, or hostile rulers might endanger vessels serving India. Yet an official's definition of security was not neutral. British cruisers protected some forms of exchange while seeking to regulate or prohibit others; residents at Bushehr and at coastal courts gathered intelligence and intervened in disputes over succession, shipping, and claims to jurisdiction. The British presence was thus simultaneously diplomatic, naval, commercial, and administrative. It expanded in a region where British authority on land remained sharply limited.

From Tehran's perspective the Gulf was not merely a weak southern frontier. It was a source of customs revenue, routes for diplomatic communication, and a means of reaching Indian markets. The Qajar state could act through governors, negotiate with Oman or Bahrain, and use commercial relationships, but its maritime resources were no match for British naval squadrons. Local rulers sometimes welcomed British assistance to counter nearby rivals while resisting British interference in other matters. The chapter therefore treats the Gulf as a negotiated political space. Foreign influence grew not simply because British ships appeared, but because maritime agreements, merchants' demands for protection, and local rivalries made repeated intervention possible.

Section 72.10

British Maritime Treaties, Regional Rulers, and the Growth of Gulf Influence

British maritime authority in the Gulf grew through military expeditions and successive written agreements. After campaigns against Qawasim shipping and coastal positions, Britain concluded the General Maritime Treaty of January 1820 with several rulers of the Arabian shore. Its language condemned attacks that British officials classified as piracy and placed constraints on maritime warfare. The agreements gave Britain new opportunities to observe, judge, and sometimes punish conduct at sea. However, they did not create one British sovereign colony along the coast, nor did they turn every Gulf ruler into a British official. They were agreements negotiated under unequal conditions, and their meaning depended on how commanders and signatories enforced them.

The terms themselves deserve historical scrutiny. British officers claimed that suppression of maritime predation would protect peaceful trade, but some coastal rulers regarded attacks, reprisals, or duties imposed on passing vessels as part of older conflicts over commerce and authority. The British division between 'piracy' and lawful maritime war consequently reflected imperial concepts and interests, even where violence against merchants was real. A historian needs to ask whose ships were attacked, which treaties applied, who possessed the power to enforce them, and whether the conduct of Britain's own naval forces was judged by equivalent standards. Protection brought benefits to some traders and constraints to others.

A series of seasonal truces later gave way to the Perpetual Maritime Truce of 1853, whose signatories undertook to abstain from hostilities at sea. Britain became an increasingly important intermediary when coastal disputes threatened maritime peace. This strengthened the position of British political residents and their local agents, who interpreted complaints, mediated disputes, and corresponded with rulers. The process was gradual: the later system of protectorate arrangements and exclusive agreements should not be projected backward as if it had existed in full in 1820 or 1853. Maritime truce did not automatically settle competing claims over inland territory, fisheries, customs, or political allegiance.

Bahrain and Oman illustrate the limits of a simple imperial model. Iranian rulers had long claimed authority over Bahrain at various periods, but the Al Khalifa rulers pursued strategies shaped by local security and commercial interests. British agents negotiated with Bahraini rulers while attempting to contain challenges from neighboring powers. The rulers of Muscat, meanwhile, managed relationships with Britain and Iran while presiding over maritime networks reaching the East African coast. Merchant groups and seafaring communities had stakes of their own. They could lobby, evade regulations, or change ports when diplomatic agreements affected trade.

British antislavery policy also developed unevenly. Diplomats and officers obtained commitments aimed at limiting maritime trafficking, yet abolitionist claims coexisted with the continued presence of enslaved workers and with the imperial government's concern for commercial stability. Treaties could provide grounds for intervention without securing immediate emancipation. Some rulers interpreted agreements narrowly, and enforcement depended on resources, political relationships, and the treatment of fugitives. A careful account avoids both dismissing the importance of antislavery measures and treating them as evidence that maritime imperial policy was exclusively humanitarian.

Qajar governments watched these changes with mixed interests. More predictable shipping could benefit Iranian commerce, and British protection might deter threats at sea. But a British officer who claimed authority to mediate a dispute involving Bahrain or other coastal populations could also undermine Iran's own claims. Sovereignty was repeatedly contested through diplomatic correspondence rather than solely by battle. The institutional outcome was a British-backed framework of maritime security that increasingly favored imperial supervision, even though the Gulf remained a politically plural region with substantial local initiative.

Section 72.11

Military Pressure from the Sea: Kharg, Bushehr, and the War of 1856–1857

The Herat crises demonstrated a geographical asymmetry in British-Iranian relations. An Iranian army could threaten or occupy a city hundreds of kilometers from Tehran, but British decision-makers could strike the coastline without marching through the inland provinces to Herat. During Mohammad Shah's siege in 1838, British vessels occupied Kharg Island to convey the possibility of further intervention. British representatives connected operations in the Gulf to the demand that Iran disengage from Herat. The episode revealed the limits of Iranian coastal defenses, although the Qajar government still retained authority over the mainland and the occupation did not permanently resolve the Herat question.

In 1856 the same logic was applied more extensively. After the Qajar capture of Herat and the rupture of diplomatic relations, the British government declared war in November. An expedition from British India took Kharg on 4 December and Bushehr on 10 December 1856. The expedition relied on shipping, coastal reconnaissance, and command of the approaches to the port. Occupying a town was more complex than landing troops: officers needed supplies, secure positions, interpreters, and information about the local population. Iranian officials had to decide whether to resist in the city, assemble forces outside it, or avoid exposing civilians to a bombardment. The port's merchants faced uncertain trade, disrupted credit, and questions about whose customs authority would now be recognized.

After the British arrival of Sir James Outram, military operations moved into the hinterland. Iranian forces attacked near Khushab in February 1857 but were defeated. Contemporary British accounts praised discipline and firepower; Iranian forces included provincial and tribal contingents whose mobility and tactical knowledge did not overcome the expedition's organization under the circumstances. It would be misleading, however, to imagine that every Qajar soldier was poorly equipped or that one battle revealed the capacities of an entire society. Routes, weather, unfamiliar ground, poor communications between regional commanders, and the need to keep a port supplied all shaped the fighting.

Meanwhile, diplomacy advanced faster than news could travel to the front. Farrokh Khan Ghaffari and British representatives signed the Treaty of Paris on 4 March 1857. British forces nevertheless attacked Mohammerah on 26 March and moved toward Ahvaz at the beginning of April, because the peace had not yet reached the expeditionary command in time to stop its operations. These actions must not be described as events that induced the signing: the signatures preceded them. The chronology illustrates a central feature of nineteenth-century diplomacy, in which transmission of information was slow even when governments had settled terms. News of peace did not instantly alter orders already issued to distant armies.

The operation against Mohammerah exposed the strategic significance of the Shatt al-Arab and Karun river approaches. River gunboats could reach positions inaccessible to a deep-draft ocean squadron; at the same time, mudbanks, currents, and fortified shores complicated their movement. Local inhabitants and rulers faced risks distinct from those discussed at European diplomatic tables. The town lay at the intersection of Iranian claims, Ottoman regional interests, Arab tribal authority, and international commerce. A bombardment that British officers treated as a limited military operation could have lasting consequences for trade and confidence in provincial government.

British troops eventually withdrew in accordance with the peace settlement. Their campaigns achieved the political aim of forcing the Iranian retreat from Herat but did not result in annexation of the Iranian coast. The distinction is important. British policymakers preferred coercive leverage and a compliant frontier settlement to the costs of administering large Iranian territories. The Qajar state preserved its independence and recovered occupied ports, yet its decisions about an eastern city had been constrained by a power operating from the southern sea. This outcome linked military geography to diplomacy more clearly than any single battlefield victory.

Section 72.12

Baluchistan, Sistan, and the Helmand: Negotiating the Southeastern Frontier

Beyond Herat, Iran's southeastern frontier was difficult to describe as a line. Makran extended along dry coastal and mountain routes between southeastern Iran and territories connected with the Khan of Kalat; Sistan consisted of oasis settlements and irrigated agriculture around the lower Helmand River and its changing delta. Seasonal water levels, pastoral migration, trading networks, and the claims of local rulers produced forms of authority that rarely coincided with a single surveyed boundary. Qajar governors asserted power from Kerman and Sistan, while Baluch leaders and Afghan authorities negotiated, resisted, or accommodated their demands. British India became involved because officials feared that disputes might threaten communications with the coast or destabilize Afghanistan's western approaches.

The development of boundary commissions marked a shift from inherited claims and local bargaining to maps, coordinates, and diplomatic awards. Sir Frederic Goldsmid supervised investigations associated with the Iran-Kalat boundary in the early 1870s. British officers collected testimony, examined routes, interpreted older grants or claims, and marked a line intended to reduce recurring disputes. But the commission's knowledge was selective: a route valuable to British postal or military planners might cut across the mobility of pastoral communities, and a boundary accepted by governments could remain incomprehensible or objectionable to households who grazed animals on both sides.

In Sistan, the dispute concerned not only prestige but cultivable land and water. Iranian officials asserted jurisdiction over settlements west and north of the Helmand delta; Afghan representatives advanced competing accounts of sovereignty, some linked to the Durrani period. In 1872 Goldsmid made an award distinguishing areas assigned to Iran from those recognized as Afghan. The Iranian government hesitated over aspects of the settlement, and political acceptance and local demarcation continued beyond the arbitral announcement. Students should not treat the 1872 award, the associated diplomacy of 1873, and later river-water disputes as a single completed act.

The Helmand made the boundary difficult even after it was delineated. Seasonal floods deposited silt, moved channels, and altered irrigation possibilities. A farmer's effective access to fields depended on canals, headworks, neighboring settlements, and years of favorable flow, not just on which state's color covered a map. Arbitrators sought to settle political jurisdiction, but deciding sovereignty over a bank did not automatically resolve the allocation of water. Later commissions would return to precisely these questions. It is therefore misleading to credit the nineteenth-century arbitration with permanently settling the entire hydrological and agricultural dispute.

Frontier negotiations also affected local elites. Some chiefs gained recognition, titles, or payments by cooperating with commission officers; others saw their bargaining power decline when distant governments accepted a fixed line. Merchants needed to know whether tolls, transit passes, and animal movements would be regulated differently. Households with relatives and obligations in multiple settlements could face conflicting authorities. British mediation sometimes favored an Iranian position against Afghan claims, demonstrating that imperial pressure did not operate uniformly against Tehran. Yet Britain gained an enduring diplomatic role as the arbiter of precisely which territorial claims would be accepted.

The broader historical issue is the difference between delimitation and demarcation. Delimitation describes a proposed border in an agreement or award; demarcation attempts to place that description on a changing landscape. Both are political processes, even when they are presented as neutral surveying. In Makran and Sistan, imperial techniques of mapping translated an unstable regional order into state frontiers. The outcome gave Tehran a clearer international claim in some districts while restricting claims in others, and it tied Iranian territorial security to decisions made by a British government whose first concern was British India.

Section 72.13

Russian Expansion into Central Asia: Tashkent, Bukhara, Khiva, and Kokand

Russian expansion into Central Asia altered Iran's security landscape without initially requiring a direct Russo-Iranian war. In the middle nineteenth century, large parts of Central Asia were governed by the khanates of Kokand and Khiva and the emirate of Bukhara, alongside tribal and oasis communities outside their control. These polities collected revenue, regulated commerce, competed over towns and irrigation resources, and negotiated with neighboring powers. Their relations with Iran were complex, including commerce, Persianate literary and religious ties, disputes over captives, and occasional diplomatic missions. They were not simply Iranian provinces waiting for Russian armies to arrive.

Russian offensives during the 1860s moved from fort lines and steppe bases toward densely populated oasis districts. Russian forces captured Tashkent in 1865, and subsequent operations placed Samarkand and major approaches to Bukhara under Russian control by 1868. Bukhara's emir survived, but with a greatly reduced freedom in external affairs. The difference between the incorporation of a city and a treaty relationship with a local sovereign was crucial. Russian officials often found it less costly to preserve an emir under supervision than to administer every village directly. A protectorate could secure strategic transit and exclude rival diplomatic influence without eliminating all local institutions.

The campaign against Khiva in 1873 brought another important center into a dependent relationship with Russia. Russian armies approached from different directions, experiencing severe shortages of water and transport across deserts; the difficulty of invasion helps explain why logistical planning mattered as much as battlefield superiority. The khan remained, but Russian demands shaped diplomacy and commerce. Kokand experienced recurrent conflict before Russian annexation in 1876 ended the khanate as a separate polity. Historians should distinguish the three outcomes: the emirate of Bukhara remained under a restricted sovereign, Khiva became a dependent khanate, and Kokand was incorporated into a Russian administrative region.

For Tehran the significance was geographical as well as political. The Russian frontier that had once been separated from Khurasan by large areas of autonomous or semi-autonomous power was moving closer. Qajar officials considered the security of Mashhad, the control of caravan routes, the fate of captured Iranian subjects, and the response of Turkmen groups living between settled states. Central Asian changes could bring new trading opportunities and possible relief from raids, but they also placed Russian military institutions within reach of northeastern Iran. Some Iranian merchants welcomed more predictable routes while worrying about customs arrangements or the treatment of travelers under Russian authority.

Diplomatic relations with Central Asian rulers did not cease as conquest advanced. Qajar officials earlier had sent missions to Khiva and Bukhara, partly to gather information and partly to negotiate over frontier insecurity and the fate of captives. Such exchanges show that Iranian rulers conceived of Central Asia as a region of active diplomacy, cultural connections, and political opportunity, not merely a blank space between empires. Reza Qoli Khan Hedayat's mission to Khwarazm in 1851, for example, offers a documented case of Iranian diplomacy and reconnaissance before the major Russian conquests. The later loss of freedom of action among Central Asian rulers narrowed these channels.

The human consequences were not confined to the movement of armies on campaign maps. Townspeople faced new officials, taxes, and commercial opportunities; religious scholars and landowners negotiated the meaning of property and law under imperial rule; pastoral and farming communities faced changed access to land and water. Russian administrative classifications sometimes created simplified ethnic and territorial categories that concealed older patterns of mobility. A history of the conquest must explain both military coercion and the choices made by rulers and populations faced with a growing empire. From Iran's perspective, it was the changing relation of power across its northeastern horizon that mattered most.

Section 72.14

The Turkmen Frontier: Raiding, Slavery, Warfare, and Regional Autonomy

Between settled Khurasan and the Caspian's eastern shore lay territories inhabited by Turkmen communities whose political organization did not conform to either Qajar provincial government or Russian imperial administration. Tekke groups lived in the Akhal and Merv regions, while Yomut and other Turkmen populations occupied different ecological zones, some nearer the coast and others toward the steppe. Kinship, community leadership, control of irrigation, and the movement of herds shaped political life. The groups were not interchangeable; rivalries could be as significant as their shared linguistic and cultural relationships. Some residents cultivated oasis fields while others moved seasonally with livestock, and economic strategies changed with climate, trade, and conflict.

Raiding was a major source of insecurity along the Qajar frontier. Armed parties, often called alaman in historical writing, attacked villages and caravan routes, seized livestock and property, and captured people for enslavement or ransom. Iranian cultivators, merchants, and travelers could suffer dispossession and long separation from their families. Persian chronicles and later travelers recorded this violence, though their descriptions sometimes generalized from particular raiders to all Turkmen peoples. Students should distinguish an account of specific attacks from claims that an entire population lived only by predation. Turkmen also traded horses, livestock, textiles, and other goods with Iranian towns, and peaceful exchanges could continue amid periodic warfare.

Slavery and captive exchange connected the frontier to larger regional markets. Captives might be held for labor, transferred to other owners, or released for ransom. Women and children were especially vulnerable, but older accounts often obscure their experiences. The Qajar government sought to secure its subjects through campaigns, negotiations, payments, and sometimes alliances with particular Turkmen leaders. Religious appeals and diplomatic demands about enslaved Muslims added moral and political pressure. Yet Iranian punitive expeditions could also devastate frontier settlements and deepen cycles of retaliation. Neither Qajar armed intervention nor Turkmen resistance should be romanticized.

The major Qajar expedition toward Merv ended disastrously in 1861. Forces commanded by Hamza Mirza Heshmat al-Dowleh marched to impose Iranian authority and confront Turkmen power. Qowshut Khan and forces associated with Merv defeated the expedition, exploiting advantages in mobility, terrain, intelligence, and the vulnerabilities of an army operating far from secure supply bases. Sources differ in their accounts of casualties and captured equipment, so exact totals should not be repeated as if securely counted. The defeat demonstrated that a large Qajar field force did not automatically control the steppe, and it narrowed the monarchy's willingness to undertake similar large-scale expeditions.

Turkmen leaders faced a difficult balance. Accepting Qajar gifts or protection could help against a local rival but risk subordinating community decisions to Tehran. Seeking Russian assistance carried parallel dangers. The growing Russian military establishment along the Caspian coast offered trade and new connections while gradually threatening independent political authority. Turkmen groups differed in whether they negotiated, resisted, or relocated, and their positions could change after a defeat or succession dispute. Historical agency did not mean unlimited freedom: powerful neighbors increasingly set the terms under which regional communities could act.

The frontier thus connects three forms of history. It is a history of state security, in which Qajar authorities sought to protect subjects and assert claims; a social history of captives, pastoralists, farmers, and merchants; and a history of imperial competition, in which Russian conquest transformed the balance between local autonomy and large states. Russia's eventual imposition of rule altered raiding patterns but brought coercive military violence and new systems of taxation and surveillance. The legitimate concerns of Iranian frontier households did not confer legitimacy upon every method by which Russia expanded. That distinction becomes indispensable when studying Geok Tepe.

Section 72.15

Geok Tepe, the Akhal Treaty, and the New Russo-Iranian Frontier, 1881–1885

Russian campaigns in Transcaspia advanced toward the Akhal oasis region during the 1870s, encountering prolonged resistance from Tekke Turkmen communities. The fortress of Geok Tepe became a focal point. A Russian expedition under General Lomakin was repulsed there in 1879; the outcome helped convince Russian commanders that the region could not be subdued by a brief raid. General Mikhail Skobelev led a better supplied expedition, bringing artillery, engineering capacity, and preparations for siege warfare. In January 1881 Russian forces breached the defenses and took the fortress. The conquest was accompanied by extensive killing, including violence against people fleeing the area. Precise casualty figures vary among sources and should be attributed, not stated as established totals.

Geok Tepe changed more than the military fortunes of one oasis. It broke an important center of organized opposition to Russian conquest, opened routes toward additional Turkmen settlements, and placed new pressure on the Qajar government's northeastern claims. Iranian authorities had sought to use Turkmen allegiances and local agreements to sustain a position beyond the settled districts of Khurasan. They now faced a state able to station troops, survey land, and conduct direct diplomacy from bases near the frontier. Russian officials argued that their conquest would make the border more peaceful, while Iranian observers had to consider the price of having a powerful imperial army where autonomous communities had previously stood.

On 21 December 1881, Iranian foreign minister Mirza Sa'id Khan Mo'tamen al-Molk and Russian envoy Ivan Zinoviev concluded a frontier convention in Tehran, often called the Akhal-Khurasan treaty. It described a boundary starting in the Caspian region and following the Atrak River toward Chat, before proceeding along terrain near the Kopet Dag foothills toward the Daragaz sector. The text and accompanying arrangements contained geographical details that cannot be reduced to a single straight line. It was a convention between states, not a declaration made by all Turkmen inhabitants who would live on different sides of the frontier. Ratification and later demarcation were distinct subsequent steps.

Additional confidential clauses and related diplomatic arrangements make the agreement more complex than a line drawn on a map. They addressed sensitive stretches toward Sarakhs and issues of influence in Turkmen territories farther east. Scholarly accounts identify the convention as part of a wider process in which Russian authorities sought freedom to consolidate their position while Iranian officials attempted to protect settled Khurasan and minimize further encroachment. Students should treat the detailed course of the frontier as a document requiring close geographical reading, and should not infer from it that all competing claims or local disputes ended immediately.

Russian forces extended control to Merv in 1884, in a political and military process that combined coercive pressure and the submission of local notables. The subsequent crisis near Panjdeh in 1885 involved Russian troops and Afghan forces, provoking serious concern in Britain. The two episodes were linked strategically, but neither was a direct Russian annexation of an established Qajar province. Merv had its own Turkmen rulers and communities; Panjdeh was contested in relation to the developing northern boundary of Afghanistan. Their subjection illustrated the narrowing space between Russian military power and the Afghan territories whose security mattered to Britain.

The boundary's effects were uneven. Iranian provincial authorities gained a more definite diplomatic line in parts of Khurasan, while some traditional grazing territories and links among settlements were divided. Merchants faced new documentation and customs arrangements; families might maintain contacts despite the presence of border officials; Russian and Iranian authorities continued negotiating local incidents. The wider political result was the transformation of a frontier of fluctuating allegiances into an increasingly regulated imperial border. For Qajar Iran this meant greater certainty about where state jurisdiction was supposed to end, but less capacity to influence politics beyond it.

Section 72.16

Regional Intermediaries: Merchants, Tribal Leaders, Consuls, and Frontier Officials

The rivalry of empires was conducted in council chambers, but many of its decisions depended on people who did not hold the rank of ambassador. Translators, merchants, tribal chiefs, provincial governors, local clerics, caravan leaders, and consular agents carried information and negotiated practical arrangements. They could enable imperial intervention, resist it, or turn it toward their own interests. Their importance becomes apparent when the boundaries of a diplomatic document are compared with the difficulties of enforcing it. A government in Tehran might instruct a governor to hold a pass, but that governor still needed guides, revenue, supplies, and cooperation from residents whose loyalties were complicated.

Merchants supplied unusually valuable information. A trader moving between Mashhad, Khiva, Bukhara, and the Caspian could report the state of caravan roads, prices, military movements, and political rumors. British and Russian agents both sought such reports, yet merchants were not simply informants for imperial states. They needed security for commercial caravans and credible mechanisms to recover debts or obtain compensation when goods were seized. Some tried to use consular protection; others feared that accepting foreign patronage would alienate local officials. Their family networks and linguistic skills gave them leverage, but they could be exposed when a patron's diplomatic standing changed.

Tribal and oasis leaders also used international rivalry strategically. A Turkmen chief might promise restraint along the Khurasan frontier in return for gifts or recognition, yet also negotiate with Russia or another Turkmen faction. Baluch rulers had comparable choices along the Makran routes, while Afghan provincial figures could seek support from Tehran, Kabul, or British agents. These choices could protect a settlement or bring weapons and subsidies. They could also draw a community into a conflict it lacked the strength to control. The presence of competing patrons did not necessarily create lasting local independence; often it made rivals more determined to classify territory and subjects under exclusive authority.

Consuls and political residents were intermediaries with different obligations. A consul's official responsibility was to represent a state and assist its nationals in commercial affairs; political residents in the Gulf exercised a broader diplomatic and supervisory role. In practice consular correspondence might address tax disputes, tariffs, mistreatment of travelers, shipping, or the suspected intentions of another power. Such papers are indispensable to historians but require caution. An officer who reported an imminent Russian threat could be expressing an informed judgment or making a case for additional resources. Local rumors frequently entered formal archives without becoming reliable evidence simply because a minister read them.

Persianate administrative and literary culture continued to connect people across borders. Secretaries drafted letters in Persian; religious scholars moved between centers of learning; shrines attracted travelers; poetry and manuscript traditions circulated despite changes of government. These connections complicate any description of imperial expansion as the sudden disappearance of earlier forms of regional life. Some networks adapted by dealing with new authorities, while others weakened when customs lines, military controls, or political hostility impeded movement. Connections that endured did not imply that border-making had no effect on communities.

The experience of intermediaries also reveals the limits of state control. A governor might claim to speak for all inhabitants while negotiating a line, but local landholders could refuse to cooperate or furnish contradictory evidence. British officers might understand a village through the testimony of one prominent man while overlooking women, tenants, and seasonal migrants. Russian authorities might promise protection against raids but demand taxes or labor. The historical task is to distinguish each actor's position, the evidence of its interests, and the actual outcomes. Iranian autonomy was affected not only by treaties signed in the capital but by how countless small transactions and local negotiations made external influence workable.

Section 72.17

Trade Routes, Telegraph Lines, and the Infrastructure of Imperial Influence

Influence over Iran did not require a foreign army in Tehran. Commercial routes and communications systems could connect the Qajar government to imperial institutions in ways that yielded benefits and generated dependence. Russian merchants and firms grew more prominent in northern Iran through Caspian shipping, caravan trade, and commercial networks oriented toward the Caucasus. British interests were powerful in the Gulf and in trade linked to India. Iranian merchants, often using family firms and credit arrangements across several cities, participated actively in both networks. They were not merely displaced by foreigners, although unequal customs arrangements, consular privileges, and the resources of large foreign firms could change the terms of competition.

Telegraph construction made these relationships unusually visible. Britain's rulers in India wanted a rapid connection with Europe that would not depend wholly on vulnerable maritime routes. One proposed line crossed Iranian territory between the Ottoman border, Tehran, and Bushehr, from which messages could continue through connections to India. Iranian authorities also had reasons to support such construction: faster communications aided provincial administration, trade, and the royal court's efforts to receive information from distant governors. An agreement was therefore an exchange of value, not merely a capitulation to foreign demands. Yet the question of who controlled a line, who could use it, and who could repair it had strategic importance.

The Anglo-Iranian engagement negotiated in December 1862 addressed the construction of a route from Khanaqin to Bushehr. A British Library treaty compilation preserves the signed arrangement and a subsequent Iranian ministerial communication in May 1863 concerning facilities at Bushehr. The Indo-European Telegraph Department, connected to the British Government of India, later managed important lines and facilities in Iran. It must be distinguished from the Indo-European Telegraph Company associated with other routes and from Iran's own telegraph administration. The existence of several organizations indicates that communications policy was not a single foreign monopoly but an evolving collection of agreements, construction projects, and administrative responsibilities.

Maintaining a telegraph required poles, wire, stations, technicians, guards, land access, and procedures for fixing damage. Local communities might supply labor, demand compensation for damaged fields, or dispute the crossing of a route. Operators developed expertise and obtained jobs, but British staff often enjoyed legal and administrative protections unavailable to Iranian employees. Iranian officials sought both the benefits of the line and the authority to regulate activities on Iranian land. International messages could serve commerce and diplomacy while giving imperial officials unusually swift knowledge of events. The material technology of a wire thereby expressed a political relationship.

Later conventions expanded routes, revised staff protections, and dealt with cable landings on the southern coast. These successive instruments must be kept distinct from the engagement of 1862; it would be inaccurate to assign all later privileges to the first document. They also illustrate the importance of studying implementation. A government might agree in principle to construction, but delayed payments, difficult terrain, maintenance disputes, and provincial resistance could alter the timetable. Telegraph service did not instantly place every Iranian settlement within one uniform communications system.

The broader transformation was a shift in the speed and geography of information. A provincial governor who once expected weeks before a report reached the capital could increasingly be questioned more quickly when connected to a telegraph line. Merchants could obtain information about prices and shipping with less delay, though access remained expensive and unequal. British and Russian diplomacy became more responsive to events, making imperial scrutiny of Qajar decisions more immediate. The monarchy gained an instrument of government at the same moment that foreign powers gained new means to monitor and influence it. That combination is characteristic of nineteenth-century Iranian autonomy: development and constraint frequently advanced together.

Section 72.18

Qajar Diplomacy and the Problem of Balancing Two Empires

For a government facing rival powerful neighbors, diplomacy often meant using differences among them to prevent either from obtaining an overwhelming advantage. Qajar officials pursued this strategy at different moments, seeking British help when Russian pressure grew and appealing to Russian interests when British demands threatened Iranian policy. Such maneuvering was not unique to Iran, but its success depended on circumstances. The court could gain time, financial assistance, or recognition; it could also provoke retaliation if a foreign government judged the arrangement hostile. The aim of balance was not to make the two empires benevolent. It was to preserve room for decisions in a system structured by inequality.

Under Mohammad Shah, the Herat siege illustrated the danger of misjudging that balance. Russian diplomatic interest did not supply the military or international protection required to overcome British objections. The shah's government could pursue an eastern territorial claim, yet the response through the Gulf made the resulting conflict far more costly. Under Naser al-Din Shah, the 1857 settlement imposed a different constraint, requiring Iranian restraint regarding Herat and recognizing a continuing British diplomatic role in disputes involving Afghanistan. These experiences encouraged caution, but they did not make the court incapable of initiative or remove its willingness to negotiate for other advantages.

The Russian advance toward Khurasan presented a parallel dilemma. Tehran could hope that Russian troops would reduce Turkmen raids and restore security for settled populations, but such security might be accompanied by a permanent imperial presence along Iranian lands. The government negotiated the 1881 boundary, recognizing a position it could not easily reverse by arms while seeking to clarify jurisdiction around vulnerable settlements. Iran's bargaining position was weak, but delimitation could also serve Iranian interests by providing a basis to protest future encroachments. To call the outcome simply surrender would obscure this limited practical calculation; to describe it as a fully equal bargain would ignore the military background.

Court politics complicated diplomacy. Ministers and princes competed for access to the shah, for governorships, and for resources, and different foreign missions sometimes cultivated different factions. British or Russian reports describing one Iranian official as a reliable ally may tell us more about the reporting diplomat's preferences than about the official's total political commitments. Some court figures sought foreign expertise or revenue through agreements; others feared that privileges would jeopardize fiscal authority or public confidence. Personal relationships could influence the timing of a concession or a diplomatic appointment, but they operated within wider institutional and economic constraints.

The shah's European journeys in the 1870s and later decades made foreign power visible to the court through railways, industrial production, public institutions, and military organization. These encounters generated possibilities for borrowing techniques, but the chapter should not convert them into a simple lesson that Iran needed to imitate Europe. Institutional reforms involved political costs, established interests, limited revenue, and disputes about legitimate authority. The full history of reform belongs to Chapter 73, while commercial concessions and foreign finance receive systematic treatment in Chapter 75. Here the central issue is how rulers understood the external environment in which those debates occurred.

By the later nineteenth century balancing produced a mixed record. Iran had not become a colony and retained treaty-making capacity, rulers, provincial institutions, and its own army. It could reject particular proposals, reopen negotiations, and seek competing sources of assistance. Yet its border settlements, communications arrangements, and growing commercial dependencies showed how much external leverage existed short of occupation. Independence was therefore a political achievement with limits, maintained through diplomacy as well as through the interests of Britain and Russia in preventing their rival from controlling Iran outright.

Section 72.19

Everyday Life in an Age of Imperial Rivalry: Merchants, Migrants, Workers, and Frontier Households

For ordinary inhabitants, imperial rivalry often arrived not as a diplomatic theory but as a change to trade, work, taxation, or security. A shopkeeper in Bushehr might notice ships arriving under unfamiliar flags, or a customs change that affected prices. A family cultivating land near the Helmand could discover that a boundary award placed a disputed canal under another government's jurisdiction. A caravan trader traveling from Mashhad toward Central Asia might face a safer route under new Russian patrols but also new documentation and fees. The consequences depended on livelihood, social position, gender, and locality; it is not possible to assign one uniform experience to all Iranian society.

Merchant communities were especially exposed to the combination of opportunity and unequal power. Iranian, Armenian, Indian, Arab, and other trading families operated through relationships that crossed borders. An experienced merchant could purchase goods at one port, arrange financing elsewhere, and distribute them inland through trusted agents. Consular protection sometimes helped recover debts or secure compensation, but it could also privilege foreigners or those able to claim a foreign connection. Iranian traders lacking comparable resources faced competition under conditions shaped partly by international treaties. The result was adaptation, resentment, and cooperation rather than the immediate disappearance of domestic commerce.

Migration created additional possibilities and vulnerabilities. Workers sought wages in expanding commercial centers; sailors and caravan personnel moved according to seasonal demand; pilgrims traveled to shrines across political lines; families fled raiding, warfare, or shortages. The Russian frontier gradually opened some routes while closing or regulating others. The identity assigned to a traveler by a state—subject, foreigner, refugee, migrant, or suspected agent—could determine the documents needed and protection available. Such labels were not necessarily how people understood their own lives, which might be organized around kinship, language, occupation, or religion rather than a new frontier.

Women participated in these economic and social changes through agriculture, textile work, domestic production, household finance, and the management of family migration. Their work is often absent from diplomatic and military chronicles, but its effects can be inferred cautiously from household organization, trade, and the consequences of disrupted harvests or absent male relatives. Where raiding led to captivity, women and children suffered particular harms that official accounts sometimes reduced to numbers or omitted. Historians must neither invent individual voices nor assume that the absence of a first-person testimony indicates an absence of political or economic significance.

State infrastructure could produce less dramatic, more persistent changes. Telegraph lines required laborers and guards, created stations and technical employment, and changed the rhythm at which officials requested reports. Some provincial authorities acquired faster access to Tehran; others remained outside the system. In Gulf towns the presence of a British naval vessel might make certain sea journeys safer while altering how local rulers settled maritime disputes. In the northeast, Russian military posts could diminish some forms of raiding yet compel communities to accommodate new officials. Benefits and burdens were distributed unequally, and the same policy might help one household while harming another.

These experiences also differed among religious and linguistic communities. Iranian Shi'i pilgrims and scholars, Sunni Muslims in frontier regions, Armenian merchants, Jews in commercial towns, Arab coastal populations, and Turkmen pastoralists were linked to different networks and institutions. Their choices cannot be explained by a simple division between a homogeneous Iranian nation and homogeneous foreign interests. In many places the state's claim to represent a community rested on negotiation with local authorities whose power derived from property, scholarship, kinship, or occupation. Everyday history reveals the range of people required to make or resist imperial policy.

Section 72.20

Historical Synthesis: Iranian Independence, Imperial Competition, and the Limits of Autonomy

The rivalries examined in this chapter did not constitute one continuous battle between Russia and Britain for possession of Iran. The two empires used military threats, diplomatic pressure, commercial relationships, boundary arbitration, communications systems, and local intermediaries. Their purposes sometimes overlapped: neither government necessarily wanted the Qajar monarchy to collapse, and both could benefit from a government in Tehran capable of maintaining basic order. Their differences nevertheless shaped Iranian decisions at crucial moments. The Qajar state survived within a system in which its own choices remained consequential but were increasingly conditioned by foreign capabilities.

Herat illustrates both Iranian initiative and its limits. Mohammad Shah and Naser al-Din Shah did not pursue eastern claims because British diplomats instructed them to do so. Their governments drew on dynastic precedent, frontier insecurity, and opportunities created by Herat's internal politics. The British response expressed strategic concerns centered on India and the fear of Russian influence, but Afghan rulers also pursued independent goals. The 1857 Treaty of Paris established a diplomatic constraint on Iran's eastern policy without transferring Herat to British sovereignty. Its outcome was neither a simple triumph of an Afghan nation already consolidated in its modern form nor proof that Qajar Iran had lacked an independent foreign policy.

The Persian Gulf demonstrates the connection between naval power and diplomacy. British treaties with Arabian coastal rulers, efforts to restrict maritime conflict, and military operations against Iranian ports gave British officials leverage beyond their formal territorial possessions. Yet Gulf towns, Iranian provincial governors, Omani authorities, and merchants continued to negotiate and resist. The fact that British troops withdrew after the 1857 settlement shows the difference between influence and annexation. The southern coast remained Iranian where Qajar government held it, but decisions concerning distant frontiers could be affected by Britain's ability to project force at sea.

The northeastern frontier reveals a different process. Russian conquest subordinated Central Asian khanates and Turkmen communities through annexation, protectorate relationships, and direct military violence. Turkmen raiding and the insecurity of Khurasan had been serious issues for Iranian households; the eventual Russian suppression of raids was accompanied by conquest and harsh coercion. After Geok Tepe, the 1881 frontier agreement made some jurisdictional limits clearer, while the subsequent incorporation of Merv and the Panjdeh crisis confirmed the new regional balance. State borders became more precisely defined as opportunities for older local political arrangements narrowed.

In Sistan and Makran, British arbitration shows that influence did not require a battlefield victory. Surveyors and diplomats redefined which claims would be internationally recognized, translating interlocking forms of authority into frontiers. Some awards protected Iranian positions while limiting others. Rivers, irrigation, grazing, and kin networks did not conform neatly to the lines. The mismatch between formal maps and local livelihoods continued to generate disagreements long after an award was issued. The process reveals that modern territorial boundaries were administrative achievements, political bargains, and sources of social conflict, not simply natural divisions waiting to be discovered.

Communications and commerce extended the same tensions into daily government. Telegraphs helped Tehran monitor its provinces and gave merchants new channels for information, but they also enabled British authorities to connect India and Europe through Iranian land and created foreign operating privileges. Iranian participation was therefore both purposeful and constrained. A similar pattern appeared in diplomatic balancing: rulers could secure resources and prevent outright domination by manipulating competition, yet each agreement risked creating new demands. The survival of Qajar sovereignty should not conceal these costs; the existence of costs should not erase the political achievement of independence.

Three conclusions follow. First, Iranian agency must be explained within material constraints rather than either celebrated without qualification or denied. Second, the history of imperial competition is inseparable from Afghan, Turkmen, Baluch, Arab Gulf, merchant, and other regional histories. Third, independence and autonomy are different concepts: preserving the dynasty's legal statehood did not guarantee a free hand over borders, security, infrastructure, or economic rules. These issues would contribute to internal demands for reform and criticism of concessions in the later Qajar period, subjects developed in Chapters 73 and 75.

The chapter's question remains open to historical judgment but not to a simple yes-or-no answer: how did the Qajar monarchy preserve Iran's formal independence while its strategic autonomy was increasingly constrained by two competing empires? The evidence points to adaptive diplomacy, local resources, and the rivalries of foreign powers, alongside material weakness and cumulative commitments. The same nineteenth-century relationships created possibilities for technical exchange and commerce while exposing the limits of sovereignty in an unequal international system.

Chapter Summary

The period from 1828 to approximately 1890 placed Qajar Iran in an increasingly unequal relationship with two expanding empires. Russia consolidated its position beyond the Caucasus and moved into Central Asia; Britain defended its Indian empire through diplomatic interventions, maritime power, and the shaping of routes and boundaries. Yet Iran continued to possess a monarch, provincial government, an army, and the capacity to conduct diplomacy. The distinction between formal independence and effective freedom of action is the chapter's governing analytical problem.

The phrase Great Game captures part of the rivalry but can conceal variations in British and Russian policy, internal disagreements between metropolitan and frontier authorities, and the independent priorities of regional rulers. Afghan, Turkmen, Baluch, Gulf, Iranian, and merchant actors were not merely the objects of imperial strategy. Their political decisions altered how crises began, how alliances were made, and which territorial arrangements could be imposed.

Herat was central because it was politically contested and strategically valued. Mohammad Shah's siege of 1837–1838 failed under pressure from local resistance and British intervention, while Naser al-Din Shah's 1856 capture led to an Anglo-Persian war. The Treaty of Paris of March 1857 obliged Iran to leave Herat and relinquish sovereignty claims. Herat's subsequent incorporation into the Afghan state under Dost Mohammad Khan was not dictated solely by the treaty; regional politics remained decisive.

The Gulf offered Britain an effective means of coercion far from Herat. British treaties with coastal rulers sought to regulate maritime warfare and protect trade, but also enlarged Britain's political role. British landings at Kharg and Bushehr and operations near Khushab, Mohammerah, and Ahvaz constrained Iran without annexing its southern provinces. The March 1857 peace was signed before the final British operations because information traveled slowly.

Sistan and Makran demonstrated the emergence of more explicitly demarcated frontiers. British arbitration translated local claims, waterways, and pastoral routes into diplomatic boundaries. Some awards supported Iranian claims; others limited them. The Helmand's shifting channels and the persistence of local networks meant that a written line did not settle every dispute about land or water.

In Central Asia Russian forces expanded through annexations and protectorate relationships, subordinating but not always abolishing local institutions. The Turkmen frontier combined trade and pastoral life with devastating raiding and enslavement. Qajar attempts to impose authority around Merv failed in 1861. Russian conquest at Geok Tepe in 1881 involved substantial violence; the ensuing Akhal-Khurasan frontier convention helped define the Russian-Iranian boundary, while Merv and the Panjdeh crisis showed the expanding consequences of Russian power.

Intermediaries—merchants, consuls, translators, tribal leaders, local governors, and religious specialists—were essential to the operation of diplomacy and the implementation of treaties. International networks also changed everyday life through taxes, employment, transport, shifting commercial protection, and patterns of migration. Economic benefits and burdens differed across social groups and regions.

Telegraph lines and other infrastructure connected Iranian government interests to the communications needs of British India. Negotiated construction provided speed and opportunities for Iranian administration while granting foreign departments operational advantages. The agreements illustrate a recurring pattern in which technical development and external influence advanced together.

Qajar diplomacy sought to preserve a balance between Russia and Britain and retained important capacities. Its achievements should not be dismissed because Britain and Russia were stronger, nor should survival be mistaken for unconstrained sovereignty. The pressures of this era formed part of the context for later reform programs, foreign commercial concessions, and domestic criticism of the political economy of imperial influence.

Chapter Reflection

Iran's history between 1828 and 1890 demonstrates that political independence is not a simple measure of freedom from external power. The Qajar state retained its dynasty, institutions, and diplomatic agency, yet its strategic choices were increasingly shaped by stronger neighbors. Its survival was partly the result of calculated negotiation and partly a consequence of rival empires' competing interests. Neither fact should be ignored when evaluating nineteenth-century Iranian political history.

The chapter's regional approach reveals how much is lost when the Great Game is imagined as a contest played only in London and St. Petersburg. Herati rulers, Turkmen communities, Gulf merchants, Baluch chiefs, Sistan cultivators, Iranian governors, and technical workers experienced these developments through their own interests and constraints. Their histories are integral to the diplomatic story, not incidental illustrations of it.

The expansion of telegraphs, commercial routes, mapped borders, and consular institutions also shows that international inequality developed through administration and ordinary economic practices as well as through armies. Modern forms of communication and state jurisdiction could bring advantages while empowering those able to impose the terms. This complicated legacy shaped later debates over institutions, commerce, concessions, and the meaning of sovereign government in Iran.

Chapters 73 and 75 continue those questions from different perspectives: reform within the Qajar state and the political economy of foreign capital. The central insight to carry forward is that Iranian decision-makers repeatedly acted within an unequal environment, and that the consequences of their choices must be judged against the real constraints, regional differences, and opportunities of their time.
