The Mongol Invasions of Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Mongol conquest of Iran was not a single campaign that ended in 1221. It was a sequence of invasions, occupations, negotiated submissions, local revolts, and renewed expeditions, extending across four decades and reshaping the political map of the Iranian world.

In 1218, a caravan of merchants traveling under Mongol authority reached the Syr Darya frontier city of Otrar, at the edge of the territories ruled by the Khwarazmshah Muhammad II. What happened to that caravan is one of the best known episodes in the history of the Mongol conquests, and also one of the most uncertain. The sources agree that merchants were killed and that a diplomatic crisis followed. They disagree about who gave the order, why, and how far the Khwarazmian ruler was responsible. Within a year, Mongol armies were moving across Central Asia on a scale that the established states of Transoxiana and Khurasan had not previously confronted.

This chapter explains that movement from the perspective of the Mongol imperial project and the societies it encountered. Chapter 44 examined the Khwarazmian Empire on the eve of the invasion: its rulers, its military and political tensions, and the dynastic struggle that continued after Muhammad II's death. Chapter 45 turns the same events around. It asks how a steppe confederation, unified only in 1206, became an imperial power able to coordinate armies across thousands of kilometers; how its commanders overcame walled cities and well-defended states; and how the people living in those cities, villages, and pastoral territories responded, whether by resisting, surrendering, negotiating, fleeing, or entering Mongol service.

The central argument is a matter of chronology and process. Popular accounts often compress the Mongol conquest of Iran into the years 1219 to 1221, ending with the fall of Merv, Nishapur, and Herat and the defeat of Jalal al-Din at the Indus. Specialist scholarship distinguishes at least three stages. Chinggis Khan's own campaigns of 1219–1223 destroyed the Khwarazmian state and established a first, partial Mongol presence. A second stage, under Ögedei and his western commanders Chormaqan and Baiju in the 1230s and 1240s, completed the conquest of western Iran and the Caucasus, subdued Isfahan, and brought Anatolian Seljuk power under Mongol influence. A third stage, under Möngke's brother Hülegü in the 1250s, eliminated the Nizari Ismaili strongholds, ended the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, and halted before the Mamluks of Egypt. Treating these as one invasion obscures how the conquest worked.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. The first would treat the Mongols as an undifferentiated destructive force, ignoring their political institutions, strategic reasoning, and capacity to absorb technicians, officials, and soldiers from conquered societies. The second would reduce conquered populations to victims or collaborators. Rulers, governors, merchants, scholars, religious leaders, and villagers made decisions under extraordinary pressure, and those decisions varied by place and time. The result was a patchwork: cities that were destroyed, cities that were spared after submission, dynasties that survived as tributaries, and districts that passed repeatedly between Mongol and local control.

Historians reconstruct these events from sources that are rich but difficult. Persian and Arabic chroniclers, some writing under Mongol rule, others as refugees or distant observers, give detailed accounts, but they also reflect shock, political allegiance, literary convention, and frequently inflated numbers. Mongolian, Chinese, Armenian, Georgian, and Syriac sources add perspectives from the empire's center and from regions under occupation. This chapter therefore explains not only what happened but what each kind of source can and cannot establish, and it deliberately avoids reproducing medieval casualty figures as verified counts.

Chapter 45 opens Volume IV and Part VIII. It describes how the conquest happened. Chapter 46 will examine what the conquest did to societies: casualties, displacement, settlement change, and the evidentiary problems of measuring demographic loss. Chapter 47 will examine how conquest became government, through the formation of the Ilkhanate under Hülegü's descendants. Keeping these three questions separate allows each chapter to stand on its own while forming a continuous account of one of the great transformations of Iranian history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Temüjin's consolidation of power and the institutions established in 1206 created an empire capable of long-distance campaigning.
  2. Analyze the breakdown of relations between the Mongols and the Khwarazmshah, distinguishing the established facts of the Otrar crisis from uncertain motives and later narratives.
  3. Describe the Mongol military system, including decimal organization, intelligence, logistics, siege methods, and the use of non-Mongol specialists and auxiliaries.
  4. Trace the principal campaigns of 1219–1223 across Transoxiana, Khwarazm, Khurasan, northern and western Iran, and the Caucasus.
  5. Compare the experiences of Bukhara, Samarkand, Gurganj, Merv, Nishapur, and Herat, distinguishing negotiated submission, resistance, punitive operations, and renewed revolt.
  6. Evaluate the range of local responses available to rulers, notables, and communities, avoiding the simple categories of hero and collaborator.
  7. Distinguish the successive stages of conquest under Chinggis Khan, Ögedei and his commanders, and Möngke and Hülegü.
  8. Explain how garrisons, tribute, regional commanders, and civilian administrators created Mongol authority before the Ilkhanate.
  9. Assess the political significance of the fall of Alamut and Baghdad and the limits of Mongol expansion demonstrated at Ain Jalut.
  10. Interpret primary sources by Ibn al-Athir, Juvayni, and Kirakos Ganjaketsi, identifying authorship, perspective, and limitation.
  11. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the speed and uneven success of the Mongol conquest.
  12. Connect the conquest to Chapters 46 and 47 without duplicating their analyses of demographic consequence and Ilkhanid government.

Historical Context

By the early thirteenth century, the Iranian plateau and its eastern borderlands were politically divided. The Khwarazmshahs, whose political history is examined in Chapter 44, had expanded from their base south of the Aral Sea to control Transoxiana, Khurasan, and parts of western Iran. They had displaced the Ghurids in the east and pressed against the Abbasid caliphate and the Qara Khitai in the west and north. Their authority, however, was recent and incompletely consolidated. Beyond the Khwarazmian domain, Iran contained a mosaic of regional rulers, among them Atabegs in Fars and Azerbaijan, local dynasties in Kirman and the Caspian provinces, the Nizari Ismaili strongholds in the Alborz and Kuhistan, and the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad, whose political position had been rebuilt as Seljuk power fragmented (the Seljuk period is the subject of Chapter 38).

Iranian cities of this period were commercially and intellectually vibrant. Nishapur, Merv, Herat, Balkh, Bukhara, Samarkand, Rayy, Isfahan, and Tabriz were centers of trade, scholarship, craft production, and religious learning, and the intellectual traditions described in Chapters 33 and 43 depended on their madrasas, libraries, and patronage networks. The rural economy rested on irrigated agriculture, much of it dependent on engineered water channels, together with pastoral economies in the surrounding steppe and highland zones. Caravan routes tied these cities to China, India, the Caucasus, Anatolia, and the Mediterranean.

On the eastern steppe, the Mongols had been unified by Temüjin only in 1206. Before that date, Mongolia was a landscape of shifting tribal confederations, including Mongols, Tatars, Kereyid, Naimans, and Merkits, linked by kinship, rivalry, and alliance. Steppe pastoralism, based on horses, sheep, goats, cattle, and camels, supported a mobile population with deep experience of organized hunting and warfare. The wider region also included the Jin dynasty of northern China, the Tangut state of Xi Xia, the Uyghurs of the Tarim basin, and the Qara Khitai in Central Asia. Control of caravan trade, tribute, and access to craftsmen and grain was central to steppe politics.

Three features of this background matter throughout the chapter. First, Mongol expansion was not simply movement of a steppe population toward richer territories. It grew from the political and military institutions built after 1206, from a leadership that incorporated non-Mongol officials and merchants, and from a strategic culture that treated submission and resistance as distinct categories of response. Second, the Iranian world was not a single target. Each region had its own rulers, resources, defenses, and political divisions, and these divisions affected how the invasions unfolded. Third, the conquest was only partly a matter of battlefield victory. Occupation required garrisons, grazing lands, administrators, tax assessments, and local intermediaries, which is why the conquest remained incomplete for decades after the first assault.

Section 45.1

Chinggis Khan and the Formation of the Mongol Empire, 1206–1218

Temüjin's rise to power was a political achievement before it was a military one. Born into a minor lineage of the Mongol steppe in the mid-twelfth century (the exact year is debated), he built his position through alliances, defeats, and the gradual accumulation of followers whose loyalty depended on service rather than descent alone. By the first years of the thirteenth century he had overcome the Tatars, the Kereyid, the Naimans, and other rivals. The sources for this period, above all the Mongolian-language Secret History of the Mongols (composed in the thirteenth century, though its exact date and authorship are debated), present these events through a narrative strongly shaped by the interests of Temüjin's lineage and should be read accordingly.

In 1206, at a great assembly held, according to the traditional account, near the sources of the Onon river, Temüjin was acknowledged as Chinggis Khan, a title whose precise meaning scholars continue to discuss. The assembly was a quriltai, a gathering of leaders that ratified major decisions. It did not make him an autocrat in a modern sense, but it formalized a new political order. Followers were organized into military and administrative units, and leadership positions were given to men who had proved their loyalty, including some of low birth and some from former enemy groups. A bodyguard, the keshig, brought young men from leading families into direct service, binding the elite to the ruler while giving him a trained corps of future commanders.

Succession and inheritance remained structural weaknesses, and the Mongol Empire was also understood as the shared property of the ruling family. Chinggis Khan assigned territories and peoples to his sons and relatives, and this system of appanages, together with the practice of selecting a Great Khan at a quriltai, would shape the politics of the next half century, including the decisions that sent armies westward after his death. The Mongols recognized a body of customary and imperial law associated with Chinggis Khan, often called the yasa, but historians debate how systematically it was codified, and its contents are known largely from later sources.

The new empire expanded quickly. Mongol armies raided the Tangut state of Xi Xia in the first decade of the century, secured the submission of the Uyghurs of the Tarim basin, and from 1211 launched a long war against the Jin dynasty of northern China. The fall of the Jin capital Zhongdu in 1215 was significant for this chapter. These campaigns taught Mongol commanders to attack fortified cities, brought Chinese, Khitan, and other specialists into Mongol service, and generated supplies, prisoners, and prestige. They also tied down substantial Mongol forces in the east, which helps explain why Chinggis Khan did not move against the Iranian world earlier.

The campaigns in northern China matter here only because they explain what arrived on the Khwarazmian frontier. By 1218, the Mongol Empire possessed a disciplined and flexible army, an experienced command, a growing body of non-Mongol officials, and a record of overcoming cities. What it lacked was a settled relationship with the powers to its west, a relationship that the next section shows deteriorating within a year.

Historical Vocabulary
Quriltai and Ulus

Definition: A quriltai was an assembly of Mongol leaders convened to approve major decisions such as the election of a khan, the launching of a large campaign, or the assignment of territory. An ulus was a people, realm, or patrimony; in imperial usage it came to describe the territories and followers allocated to a member of the ruling family, such as the ulus of Jochi.

Historical context: Quriltais ratified Chinggis Khan's authority in 1206, confirmed Ögedei's accession in 1229, and chose Möngke in 1251. Because power was understood as family property, the allocation of ulus territories shaped relations among Chinggis Khan's descendants, including the later relationship between Hülegü and the other branches of the family.

Why it matters: These institutions explain why Mongol decisions about Iran were made in an imperial family context, not by individual commanders acting freely, and why succession struggles in Mongolia could halt or redirect campaigns in the west.

Potential misunderstanding: A quriltai was not a democratic assembly. Its outcomes were shaped by kinship, military backing, and negotiation, and decisions could be contested or overturned.

Section 45.2

The Mongols and the Khwarazmian World: Diplomacy, Commerce, and the Crisis of 1218

The Mongol approach to Central Asia began through the territories of the Qara Khitai (the Western Liao), a state that had long dominated much of the region between the Tarim basin and Transoxiana. In the years before 1218, the Naiman prince Kuchlug had seized power there. Chinggis Khan sent his commander Jebe against him, and Kuchlug was defeated and killed in 1218. Muslim sources, notably Juvayni, report that Mongol forces then proclaimed religious tolerance in cities where Kuchlug had imposed restrictions on Muslim practice. Whatever the precise circumstances, the result was that Mongol power now bordered the Khwarazmian domain directly, with no major intervening state.

Contact between the two rulers preceded the crisis. Merchants from Muslim lands, including individuals from Transoxiana and Khwarazm, were already active in Mongol territories and in the Mongol-controlled caravan economy, and some entered Mongol service as envoys and intermediaries. Sources describe an exchange of embassies in which Chinggis Khan proposed peaceful relations and commercial exchange. Muhammad II's response, as reported by Persian and Arabic authors, was ambivalent, and the sources are not consistent about the exact terms. Chapter 44 considers how Muhammad II's court assessed these overtures; this chapter concentrates on the Mongol side.

The breakdown came at Otrar, a city on the Syr Darya. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources agree on the core facts: a trading caravan, perhaps several hundred men according to Juvayni's report (the figure should be treated with caution), arrived under Mongol sponsorship; its members were seized, and most were killed. The governor of Otrar, named in the sources as Inalchuq (also given a title, Ghayir Khan), is generally identified as the official responsible locally. The sources then diverge. Some say that Inalchuq suspected the merchants of espionage. Others imply greed. Others suggest that Muhammad II authorized or later endorsed the act. Because the major accounts were written after the Mongol conquest, when blame for the catastrophe was a political question, historians approach all of them cautiously.

A later stage of the crisis is better attested. According to Juvayni and others, Chinggis Khan sent envoys to demand redress, including the surrender of the governor. Muhammad II's response, in the sources' account, included the killing of at least one envoy, an act the Mongols treated as a grave violation of the conventions protecting messengers. The sources' details differ, and some modern scholars suspect that the narrative of an outraged Mongol response was shaped to present the invasion as a response to provocation. Other scholars point out that the Mongols had strategic reasons to attack the Khwarazmian state regardless, including access to trade, Khwarazmian influence among steppe peoples, and the opportunity presented by Khwarazmian divisions.

The historian should therefore separate three levels. The existence of a diplomatic and commercial crisis in 1218 is secure. The responsibility of particular individuals, and the exact role of Muhammad II, are uncertain. The question of whether the incident caused the invasion or merely supplied its justification is interpretive. What can be said is that Chinggis Khan, having secured his rear in China to the extent needed, chose in 1219 to commit a large army to a campaign against the Khwarazmian domain, and that the incident gave the campaign a narrative of legitimate retaliation that Mongol sources and their successors would emphasize.

Section 45.3

The Mongol Military System: Command, Intelligence, Mobility, and Siege Warfare

Mongol military power rested on organization more than on any single weapon. The army was arranged on a decimal system: units of ten (arban), hundred (jaghun), thousand (mingghan), and ten thousand (tümen), each under a commander, with higher commanders called noyans. This structure cut across older tribal loyalties and made it possible to detach, combine, and redirect forces. It also supported discipline: collective responsibility within units, and strong penalties for desertion or disobedience, were reported by observers from many societies. Command was hierarchical, but commanders were expected to exercise initiative within a shared strategic plan, and several, including Jebe and Subutai, became famous for their ability to operate semi-independently.

The typical Mongol warrior was a mounted archer, equipped with a composite bow, spare horses, and, depending on rank and campaign, armor and secondary weapons. Warriors usually brought several horses, which allowed rapid movement and rotation of mounts. Light cavalry could harass, feign retreat, and draw opponents out of formation; heavier units provided shock. These tactics were practiced in collective hunts and exercises that trained coordination across large areas. Historians caution against reducing the system to horse archery, however. The same army fought sieges, built siege works, crossed rivers, and operated in mountains and forests, and a long list of conquests, from northern China to Iran, depended on combined-arms operations.

Intelligence and diplomacy were integral to the system. Mongol commanders collected information from merchants, envoys, deserters, and local guides, and they sent reconnaissance forces ahead of main armies. Demands for submission accompanied campaigns, and the offer of terms was part of strategy: communities that surrendered early could be spared, while resistance risked severe reprisal. Reputation for ruthlessness, whether or not exaggerated by chroniclers, therefore functioned as a strategic instrument as well as a form of violence. An imperial courier network, the yam, provided relay stations with horses, supplies, and shelter, and carried messages and officials across great distances.

Siege warfare required resources the steppe did not itself produce. Through the campaigns in China, the Mongols acquired Chinese, Khitan, and other engineers, along with knowledge of counterweight and traction engines, incendiary devices, and mining and trench techniques. In Iran, they added local specialists, and Muslim engineers later contributed to Mongol siege capacity. The Mongols also used forced labor, often called hashar in Persian sources, drawing on prisoners and local populations to dig, haul materials, fill moats, and sometimes advance ahead of Mongol troops. This practice contributed to the effectiveness of siege operations and to the human cost of campaigns.

Logistics set limits. Large armies needed pasture, water, and fodder, and campaigns had to be scheduled around seasons and grazing. Mongol commanders often wintered armies in suitable regions, and the availability of pasture in Azerbaijan, Mughan, and parts of Khurasan influenced where garrisons were later established. Estimates of Mongol army sizes in the medieval sources are frequently inflated; modern historians generally propose much smaller numbers, but they remain uncertain, and this chapter does not rely on precise figures.

Historical Vocabulary
Noyan, Tümen, and Keshig

Definition: A noyan was a Mongol commander or lord. A tümen was the largest standard unit of the decimal army, nominally ten thousand men, though actual strength varied. The keshig was the imperial guard that served as personal escort, household staff, and training ground for future commanders.

Historical context: These structures were created or elaborated after 1206 and were used in campaigns from China to Iran. Commanders such as Jebe, Subutai, Chormaqan, and Baiju held authority over multiple units and often operated hundreds or thousands of kilometers from the Great Khan.

Why it matters: Understanding the decimal system explains how Mongol forces could be divided and combined without losing coordination, and why commanders in the west could pursue long-term strategies after the Great Khan's death.

Potential misunderstanding: Nominal numbers should not be taken as exact. A tümen might fall well short of ten thousand, and medieval authors often use such terms loosely.

Military and Strategic Study

How Mongol Armies Defeated Fortified Cities

Students should treat a successful Mongol siege as the result of several interacting elements rather than a single decisive advantage.

  • Reconnaissance and diplomacy: Envoys, merchants, and scouts identified defenders' strength, internal divisions, and approaches, while offers of terms encouraged factional disagreement inside cities.
  • Engineering: Specialists built and operated stone-throwing engines, constructed approach works, undermined walls, and diverted or dammed water where the geography allowed.
  • Labor: Prisoners and levied local laborers filled moats, built ramps, and carried materials, placing the human cost of siege work on conquered populations.
  • Coordination: Multiple army groups could converge on a region at once, preventing a defender from concentrating forces.
  • Psychological pressure: The reputation created by earlier destruction affected decisions of later garrisons and civilian leaders, though it did not prevent all resistance.

Analysis Questions:

  1. Why would reliance on local laborers and specialists make Mongol armies more effective but also more dependent on conquered societies?
  2. How might reputation for severity both encourage surrender and stiffen resistance?
  3. What limits did pasture and seasonal movement impose on Mongol campaigns?
Section 45.4

The Geography and Sources of the Mongol Conquest

The geography of the invasion followed several distinct corridors. From the Mongolian plateau, armies moved west through the Altai region and the Irtysh valley toward the Semirechye and the Syr Darya, the principal approach to Transoxiana. From Transoxiana they could move south across the Amu Darya (the Oxus) into Khurasan and the Hindu Kush, west across the Karakum desert margins toward the Caspian and northern Iran, or northwest toward the lower Amu Darya and Khwarazm. From northern Iran, routes led through Rayy, Qazvin, and Hamadan into Azerbaijan, the Mughan steppe, and the Caucasus, and from Hamadan and Iraq-i Ajam southward to Isfahan and Fars or westward to Baghdad. Each corridor posed different problems of water, pasture, and defensibility.

The terms used in this chapter are historical. Transoxiana (Mawarannahr) refers to the lands between the Amu Darya and Syr Darya. Khwarazm lay south of the Aral Sea, with its capital at Gurganj (modern Konye-Urgench in Turkmenistan). Khurasan in this period refers to a large region extending across what is now northeastern Iran, parts of Turkmenistan, and western Afghanistan, with major cities at Merv, Nishapur, Herat, and Balkh. These regions do not correspond to the borders of modern Iran, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Armenia, or Georgia, and much of the chapter's narrative takes place in territory that lies outside the present-day Iranian state.

The sources fall into several traditions. Arabic and Persian chronicles include Ibn al-Athir's al-Kamil fi'l-Taʾrikh, written in Mosul by an author who relied on reports from refugees and travelers; al-Nasawi's biography of Jalal al-Din, written by his secretary; Juzjani's Tabaqat-i Nasiri, composed in the 1250s by a scholar who had lived through events in Khurasan and Delhi; and Juvayni's Tarikh-i Jahan-gushay, completed around 1260 by an official in Mongol service. Mongolian sources, above all the Secret History, present the story from the viewpoint of the imperial family. Chinese sources, including envoys' reports and the dynastic history of the Yuan, add information about Mongol institutions and campaigns. Armenian and Georgian chroniclers, particularly Kirakos Ganjaketsi, Grigor of Akanc, and Vardan Arewelc'i, provide evidence for the Caucasus, and Syriac writers contribute to the history of the later campaigns. The later Persian world history of Rashid al-Din, examined in Chapter 49, draws on many of these materials but belongs to a later period of Mongol rule.

Each tradition has strengths and limits. Chroniclers writing in exile conveyed the emotional impact of invasion but often lacked access to events in distant regions. Authors writing under Mongol rule had access to Mongol informants and archives, but they also had political reasons for emphasizing Mongol legitimacy or Muslim suffering. Medieval authors in all traditions used numbers rhetorically. Modern studies of the Khurasan campaigns, including recent research by Shivan Mahendrarajah, reconstruct campaign sequences by comparing these sources with archaeological and geographical evidence, and the resulting accounts are more cautious about numbers and more attentive to operational logic than earlier narratives.

Editorial convention. Names follow this textbook's established practice: widely recognized English forms where they exist (Chinggis Khan, Baghdad, Samarkand), and consistent scholarly transliteration for less familiar names. Mongol names such as Möngke, Ögedei, Hülegü, and Chormaqan use the forms most common in modern scholarship; variant spellings appear in the older literature.

Division II

The First Great Invasion, 1219–1222

Sections 45.5–45.8
Section 45.5

The Opening Campaign: Otrar, the Syr Darya, and the Division of Mongol Forces, 1219–1220

Chinggis Khan assembled his forces in the summer and autumn of 1219 on the upper Irtysh, having left a substantial portion of the army to maintain Mongol positions in northern China. The invasion that followed was coordinated rather than a single thrust. Medieval sources describe several army groups moving along different lines, and although their numbers are unreliable, the pattern is credible: a large force under Chinggis Khan and Tolui, forces under Chaghatai and Ögedei directed at Otrar, a force under Jochi moving down the Syr Darya, and detachments sent toward the upper river and the Ferghana valley. The division complicated Khwarazmian defense, which relied on garrisons distributed across cities and could not easily concentrate against multiple advances.

Otrar was the first major siege. Chaghatai and Ögedei besieged the city, whose governor Inalchuq, according to the sources, had every reason to expect no mercy. The siege lasted several months, with the date of its end usually placed in the early months of 1220, though sources differ. The citadel held after the outer city fell. When it was finally taken, Inalchuq was executed. The treatment of the city's population is described variously in the sources, and historians treat the claims of total massacre cautiously, noting that the Mongol practice was to separate groups: those who were killed, those taken as laborers or soldiers, and artisans who were sent away for service elsewhere.

Elsewhere along the Syr Darya, Mongol commanders moved against other fortified towns. Some, such as Sighnaq and Ashnas, were attacked after refusing submission, and others, including some smaller places, submitted. Jochi, whose later ulus would lie in the western steppe, was involved in operations along the lower river, including the region of Jand, and the Mongol approach toward Khwarazm itself was partly prepared by these movements. The Mongol strategy therefore combined direct assault with opportunistic use of surrender, and it used the first successes to create a reputation that would influence later decisions.

Meanwhile Chinggis Khan, instead of following the obvious road to Samarkand, took a force across the Kyzylkum desert to Bukhara, reaching it from the west, an unexpected direction that cut communications between the city and the interior and may have disoriented defenders. This route, reported by Persian sources, is often cited as an example of Mongol operational deception, although historians note that the available accounts do not allow us to reconstruct its logistics in detail. The move placed Chinggis Khan in a position to divide the Khwarazmian domain: Samarkand to the east, Bukhara in the center, and the road to Khwarazm and Khurasan beyond.

Muhammad II, as Chapter 44 explains, chose not to meet the Mongols in a single decisive battle. He left garrisons in major cities and withdrew to Khurasan. The consequence, from the Mongol perspective, was that campaigns became a series of sieges, which the Mongols were prepared to conduct, and that the Khwarazmian ruler himself became a target of pursuit, a task entrusted to Jebe and Subutai (Section 45.9). The opening campaign established the pattern of the first invasion: coordinated movement along multiple routes, siege warfare against divided defenders, and the use of terror and negotiation as complementary tools.

Section 45.6

The Conquest of Bukhara, Samarkand, and Gurganj, 1220–1221

Bukhara was one of the great intellectual and commercial centers of the Islamic world, home to the legacy of the Samanids (Chapter 35) and a major madrasa culture. According to Persian sources, when Chinggis Khan arrived in early 1220, a portion of the garrison attempted to break out and was destroyed, and the city's civil leaders negotiated the surrender of the city while the citadel's garrison held out for about twelve days. The Mongols took the city, plundered it, and, according to Juvayni, a fire destroyed a large part of the urban fabric. Residents were treated according to a calculated hierarchy: those with skills were separated for service, others were conscripted for siege labor or sent to serve in the army, and many were killed or displaced. Narratives of what Chinggis Khan said to the city's notables, including a speech attributed to him in the main mosque complex, are literary constructions and should not be read as transcripts.

Samarkand, the principal city of Transoxiana, fell soon afterward, probably in the spring of 1220. It was a large and well fortified city with a substantial garrison, much of it drawn from Turkic military groups loyal to the Khwarazmian ruler's family. Sources report that the city's religious and civil leaders sought terms while the garrison's loyalty was divided. After a few days of fighting, Mongol forces entered, and the sources describe the separation of soldiers, artisans, and ordinary residents, with the soldiers receiving the harshest treatment. Reports that tens of thousands of artisans were sent east to serve the Mongol Empire are plausible as a general description of policy, though the numbers should not be treated as exact.

The sequence at Bukhara and Samarkand illustrates a theme of the entire conquest: the difference between taking a city and controlling a region. Neither city became the seat of a stable Mongol administration immediately. Chinggis Khan left officials, including individuals with local knowledge, to oversee the collection of resources, and Mongol commanders returned to the area repeatedly. Transoxiana only gradually acquired a more regular governance, under officials such as Mahmud Yalavach and later his son Masud Beg, whose careers extend beyond the period of this chapter.

The siege of Gurganj, the capital of Khwarazm, followed in the winter and spring of 1221. Jochi's forces approached from the north, and the army of Chaghatai and Ögedei joined them. The sources report a prolonged struggle, house-to-house fighting, and serious disagreement among the Mongol princes, which delayed coordination and, according to Juvayni, required Chinggis Khan's intervention to restore command. Defenders included both local forces and members of Khwarazmian military communities. The city fell in 1221 (the exact month is debated, with spring most commonly proposed). Its fall is also associated with the damage done to the river system on which the region depended, which some sources describe as the breaking of dikes and flooding. The longer-term environmental consequences are treated in Chapter 46.

By the end of the winter campaign, the Khwarazmian domain north of the Amu Darya was broken. Muhammad II, as Chapter 44 explains, had died in exile, and his son Jalal al-Din was leading a continuing resistance. For the Mongols, the central question became how to pursue and eliminate the remaining Khwarazmian forces and secure control over Khurasan, which required a new campaign across the Amu Darya under Chinggis Khan's youngest son.

Primary Source Window I

Ibn al-Athir, al-Kamil fi'l-Taʾrikh (The Complete History), on the Mongol Invasion

Creator: ʿIzz al-Din Ibn al-Athir (1160–1233), an Arabic-language historian in Mosul · Date: composed in the 1220s, covering events to about 1231 · Type: Arabic annalistic world chronicle · Translation: Portions have been translated into English; students should consult a reliable translation rather than rely on this paraphrase.

Context: Ibn al-Athir wrote from northern Iraq, at a distance from the main events in Transoxiana and Khurasan. His account of the Mongol invasion draws on reports from refugees, merchants, and travelers. He was a contemporary of the early conquests, which gives his narrative immediacy, but he did not witness the events he describes in the east.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): Ibn al-Athir introduces the Mongol invasion by explaining that he had long hesitated to record it, because the events seemed so terrible that he could hardly bear to write about them. He presents the invasion as a catastrophe of an unprecedented kind for Muslim lands, describes the destruction of major cities and the killing and enslavement of their inhabitants, and conveys a sense that the old political order had suddenly collapsed.

Analysis: The passage is valuable as testimony to the shock of the invasion among Muslim contemporaries and to the speed with which reports of destruction spread through the Islamic world. It also shows how a distant observer organized the narrative: by emphasizing scale and moral significance rather than operational detail. Historians use Ibn al-Athir to date events, to identify the cities involved, and to understand reactions in Iraq and Syria.

Limitations: Ibn al-Athir did not observe the campaigns he describes, and his information about particular sieges reflects secondhand reports. His numbers, like those of other medieval chroniclers, are rhetorical and are not reliable demographic evidence. His moral framing, appropriate to a Muslim chronicler of the period, shapes what he includes.

Perspective: The account represents an educated Arabic-speaking Muslim author in the Jazira region. It does not represent Mongol officials, Khwarazmian soldiers, peasants, or the inhabitants of the cities described.

Section 45.7

Tolui's Campaign in Khurasan: Merv and Nishapur, 1221

Khurasan, the old heartland of eastern Iranian urban life, was the next major objective. In late 1220 and early 1221, Chinggis Khan sent his youngest son Tolui across the Amu Darya with a strong army, while other forces moved into the Hindu Kush foothills and toward Balkh. The campaign had immediate strategic purposes: to eliminate the remaining Khwarazmian garrisons, to prevent a regrouping of Khwarazmian forces, to secure the cities and routes of the region, and to punish resistance. Because Muhammad II's family and many of his soldiers had retreated across Khurasan, the region also became the scene of sustained pursuit.

Merv, one of the great cities of the medieval Islamic world and a major center of agriculture, scholarship, and trade, was the first target. The Mongols arrived in early 1221 after a rapid advance and, according to Juvayni and others, began a siege that lasted several days, during which the governor negotiated with Tolui. The sources agree that the city surrendered and that the aftermath was extremely destructive. They also agree that Merv's population, including its notables and officials, was subjected to a systematic process of selection, with some sent to labor or military service and many killed. The medieval casualty figures, which reach into the hundreds of thousands or more, cannot be verified and exceed plausible estimates of the city's population. Modern scholarship accepts that the city suffered catastrophic losses while treating the numbers as rhetorical. Their evaluation belongs to Chapter 46.

Nishapur followed in the spring of 1221. It was a leading center of scholarship and trade, and the Mongol approach was linked to a specific incident: during the siege, according to the sources, Chinggis Khan's son-in-law Toghachar was killed by an arrow or similar missile. The sources describe the Mongol response as unusually severe, and a number of authors treat this as the reason for the particular harshness of the reprisals. The city was taken after a short assault, and the sources describe the killing of much of the population, the systematic destruction of buildings, and subsequent revisits by Mongol forces to ensure that no surviving inhabitants remained. Historians treat such descriptions as an indication of the policy of exemplary punishment that Mongol commanders used against cities that had killed a Mongol prince, while noting the limits of the evidence.

Other cities in Khurasan experienced different sequences. Tus, near Nishapur, was attacked, and smaller towns either submitted or were raided. Balkh and the other northern centers were affected by operations led by Chinggis Khan himself. The result was a differentiated pattern: some cities were destroyed or devastated, some were spared in return for submission, and some, as the next section shows, submitted and later revolted. This variety should caution students against treating the conquest of Khurasan as a single event with a uniform outcome.

For the Mongols, the campaign accomplished its military objectives by early 1221, removing Khwarazmian garrisons from most of the main cities and eliminating the possibility of a unified defense of Khurasan. It did not create a stable administrative framework. Tolui moved on, leaving a limited presence, and the region remained exposed to the return of Khwarazmian forces under Jalal al-Din, which is why the winter of 1221–1222 saw renewed fighting.

Section 45.8

Herat, Balkh, Sistan, and the Wider Eastern Iranian Frontier, 1221–1222

Herat illustrates the difference between submission and settlement. In 1221, according to the sources, the city's leaders surrendered to Tolui's forces. Mongol officials were installed, Herat's population was largely spared, and a small Mongol garrison was left. For a moment, Herat appeared as an example of the benefit of early submission, and later authors sometimes contrasted it with the fates of Merv and Nishapur.

The situation changed after Jalal al-Din's victory over a Mongol force at Parwan in 1221, an episode explained in Chapter 44. News of the victory spread through the region, and in Herat, as in some other places, local leaders and soldiers took up arms against the Mongol representatives. The revolt was part of a wider pattern in which communities that had submitted calculated that Mongol power might be weaker than it had seemed. In response, Chinggis Khan sent a new army under Eljigidei. The siege of Herat that followed lasted for several months, and the city fell in 1222 after prolonged fighting. The sources describe it as extremely destructive, again with unreliable numbers, and Herat's long-term history shows both the severity of the blow and the city's later recovery under local dynasties, treated in later chapters.

Balkh, on the northern edge of Khurasan, is described in Juvayni's account as having submitted early, yet suffering heavy damage. The sources are not fully consistent about the sequence, and modern historians suspect that some of the destruction attributed to the first campaign occurred in later operations. Farther east, the campaigns of Chinggis Khan and his forces in the Hindu Kush region, including the siege of Bamiyan, resulted in significant destruction, and Ghazni and Ghur were affected by operations connected to the pursuit of Jalal al-Din.

Sistan, in the southeast, lay on the margins of the principal campaigns. Mongol forces operated in the region in the early 1220s, and its capital and cities suffered raids and sieges, but its history in these decades is poorly documented, and local dynasties continued to exist under changing conditions of overlordship. For this reason, historians characterize the eastern Iranian frontier as a zone of overlapping and shifting authority rather than a region cleanly subjected to Mongol rule by 1222.

In late 1221, Jalal al-Din was defeated at the Indus river, and he escaped across it with a small following. Chinggis Khan then moved against the surrounding regions, pausing to establish control in Afghanistan, and he left Transoxiana and Khurasan in the care of commanders and officials before returning to Mongolia by 1225. The conquest of eastern Iran was thus both a military victory and an incomplete occupation. The existence of Herat's revolt, the continuing resistance of local rulers, and Jalal al-Din's survival would shape Mongol decisions for the next decade.

Cause and Effect

Mobility, Coordination, Siege Warfare, and Fragmented Resistance

Long-term causes

Mongol military organization after 1206; experience of sieges in northern China; access to non-Mongol specialists; and the political divisions of the Khwarazmian world, whose rulers, military elites, and cities did not form a unified defense.

Short-term causes

The Otrar crisis; the decision of Khwarazmian commanders to disperse forces among cities; and Chinggis Khan's ability to coordinate several army groups along separate routes.

Immediate trigger

The collapse of diplomatic relations in 1218 and the Mongol decision to commit a large army to the campaign of 1219.

Transformation

Within roughly three years, Mongol armies took the principal cities of Transoxiana, Khwarazm, and Khurasan, and destroyed the Khwarazmian central state.

Immediate effects

Severe destruction in several cities; forced movement of artisans and laborers; local revolts such as that at Herat; and the escape of Jalal al-Din.

Long-term consequences

A fragmented political landscape, continued Mongol campaigns, and the need for sustained occupation, treated in Sections 45.13–45.16 and in Chapters 46 and 47.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the pace of the conquest. Mobility without siege capacity would have left cities untouched; siege capacity without coordination would have exhausted armies; and neither would have been decisive without the divisions among defenders. The fragmentation of resistance was both a cause of Mongol success and a result of Mongol strategy.

Historical Debate

Was Mongol Military Superiority Sufficient to Explain the Rapid Conquest of the Khwarazmian Empire?

The question: The Khwarazmian Empire fell within a few years to an army that had come from beyond the settled world. Historians debate how much of this outcome reflects Mongol military superiority and how much reflects Khwarazmian weaknesses and wider circumstances.

Interpretation A — Mongol organization and tactics were decisive: Some accounts emphasize the decimal organization, discipline, mobility, intelligence-gathering, and combined-arms capability of Mongol forces, arguing that the empire built a military system that few contemporary states could match.
Interpretation B — Khwarazmian political and military weaknesses were at least as important: Others emphasize that Muhammad II's empire was recently formed, politically divided, and dependent on garrisons whose loyalty was uncertain, and that the decision to disperse forces rather than concentrate them increased the Mongols' advantage.
Interpretation C — The explanation lies in the interaction of several factors: A third approach treats the outcome as the product of Mongol strategy, Khwarazmian divisions, the importance of local defection and submission, and the capacity of the Mongols to incorporate local specialists and auxiliaries.

Evidence: The debate draws on the same sources discussed in Section 45.4, plus comparative evidence from other Mongol campaigns, including those where the Mongols failed or were checked, such as Parwan in 1221 and Ain Jalut in 1260.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely interpretive, concerning how to weigh causes, though it also involves problems of evidence, since the medieval sources rarely give reliable information on army sizes and logistics.

Why it matters: If Mongol superiority was overwhelming, the conquest looks inevitable; if it depended on circumstances, the conquest appears more contingent, and the decisions of local rulers and commanders become more significant.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that would help determine the relative weight of Mongol strength and Khwarazmian weakness, and explain what each would show.

Section 45.9

Jebe and Subutai: The Pursuit across Iran and the Caucasus, 1220–1223

While Chinggis Khan and Tolui were besieging cities in Transoxiana and Khurasan, two of his most trusted commanders, Jebe and Subutai, were assigned a different task. With a force of perhaps two tümens (the exact size is uncertain), they were sent in 1220 to pursue Muhammad II, who had fled westward from Transoxiana. The mission was one of pursuit, reconnaissance, and disruption, not of permanent occupation. It was nevertheless to become one of the most remarkable expeditions of the Mongol era.

Their route took them through Khurasan, in the area of Nishapur, and then through Mazandaran and toward Rayy. Muhammad II eluded them, and according to Persian sources, he died in late 1220 or early 1221 on a small island in the Caspian Sea near Abaskun, in poor health and with few followers, an episode examined from the Khwarazmian perspective in Chapter 44. Jebe and Subutai turned from pursuit to campaign. They moved through northern and western Iran, taking or receiving the submission of cities and, in several cases, extracting tribute. Rayy, Qum, Hamadan, Zanjan, and Qazvin all experienced the arrival of Mongol forces, with differing outcomes: some negotiated agreements, some were plundered, and some resisted and were punished.

In Azerbaijan, Tabriz secured peace through payment of tribute, a choice that spared the city but also exemplifies how large cities could use negotiated agreements to preserve themselves. The Mongols then moved into the Caucasus. In early 1221 they defeated a Georgian army, reportedly in a feigned-retreat maneuver, and the Georgian kingdom, which had been at the height of its medieval power, suffered a severe blow. The raiders then returned south and moved through parts of Iran again, passing Maragha, which was attacked, and Hamadan, which had rebelled against Mongol-appointed officials and was punished. The pattern of return visits shows again that a first submission did not guarantee continued obedience.

Their final movement was north. Passing through the Derbent corridor, which the sources describe as achieved through deception or negotiation with local rulers, the two commanders entered the steppe north of the Caucasus, where they defeated Alans and Qipchaqs and, in 1223, a coalition of Rus' princes and Qipchaq forces at the Kalka River. They then crossed to the Volga region, were defeated or checked by the Volga Bulgars in some accounts, and rejoined Chinggis Khan in Central Asia in 1223–1224. Jebe died around this time. Subutai later led Mongol campaigns in Eastern Europe in the 1230s and 1240s, which belong to another history.

The expedition's importance for Iran is twofold. It showed that Mongol forces could operate across northwestern Iran and the Caucasus against varied opponents, gathering information that would later be useful. And it showed that raiding was not equivalent to conquest: the expedition did not leave behind a stable administration, and many regions it crossed remained politically independent or recovered quickly. The establishment of lasting Mongol authority in the west would require the sustained campaigns of Chormaqan and Baiju in the following decades.

Section 45.10

Resistance after the First Invasion: Jalal al-Din and Regional Warfare, 1221–1231

After Chinggis Khan's armies withdrew toward Mongolia, a political vacuum persisted in Iran. Chapter 44 follows Jalal al-Din Mingburnu's career in detail. Here the relevant point is how his activities shaped Mongol decisions. After his escape across the Indus in late 1221, Jalal al-Din spent several years in northern India before returning through Makran and Kirman to western Iran in the mid-1220s. He asserted authority in Fars, Iraq-i Ajam, and Azerbaijan, took Tabriz in 1225, and fought several wars against neighboring rulers, including the Georgians, the Abbasid caliph's forces, and, at the end, the Seljuks of Rum and the Ayyubids in a battle in 1230.

For the Mongols, Jalal al-Din represented an unresolved problem. He retained the claim to the Khwarazmian inheritance, commanded troops and followers, and disrupted regional politics, while Mongol commanders left to administer eastern territories lacked the forces to eliminate him. He was also, however, a destabilizing presence for local rulers, some of whom preferred a distant Mongol overlord to a nearby Khwarazmian strongman. The Mongol response included periodic raids and, from around 1230, the dispatch of Chormaqan with a substantial army charged with eliminating Khwarazmian power in the west. In 1231, Jalal al-Din was killed in the mountains of the Diyarbakir region, reportedly by a local Kurdish assailant, after his defeat by Mongol forces.

The elimination of Jalal al-Din did not end regional warfare. His soldiers dispersed. Some entered the service of local rulers, including the Seljuks of Rum and the Ayyubids, and others continued as independent raiders. The political landscape in Iran and Afghanistan after 1221 remained fragmented, with Mongol garrisons, local dynasties, rival claimants, and military bands overlapping. Some regions, such as Kirman under the Qutlugh-Khanid line, accepted Mongol overlordship early and survived as tributary dynasties, and the Salghurid atabegs of Fars preserved their position through submission and tribute, as the sources indicate. Others, including parts of northern and western Iran, experienced repeated incursions without stable settlement.

This section's chronology shows why the conquest must be treated as a process. The destruction of the Khwarazmian state in 1220–1221 and the death of Jalal al-Din in 1231 are separated by a decade in which Mongol authority in Iran was uneven. The first invasion destroyed a central power; it did not replace it with a Mongol government. The second stage, covered in Division IV, did.

Section 45.11

Cities and Local Rulers: Surrender, Negotiation, Resistance, and Accommodation

Between 1219 and the 1250s, rulers and communities across Iran faced the same strategic question: whether to resist or submit. The answers differed. Understanding why requires attention to the options available, the information they had, and the political context of each place. A governor with a strong garrison and walls may have thought resistance viable. An urban notable group faced with a divided military may have preferred negotiation. A regional dynasty with limited forces and a distant overlord might hope to retain its position by submitting early. These decisions were made quickly, under uncertainty, and often with imperfect knowledge of what happened in other places.

Mongol demands were generally of a recognizable kind: surrender, provision of supplies and troops, payment of tribute, acceptance of Mongol officials, and sometimes personal attendance at the Mongol court. In return, communities that complied were typically spared the worst consequences of conquest, though compliance did not guarantee immunity, as the examples of Herat and Balkh show. Rulers who submitted could often retain their positions as vassals: the Qutlugh-Khanids of Kirman, the Salghurids of Fars, the Kart rulers of Herat after later confirmation, and many Armenian and Georgian princes illustrate the pattern. Others, who resisted, could lose their lives and their cities.

Local divisions often decided outcomes. In cities where civil leaders, religious scholars, and military commanders disagreed, the Mongols could exploit divisions, accept partial submissions, or attack the more resistant faction. Sources on Isfahan emphasize long-standing rivalries among its legal and religious factions, which are said to have contributed to its vulnerability (Section 45.14). The historian should treat such explanations with caution, because they may reflect later attempts to assign blame, but the general principle is plausible: a city without a unified leadership was difficult to defend.

The terms hero and collaborator do not capture these choices. Some who resisted did so because they judged that Mongol promises were unreliable; others because they could not surrender without risking their own deaths; others because they were bound by obligations to a ruler. Some who submitted did so to protect a city's population, while others pursued their own interests. Many who entered Mongol service, as officials, secretaries, translators, tax collectors, or engineers, remained deeply embedded in their own communities and used their positions to protect or advance local interests. The later history of figures such as Juvayni shows how an official in Mongol service could also be a major historian and defender of Iranian cultural life.

Multiple Perspectives

Facing the Mongol Demand: Local Views of Surrender and Resistance

Note: these perspectives are analytical reconstructions based on patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals.

Urban notables and religious leaders: Often concerned with protecting the city's population, preserving institutions such as mosques and madrasas, and avoiding the destruction that resistance might bring. Their capacity to negotiate depended on whether the military commanders agreed.
Military governors and garrison commanders: Responsible for defense and often personally exposed to Mongol reprisal. Their decisions depended on the strength of their forces, their loyalty to a ruler, and their expectation of relief or reinforcement.
Regional dynasts and tributary rulers: Sought to preserve their positions by early submission, payment of tribute, and provision of troops. Their agreements could protect their domains but bound them to Mongol demands.
Merchants and artisans: Faced selection for service, loss of property, or displacement. Some skills, particularly craft and engineering expertise, made individuals valuable to the Mongols, but that value could mean forced relocation.
Rural communities and pastoralists: Exposed to requisitioning, passing armies, and disruption of irrigation and markets. Their options were limited to flight, concealment, or submission, and they left few records of their own.
Mongol commanders: Worked within instructions from the Great Khan, assessing whether cities could be taken quickly, whether terms would secure resources, and how reprisals would affect other communities.

Complexity: These groups did not have equal power. Mongol commanders held military initiative, while local communities responded under constraint. The point is not equivalence but that each group's decisions shaped the pattern of conquest.

Section 45.12

Military Conquest and the Experience of Ordinary Communities

For most inhabitants of Transoxiana, Khurasan, and western Iran, the Mongol invasion was experienced through disruption of daily life rather than through the high politics of rulers and commanders. The arrival of an army, whether Mongol or local, meant demands for food, fodder, animals, and labor. Villages along the routes were forced to supply provisions, and districts near campaign areas faced requisitioning that could undermine the agricultural cycle. In some places, communities fled to mountains, deserts, or distant cities. In others, they were gathered and forced to work on siege operations or to follow the army.

Urban populations faced a different set of threats. In walled cities, residents had to decide whether to support a defense, evacuate, or negotiate. The siege itself brought shortages, bombardment, and fear. After a city fell, Mongol practice often involved sorting the population: soldiers, artisans, scholars, merchants, women, and children were treated differently, and the categories of those killed, enslaved, relocated, or spared varied with circumstances. Artisans, engineers, physicians, astrologers, and scribes were often valued and sent to serve in Mongol camps or in distant parts of the empire. Women and children might be taken as captives. The sources suggest that some inhabitants survived by hiding, by being counted among the useful, or by being left behind after the main army moved on.

Not all communities were equally exposed. Pastoral populations, such as Turkmen and Kurdish tribal groups, could move with their herds and, in some cases, enter relationships with Mongol forces. Others served as auxiliaries. Local guides and informants, some coerced and some voluntary, helped Mongol commanders navigate unfamiliar terrain. Some Khwarazmian troops and Turkic soldiers entered Mongol service. Particular religious communities, notably Christians in parts of the Caucasus and Syria, later received favorable treatment in some Mongol policies, though the Mongols did not follow a single religious policy, and Muslim, Christian, Buddhist, and other communities experienced different outcomes in different places.

The evidence for these experiences is uneven. Chroniclers wrote about cities, rulers, and scholars, and they rarely described villages or pastoral groups. The voices of women, laborers, peasants, and the enslaved appear only indirectly, through the descriptions of others. Archaeology, including survey of abandoned settlements and irrigation works, provides complementary evidence, and the demographic and economic effects are examined in Chapter 46. For now, the key point is that the conquest was a social as well as a military event, and its meaning varied with a person's status, skills, location, and luck.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through a Campaign

The immediate experience of conquest depended on where a person lived and what role they could play. A farmer in an irrigated district faced requisitioning of grain and animals and the possibility that canals or fields would be neglected or damaged. A craftsman in a city might be selected for transfer to another place. A merchant might lose goods in plunder but could also be used as an intermediary by Mongol officials. A scholar or physician might find that skills protected him but separated him from home and family. Women's experiences included both vulnerability to capture and, in households that survived, responsibility for maintaining families and property in the absence of men killed or taken.

Because most sources were produced by an educated urban elite, historians reconstruct rural and pastoral experience indirectly, using references in chronicles, evidence of abandoned settlements, changes in agricultural patterns, and later administrative records. This is a reminder that the apparently full narrative of campaigns and sieges rests on a selection of voices, and that many experiences of the conquest are known only in outline.

Section 45.13

After Chinggis Khan: Ögedei, Imperial Succession, and Renewed Western Campaigns

Chinggis Khan died in August 1227, during a campaign against the Tangut state of Xi Xia. His death raised the problem of succession, and for two years his youngest son Tolui acted as regent, according to Mongol custom regarding the holder of the family's home territory. In 1229, a quriltai chose Ögedei, Chinggis Khan's third son, as Great Khan. The choice reflected both Chinggis Khan's own preference, as the sources report, and the balance of forces within the family.

Under Ögedei, the empire continued to expand on several fronts: against the Jin dynasty, which was destroyed in 1234; in Korea; and in the west. For Iran, the decision to renew campaigns reflected several considerations. Jalal al-Din's continued activity and the persistence of independent powers in western Iran and the Caucasus left unfinished business. The wealth of western cities and the strategic value of Azerbaijan's pastures were evident from earlier raids. And the Great Khan's authority, newly established, benefited from further conquests. Historians also note that the prosperity of the Mongol court depended on revenue from conquered territories, which required more systematic arrangements for collection and control.

The relationship between the Great Khan and regional commanders was central. Chormaqan and later Baiju held their positions through appointment by the Great Khan, but distance gave them considerable autonomy, and the Mongol court in Mongolia had limited information about local conditions. Commanders had to balance loyalty to the court with local pressures, and rivalries between Mongol officials, including between military commanders and civilian administrators, sometimes required adjudication from the court. After Ögedei's death in 1241, the regency of his widow Töregene and the short reign of his son Güyük (1246–1248) were followed by a succession crisis that brought Möngke to power in 1251, with consequences considered in Section 45.17.

The Mongol decision to continue operations in the west also involved the broader imperial division of responsibilities. Subutai and Batu, a grandson of Chinggis Khan, led armies into Eastern Europe in the 1230s and 1240s, while Chormaqan's forces concentrated on the Iranian and Caucasian regions. These campaigns were conceptually distinct but were part of a single imperial strategy of extending Mongol power across western Eurasia, and the capacity of the empire to support multiple large operations at once is a sign of its institutional development.

Section 45.14

Chormaqan and the Conquest of Western Iran and the Caucasus, c. 1230–1241

Ögedei appointed Chormaqan, a Mongol noyan of the Sunit tribe, to command in the west around 1229–1231. Sources describe his force as large, commonly given as thirty thousand men, though like other figures this is approximate. His initial mission was to eliminate Jalal al-Din, which the death of the Khwarazmian prince in 1231 largely achieved. Chormaqan then remained in the region and established a base on the Mughan steppe in Azerbaijan, a grassland suitable for large herds and strategically placed between Iran, the Caucasus, and Anatolia.

From this base, Chormaqan's forces conducted a long series of operations. Remaining Khwarazmian units were hunted down. In the Caucasus, Mongol armies took Ganja during the early to mid-1230s—the precise date differs among Armenian accounts—and moved into the Georgian kingdom and Armenian principalities. Ani, the old Armenian capital, fell in 1236, and Georgian and Armenian leaders, including the queen of Georgia and a number of princes, ultimately submitted or reached agreement with the Mongols, though the details and the chronology of these submissions vary and extend into the early 1240s. The Mongols imposed tribute, demanded troops, and installed supervisory authorities, while leaving many local rulers in place. Kirakos Ganjaketsi, an Armenian historian discussed in Section 45.14's source window below, gives a vivid account of these events from the perspective of the occupied.

In western Iran, the capture of Isfahan in 1237 is often treated as a milestone. Isfahan, one of the great cities of the Iranian plateau, had been raided earlier. When Mongol forces moved against it in the 1230s, it was divided by factional conflict, and its defense was uncertain. The city was taken, and the sources describe heavy losses, although they do not allow precise estimates. The conquest of Isfahan signaled that the Mongol campaign was extending to the central plateau, and the subjugation of Fars and Kirman largely proceeded through submission by local dynasties rather than through direct assault.

The key feature of Chormaqan's campaigns was persistence. Unlike the raids of 1220–1223, they involved sustained garrisons, repeated expeditions, and the gradual establishment of Mongol authority. Chormaqan was reported to have become ill in later years, possibly losing his hearing and speech, and he handed effective command to subordinates before his death or replacement, an episode that led to the succession of Baiju, discussed in the next section. Historians view this period as the decisive stage in establishing Mongol power in the west: it created the military and territorial foundation on which Hülegü would build.

Section 45.15

Baiju, the Caucasian Frontier, and the Battle of Köse Dağ, 1242–1243

Baiju, another Mongol noyan, succeeded Chormaqan around 1242. His succession reflected both Chormaqan's incapacity and the court's confidence in a commander already experienced in the region. Baiju continued the established pattern of operating from the Mughan steppe, maintaining garrisons in Azerbaijan, and conducting campaigns in the Caucasus and eastern Anatolia. His tenure lasted until the arrival of Hülegü, and he participated in the later campaigns of that expedition.

Under Baiju, Mongol forces advanced into eastern Anatolia. They took Erzurum in 1242 and began a series of operations against the Seljuk sultanate of Rum, whose rulers had themselves expanded in the region. The sultanate was a major regional power, ruling much of Anatolia from Konya, and its rulers faced internal tensions and external threats. On 26 June 1243, at Köse Dağ in northeastern Anatolia, Baiju's army, which included Georgian and Armenian auxiliaries as well as Mongol and Turkic troops, defeated the army of the sultan Kaykhusraw II. The sources suggest that the Seljuks were numerically strong but had difficulty coordinating, and their defeat is often cited as a decisive event for the history of Anatolia.

The consequences extended beyond the battlefield. The Seljuk sultanate became a tributary of the Mongols, and its internal politics increasingly depended on Mongol approval. The Mongols also gained leverage over the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia and other neighbors, several of which sought agreement with them. The victory expanded the zone of Mongol influence beyond what is usually called Iran, showing that the campaigns of this period were part of a wider imperial pattern of establishing overlordship through campaign and tribute.

Köse Dağ also marks a transition in the nature of Mongol authority in the west. The Mongols did not replace the Seljuk administration with their own. They created a relationship in which a local dynasty acknowledged Mongol suzerainty, paid tribute, and operated under Mongol supervision. This model, one of several used in these decades, is a reminder that Mongol power rested on a spectrum of arrangements, from direct administration to loose overlordship, and that its extent in any given year cannot be read simply from a map of conquests.

Section 45.16

Military Occupation, Tribute, and Provincial Authority before 1256

By the 1240s, Mongol power in Iran operated through several parallel structures. Military commanders such as Chormaqan and Baiju controlled forces and pasture in the west and northwest. Garrisons and frontier forces, sometimes called tamma units in the scholarship, were stationed in conquered or contested regions. Civilian administrators handled taxation and census work. Local rulers, in many places, remained in office under Mongol overlordship, paying tribute and supplying troops. These structures were not neatly coordinated, and their relations reflect the competing interests of the court, military commanders, and officials.

In the east, Mongol administration in Khurasan and Mazandaran developed through a succession of governors. Chin Temür, a Mongol-appointed commander, exercised authority in Khurasan in the early 1230s, and Körgüz, an Uyghur official, became an important civilian administrator, serving as governor and implementing assessments and tax arrangements. Körgüz's career ended in conflict at the imperial court, and he was executed in the political struggles of the 1240s, though the details remain debated. He was succeeded in the civilian administration by Arghun Aqa, an Oirat Mongol official who held responsibility for Iran and the western territories from the 1240s and who would continue to serve through the arrival of Hülegü. These figures, whose careers belong to the history of Mongol administration, show that the Mongols relied on a mix of Mongol, Central Asian, and local personnel.

Financial arrangements were uneven. The Mongols conducted censuses and imposed taxes, including head taxes and levies on produce, and they demanded supplies for the army and the courier system. These burdens fell heavily on peasants and townspeople, and Persian sources record complaints about the severity and arbitrariness of some officials. Yet Mongol officials also experimented with ways to regularize collection, and reform efforts, including those later associated with Ghazan Khan, would build on the problems revealed in this period (Chapter 48).

Regional variation was marked. Areas near Mongol bases, such as Azerbaijan and Khurasan, experienced direct military presence and administrative pressure. Tributary regions, such as Fars, Kirman, the Seljuk sultanate, and the Georgian and Armenian territories, preserved local rulers but were subject to demands. Some areas, including the Nizari strongholds and the Abbasid caliphate, remained outside Mongol control. Detailed analysis of the Ilkhanid state's administration belongs to Chapter 47; here the important point is that Mongol rule in Iran did not begin in 1256. It was built in the preceding quarter century by commanders, officials, and local intermediaries.

Historical Vocabulary
Tamma and Darughachi

Definition: Tamma refers to military forces, often drawn from several peoples, stationed on the frontiers of conquered territory to hold and extend Mongol control. A darughachi was a Mongol-appointed official or overseer posted in a conquered place, with responsibility for supervising administration, taxation, and the loyalty of local rulers.

Historical context: Garrison forces and resident overseers became increasingly common after the first invasions, as the Mongols moved from raiding to occupation. They worked alongside local rulers, tribute arrangements, and civilian administrators, and their specific roles varied by region and period.

Why it matters: These institutions show how conquest became lasting control, and they help explain why Mongol authority could persist where no large field army was present.

Potential misunderstanding: The terms did not always describe identical offices across the empire, and usage differs between sources and periods. A related title, Ilkhan, later used for the Mongol rulers of Iran, is discussed in Chapter 47; its precise original meaning and the date of its adoption are debated.

Section 45.17

Möngke Khan, Hülegü, and the New Western Expedition, 1251–1256

Möngke became Great Khan in 1251 after a contested succession. He was the son of Tolui, and his accession shifted the center of imperial authority from the line of Ögedei to the line of Tolui. The change was accompanied by political purges of rivals and by a program of administrative reorganization, including more systematic census and taxation across the empire. His policy toward the west was shaped by these concerns: he wished to assert imperial authority over the regions where Mongol commanders and administrators had operated for decades, and to complete the subjugation of powers that had not yet submitted.

Möngke assigned his younger brother Hülegü to lead a major expedition westward. The sources and later tradition indicate that Hülegü's tasks included reaffirming the authority of the Great Khan over existing Mongol commanders and local rulers, eliminating the Nizari Ismaili strongholds, bringing the Abbasid caliph to submission, and possibly extending Mongol power further west. This mandate came from Möngke, as Great Khan, and the expedition should be understood as an act of imperial policy. Later accounts that attach this decision to other figures, such as Qubilai in a capacity he did not yet hold, are mistaken.

Mobilization was extensive. The army was drawn from across the empire and included Mongol units, Turkic and other troops, and specialists, including Chinese engineers experienced in siege warfare, who brought catapults, incendiary devices, and other equipment. Local rulers along the route were required to provide supplies, pasture, and contingents. Preparations took years: the advance guard under Kitbuqa moved into Iran earlier, and Hülegü himself left Mongolia in the early 1250s, moving slowly to allow the assembling of forces and the provisioning of the route. He crossed the Amu Darya around the beginning of 1256, entering Iran with an army that existed on a scale and with a technical capability beyond what earlier western commanders had commanded.

This expedition differed from earlier operations in several respects. It was directed by a prince of the ruling family rather than by a commander operating at a distance. It was planned as part of a systematic program to remove the remaining independent powers. It was supported by the resources of the empire as a whole, including engineers and specialists. And it coincided with efforts to regularize administration, as the arrival of Hülegü brought together military and civilian leaders, such as Arghun Aqa, in a more coordinated structure. When he entered Iran, Mongol authority already extended over much of the country, but several major centers of power remained, and these became his principal targets.

Section 45.18

The Campaign against the Nizari Ismailis and the Fall of Alamut, 1256

The Nizari Ismaili state consisted of a network of fortresses in the Alborz mountains, Kuhistan, and elsewhere, with Alamut as its best known center. Its religious and institutional history is treated in Chapter 41. For this chapter, the relevant point is that the Nizaris had controlled territory and fortresses for more than a century, had resisted Seljuk and other powers, and, in the Mongol view, represented an independent political force that had to be reduced. Möngke's instructions to Hülegü specifically included the Nizaris, and earlier Mongol forces under Kitbuqa had begun attacks on their fortresses in Kuhistan in the early 1250s.

When Hülegü entered Iran, he moved against the strongholds in a coordinated campaign, using the siege expertise at his disposal. The Nizari imam at the time, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, who had succeeded his father, Ala al-Din Muhammad, after the latter's assassination in late 1255, faced a difficult strategic situation. Sources describe a period of negotiations in which Rukn al-Din sent envoys and attempted to delay, while Hülegü pressed an advance. The Mongol army surrounded and bombarded the fortress of Maymundiz, where the imam was staying. After a siege, Rukn al-Din surrendered in November 1256. Alamut, the fortress that had become synonymous with the Nizaris, was then handed over in December 1256, after some hesitation in its garrison, though the exact sequence and dates differ among sources. The distinction between the two events is important: they were separate, though connected, surrenders.

Rukn al-Din was treated for a time as a prisoner of consequence and sent toward Mongolia, but he was killed on the journey in 1257, according to the sources, reflecting the Mongol view that the Nizaris were irreconcilable. The sources also report the massacre of many Nizari communities, though the scale is disputed. Juvayni, who accompanied Hülegü's army, visited the library at Alamut and, in his account, selected certain books for preservation while discarding others he considered heretical, a statement that illustrates both the hostility of his account and the fate of Nizari writings. Other Nizari fortresses, including Lamasar and Gerdkuh, held out beyond 1256, and Gerdkuh did not fall until 1270, a reminder that conquest was not complete with the surrender of the imam.

The fall of Maymundiz and Alamut ended the Nizari territorial state in Iran. It did not end the Ismaili community, which survived in Iran, Syria, and other regions under changed conditions, as Chapter 41 explains. For the Mongols, the success removed a long-standing independent power, secured the Alborz region, and cleared the way for Hülegü's march toward Baghdad.

Section 45.19

The Fall of Baghdad, 1258, and the Limits of Mongol Expansion, 1260

The Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad had survived the Seljuk and Khwarazmian eras as a diminished but symbolically important institution, ruling Iraq and exercising religious prestige across the Islamic world (earlier phases of this relationship appear in Chapters 31, 36, and 38). In 1257, Hülegü sent demands to the caliph al-Mustaʿsim for submission and for the provision of troops. The caliph's response, according to the sources, was one of defiance mixed with hesitation, and his court was divided over how to respond. The caliph's military forces were limited, and the sources indicate that some advisers urged negotiation while others urged resistance.

Hülegü advanced with a large army arriving from several directions, including contingents from Baiju's forces in the northwest, and auxiliaries from Georgia and Armenia. The siege of Baghdad began in late January 1258. Mongol engineers built siege works around the city, used bombardment, and breached the defenses, and the caliph's forces were unable to hold out. The caliph surrendered in early February, and Mongol troops entered the city. The sack that followed lasted about a week, and the caliph was executed within days, reportedly by a method that avoided spilling blood, in accordance with Mongol custom for persons of high rank. The dates of the surrender and entry fall in the second week of February, though the precise days are given differently in the sources.

The destruction in Baghdad was severe, and sources describe the killing of large numbers of inhabitants, the burning of buildings, and the loss of books and manuscripts. Medieval figures for the dead vary enormously and cannot be verified, and modern estimates are lower though still uncertain (their evaluation belongs to Chapter 46). At the same time, the Mongols spared certain groups, notably Christians, in part through the influence of Hülegü's Christian wife Dokuz Khatun and the Nestorian patriarch. The consequences were political and symbolic: the end of the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad removed the institution that had been the nominal center of the Sunni Muslim world for five centuries. It did not end Islamic scholarship or learning, which continued in many centers, including under Mongol patronage; Nasir al-Din Tusi, for instance, began constructing the Maragha observatory under Hülegü’s patronage in 1259, after the pre-Mongol scholarly period examined in Chapter 43.

Hülegü then advanced into Syria. Aleppo fell in January 1260, and Damascus submitted soon afterward under Kitbuqa. But the Mongol position quickly changed. Möngke had died in 1259, creating a succession crisis that drew Hülegü's attention to affairs in the east, and he withdrew much of his army, leaving Kitbuqa with a reduced force. The Mamluk sultanate of Egypt, led by Qutuz and the commander Baybars, advanced into Palestine and met Kitbuqa's force at Ain Jalut on 3 September 1260. The Mamluks won, Kitbuqa was killed, and Mongol forces withdrew from Syria.

Ain Jalut was not the end of Mongol power, and Mongol armies returned to Syria several times. But it marked a limit. Historians point to a combination of factors: the reduced size of the Mongol force, the less favorable pasture conditions of Syria for large herds, the effectiveness of the Mamluk army, and the distraction of Mongol leadership by succession struggles. Open warfare between Hülegü and Berke of the Golden Horde began in 1261–1262, after Ain Jalut, further limiting later Mongol operations against the Mamluks. The result was that the Mongol territory in the Middle East stabilized around Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus, and parts of Anatolia, with the Euphrates and the Syrian frontier as a zone of contest. This outcome helps explain why the next phase of Mongol history developed around an Iranian-centered regional state, the subject of Chapter 47.

Section 45.20

Chapter Synthesis and Review: Conquest, Resistance, and the Establishment of Mongol Power

The preceding sections show that the Mongol conquest of Iran unfolded in three overlapping stages, each with its own objectives, methods, and consequences. In the first stage, between 1219 and 1223, Chinggis Khan and his commanders destroyed the Khwarazmian state by dividing their forces, besieging divided defenders, and using terror and negotiation in combination. This stage left a politically fragmented landscape and a series of devastated cities, but not a stable Mongol government. In the second stage, between roughly 1230 and 1250, Chormaqan, Baiju, and civilian officials established sustained occupation in western Iran, the Caucasus, and Khurasan, using garrisons, tribute, and local intermediaries. In the third stage, between 1251 and 1260, Hülegü's expedition removed the Nizari strongholds and the Abbasid caliphate, then met its limit in Syria.

Across these stages, several patterns recur. Mongol success depended on organization, coordination, intelligence, and siege capability, but also on the divisions of their opponents and the willingness of local rulers to submit. Responses varied: some cities resisted and were destroyed; others submitted and were spared; others submitted and later revolted. Local rulers survived as tributaries where they accommodated Mongol demands. Mongol officials, including non-Mongols, created administrative arrangements that foreshadowed later government. And across the entire period, the severity of violence, the forced movement of people, and the burden of requisitions were central experiences for the populations affected, even when their scale cannot be quantified.

The chapter also shows why a clear division of historical labor matters. The consequences of conquest for population, settlement, and economy (Chapter 46) and the creation of the Ilkhanate as a regional state (Chapter 47) require separate analysis. Chapter 45's task was to explain how the conquest happened: what the Mongols did, how others responded, and how military campaigns created the conditions for occupation. That analysis completes the account of Mongol power as it stood in 1260, with Hülegü established in Azerbaijan, Mongol authority extending over much of Iran, and the empire's western frontier fixed against the Mamluks.

Historical Debate

Was the Conquest of Iran a Single Imperial Invasion or a Series of Overlapping Campaigns and Settlements?

The question: Historians use "the Mongol conquest of Iran" to describe events spanning four decades. Is it analytically useful to treat these as one conquest, or do the differences between stages argue for treating them separately?

Interpretation A — A single imperial project: Some scholars emphasize continuity of imperial purpose from Chinggis Khan to Möngke: the aim of establishing Mongol overlordship over the western world, pursued through successive generations of commanders under the authority of the Great Khan.
Interpretation B — Distinct campaigns with different aims: Others stress that the campaigns differed in objectives, methods, and personnel, from punitive and exploratory operations to systematic occupation, and that treating them as a single conquest obscures contingencies, local variations, and changes in imperial policy.
Interpretation C — A process of overlapping settlements: A third approach focuses on local political arrangements, arguing that different regions were incorporated at different times and by different means, so that conquest is better understood as a series of negotiated and imposed settlements.

Evidence: The debate draws on the chronology of campaigns, the sources for imperial decision-making, evidence of local arrangements, and comparison with other regions of Mongol expansion.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation more than the sequence of events. It also touches on causation: whether continuity of imperial purpose or local contingency better explains the course of conquest.

Why it matters: The way the question is framed affects how historians assign responsibility, evaluate local agency, and explain the timing of the Ilkhanate's emergence.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pre-Mongol Regional Authority to the Foundations of the Ilkhanate

What changed? The Khwarazmian state, the Nizari territorial state, and the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad were removed as independent powers. Mongol commanders and officials exercised authority in much of Iran, and regional rulers became tributaries. Several major cities suffered severe destruction, and many people were displaced or forced into service.

What remained continuous? Many local dynasties and urban elites survived, either as tributaries or as officials. Persian-language administrative practice and Iranian officials remained central to government, as the careers of Juvayni and others show. Religious and scholarly institutions continued, and trade routes persisted. The geography of pasture, water, and routes continued to shape where armies and governments operated.

When did change occur? The most abrupt changes occurred in 1219–1223 and 1256–1258. The intervening decades saw gradual change, with variation by region.

Pattern of change: Change was uneven and contested, with revolts, renewed campaigns, and shifting arrangements.

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of cities that were besieged and sacked, artisans and others forcibly relocated, and rulers who lost their positions. Who experienced greater continuity? Some rural communities, tributary dynasties such as the Salghurids and Qutlugh-Khanids, and officials who moved into Mongol service.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against both an image of total replacement and a claim that nothing fundamental changed. The Mongol conquest transformed the political structure of Iran while leaving many social and cultural institutions in place, a balance that later chapters examine.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained the Mongol conquest of Iran as a prolonged, multi-stage process rather than a single invasion. It began with Temüjin's rise and the political institutions established in 1206, which gave the Mongols an army and an administration capable of long-distance operations. The campaigns in northern China taught Mongol commanders siege warfare and brought skilled non-Mongol personnel into their service, and the destruction of the Qara Khitai usurper Kuchlug in 1218 brought Mongol power to the Khwarazmian frontier.

The diplomatic crisis of 1218, centered on the killing of a Mongol-sponsored caravan at Otrar, is firmly attested as a crisis, but the motives and responsibility of individuals remain uncertain, and later narratives presented the invasion as a response to provocation. Historians weigh this explanation against broader strategic and commercial considerations. The Mongol military system, based on decimal organization, mounted archery, intelligence, logistics, and the incorporation of siege engineers and auxiliaries, was effective, but it did not operate alone: the political divisions of the Khwarazmian world and the decision to disperse defenders among cities amplified Mongol advantages.

The first great invasion, from 1219 to 1222, was coordinated across multiple routes. Otrar fell after a months-long siege; Bukhara and Samarkand were taken in 1220; Gurganj fell in 1221 after a prolonged struggle; Tolui's forces devastated Merv and Nishapur; and Herat, after an initial surrender, revolted and was retaken in 1222. These cities experienced different sequences, and medieval casualty figures, which cannot be verified, should not be used as demographic data. Jebe and Subutai's expedition across northern Iran, the Caucasus, and the steppe demonstrated Mongol operational reach but did not create lasting occupation.

The decade after 1221 was marked by fragmentation. Jalal al-Din's resistance, local revolts, and the persistence of regional dynasties meant that Mongol authority was partial. Local rulers and communities made choices ranging from resistance to submission, and the categories of hero and collaborator do not capture their situations. Dynasties such as the Qutlugh-Khanids of Kirman and the Salghurids of Fars survived through early accommodation, while other regions experienced repeated raids. Ordinary communities experienced conquest through requisitioning, displacement, forced labor, and the sorting of urban populations by skill and status, though the sources for their experiences are limited.

The second stage began after Chinggis Khan's death in 1227 and Ögedei's accession in 1229. Chormaqan, establishing a base on the Mughan steppe, eliminated remaining Khwarazmian power, campaigned in the Caucasus, and oversaw the conquest of Isfahan in 1237. Baiju, his successor, defeated the Seljuks of Rum at Köse Dağ in 1243, extending Mongol influence into Anatolia. Mongol authority in these decades rested on garrisons, tribute, local rulers, and civilian administrators such as Körgüz and Arghun Aqa, a combination that created the territorial and administrative foundations on which later rule would build.

The third stage followed Möngke's accession in 1251. Möngke sent Hülegü westward with an army that included Chinese engineers and contingents from across the empire. In 1256, Hülegü reduced the principal Nizari strongholds, with Maymundiz surrendering in November and Alamut in December, though some fortresses held out longer. In February 1258, Baghdad fell and the caliph al-Mustaʿsim was executed, ending the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad. The consequences were severe and politically transformative, but they did not end Islamic scholarship or civilization.

Hülegü's advance into Syria in 1260 ended in defeat at Ain Jalut, where the Mamluks defeated a reduced Mongol force. Succession politics, the death of Möngke, logistical limits, and Mamluk military capacity combined to halt expansion. The result was a Mongol state centered on Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus, and parts of Anatolia, an outcome that shaped the emergence of the Ilkhanate.

The chapter has also demonstrated how historians know what they know. Persian, Arabic, Armenian, Mongolian, and Chinese sources provide different perspectives, each shaped by authorship, audience, and purpose. Ibn al-Athir offers the testimony of a distant contemporary; Juvayni the account of an Iranian official in Mongol service; Kirakos the viewpoint of an Armenian cleric under occupation. Their numbers, speeches, and moral framing require critical reading, and the experiences of peasants, pastoralists, women, and the enslaved remain only partially recoverable.

Finally, the chapter has maintained a division of labor with its neighbors. Chapter 45 explained how the conquest happened; Chapter 46 will examine its effects on population, settlement, and economy; and Chapter 47 will analyze how Mongol conquest became Ilkhanid government. Together they present the Mongol conquest as a transformation that was violent, uneven, contested, and consequential, reshaping the political landscape of Iran and the wider Persianate world.

Key Terms

Quriltai
An assembly of Mongol leaders that ratified major decisions such as the choice of a khan or the launching of a campaign.
Ulus
A people, realm, or patrimony; in the imperial context, the territories and followers assigned to a member of the Chinggisid family.
Great Khan
The supreme ruler of the Mongol Empire, selected at a quriltai from the descendants of Chinggis Khan.
Chinggisid
A descendant of Chinggis Khan, a status that conferred political legitimacy in the Mongol world.
Noyan
A Mongol commander or lord.
Tümen
The largest unit of the decimal army, nominally ten thousand men, often smaller in practice.
Keshig
The Mongol imperial guard, which served as escort, household, and training ground for commanders.
Yam
The Mongol relay and courier system, providing mounts, supplies, and shelter for messengers and officials.
Yasa
The body of imperial law and custom associated with Chinggis Khan, whose exact content and codification are debated.
Composite bow
A bow made of layered materials, powerful at short length and well suited to use from horseback.
Hashar
In Persian sources, forced labor levied from prisoners or local populations, used notably in siege operations.
Tamma
Frontier military forces stationed in conquered or contested regions to hold and extend Mongol control.
Darughachi
A Mongol-appointed overseer posted in a conquered place to supervise administration and taxation.
Ilkhan
A title used by the Mongol rulers of Iran, examined in Chapter 47; its meaning and early use are debated.
Transoxiana
The region between the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, known in Arabic as Mawarannahr.
Khwarazm
The region south of the Aral Sea, the original base of the Khwarazmshahs, with its capital at Gurganj.
Khurasan
A large medieval region of eastern Iran, western Afghanistan, and neighboring territories, centered on Merv, Nishapur, Herat, and Balkh.
Atabeg
A Turkic title for a military guardian and governor, held by rulers of several regional dynasties in Iran and the Caucasus.
Tribute
Regular payments or deliveries demanded by a stronger power from a subordinate ruler or community in exchange for recognition or protection.
Auxiliary
A non-Mongol soldier or unit serving alongside Mongol forces, whether by compulsion or agreement.
Nizari Ismailis
A branch of Ismaili Shiʿism whose fortress state in the Alborz and Kuhistan was reduced by Hülegü in 1256 (Chapter 41).
Seljuks of Rum
The Seljuk dynasty ruling much of Anatolia from Konya, defeated by the Mongols at Köse Dağ in 1243.
Abbasid caliphate
The institution of the caliph in Baghdad, symbolic leader of the Sunni Muslim world until 1258.
Mamluks
The military regime of Egypt and Syria, whose army defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260.
Siege engine
A machine used to attack fortifications, including stone-throwing engines, towers, and mining equipment.
Reprisal
Punitive violence against a community for resistance or for killing representatives of the attacker, used by the Mongols as an instrument of policy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains a transformation that reshaped the political geography of the Iranian world. Before the Mongol invasions, Iran was divided among Khwarazmian, Abbasid, Nizari, and regional dynastic authorities. By 1260, those arrangements had been dismantled or subordinated, and a Mongol-centered order was emerging. The pace and unevenness of this change, and the variety of responses to it, show that conquest was a political and social process shaped by many actors, not an event that occurred at a single moment.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the history of Eurasia. The Mongol Empire connected Mongolia, China, Central Asia, Iran, the Caucasus, and the Middle East within a single imperial system, and the movement of soldiers, engineers, officials, artisans, merchants, and captives across these regions shaped the societies involved. The concentration of administrators, scholars, and craftspeople from many places would influence the cultural and intellectual life of Mongol Iran, as Chapters 47 through 50 show. Iran, in this period, was not merely a victim of external forces but a region whose people, institutions, and traditions interacted with Mongol rule in complex ways.

Several continuities survived. Local dynasties and urban elites, Persian-language administration, religious scholarship, and the networks of trade persisted through the conquest and continued to shape Iranian society. These continuities would be crucial to the emergence of the Ilkhanate as a state that, over time, became rooted in Iranian institutions. At the same time, the destruction and displacement of the conquest left enduring marks, whose scale historians continue to debate and which Chapter 46 will examine.

As the opening chapter of Volume IV, Chapter 45 establishes the questions that run through the next phase of the textbook: how conquerors become rulers, how conquered societies adapt, how religion and political legitimacy are renegotiated, and how the Iranian world is reconnected to a wider Eurasian system. The legacy of the conquest would shape the politics of the Ilkhanate, the successor states, the invasions of Timur (Chapter 52), and ultimately the conditions from which the Safavid state emerged.
