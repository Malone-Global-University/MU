Print, Education, Intellectual Reform, and New Political Languages

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between the 1830s and the first years of the twentieth century, a growing though still small number of Iranians learned to print, translate, teach, travel, and argue about government in ways that earlier generations could scarcely have anticipated. No single event produced this change. It was assembled from lithographed pages, schoolrooms, steamship passages, smuggled newspapers, and conversations that gradually made it possible to say, in public and in Persian, that a kingdom ought to be governed by law.

Consider the path taken by a single idea. In 1870 an Iranian diplomat serving in Paris, Mirza Yusuf Khan, wrote a treatise arguing that the strength of Europe could be traced to one thing: law. The text was written abroad, drew on French political documents, and cited the Qur’an and Islamic tradition to persuade Muslim readers that its argument was not foreign to their own moral world. It was then copied, reprinted, discussed, and quoted by Iranians who had never been to France. Two decades later a Persian-language newspaper published in London made the same demand in sharper form, and copies of it were passed from hand to hand inside Iran despite government prohibitions. An idea that began as a diplomat’s memorandum had become part of a wider conversation about what a state owed its subjects.

This chapter studies how that conversation was made possible. It is a history of media, institutions, and vocabulary as much as of individual thinkers: of how political ideas were produced, how they were transmitted, how they were debated, and how they were gradually made available to wider audiences. It examines the spread of lithographic printing and the early Persian press; the translation of European works; the networks that tied Iranian intellectuals to Istanbul, Tiflis, Cairo, Calcutta, London, and Paris; the expansion of new schools alongside long-established ones; and the writings of reformers who gave new meanings to words such as law, justice, liberty, nation, progress, and constitution.

The chapter’s central argument is that the nineteenth century changed the language in which Iranians could imagine political authority. It does not argue that a unified reform movement emerged. Reformers disagreed with one another about religion, about whether and how a constitution could be justified, about national identity, about social hierarchy, about the education of women, and about what “progress” meant. Some were bureaucrats whose careers depended on the shah; some were exiles; some were clerics; some were merchants; some were skeptics who criticized religion itself. Their disagreements are part of the history, and the chapter will resist reducing them to a single “modernizing” current.

The new media also did not enter an empty field. Iranians already possessed rich manuscript, religious, oral, and administrative cultures through which information and argument circulated. Printed newspapers and reform treatises were new instruments added to an existing communications world, and their effects depended on how they connected with pulpits, bazaars, madrasas, courts, and households. Understanding this prevents two errors: imagining a stagnant society awakened by European print, and imagining that print mattered little because most Iranians could not read.

Historians reconstruct this subject from newspapers and journals, reform treatises, travel accounts, memoirs, diplomatic reports, educational records, the records of missionary and foreign schools, and later recollections. Each source type has limits. Newspapers reveal what editors chose to say and, indirectly, what censors tolerated; treatises show how ideas were argued but not how widely they were read; memoirs are frequently written long after the events they describe. Reliable figures for literacy, circulation, and school enrollment are scarce, and this chapter will avoid false precision about them.

The chapter fits between its neighbors in the following way. Chapter 73 examined the early reforms of Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir, including the founding of the Dar al-Funun; Chapter 75 examined the political economy of concessions; Chapter 76 examined the Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892 as an episode of organized mass mobilization. This chapter asks what kind of intellectual and communicative environment had developed by the time such mobilization became possible. It ends at the threshold of 1905, leaving the mobilization of 1905–1906 and the creation of the Majles to Chapter 78, and the constitutional struggle to Chapter 79.

A note on terms. Words such as “reform,” “modern,” and “intellectual” are used here as analytical conveniences. The people discussed rarely used them in a single consistent way, and many of the key Persian terms examined in this chapter changed meaning in the very decades under study. Where a term’s meaning was unstable, the chapter says so.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the manuscript, oral, religious, and administrative communication culture into which printing and newspapers were introduced.
  2. Explain why lithography suited Persian-script publishing and how it related to manuscript traditions.
  3. Trace the development of the Persian press from official gazettes to periodicals published abroad, and assess the effects of censorship.
  4. Analyze the roles of translation, student travel, diplomacy, and exile publishing in transmitting and reshaping political ideas.
  5. Compare traditional and new educational institutions without treating older education as uniformly stagnant.
  6. Assess the long-term role of the Dar al-Funun and the contribution of the new-school movement associated with Mirza Hasan Roshdieh.
  7. Compare the arguments of Akhundzadeh, Malkom Khan, Mostashar al-Dowleh, and Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, distinguishing their sources, aims, and audiences.
  8. Interpret the changing meanings of qānūn, ʿadālat, āzādī, mellat, taraqqī, and mashrūṭeh.
  9. Evaluate competing views of the relationship among religious authority, reform, and constitutional ideas.
  10. Distinguish dynastic loyalty, religious community, cultural belonging, and emerging nationalism in nineteenth-century Iranian identity.
  11. Evaluate whether intellectual reform transformed Iranian society or remained primarily an elite project, using evidence and acknowledging its limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did printing change communication in a society that already had sophisticated manuscript and oral cultures?
  2. Why did so much of the independent Persian press before 1906 appear outside Iran, and what did that mean for its readers and writers?
  3. How do historians estimate a “reading public” when circulation figures are unreliable and reading was often collective?
  4. What happens to a political concept when it is translated into a language and legal culture with different assumptions?
  5. Were reform ideas imported from Europe, reworked from Islamic and Persian traditions, or both, and what evidence bears on the question?
  6. Was religious authority an obstacle to reform, a participant in it, or both?
  7. How did notions of “Iran” and “the nation” differ from dynastic loyalty and religious community?
  8. Whom did reform writing reach, and whom did it leave out?
  9. In what sense, if any, did new political languages make the Constitutional Revolution possible without making it inevitable?

Historical Context

By the 1830s the Qajar state had experienced the military defeats and territorial losses examined in Chapters 70 and 71. Those defeats prompted debate within the court and bureaucracy about the sources of European strength and the means of responding. The reforms associated with Abbas Mirza and later Amir Kabir (Chapter 73) were primarily military, administrative, and educational, and they were sponsored by the state or by officials close to it. They created a small cadre of men who had studied European languages and technologies or who worked as translators and administrators.

Naser al-Din Shah reigned from 1848 until his assassination in 1896. His long reign saw the expansion of telegraph lines, the growth of the state bureaucracy, the opening of Iran to foreign trade and capital, and the concession politics examined in Chapter 75. It also saw growing awareness, among officials and educated subjects, that political and economic decisions were being made under conditions of foreign pressure. The shah himself traveled to Europe on three occasions (1873, 1878, and 1889), and his accounts of these journeys formed part of a broader Persian-language literature of observation about the outside world.

Iran’s religious institutions formed a second major context. Twelver Shi’i scholars (ulama), especially the senior jurists (mojtaheds) whose institutional development was examined in Chapters 58 and 65, were central to education, law, preaching, and the moral life of communities, and their networks extended to the shrine cities of Ottoman Iraq. Merchants and guildsmen of the bazaar formed a third network: they traveled, corresponded, and sometimes funded schools, publications, and religious institutions. Provincial and tribal society, rural communities, and non-Muslim minorities such as Armenians, Jews, and Zoroastrians each experienced the period differently and often had their own educational institutions and external connections.

The Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892 (Chapter 76) demonstrated that communication networks linking clerics, merchants, and ordinary urban populations could be mobilized rapidly against a royal decision. It is therefore tempting to treat this chapter as a prelude to that event or to the Constitutional Revolution. The chapter resists that temptation: most of the developments described here had their own chronology, motives, and internal disputes, and the importance of the later events should not be allowed to flatten them into mere preparation.

Information, Literacy, and Communication in Early Qajar Iran

Before the expansion of printing, information moved through several overlapping channels. The most formal was the written culture of the court and bureaucracy. Scribes and secretaries (munshis) drafted decrees, correspondence, tax assessments, and diplomatic letters in a trained epistolary Persian, and they were taught through collections of model letters and literary prose. This chancery tradition prized ornate and highly formal expression. One notable exception was the statesman Abu’l-Qasem Qa’em-Maqam Farahani (d. 1835), whose letters are often cited as an early move toward plainer, more direct prose, a development that would matter when newspapers needed a language ordinary readers could follow.

A second channel was manuscript copying. Professional and semi-professional copyists produced Qur’ans, devotional works, legal and philosophical texts, poetry, and histories, and calligraphers and illuminators formed a skilled craft community. Books were sold in the bazaar, held in madrasa libraries, and preserved in endowed collections at major shrines. Manuscripts were costly and their circulation was limited, but they were not static: copyists made corrections, added marginalia, and compiled anthologies, so that texts changed as they traveled. Merchants, meanwhile, maintained dense commercial correspondence across the Gulf, the Caucasus, Anatolia, Central Asia, and India, carrying news with their goods.

Much of the most socially important communication was oral. Preachers delivered sermons from the pulpit; reciters narrated the sufferings of the Shi’i Imams in mourning assemblies; passion plays (ta’ziyeh) dramatized sacred history; coffeehouse storytellers recited episodes from the Shahnameh to mixed audiences. Rumor, bazaar conversation, and petitions to governors and ulama carried public grievances. These forms were not backward alternatives to writing; they were the principal means through which many people encountered religious, historical, and political ideas. Reading itself was frequently social and aloud, so a single text could reach people who could not read it themselves.

Literacy is difficult to measure. No census recorded it in this period, and later estimates are uncertain. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the ability to read and write was a minority skill, concentrated among clerics, officials, merchants, scribes, and some urban craftsmen, and that it varied sharply by region, class, and sex. Moreover, “literacy” covered distinct competences: many people could recite or read the Qur’an and simple devotional texts without being able to compose letters or read unfamiliar prose; others could read but not write. When historians speak of a “reading public,” they should therefore remember that the audience for a text was almost always larger than the number of people who could independently read it.

The different communication systems also operated at different speeds and claimed different kinds of authority. A royal order might travel from Tehran through a governor and his scribes before it reached a district official; a merchant could learn of a price change from correspondents across the Caspian or the Persian Gulf; and a preacher could make an argument audible to hundreds of listeners within an afternoon. Such routes crossed. A complaint drafted by merchants might be read by a scholar, conveyed from his pulpit, copied for supporters, and then reported to officials as evidence of popular agitation. Historians who study only a newspaper or only government correspondence miss these stages of transmission. A public argument did not necessarily start in print, and its effects cannot be measured by printed copies alone.

Literacy likewise had institutional as well as social dimensions. A merchant's ability to read invoices, a cleric's training in jurisprudential commentary, a woman's ability to read devotional poetry, and a clerk's skill in composing formal petitions were not identical. The languages of learning varied: Arabic was central in advanced religious instruction, Persian in administration and literature, Azerbaijani Turkic and other vernaculars in many households and marketplaces, while Armenian and other languages served minority institutions. A printed Persian tract therefore encountered communities whose relationship to written language was multiple, not uniform. These conditions help explain why reformers sought simpler prose and repeated their arguments through lectures, conversation, and personal recommendation rather than assuming that a bound book would speak for itself.

The growth of communications networks also carried unequal consequences. A capital-city official might receive news from Europe through diplomatic channels, whereas a cultivator in an upland village might encounter the same discussion weeks later, filtered through a tax collector, traveling merchant, or local religious authority. The new periodical press made some events more visible, but it also privileged the concerns of those who possessed the means to publish. Even before censorship is considered, the structure of access to information shaped whose understanding of Iran's difficulties became part of the written record.

The point of this survey is to prevent a misleading picture. Printed newspapers and pamphlets entered an existing communications culture that already had experts, institutions, audiences, and conventions. Their novelty lay less in the idea of circulating information than in the speed, regularity, volume, and, eventually, the political uses to which new technologies could be put. They did not replace sermons, manuscripts, or bazaar conversation; they intersected with them, and the most influential ideas reached beyond the literate through those intersections.

Historical Vocabulary
Manuscript Culture, Lithography, and Public Communication

Definition: Manuscript culture refers to a communications system in which texts are reproduced by hand and circulate through copyists, scribal workshops, libraries, and personal exchange. Lithography is a printing process in which an image or text is drawn or transferred onto a flat stone or plate and printed from the prepared surface rather than from movable metal type. Public communication here denotes the ways information and argument reached audiences beyond the writer’s immediate circle: sermons, readings, petitions, newspapers, and rumor among them.

Historical context: In nineteenth-century Iran these systems coexisted. Lithography allowed a calligrapher’s handwritten page to be reproduced many times, so printed books could resemble manuscripts and manuscript skills were turned to print production.

Why it matters: The distinction helps explain why print did not abruptly end manuscript production and why the transition is better understood as an overlap than a replacement.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not equate the arrival of printing with the arrival of intellectual life, or assume that handwritten transmission implied static or unchanging texts.

Division I · Section 77.2

The Introduction and Expansion of Printing in Iran

Printing with movable type was introduced to Iran under state patronage in the early nineteenth century. Abbas Mirza, the crown prince and governor of Azerbaijan whose military and educational initiatives were examined in Chapter 73, sent students to England in the 1810s. One of them, Mirza Saleh Shirazi, studied European languages and printing, and on his return around 1819 he brought a press and type to Tabriz. The earliest products of this press included works associated with the war effort and the court. A press was also established in Tehran in the following years. These early efforts were experimental and closely tied to the state: they served official needs and the interests of a patron, not a mass market.

Typography posed particular difficulties for Persian. The script is cursive, letters change shape depending on their position in a word, and the nastaliq style favored for Persian literary texts is especially difficult to reproduce with movable type. Cutting, casting, and setting type for such a script was expensive and, compared with the skilled work of calligraphers, often aesthetically unsatisfying to readers accustomed to beautiful handwriting. For many kinds of book, the economics were also unfavorable: the type, the presses, and the trained compositors were scarce.

Lithography offered a solution. From the 1830s, with a securely documented phase of lithographic printing dated to about 1832–1833 in Tabriz, a calligrapher could write the text directly onto a stone or transfer paper, and the page could then be printed in as many copies as needed. The result preserved the appearance of manuscript calligraphy, including nastaliq, and did not require casting type. Equipment was cheaper and the work could be done by scribes already trained in the craft. Lithographic presses spread through Tabriz, Tehran, Isfahan, Shiraz, Mashhad, and other cities during the following decades, and lithography remained the dominant method of Persian book production for much of the century.

The books produced reflected existing reading tastes as much as new ones. Lithographed Qur’ans, prayer books, commentaries, and works of religious law were prominent; so were classical poetry collections, popular narratives, illustrated tales, and texts used in the ta’ziyeh and mourning traditions. Histories, medical and scientific manuals, translations, and reformist works were a smaller but growing part of output. Scholars have observed that this pattern indicates how print was integrated into established devotional and literary culture before it became a vehicle for political argument. Reports of clerical hostility to the press should also be treated carefully: the evidence suggests that members of the religious establishment used print themselves, and objections tended to concern specific content or possible misuse rather than printing as such.

There is a further chronological complication in the history of lithography. Abbas Mirza sponsored the importation of equipment from the Caucasus in the early 1820s, and scholars have discussed evidence for experimental lithographic work before the well-attested Tabriz publications of 1832–1833. That latter date should therefore be understood as a secure landmark in continuing production, not proof that no earlier experiments occurred. The first books attributed to the process have to be distinguished from typographically printed works issued in the 1810s and 1820s. When historians call an object the first Iranian printed book, their conclusion depends on whether they mean movable type, lithography, a Persian-language book, or a book produced within the borders of Iran. Persian books had also been printed in India and Europe long before domestic presses became established.

Studying a lithographed page shows why the technology was culturally adaptable. A calligrapher could arrange lines, decorative headings, marginal notes, and sometimes illustration in ways that resembled familiar manuscripts. The printer reproduced the designed page without requiring every connected Persian letter to be assembled from standardized metal pieces. A purchaser could thus receive an object at once visibly traditional and mechanically multiplied. Copyists did not simply disappear: skilled scribes and illustrators found employment preparing lithographic masters. Printing changed the economy of bookmaking while preserving important elements of the older craft.

The commercial story is equally important. Printers needed paper, stones, presses, skilled labor, and buyers willing to finance editions before sales were secure. Booksellers and sponsors had to judge whether a devotional work, popular narrative, school text, or classical poem could recover its costs. As the market widened, more readers could obtain copies of works previously accessible chiefly through libraries, patrons, or manuscript exchange. Yet prices remained a barrier for many households, and the cheaper production of books did not create universal literacy. A printed textbook might become materially more available well before there were enough schools or teachers to make it useful to a broad population.

It is helpful to distinguish early experimental printing from the later commercial expansion. The first phase, from roughly the 1810s to the 1830s, was dominated by state sponsorship and small volumes of output. The second, beginning with the spread of lithography, involved private entrepreneurs, publishers, booksellers, and calligrapher-lithographers, and it reduced the cost of books. Even then, manuscripts continued to be produced, particularly for luxury items and certain scholarly texts. The long-term significance of lithography is that it created the technical and commercial basis on which periodicals and cheaper books could later be built.

Division I · Section 77.3

Early Newspapers and the Qajar State

The first Persian-language newspaper inside Iran is generally identified as Kāghaz-e Akhbār (roughly, “News Paper”), which appeared in Tehran in 1837 under the editorship of Mirza Saleh Shirazi. It was modest in format and irregular in appearance, and it served the information needs of the court and officials more than a general readership. Its historical significance lies less in its reach than in its demonstration that the state could use a periodical to circulate regular reports.

A more durable beginning came in 1851 with Rūznāmeh-ye Vaqāyʿ-e Ettefāqīyeh (“Newspaper of Events That Occur”), launched during the premiership of Amir Kabir, whose broader program is treated in Chapter 73. The paper was a government gazette. It published royal announcements, appointments and dismissals, official decisions, news of provincial affairs, and reports on foreign countries, and it was distributed primarily to officials, provincial governors, and members of the political elite. Later in the century it was published under other names reflecting its status as the official organ of the state, and the government also supported additional publications, among them journals associated with the Dar al-Funun, the capital’s translation and publications bureaus, and an illustrated paper that included images and scientific and general-interest material.

These newspapers need to be read for what they were. Their editorial independence was minimal: contents were selected by officials, criticism of the monarchy was absent, and the tone was that of administrative announcement. Yet they altered communication in several ways. They created a regular, dated, numbered, and printed channel by which the state addressed its servants and, to some extent, the wider literate population. They introduced the genre of the “news report,” with its separation of domestic from foreign affairs and its implicit claim that events in the world were relevant to Iranians. And they developed a vocabulary: terms for institutions, offices, and ideas drawn from European and Ottoman usage were tested and circulated through their pages.

The contents of a gazette were themselves evidence of state priorities. A notice of appointments displayed who held an office and under whose authority; an account of a new road or provincial harvest represented the government's claim to knowledge about its territories; and news from Europe introduced readers to events beyond the dynastic court. The distinction between reporting an event and praising the ruler was not always clear, and editors selected details according to administrative interests. Even so, the use of dated issues created an archive of decisions that later historians can compare with official orders, travel diaries, and reports from foreign consuls. Students should ask which events were considered printable, which were omitted, and how the language of the paper located responsibility for an action.

An instructive example comes from the issue dated 13 January 1860 (no. 458), which publicized procedures for admission to Dar al-Funun and the military academy at Tabriz, together with proposals to extend educational facilities elsewhere. It reveals a government presenting educational selection and institutional expansion as matters of public administration. Such a notice does not show that every announced project was completed, nor that applicants outside elite networks had equal chances. It does, however, identify the vocabulary by which officials attempted to establish orderly procedures rather than rely entirely on informal personal recommendations. The difference between published rules and their actual implementation is itself a fruitful historical question.

Official journalism also changed in form. Illustrated issues and specialist publications associated with the science ministry provided visual and scientific material alongside conventional proclamations. The monthly government scientific paper issued in the 1860s employed European-trained teachers and Iranian contributors, demonstrating how the press and Dar al-Funun reinforced each other. Such periodicals were neither an independent opposition nor empty propaganda: within their limits they documented the entry of scientific subjects, translated vocabulary, and new editorial practices into the state's presentation of itself.

The paper’s official sponsorship was a source of both strength and weakness. It lent it authority and continuity, and it ensured access to information. It also meant that the public discussion it fostered was bounded by what the state wished to say. Intellectuals who wanted to criticize the government or to advocate changes in how it worked had to look elsewhere, which helps explain the later growth of periodicals published abroad (Section 77.7). The chapter’s discussion of newspapers deliberately does not revisit Amir Kabir’s career, which is the subject of Chapter 73; the concern here is how the press, once established, evolved as a medium.

Newspapers, Censorship, and the Emerging Reading Public

Inside Iran, the periodical press before 1906 was overwhelmingly official or semi-official. Independent political newspapers, in the sense later familiar after the Constitutional Revolution, were largely absent from the country because the state controlled publishing and treated the press as an instrument of administration. The consequence is one of the most important structural features of the period: much of what is now regarded as the independent Persian press of the late nineteenth century was produced outside Iran, in places such as Istanbul, Cairo, Calcutta, and London, and had to be brought into the country by merchants, travelers, and sympathetic officials.

The state attempted to manage this. Authorities prohibited the import and possession of certain periodicals, including Akhtar and Qānūn, and recipients of banned papers risked official displeasure. Control of the press was not the same as control over ideas, however. Foreign postal services, merchants’ correspondence, pilgrims returning from Ottoman Iraq, and diplomats’ connections all provided routes for circulation. Copies passed from person to person and were sometimes recopied by hand, so that a single issue could influence many readers. Censorship thus raised the risk of reading without eliminating it, and in some cases it increased the interest of banned material.

Subscription and distribution networks depended heavily on merchants and on the personal connections of editors. Iranian merchant communities abroad helped fund and distribute periodicals; commercial correspondence provided information about where to send copies and whom to trust; and readers in Iranian cities might gather privately to read and discuss. Sources also describe readings in semi-public settings, such as gatherings in homes and, as the century advanced, coffeehouses. These practices meant that the circle of people encountering a periodical was wider than its direct subscribers.

Censorship was not one immutable policy. Under Naser al-Din Shah, foreign newspapers and politically critical writings could bring searches, detention, or punishment. An Italian officer's police regulations of 1879 prescribed penalties for unauthorized attacks on the ruler and for public notices judged offensive to state authority. Yet ministries and provincial officials exercised control unevenly, while the premiership of Amin al-Dowleh in 1897–1898 temporarily widened opportunities for private publications within Iran. Later restrictions could be renewed. These variations matter because the nineteenth-century press was not divided absolutely into a silent interior and a free exterior; rather, publication depended on political circumstance, the support of patrons, and the risks editors were willing to run.

A particularly revealing medium was the shabnameh, or night letter, usually a short anonymous sheet pasted in a public place or quietly delivered to intended readers. Such papers could be handwritten and copied locally; the technology required was modest compared with establishing a newspaper. They could spread accusations, calls for action, or news in language designed to be read aloud. Authorities struggled to identify their authors, while readers could not always know whether a declaration represented a genuine movement, factional maneuver, or rumor. This ambiguity made the night letter both effective and problematic as a historical source. Its importance during the Tobacco Protest demonstrates again how manuscript practices and new forms of political agitation could reinforce each other.

Reading a banned paper also had a social geography. The elite Tehran drawing room, an Istanbul merchant's office, a provincial school, and an informal discussion among shopkeepers were different environments. A single issue could be forwarded, summarized in a letter, or read aloud to several people, creating audiences beyond those listed as subscribers. That reach should not be romanticized. Printed criticism could remain concentrated in a thin stratum of officials, merchants, and students while much of rural Iran remained outside its distribution networks. The historian's task is to reconstruct the intermediaries, not to infer a mass public from the mere survival of a newspaper.

Estimating readership is therefore difficult. Editors’ statements about print runs were sometimes promotional, subscription lists survive unevenly, and shared and oral reading means that circulation figures, even when known, understate audience while also not showing how readers responded. A careful account should say that the audience for reformist periodicals was concentrated among officials, merchants, clerics, and educated urban men, that it included people who heard rather than read, and that its precise size is unknown. Whether it was large enough to constitute a political force is a matter of interpretation, taken up in the debate in Section 77.19.

Division II · Section 77.5

Translation and the Circulation of European Knowledge

Translation was one of the principal means by which new knowledge entered Persian. From the early nineteenth century, officials, teachers at the Dar al-Funun, diplomats, and private individuals translated works on military science, medicine, mathematics, geography, history, engineering, and administration. The state developed institutional arrangements for translation and publication under the direction of officials such as Mohammad Hasan Khan E’temad al-Saltaneh in the later decades of the century, and he himself compiled historical and geographical works that drew on European as well as Persian sources.

The translated corpus gradually widened beyond technical subjects. European histories, political narratives, and novels were rendered into Persian, including adventure and historical fiction by French authors. A satirical English novel about Iran, James Morier’s The Adventures of Hajji Baba of Ispahan, was translated into Persian by Mirza Habib Isfahani around 1886 while he lived in Istanbul, though it first appeared in print in Calcutta in 1905; its reception illustrates how Iranian intellectuals could engage a foreign portrayal of their own society critically, and sometimes admiringly. Because many Iranian intellectuals read Ottoman Turkish, Russian, or French rather than English, texts often reached Persian through intermediary languages, which could introduce changes and omissions.

Translating political and social concepts posed especially difficult problems. Persian had a long vocabulary for kingship, justice, administration, and law, but it did not contain exact equivalents for modern notions of constitution, parliament, citizenship, or rights, and the existing words carried their own religious and ethical associations. Translators had choices: adapt an existing term, coin a new compound, borrow from Arabic roots, use Ottoman Turkish formulations, or transliterate a European word. Each choice shaped meaning. Rendering a word for “law” as the Arabic-derived qānūn, rather than as the term for Islamic jurisprudence (shari’a or fiqh), for example, allowed a distinction between administrative regulation and religious law that might not have been implied otherwise, and that distinction became a point of dispute.

Translation often proceeded in institutional settings that shaped what counted as useful knowledge. At Dar al-Funun, a European instructor's lecture might be interpreted by an Iranian assistant, converted into notes, and eventually printed as a Persian manual. In the court's translation offices, a foreign history or geographical compendium could be selected because an official wanted to inform the shah, instruct officers, or display the sophistication of the government. The same text might be abbreviated to fit its intended audience. Consequently, the Persian version could have a different intellectual purpose from the source-language work. To establish what readers actually encountered, historians must compare the translated text with its intermediary and original versions where these survive.

Mirza Habib Isfahani is a useful case because he was not simply importing information. In Istanbul he produced a Persian grammar and literary work, translated European writings, and crafted a free Persian adaptation of James Morier's Hajji Baba around 1886. Morier's English novel had satirized Iranian life from the vantage point of a British observer. Mirza Habib's lively Persian rendition could be read as a display of prose style and social criticism, but the text did not appear in print until 1905. The distinction between composition, manuscript circulation, and publication is essential: a translation can belong intellectually to one decade while reaching a much wider reading public in another.

Translation also had political consequences even when it did not mention a constitution. A medical textbook implied specialized credentials, a geography text offered a new representation of territorial space, and an administrative manual described offices as functions that might be specified and compared. Such writings could help officials imagine governing through procedures rather than solely through personal patronage. Others reinforced existing hierarchies by giving the court new technical tools. The historical problem is therefore not whether a work was Western or Iranian, but who commissioned it, what was changed in its Persian form, which readers used it, and which institutions possessed the resources to act on its information.

Translators were never simple conduits. They selected what to translate, abridged or glossed it, and added explanatory material for readers unfamiliar with the context. Some presented foreign institutions as models; others used the foreign example to criticize domestic failings obliquely; still others wanted principally to equip the state with technical capacities. Selective adaptation, not wholesale adoption, was the normal pattern. The consequence is that European ideas did not simply arrive in Persian; they were interpreted and recombined in the act of translation, a point that connects directly to the debate in Section 77.16 about import and indigenous reworking.

Division II · Section 77.6

Iranian Students, Diplomats, and Travelers Abroad

Iranian encounters with other societies took several forms. Diplomatic missions to Europe began early in the century, and their members sometimes left accounts. The embassy of Abu’l-Hasan Khan Ilchi to London in 1809–1810 produced a travelogue, and Mirza Saleh Shirazi’s account of his stay in England in the 1810s described institutions, sciences, and customs he observed. Students were sent abroad in the 1810s, and in 1858–1859 a larger group of forty-two students was selected for study in France, departing with the Iranian diplomatic mission in April 1859. Some studied military and technical subjects; others became translators, teachers, or officials on their return. Merchants, pilgrims, and laborers also traveled, often to the Ottoman Empire, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and India, and their experiences contributed to a wider awareness of alternative arrangements.

Naser al-Din Shah’s European journeys of 1873, 1878, and 1889 were exceptional in that they involved the monarch himself. The diaries associated with his travels recorded observations about palaces, theaters, factories, and everyday scenes, reflecting both fascination and the perspective of a ruler for whom the foreign was primarily a spectacle and a source of resources. The journeys had political consequences for the state’s finances and concession policy (Chapter 75), and they brought a retinue of officials into extended contact with European institutions.

Other travel accounts were written by Iranians with different motives. Haj Sayyah Mahallati, who traveled extensively in the second half of the century, wrote about Europe, the Americas, and Asia and later participated in political life. Zayn al-Abidin Maraghe’i, an Iranian merchant based in the Caucasus and Istanbul, wrote the fictionalized Siyāḥatnāmeh-ye Ebrāhīm Beyg, whose protagonist travels through Iran and describes its conditions in sharply critical terms; its parts appeared from the 1890s. Abd al-Rahim Talebov, from a Tabrizi family settled in the Caucasus, wrote didactic and scientific works in a simple Persian intended to teach ordinary readers about the world. These texts show that Iranians abroad did not merely admire Europe but used comparison with it as a tool for criticizing the conditions they saw at home.

The large French student mission deserves a precise account. Forty-two students, many associated with Dar al-Funun, were selected in 1858–1859 for scientific, military, and medical training. They departed with the retinue of Hasan-Ali Khan Garrusi, Iran's minister to the French court, in April 1859. The mission represented an unusually concentrated investment in overseas education during Naser al-Din Shah's reign, not the beginning of uninterrupted annual scholarships. The recipients encountered languages, institutions, examinations, and professional expectations that did not map readily onto employment within Qajar administration. Their return created valuable expertise, but it also exposed the limits of a government unable consistently to employ specialists in positions corresponding to their training.

Accounts by travelers should be read as arguments structured by observation. A visitor impressed by a Parisian hospital might praise its organization while overlooking social inequalities in the city; a royal diarist could describe a military review with enthusiasm while omitting questions of parliamentary accountability. Conversely, travelers who criticized foreign imperialism did not necessarily reject European medicine, technology, or schools. A published travelogue might be edited for patrons and readers, while a private notebook could contain irreverent details removed from public versions. The circumstances of composition and publication are therefore integral to the source, not merely background to the author's observations.

Commerce produced other forms of travel more difficult to reconstruct than embassy missions. Iranian Armenian merchants, Azerbaijani-speaking traders, pilgrims, craftsmen, and seasonal workers moved across the Ottoman, Russian, and British imperial worlds. News of wages, public order, municipal rules, and political upheavals returned through letters and oral accounts. They did not all compose treatises, but such encounters altered comparison within families and commercial networks. Intellectual circulation should not be equated exclusively with the itinerary of famous diplomats: a pamphlet moved through an infrastructure sustained in part by people whose names scarcely appear in official histories.

Travel produced diverse conclusions. Some travelers concentrated on technology and military organization; others emphasized the rule of law, the security of property, the existence of schools and newspapers, or the accountability of ministers. Others criticized aspects of European society, such as materialism, class inequality, and above all imperial conduct in Asia. It would be misleading to assume that all travelers concluded that Iran should adopt European political models. The observations they recorded were filtered through religious assumptions, Qajar social position, and varied political interests.

Division II · Section 77.7

Istanbul, Tiflis, Calcutta, Cairo, and Exile Publishing

Because Qajar authorities controlled publishing at home, much independent Persian-language journalism emerged abroad, where Iranian merchants, officials, and exiles could use local printing facilities. These centers were not marginal to Iranian intellectual life; they were integral to it. The cities offered resources unavailable inside Iran: freer presses, Iranian communities with money and literacy, contact with other reformers, and in some places protection from Qajar reach.

Istanbul was the oldest and one of the most important. Akhtar (“Star”), published there from 1876 until the 1890s, was a Persian-language periodical that combined news, scientific and literary material, and commentary on conditions in Iran. Its readers included Iranians in Istanbul and elsewhere in the Ottoman Empire, as well as subscribers in Iran who obtained it through merchant and diplomatic channels. The paper’s tone was generally moderate and reformist, and it was periodically banned by Qajar authorities. Istanbul also hosted a variety of Iranian exiles and intellectuals who discussed reform, religion, and politics in a milieu shaped by the Ottoman reform era.

Qānūn (“Law”), published in London by Malkom Khan beginning in 1890 and continuing in the 1890s, was more explicitly political. It argued for law, accountability, and consultative institutions, and it addressed clerics as well as officials in an effort to build a coalition for reform. It was widely discussed in Iran despite its prohibition. In Cairo, Ḣekmat (“Wisdom”), begun in 1892, offered scientific, literary, and general commentary from within a vibrant Arabic-language intellectual environment. In Calcutta, Ḣabl al-Matīn (“The Firm Cord”), begun in 1893 and edited by Sayyid Jalal al-Din Kashani (Mu’ayyad al-Islam), became one of the longest-lived Persian newspapers, combining concern for Islamic solidarity with criticism of Iranian conditions and, later, support for constitutional change.

The Caucasus occupied a distinct position. Tiflis and, increasingly, Baku with its oil economy contained substantial populations of Iranian origin, including many laborers from Iranian Azerbaijan, and they had printing in Persian, Azerbaijani Turkic, and Russian. Akhundzadeh worked in Tiflis (Section 77.13), and the region provided a path of influence from Russian-language intellectual life and from the political ferment that grew in the Russian empire. These centers should be distinguished: the Ottoman capital offered an Islamic and imperial context; Cairo offered a vibrant intellectual setting and, from 1882, British occupation; Calcutta combined a British colonial environment with a large Iranian merchant community; London offered political asylum and press freedom; and the Caucasus offered proximity, labor migration, and Russian intellectual currents.

The chronology of the expatriate press also helps prevent an impression of continuous, uninterrupted publication. Akhtar was founded in Istanbul in the mid-1870s; its exact beginning is variously given as 1875 or 1876 depending on the publication date used. Qānūn began with an issue dated February 1890, while the dates assigned to its final issues differ among bibliographies. Ḥabl al-Matīn began in Calcutta in 1893 and had a considerably longer life. These were not interchangeable journals. They had different proprietors, financial needs, relations with host authorities, and attitudes toward religion, merchants, and the royal government. The description 'opposition press' therefore identifies a broad condition of publication rather than a common editorial program.

A revealing example of the press's educational effect is the account that Mirza Hasan Roshdieh encountered discussion of new teaching methods in Akhtar, encouraging him to investigate schools in Ottoman lands. This reported connection is important not because it proves that a single article caused an educational revolution, but because it shows how a periodical could link a proposed reform to an individual able to carry it elsewhere. Similarly, merchants distributing Ḥabl al-Matīn connected critiques of government to existing networks of credit, trust, and transport. A newspaper's capacity to assemble arguments from different cities mattered as much as its willingness to denounce a minister.

Publishing abroad did not guarantee safety or intellectual independence. Exile writers depended on subscribers, patrons, host-country laws, and sometimes diplomatic protection, and they could be vulnerable to pressure from Qajar representatives. Editorial claims about unity or patriotism often concealed rivalries among individuals and political factions. Surviving issues show what editors wished to circulate, but letters, memoirs, and the evidence of censorship are required to establish where copies went. When the constitutional movement later drew on this dispersed print culture, it inherited both a vocabulary of political criticism and a set of networks already shaped by distance and constraint.

The audience for exile publications must be understood carefully. They reached educated Iranians abroad and a minority within Iran, through channels described in Section 77.4. Their political orientations differed: some editors aimed to pressure the state to reform, others to organize an opposition, and others to foster Muslim unity or scientific literacy. Their shared feature was their location outside the monarchy’s control, which allowed them to express criticism that would have been dangerous at home.

Division II · Section 77.8

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and the Politics of Islamic Reform

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/39–1897) was one of the most mobile and controversial intellectuals of the nineteenth-century Muslim world. His life ranged across Iran, Iraq, India, Afghanistan, Egypt, Paris, London, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire, and he influenced and was influenced by reformist and political currents in each. His supporters regarded him as an awakener of Muslim societies; his critics regarded him as an agitator or opportunist; and historians have tended to emphasize that he adapted his message to his audience.

The question of his origin illustrates the difficulty. He presented himself as Afghan, a claim that would have given him a Sunni identity and distanced him from Iran, but the weight of scholarly evidence indicates that he was born in Iran near Hamadan and raised as a Shi’i, and that he educated himself partly in the shrine cities of Iraq. Historians generally explain the false “Afghan” identity as a strategic choice that helped him operate in Sunni environments. This dispute is not merely biographical; it affects how his statements about religion and politics are interpreted.

His ideas combined several themes. He criticized European, and especially British, imperial domination of Muslim lands; he called for unity among Muslims against such domination; he advocated education and the acquisition of modern science; and he argued that Muslim societies required religious and political renewal. He also wrote and spoke about the relationship between religion and philosophy in ways that scholars still debate: some interpret his public Islamic reformism as sincere, others see it as a political instrument. In 1884 he collaborated in Paris with the Egyptian reformer Muhammad Abduh on a periodical advocating Muslim solidarity against imperial control.

His relationship with Iran and the Qajar monarchy was turbulent. He visited the country in the 1880s, and Naser al-Din Shah at first received him but soon came to view him as a danger. In 1891 he took sanctuary (bast) at the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim near Tehran, from which he was removed and expelled to Ottoman Iraq in conditions that provoked outrage among his followers. From abroad he continued to criticize the Qajar government. His influence on Iranian political debate was real but should not be exaggerated into control: he inspired disciples, including Mirza Reza Kermani, who assassinated Naser al-Din Shah in 1896, but he did not direct a single organized movement, and the extent of his responsibility for that act remains debated.

In Paris in 1884 Afghani worked with Muhammad Abduh on the Arabic-language periodical al-Urwa al-Wuthqa, an important vehicle for anti-imperial argument and Muslim solidarity. The journal's brief life contrasted with its wider intellectual influence, and its Arabic language meant that its relationship to Persian-reading audiences was mediated by multilingual scholars and reformers. Afghani's positions were not reducible to a consistent theory of parliamentary government: in some settings he appealed to rulers for strong reforming leadership, while elsewhere he sought to organize intellectual and popular resistance to rulers he considered dependent on foreign powers. This flexibility helps explain both his influence and the difficulty of locating him in a single ideological school.

His Iranian activities brought the limits of court-sponsored reform into focus. Invited into Qajar political circles, Afghani eventually came into conflict with Naser al-Din Shah and his ministers. After taking refuge at the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim, he was expelled in 1891. Supporters interpreted his treatment as evidence of royal intolerance; officials feared his capacity to unsettle social and religious networks. Later writers connected his message with the assassination of the shah by Mirza Reza Kermani in 1896. The assassin's own agency, the changing relationship between teacher and follower, and the agendas of later commentators should prevent a simple line of causation from being drawn between Afghani's writings and the killing.

Afghani's prominence also reveals what was distinctive about reform through religious language. His appeals could reach readers suspicious of European influence because he presented political revival as compatible with, and sometimes demanded by, Islamic commitments. That did not make his thought identical with the views of senior Shi'i jurists or with Malkom Khan's administrative reformism. Reformers could agree that imperial pressure was dangerous while disagreeing profoundly about theology, the proper source of political authority, and the acceptable instruments of change.

Afghani’s ideas differed from those of other reformers discussed in this chapter. Malkom Khan pursued law and administrative accountability through argument aimed at the elite; Akhundzadeh criticized religious tradition itself; Mostashar al-Dowleh sought to show constitutional principles within Islam; Afghani emphasized Muslim solidarity and resistance to imperialism, with religious reform as a means. Recognizing these differences prevents the common error of folding all nineteenth-century reformers into a single “modernist” type.

Division III · Section 77.9

Traditional Education and the Expansion of New Schools

Elementary education in the nineteenth century was usually provided by the maktab, a school often attached to a mosque or run from a teacher’s home, where boys, and in some places girls, learned to read the Qur’an and simple devotional texts and then classical Persian works such as the Golestan and Bustan of Sa’di and poetry of Hafez. Instruction emphasized recitation, memorization, calligraphy, and moral conduct, and it often included limited arithmetic. Teachers were commonly clerics or scholars, and pupils advanced individually rather than in grades. Discipline could be harsh, and critics later singled out rote methods, but the maktab also transmitted a shared literary and moral culture and enabled many pupils to enter trades, clerical training, or administrative service.

Higher learning took place in the madrasa, where students studied Arabic grammar and rhetoric, logic, Islamic jurisprudence and its principles, theology, and, in some centers, philosophy and the sciences. Prominent madrasas in Isfahan, Mashhad, Qom, Tabriz, and Shiraz, and above all in the Iraqi shrine cities, attracted students and teachers whose scholarly networks crossed political boundaries. The institutional development of Shi’i learning and of clerical authority is treated in Chapters 58 and 65. For present purposes, it is essential to note that traditional higher education was an active intellectual field: scholars debated legal methodology, wrote commentaries, and, in the case of philosophical traditions, preserved and developed complex metaphysical thought. Describing it as uniformly stagnant misrepresents the evidence.

Other forms of instruction existed alongside these: private tutoring in elite households; apprenticeship in crafts and commerce; training of scribes and accountants; and instruction in religious minority communities, including Armenian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian schools with their own curricula. From the 1830s, foreign missionaries, notably American Presbyterians in Urmia, established schools in northwestern Iran that taught European languages and subjects and that educated both Christian and, in some cases, Muslim pupils. French and other Catholic missions and, from the late 1890s, the Alliance Israélite Universelle operated schools as well. These institutions were often valued for their language and science instruction and mistrusted for their religious or political associations.

Classroom practices varied even within the same type of institution. Some maktabs were rigorous environments where pupils gained command of reading, arithmetic, and classical texts; others offered little sustained instruction beyond memorization. Advanced madrasa students undertook demanding study of Arabic grammar, logic, jurisprudence, legal theory, philosophy, and sometimes mathematics, though the mixture differed by teacher and center. A contrast between 'religious rote learning' and 'modern practical learning' would therefore obscure both the variety of older schools and the continuing place of memorization in new institutions. For the historian, the useful questions concern curriculum, teacher training, the social composition of students, and the authority to certify learning.

Different confessional communities provided especially important contrasts. Armenian schools, aided by communal organizations and transregional commercial links, offered possibilities of language instruction and modern subjects in some cities. Jewish and Zoroastrian educational initiatives developed through local philanthropy and international networks; their trajectories could depend on minority status and the attitude of local authorities. Missionary establishments sometimes offered medical and scientific training but also created anxieties over religious conversion or foreign influence. These histories demonstrate that Iranian educational reform was not solely an activity of Muslim court officials, nor did all subjects learn through the same channels.

The emerging distinction between a graded classroom and individual instruction reshaped relationships within education. Age, timetable, textbooks, examinations, and prescribed lessons made it easier to reproduce a school's program across several sites. They also demanded reliable income for teachers and buildings, an administrative problem which small charitable enterprises could find harder than the act of founding a school. Parents weighed these practical uncertainties against the promise that their children would acquire languages or skills useful for work. The persistence or closure of a school could depend on subscriptions and community acceptance, not simply on an intellectual controversy about science.

What distinguished the “new” schools was not merely that they taught different subjects but that they were organized differently: classes graded by level, fixed curricula, printed textbooks, timetables, and sometimes examinations, with an emphasis on European languages, sciences, geography, and history alongside religion. Responses were varied. Some Iranian parents sought such schools for the career prospects they offered, especially in state service. Some clerics objected to curricula they believed would undermine religious instruction or to institutions linked to foreign missions; others supported or taught in new schools. The cost of private schooling also made access uneven. The picture, then, is of coexistence and competition among institutions with overlapping purposes, not of a clear contest between ignorance and enlightenment.

Division III · Section 77.10

Dar al-Funun and the Institutionalization of Modern Sciences

The Dar al-Funun (“Polytechnic”), opened in Tehran in 1851 on the initiative of Amir Kabir, is treated in Chapter 73, which owns its founding and the original objectives of its sponsors. This section concerns its longer-term development and consequences. After its first years, the school continued under Naser al-Din Shah with a mixed record: it survived political changes and funding shortages, expanded some programs, and trained a generation of officers, officials, physicians, engineers, and translators.

Its teachers were drawn from Europe, initially from Austria, Italy, and France among other countries, and from Iranian instructors. Subjects included military sciences, medicine and pharmacy, engineering, mining, mathematics, natural science, and languages such as French, Russian, and English. European instructors wrote or helped produce textbooks, and Iranian staff translated or adapted others, so the school became a center of translation and of printing. The Austrian physician Jakob Eduard Polak, who taught medicine there, later published an influential European account of Iran. Graduates included men who served in the army, the translation and publication offices, diplomacy, medicine, photography, painting, and the press, making the school a channel through which European sciences and techniques entered official life.

Its impact should be assessed with care. The number of students was small compared with the population, and entrance was effectively restricted to the sons of the elite and officials. Its programs depended on the monarch’s support and on budgets that fluctuated; it faced conflicts over administration; and its graduates often entered government service in an administration where patronage and personal connections, not merit, governed advancement. It did not reach rural populations, and it did not fundamentally alter the structure of elementary education. Yet its influence on the formation of a small, state-connected intelligentsia and on the vocabulary of science and administration was lasting.

The institutional record provides a fuller picture than the school's founding date alone. Its early admissions favored sons of leading families, often adolescents whose relatives had access to the court, but the institution subsequently developed more formal procedures. A government notice published in January 1860 addressed admissions at Dar al-Funun and the Tabriz military academy, showing that the ministry sought to define and publicize the process. Such notices were also aspirations: rules could be announced without overcoming the force of patronage. The archive of examinations and student lists allows historians to ask who entered, who completed training, and what occupations graduates actually pursued, rather than assuming that enrollment immediately produced a class of reformers.

Medicine offers a particularly concrete example. Jakob Eduard Polak taught anatomy, clinical methods, and medical practice, while Iranian students learned to work between European instruction and existing understandings of disease. A graduate might find employment in the royal court, a military hospital, or provincial service, but technical knowledge alone did not abolish older medical practice. The transfer of techniques required interpreters, instruments, medicines, hospitals, and patients willing to trust unfamiliar procedures. Engineering, military surveying, and mathematics similarly connected the classroom to material needs of the state. The adoption of new expertise was therefore a process of institutional negotiation, not the passive transfer of superior facts.

Language teaching and publication extended the school's reach beyond its small enrollment. The French instructor Jules Richard, known in Iran as Mirza Reza Khan, taught for decades; other European and Iranian staff prepared or translated instruction materials. Scientific terminology had to be rendered into Persian and explained through illustrations, demonstrations, and repeated use. Graduates later worked as teachers and translators, making Dar al-Funun important as a multiplier of professional skills. The state also supported journals and translation projects that introduced readers beyond the school to new subjects. An institution intended to strengthen royal government thus helped expand the language by which officials and educated readers compared how governments functioned.

Yet the school's longevity should not be mistaken for uninterrupted progress. Ministries changed, budgets fluctuated, favored instructors departed, and the court might support medicine while resisting discussion of political accountability. By 1871 enrollment had risen to roughly two hundred, still a minute proportion of the population. The school could produce technical specialists while leaving elementary education, literacy, and provincial access largely untouched. That uneven scale is central to an assessment of its significance: its influence rested more on the future positions of a relatively small cohort and the circulation of texts than on mass enrollment.

The Dar al-Funun also illustrates an important dynamic of the period: state-sponsored modern institutions produced people whose training and perspectives did not always align with the court’s purposes. Some graduates and translators later became critics of arbitrary rule or participants in reformist circles. The institution thus contributed to the intellectual environment without being a reformist movement in itself.

Modern Science, Educational Reform, and Institutional Knowledge

Definition: In nineteenth-century Persian usage, terms such as ʿolum-e jadīd (“new sciences”) and fonūn (“arts” or “techniques”) referred to European-derived fields such as engineering, medicine, chemistry, and military science, often taught in new institutions. “Educational reform” denotes efforts to reorganize teaching, curricula, and institutions. “Institutional knowledge” refers to knowledge organized and certified by schools, offices, and professions rather than transmitted through individual teachers and personal networks.

Potential misunderstanding: “Modern” was not a neutral description; it carried claims about superiority that people at the time contested.

Division III · Section 77.11

Mirza Hasan Roshdieh and the New School Movement

Mirza Hasan Roshdieh (1851–1944), born in Tabriz, is often called the founder of modern elementary education in Iran. His career illustrates both the achievements of the new-school movement and the way later memory simplifies a more collective story. After studying in Tabriz, he traveled to Ottoman lands, including Beirut and Istanbul, where he encountered the new methods of teaching reading and elementary instruction then being introduced in parts of the Ottoman Empire and the wider Muslim world. These methods, often described as the “new method” (usul-i jadīd), taught reading through the sounds of letters rather than through long periods of rote memorization of letter names and sacred texts, and they placed children in graded classes with printed textbooks.

Roshdieh opened a school in Tabriz in the late 1880s, followed by others in Tabriz and elsewhere, and he wrote textbooks for use in them. He also introduced the word dabestān for the primary school, a term that later became standard, though attributions of such coinages in later memory should be treated with caution. His schools charged fees at first and drew pupils from families able to pay, though he is also remembered for efforts to widen access. He moved among Tabriz, Tehran, and other cities in the following decades.

Opposition was significant. Accounts describe hostile crowds, accusations of undermining religion, and periods of closure, in part because the new method and the new curriculum challenged the authority of existing teachers and, in some views, of clerical oversight of education. Support also existed: some clerics, merchants, and officials backed new schools, and in some places the relationship between the new schools and religion was one of negotiation rather than outright conflict. Disputes about the new schools thus reflected competing interests, including economic ones, as well as principle.

The stages of Roshdieh's career can be dated more firmly than a general reference to the late 1880s suggests. He established a Persian-language school in Erevan in 1883, drawing on new teaching practices encountered in the Ottoman world. In 1888 he opened a modern primary school in Tabriz, where repeated controversy and closures demonstrated the difficulty of sustaining a new method without stable local protection. In 1897, during Amin al-Dowleh's premiership, he founded a school in Tehran. These ventures were related but should not be collapsed into a single founding event; each operated in a different political and educational environment.

The movement was also institutional rather than exclusively biographical. Under Amin al-Dowleh's patronage, Mahmud Khan Ehtesham al-Saltaneh, Yahya Dowlatabadi, and associates organized the Anjoman-e Ma'aref in 1897 to sponsor and supervise schools. Its initiatives included the Elmiya school, with primary and secondary instruction, and further institutions established in the following years. Other philanthropists founded schools for orphans and for families seeking new educational opportunities. Their records reveal uneven success: changes in ministries and the loss of donations could force schools to merge or close. The capacity to coordinate funding, teachers, and curriculum was as important as an individual educator's ingenuity.

Roshdieh was not alone. Missionary schools, state initiatives, and private schools founded by Iranian reformers, including the Tehran institutions sponsored from 1897 by the Anjoman-e Ma’aref under the patronage of Amin al-Dowleh, with organizers such as Mahmud Khan Ehtesham al-Saltaneh and Yahya Dowlatabadi, contributed to a broader movement. Presenting Roshdieh as the sole originator obscures these parallel efforts and the extent to which the movement depended on networks of patrons, teachers, and parents. The movement’s scale before 1900 remained small; the larger expansion of modern schooling came after the Constitutional Revolution, a development examined in later chapters.

Division III · Section 77.12

Women’s Education, Literacy, and the Debate over Social Reform

Women’s access to education was shaped by class, religion, region, and household. In some towns, girls attended maktabs run by a woman teacher, often called mulla baji, who taught the Qur’an and elementary reading, and some women of clerical, merchant, and aristocratic families learned to read and sometimes to write at home. Women in Qajar court circles were occasionally taught by private tutors. Women of the royal household and aristocracy are better documented than others; Taj al-Saltaneh, a daughter of Naser al-Din Shah, left a memoir (written in the early twentieth century) that describes her education and her criticism of the court. Most women, however, had no formal schooling, and rural and poor women generally had the least access.

Missionary schools were one of the earliest institutions to offer organized education to girls. American Presbyterian missionaries opened a girls’ seminary in Urmia in the 1830s, and similar schools later appeared in Tabriz, Tehran, and elsewhere, primarily educating non-Muslim girls but sometimes Muslim girls as well. Their curricula and religious aims aroused suspicion, which limited Muslim attendance. Iranian-run schools for girls, discussed in the context of the Constitutional Revolution, developed mainly after 1906.

Reformers differed about women’s education. Some writers, including Akhundzadeh, argued that the condition of women was a mark of social backwardness; others, working within Islamic frameworks, argued that learning was a religious duty and that women should be educated within a framework of modesty and domestic roles. Conservative opponents objected that education would unsettle gender relations and household authority. Women themselves participated in the debate. Bibi Khanum Astarabadi wrote Ma’āyeb al-Rejāl (“The Vices of Men,” 1894) in response to a polemical manuscript about women’s alleged faults, and the work is an important early example of a woman’s written defense of her sex. It was circulated in limited form during her lifetime and is better known through later editions, which should be borne in mind when assessing its immediate reach.

The household helps explain why the debate about women's learning was never confined to formal schools. Educated women could use writing to manage correspondence and property, teach children, compose poetry, or participate in religious study. In courtly settings, access to manuscripts and private tutors depended on rank; in commercial and religious families it could depend on the learning of particular relatives. Accounts of literate women survive unevenly because autobiographies, school registers, and official histories were more frequently produced by men. The scarcity of records cannot be read as evidence that women had no intellectual agency, but neither can examples from elite households be generalized to all women.

Bibi Khanum Astarabadi's Ma'ayeb al-Rejal, composed in 1894, is an unusually revealing intervention. In answering a male author's catalogue of supposed female defects, she inverted the form of the argument and exposed shortcomings in male conduct. The work makes women's moral and intellectual judgment visible within a literary public that routinely treated women as objects of reform rather than participants in debate. Its manuscript circulation and much later publication caution against claiming that it immediately reached a mass audience. The importance of the text lies in what it demonstrates about the possibility of critical authorship before a broad network of girls' schools and women's periodicals had emerged.

Women also took part in public life in other ways, as the Tobacco Protest (Chapter 76) demonstrated, and some wrote poetry, ran households that sponsored religious assemblies, and managed property. The larger organization of women’s associations, schools, and journals took shape during and after the Constitutional Revolution and is treated in later chapters. The pre-1905 period is best understood as one in which arguments were articulated and exceptional individuals emerged, but in which institutions for women’s education remained few.

Division IV · Section 77.13

Mirza Fath-Ali Akhundzadeh: Criticism, Reason, and Reform

Mirza Fath-Ali Akhundzadeh (1812–1878) was born in Nukha (Sheki) in the South Caucasus, then passing from Iranian to Russian control, and he spent his career as a translator in the Russian administration in Tiflis. He was educated in a religious environment, moved away from it, and became one of the first Muslim writers of the region to publish plays in the European manner. His comedies, written in Azerbaijani Turkic in the 1850s, satirized superstition, bureaucratic corruption, and the credulity of villagers and officials, and they were later translated into Persian and circulated among Iranian readers.

His critical prose argued for rational inquiry and against what he regarded as obstacles to progress: arbitrary monarchy, clerical authority over intellectual life, belief in the supernatural, and what he judged the inadequacies of inherited customs and of the Arabic script. He wrote a series of imaginary letters, usually known in English as the Letters of Kamal al-Dawla, in which an Indian prince criticizes Iranian history and religion from a rationalist standpoint. The text circulated in manuscript and was published in Persian later, and its distribution inside Iran was restricted. The work also reflects the influence of European Enlightenment authors, mediated through Russian and French reading, together with a strong interest in pre-Islamic Iranian history, which he regarded as a source of national pride, an issue returned to in Section 77.18.

Akhundzadeh also proposed reforming the Arabic script used for Persian, Turkic, and Arabic, arguing that a simplified or Latin-based alphabet would speed literacy and enable the printing of texts. In the 1860s he sent proposals to Ottoman and Iranian officials and discussed the matter with Malkom Khan. The proposals were not adopted, and many contemporaries objected to altering the script of the Qur’an and the Persian literary tradition; the question was revisited by later reformers in several countries. The episode shows that reformers could advocate concrete technical changes motivated by their analysis of literacy and communication.

Akhundzadeh's literary technique deserves emphasis alongside his philosophical conclusions. In comedy he placed a corrupt official, credulous believer, or self-interested intermediary into a recognizable social conflict and invited audiences to judge through laughter. Such writing did not require agreement with his religious skepticism to encourage criticism of abuses. In the letters attributed to the fictional Kamal al-Dawla, by contrast, the speaker made sweeping judgments about religious and political history. The contrast between these genres reminds students that a reformer's methods could attract different audiences: drama might be accessible to those unable or unwilling to endorse a radical philosophical tract.

His script proposals also expose a larger disagreement among reformers. He considered the difficulty of learning the Arabic-derived writing system a barrier to scientific dissemination and common literacy; opponents valued continuity with Qur'anic learning and the immense manuscript and literary inheritance. Malkom Khan and other correspondents debated the proposals without necessarily sharing Akhundzadeh's anti-clerical assumptions. The failure to change the alphabet was not proof that the problem of literacy was imaginary. It showed that education reform involved questions of authority, cultural affiliation, cost, and the accumulated investment embodied in existing texts and teachers.

There was a gap between Akhundzadeh’s ideas and many of his potential readers. His open skepticism toward religion and his hostility to clerical authority were not shared by the majority of Iranians or by most other reformers, and this limited his direct political influence and made him a controversial figure for those seeking support from the ulama. His importance lies in the intellectual possibilities he opened: a model of secular rational critique, the use of satire and drama as instruments of social criticism, and an early, vivid demonstration that Iranian and Muslim thinkers could engage European thought critically in Persian and Turkic languages. His work also reached readers across the Caucasus and the Ottoman Empire, again showing the transregional geography of reform.

Division IV · Section 77.14

Mirza Malkom Khan: Law, Government, and Constitutional Ideas

Mirza Malkom Khan (c. 1833–1908) was born in Isfahan into a family of Armenian background that had converted to Islam, and he was educated in Paris, where he studied technical subjects and absorbed French political writing. On his return he held posts in the state, and in 1858 he formed a society called the Farāmūshkhāneh (“house of forgetfulness”), modeled in part on European lodges, which gathered officials and intellectuals to discuss reform. It was soon suspected of subversion and was closed in the early 1860s. Malkom was exiled, became a diplomat in the Ottoman Empire, and later served as Iranian envoy in London from the 1870s until 1889.

His early writings, circulated in manuscript and short printed pamphlets, argued that Iran needed administrative reorganization and written law, and they often presented European institutions as techniques that could be adopted without abandoning Islam. He described European civilization as an assemblage of practices that Iranians could borrow, and he sought to persuade rulers that law and orderly administration would increase state strength. This approach has been called pragmatic and, by critics, instrumental. It addressed the shah and his officials as the agents of reform, not the people at large.

His career took a controversial turn in 1889, when he was involved in a lottery concession granted to a foreign group, an affair that caused a scandal in London and led the Qajar government to dismiss him and strip him of his titles. This reversal is central to any assessment: the man who wrote about law and accountability had himself taken part in the system of concession-seeking and patronage that reformers criticized (the concession politics of the period are examined in Chapter 75). Historians differ on how to interpret the contradiction. Some see opportunism; others see a reformer who operated within the only channels available; others emphasize that he subsequently changed his position fundamentally.

An earlier document gives greater specificity to Malkom's administrative vision. In the Ketabcha-ye Ghaybi of 1859, he proposed governing through defined offices, regulated responsibilities, and institutional discipline. His concern was practical: an order should not depend entirely on a minister's personal whim if the state wished to collect revenues, employ specialists, and administer the provinces. This emphasis on systematic organization complicates any reading of Malkom as only an opposition journalist. For much of his career, he hoped that the monarchy itself would adopt reform from above.

The first issue of Qānūn, dated February 1890, signals the change in audience. It argued that scattered individuals with useful views could acquire strength when their ideas were assembled and circulated through a newspaper. That is an argument about communication as a political institution, not only about what laws should say. The editor wanted the periodical to connect people who could not otherwise consult one another. Its rhetoric joined the language of legal order to the practical mechanisms of collective discussion; yet the editor still depended on trusted intermediaries and a limited reading public to reach Iran.

His career remained morally and politically ambiguous. The lottery concession scandal damaged his claim to stand above court intrigue, while his newspaper was willing to recruit clerical support through religiously inflected argument. Historians should compare his proposals for government from above with his later tactics of public criticism rather than assign a single unchanging political identity to him. The same institutional vocabulary could serve a reforming minister, an exile attacking that minister, or a coalition demanding more fundamental limits on royal discretion.

After his dismissal, Malkom Khan began publishing Qānūn in London in 1890. The paper called for law as the basis of government, for a consultative assembly, and for the protection of the rights of subjects, and it addressed the ulama as well as secular readers. It argued in religious as well as political terms, an approach designed to build a broad coalition, and it criticized the shah’s ministers and the concession policy. When the Tobacco concession became public in 1890–1891, Qānūn denounced it, and its arguments reached readers who were part of the networks that later mobilized (Chapter 76). The extent of its causal role in the protest is a matter of interpretation; it was one of several channels of information and criticism.

Division IV · Section 77.15

Mirza Yusuf Khan Mostashar al-Dowleh and the Idea of Constitutional Law

Mirza Yusuf Khan Mostashar al-Dowleh (d. 1895), from Tabriz, was an official and diplomat who served in the Iranian mission in Paris. In 1870 he wrote Yek Kalameh (One Word), a treatise that argues that the progress of Europe and the remedy for Iran’s weakness can be summarized in the single word “law” (qānūn). It was written in a period when Iranian officials, among them the reformist statesman Mirza Hosein Khan Moshir al-Dowleh, were interested in administrative reform, and it was printed in Paris before being reprinted and copied.

The argument has two parts. First, the author maintained that the European states derive their order and prosperity from codified law that defines rights and obligations and restrains arbitrary power. Second, and more distinctive, he argued that the principles of this law are compatible with Islam. He examined the seventeen articles of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen of 1789 and, article by article, sought to show that their substance corresponded to teachings found in the Qur’an and in Islamic tradition, such as justice, equality before the law, and the protection of life and property. The strategy was to demonstrate that adopting codified law would be a recovery or implementation of Islamic ideals, not a surrender to foreign ways.

The treatise’s significance is both in its argument and in its method. By citing religious sources for a political proposal, it created a template that later reformers would use, and it offered a reply to those who would reject constitutional ideas as foreign. The comparison also had limits. Critics have noted that the author’s reading of European texts was selective and that matching French articles with Islamic precepts glossed over differences, for example concerning the source of legal authority, since European constitutionalism rested on popular or national sovereignty and Islamic jurisprudence on divine law as interpreted by scholars. The text did not resolve this tension.

The text was developed during Mostashar al-Dowleh's service in Paris in the late 1860s and appeared as Yek Kalameh around 1870. Its title is deliberately reductive: it suggests that varied public problems might be addressed through the single principle of law. Yet the treatise did not merely repeat French articles. It arranged comparative examples to convince Iranian readers that the claims of liberty, equality, and legal security could be defended through Qur'anic and other Islamic references. This was an argument made within a political culture in which an openly secular foundation for legitimate government would have alienated many potential allies.

The comparison depended on unresolved choices. Equality before courts might be urged as a principle while existing legal practice recognized different religious and social statuses. A public law binding ministers could be imagined without specifying who was authorized to legislate or how rulers could be made to obey. Students should therefore separate the author's ethical case for law from later constitutional solutions to representation, executive authority, and legal review. The value of the treatise lies in its effort to make these questions discussable, not in an assumption that it already supplied a complete constitutional blueprint.

Mostashar al-Dowleh's later treatment by the Qajar authorities demonstrates the stakes of such argument. He was punished and imprisoned amid suspicion of dissident writing and political connections. His death is conventionally dated to 1895. The biographical outcome should not be used to suggest an uninterrupted trajectory from his Paris treatise to 1905; instead, it shows how reformist service within the bureaucracy could coexist with personal exposure to repression.

It is also important to distinguish constitutional law as an intellectual proposal from the actual constitutional institutions established after 1905. Yek Kalameh did not describe a written constitution with an elected parliament, and its author did not lead a movement. It was one of several writings that prepared the ground by making the vocabulary of law, rights, and legal limits on government available and plausible. Its influence on later constitutional debate was real, but the practical political demands of 1905–1906 had their own sources, treated in Chapter 78.

Division IV · Section 77.16

Liberty, Justice, Nation, Progress, and Constitutionalism

The writers discussed above shared a set of terms, but those terms did not have one stable meaning. Comparing them shows how political vocabulary was built from older words, translated concepts, and arguments between rivals, and why historians must be careful about assuming that similar words signified the same thing across time.

Qānūn (law) is a word of Greek origin that entered Arabic and Persian and traditionally referred to administrative or royal regulation, as distinct from the religious law (shari’a). For reformers, it came to mean a written, public, systematic body of law that binds rulers. The choice of this word, not shari’a, was significant: it implied that rules could be made and revised by human institutions, which some religious authorities contested.

ʿAdālat (justice) had deep roots in Persianate and Islamic political thought, where just rule was the central duty of kings. Nineteenth-century writers drew on this legacy while changing its emphasis: from the personal justice of the ruler to impersonal institutions, such as courts and law, that secure justice. The demand for a “house of justice” (ʿadālat-khāneh) in 1905 drew on this older vocabulary, as Chapter 78 will show.

Āzādī (liberty) traditionally denoted freedom from bondage or being free-born and, in some contexts, a noble quality of character. As European usage was translated, it also acquired a political meaning: freedom from arbitrary power, and freedom of expression and association. The shift was gradual, and writers disagreed about its limits, particularly regarding religion and women.

Mellat derives from an Arabic word long used for a religious community, so that “the Muslim mellat” meant the community of believers, and non-Muslim minorities were also described as mellats. In the later nineteenth century it began to be used for a political community or nation, bound by territory, history, and language, though the older and newer senses overlapped for decades. Taraqqī (progress), a term from the Arabic root for advancement, became a rallying idea for those who argued that societies could improve through education, law, and technology, while critics questioned the assumption that European history was the measure.

Mashrūṭeh literally refers to conditionality, to something “conditioned” or “limited by conditions,” and it became the standard word for constitutional government, apparently in part through Ottoman Turkish usage after the Ottoman constitutional experience of 1876. Its widespread use in Iran belongs mainly to the years around 1905–1906, which is a reminder that the vocabulary of “constitution” was only becoming settled at the time that the movement began. Its relationship to mashrūʿeh, a similar-sounding word associated with conformity to religious law (shari’a), was exploited in later disputes, and the ambiguity became politically significant (Chapter 79).

Political vocabulary had to cross boundaries among genres. The word for justice in a royal decree might praise a sovereign's generosity; in a merchant petition it might demand fair taxation; in a reformist tract it might point toward courts whose decisions should not be overturned by an arbitrary official. These are not merely stylistic differences. They disclose competing ideas of where an obligation originated and who could demand its enforcement. When a word began to circulate among government employees, religious scholars, and newspaper readers, agreement in vocabulary could conceal disagreement about institutions.

Mellat is a particularly instructive example. In older Persian and Arabic usage it often designated a religious community, while late-nineteenth-century reform discourse increasingly employed it in senses approaching a political nation. The coexistence of both meanings could make political appeals more inclusive in one setting and more exclusionary in another. Vatan likewise moved between an immediate home district and a larger homeland whose condition imposed duties on its inhabitants. Neither term should be translated automatically into its twenty-first-century equivalent. Students need to ask who was included, what territory was invoked, and whether the writer meant an existing community or one being urged into being.

The word mashruteh was not yet a settled institutional package in the 1870s or 1880s. Reformers could favor consultation and limits on a minister's discretion without agreeing on an elected legislature, religious oversight of laws, or the distribution of sovereignty. In the years around 1905 these questions became more immediate, which is why the chapter's endpoint matters. The language of a constitution had antecedents, but constitutional institutions and their rival interpretations were made through a later political conflict, not simply inherited ready-made from an earlier pamphlet.

Three general points follow. First, meanings were built through translation and adaptation, not simply received. Second, religious interpretation was part of the process; many reformers sought to ground new terms in Islamic or Persian precedents. Third, political experience changed meanings: the words used in 1870 did not carry the same weight after the events of 1905 and after.

Historical Debate

Were Iranian Reform Ideas Imported from Europe or Reworked through Indigenous Intellectual Traditions?

The question: Did nineteenth-century Iranian reformers mainly take ideas from Europe and apply them to Iran, or did they adapt and reinterpret long-standing Persian, Islamic, and Shi’i political and ethical traditions in response to new circumstances?

Interpretation A — Reception of European ideas: Some scholarship emphasizes the role of European texts, travel, and institutions in introducing concepts such as constitutionalism, legal codification, and secular education, and it shows how closely certain arguments echo French or Ottoman models.
Interpretation B — Reworking of indigenous traditions: Other scholarship emphasizes the importance of Islamic jurisprudence, the Persianate literature on just rule, Shi’i theological debates, and existing practices of petition and sanctuary in shaping how new ideas were understood, and it argues that reformers were responding to Iranian problems with a range of available resources.

Evidence: The evidence includes the textual comparison of reform writings with European sources, the use of religious citations by authors such as Mostashar al-Dowleh, the role of the Ottoman Empire as an intermediary, and the pre-existing vocabulary of justice and counsel.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than basic facts, and many historians now argue that the dichotomy is too stark: reformers drew on both, and the same text could be read in either way.

Why it matters: The answer affects how later political movements are characterized, for example whether constitutionalism can be described as foreign to Iranian culture, a claim that was itself used politically at the time and since.

Student task: Select one reformer discussed in this chapter. Identify one feature of the argument that appears to derive from European sources and one that draws on Iranian or Islamic traditions, and explain whether the distinction helps or obscures understanding.

Division V · Section 77.17

Religion, Political Authority, and the Possibilities of Reform

The relationship between religion and reform was not a simple opposition. Twelver Shi’i scholars held positions of immense social authority, as examined in Chapters 58 and 65: they judged disputes, collected and distributed religious dues, led prayers, ran madrasas, and, through networks centered partly in Ottoman Iraq, were not wholly dependent on the Qajar state. That independence meant they could, in some circumstances, resist the court, as in 1891–1892, when a senior jurist’s ruling on tobacco gave decisive force to a protest (Chapter 76). It did not mean that the ulama shared a single view on reform.

Within the clerical world, a wide spectrum existed. Some scholars were suspicious of foreign influence, of changes in education that might reduce clerical oversight, and of any program that appeared to subordinate religious law to human legislation. Others supported measures such as the limitation of arbitrary power, the improvement of education, and the defense of Muslim lands against foreign encroachment, and some of them later became important participants in constitutional politics (Chapter 78). Still others held that the monarchy was legitimate only when it respected religious law and justice, which could be used to criticize or to defend the existing order. It would therefore be wrong to treat the clergy as uniformly hostile to reform.

Reformers and critics outside the clerical establishment also differed. Mostashar al-Dowleh argued that constitutional law was consistent with Islamic principles. Malkom Khan, in Qānūn, appealed to religious figures. Afghani proposed a renewal of Islam to resist imperial power. Akhundzadeh and others were critical of religion’s influence. Writers associated with the Babi and Baha’i movements (Chapter 74), including some, such as Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani and Sheikh Ahmad Ruhi, connected with Azali Babi circles in Istanbul, wrote criticisms of the clergy and the state and were persecuted for them. Intellectual modernization was therefore not necessarily secular: religious reformers and religious critics of the state formed a significant part of the picture.

Some religious figures supported new schools and the teaching of modern subjects while resisting proposals that appeared to challenge the authority of jurists in matters of personal status or religious endowments. Others feared that a state empowered to codify law could also claim jurisdiction over fields traditionally managed by scholars. This meant that the argument about 'law' was institutional as well as theological. A reformer might praise publicly known rules without specifying whether a court of state officials or a mujtahid would interpret them. Such ambiguities permitted cooperation, but they also made later disputes likely.

The Tobacco Protest had shown that clerical authority and commercial organization could cooperate to restrain a royal decision. It did not demonstrate that those participants favored parliamentary sovereignty or secular schooling. Conversely, the secular criticism of writers such as Akhundzadeh could alienate religious scholars whose standing would be indispensable to many public movements. The spectrum included advocates of cautious Islamic reform, defenders of established jurisprudence, state-sponsored modernizers, and opponents of clerical political influence. Mapping these distinctions is more useful than assigning all participants to two camps labeled tradition and modernity.

Central disputed questions included the following. Could a legislative body make laws without infringing the sovereignty of divine law? What role should religious scholars have in assessing laws and in government more generally? Could European sciences be taught in schools without threatening religious knowledge? Could consultation (shura), a concept found in Islamic sources, serve as a basis for representative institutions? These questions were not settled in the nineteenth century. They became sharply political in 1905–1909 and recurred throughout later Iranian history, in forms examined in later volumes.

Division V · Section 77.18

Historical Memory, Language, and the Reimagining of Iranian National Identity

Reform writers, historians, poets, and officials drew on several memories to describe the community to which they belonged. One was the memory of pre-Islamic Iran: kings, empires, and monuments such as Persepolis, known through the Shahnameh (Chapter 42) and, increasingly, through European archaeological and philological scholarship that introduced new knowledge of the Achaemenids and Sasanians. Qajar monarchs themselves had displayed interest in ancient imagery, and some nineteenth-century writers, including Akhundzadeh and Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani, presented the pre-Islamic past as a source of national glory and in some cases blamed the Arab conquest or Islam for subsequent decline. Their arguments were influenced by European racial and linguistic theories about “Aryans,” ideas whose scientific basis has long since been rejected and whose uses were frequently ideological.

A second memory was Islamic and Shi’i: the history of the Prophet’s family and the Imams, the shrines, and the religious calendar that structured communal life. For most Iranians, this was the most immediate source of collective identity, and the Qajar monarchy itself sought legitimacy through Shi’i symbolism. A third was the Persian literary tradition, with poets such as Firdawsi, Sa’di, and Hafez, which tied together readers across political boundaries and religious differences, and the Persian language, which writers began to treat as a marker of nationhood and as something to be reformed or simplified.

Territory was also important. The Qajar state described itself formally as the “Well-Guarded Domains of Iran,” a phrase that identified a realm under dynastic rule, and the losses of the Caucasus (Chapter 71) and encroachments by Russia and Britain shaped a sense of vulnerable boundaries. Vatan, a word meaning homeland or place of origin, took on a more political meaning as writers called on readers to defend and improve it.

The recovery of antiquity depended on new scholarly tools as well as inherited memory. European publications on inscriptions and archaeology circulated among Persian intellectuals, while Iranian historians and officials collected local evidence and wrote new accounts of national antiquities. Such knowledge could encourage pride in a territorial past extending beyond any single dynasty. Yet a revival of interest in ancient empires did not compel rejection of Islam: writers often integrated Achaemenid or Sasanian exemplars with Islamic ethical language and classical Persian poetry. Others employed sharper oppositions, portraying conquest as rupture and decline. These differences should remain visible rather than being gathered into one inevitable march toward modern nationalism.

Language was both a means of belonging and a source of tension. Reformists promoted accessible Persian prose, but people living within the Qajar domains spoke Azerbaijani Turkic, Kurdish, Arabic, Baluchi, Armenian, and other languages alongside Persian. A Persian newspaper could form connections across regions without implying that all citizens understood its vocabulary equally or adopted the same national identity. The historians' task is to examine how elite definitions of Iran circulated, how local and confessional attachments persisted, and how claims about the nation's past could include or exclude contemporary communities.

It is important to separate several things that later nationalist narratives combined. Dynastic allegiance meant loyalty to the shah and his house. Religious community meant belonging to the Shi’i, or more broadly the Muslim, community, and also to a minority community of Armenians, Jews, or Zoroastrians. Cultural belonging referred to the shared Persianate heritage, which extended beyond Iran’s frontiers and which many Turkic-speaking, Kurdish, Arabic-speaking, Baluch, and other populations of the Qajar realm participated in or approached differently. Nationalism proper, the idea of a sovereign people defined by shared language, history, and territory, was only emerging, and it competed with these other identities. Many of its earliest advocates were themselves Azerbaijani Turkic speakers or Armenian-descended, which shows that Iranian national discourse was never simply Persian-speaking in origin.

  • The Persian literary tradition remained a central cultural reference
  • Regional, linguistic, and tribal loyalties persisted

Why the pattern matters: It prevents projecting a fully formed modern nation backward and shows that the nation as a political idea was being constructed through debate, not inherited intact.

Division V · Section 77.19

The Limits of Reform: Censorship, Social Inequality, and Competing Political Visions

The obstacles to intellectual reform were substantial. Literacy, as noted, was low and unevenly distributed, which restricted the direct audience for any printed work. Censorship and the threat of punishment affected writers and readers; the executions in Tabriz in 1896 of Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani and Sheikh Ahmad Ruhi, following the shah’s assassination, were a stark demonstration of the risks for those linked to exile circles. Access to modern education depended on wealth and place, with the largest opportunities in Tehran, Tabriz, and a few other cities. Social hierarchy, gender relations, and religious differences constrained who could participate and what they could say.

Reformers themselves were divided and sometimes compromised. Many depended on state employment or patronage and thus shaped their arguments to what the court would tolerate. Some, like Malkom Khan, were implicated in the concession politics they criticized. Reformers were also vulnerable to being labeled agents of foreign powers, Freemasons, or Babis, accusations that could discredit them regardless of their views. Differences over religion, over monarchy, and over the pace and scope of change meant that no consensus program existed.

Moreover, reform ideas could serve different political projects. Some officials supported educational and administrative change in order to strengthen the existing monarchy and bureaucracy. Others used the language of law and justice to demand limits on royal power. Still others used the language of Islamic solidarity to resist foreign domination or to promote the authority of scholars. These visions could coexist for a time because they used overlapping vocabulary, but they diverged when concrete institutions were proposed.

There were also institutions through which reform was not easily converted into policy. A journal could advocate universal schooling, but a provincial teacher still needed a building, funds, and protection from hostile officials. A diplomat could praise European law while depending upon royal favor to retain his post. A newspaper could call for equality without giving women, rural laborers, and religious minorities a recognized role in framing that demand. Such differences between a stated principle and the capacity to implement it are central to assessing the reformers' historical impact.

Repression could strengthen the symbolic importance of an author while limiting the actual circulation of his work. Mostashar al-Dowleh's punishment, attacks on new schools, and the execution of exiled critics after 1896 contributed to a memory of persecuted reform, but the surviving narrative tends to favor identifiable writers over less visible collaborators. A balanced history must ask not only which books survived but which teachers, printers, couriers, donors, and readers made them consequential. Their social positions were unequal, and their purposes were not always revolutionary.

Against these limits, historians also point to channels through which reform arguments reached beyond the elite: merchants who carried and funded periodicals; clerics who discussed ideas in sermons and assemblies; students and teachers in new schools; and the practice of reading aloud and sharing. The difficulty is to determine how far these channels extended, and the question has produced a significant scholarly debate.

Historical Debate

Did Intellectual Reform Transform Iranian Society or Remain Primarily an Elite Project?

The question: To what extent did the print culture, schools, and writings described in this chapter shape the beliefs and actions of wider Iranian society before 1905, as opposed to affecting only a small educated minority?

Interpretation A — A largely elite phenomenon: Because literacy was low, circulation limited, and many reformers were officials or exiles, some historians argue that the intellectual movement had little direct reach among the majority and that the mass mobilization of 1905–1906 owed more to economic grievances, bazaar and clerical networks, and political events than to reform texts.
Interpretation B — Wider influence through intermediaries: Other historians argue that ideas reached wider audiences through oral transmission, sermons, merchants, and informal reading groups, and that the vocabulary of law and justice helped shape the claims made by the protesters of 1905–1906 and the Tobacco Protest earlier.

Evidence: Relevant evidence includes the content of petitions and protest demands, the social composition of participants, memoirs and contemporary reports, the geography of periodical circulation, and records of new schools. Quantitative evidence is scarce.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely over causation and scale, and over how to infer influence from limited evidence.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the Constitutional Revolution is understood as an intellectual movement that grew into a mass one, a movement of social and economic grievance that adopted an available vocabulary, or an interaction of the two.

Division V · Section 77.20

Historical Synthesis: New Political Languages and the Road to Constitutional Revolution

The developments examined in this chapter changed the conditions under which political argument could take place. Printing, and especially lithography, lowered the cost of reproducing texts. The press, first official and then, abroad, independent, created regular channels of information and criticism. Translation and travel introduced new knowledge and new comparisons. Schools, in competition with older institutions, produced a larger number of people trained in new subjects. Reform thinkers made arguments that combined European, Ottoman, Islamic, and Persian materials, and their vocabulary of law, justice, liberty, nation, and progress was refined through repeated use and dispute.

These changes made constitutional claims more intelligible and more usable. By the 1890s, a literate Iranian could encounter the claim that government should be bound by law, that justice required institutions and not only a just ruler, that the community had interests that could be represented, and that such ideas could be justified in religious terms. They could also see in the Tobacco Protest that organized collective action could change royal policy. These were necessary conditions for a certain kind of political movement, but they were not sufficient. A movement also required a trigger, organization, leadership, and favorable circumstances.

One can now distinguish four overlapping forms of change. The first was material: lithographic presses, paper markets, printing shops, booksellers, postal connections, and telegraph lines made texts easier to multiply and carry. The second was institutional: classrooms, examinations, translation offices, journals, and professional instruction gave parts of the state and society new routines for organizing knowledge. The third was social: merchants, teachers, students, court officials, clerics, and readers abroad created networks through which ideas could gain an audience beyond their authors' friends. The fourth was conceptual: familiar terms for justice, homeland, community, and law acquired additional uses that could call established political assumptions into question. None of these forms operated by itself, and none reached the whole population in equal measure.

The comparison among reform thinkers is equally important. Akhundzadeh made secular criticism and script reform imaginable; Malkom Khan treated administrative law as a technology of government and then used a newspaper to mobilize criticism; Mostashar al-Dowleh joined a European language of rights to Islamic justification; Afghani attacked imperial domination through arguments about Muslim revival and political agency. Their differences explain how the same general discontent could lead to competing remedies. A stronger bureaucracy, a more accountable monarchy, a religiously justified law, and more radical criticism of inherited authority were not synonymous projects.

The limits of the transformation must be included in its explanation. A lithographed book was still inaccessible to a reader without education or money; a school depended on patrons; a clandestine newspaper exposed its readers to penalties; and many women's, rural, and minority experiences appeared only indirectly in elite accounts. Reform was therefore uneven and contested. Nonetheless, the accumulation of new institutions and arguments created resources on which other social actors could draw when political circumstances changed. The Tobacco Protest showed a capacity for collective action; the writings and networks examined here made it possible to frame further demands in more varied legal and political language.

To understand the threshold of the Constitutional Revolution, students should distinguish intellectual possibility from political outcome. The ideas of the 1870s did not automatically produce the demonstrations and bargaining of 1905, and an advocate of administrative regulation should not retrospectively be converted into a supporter of an elected Majles. The next chapter examines the crises, alliances, and decisions that transformed a repertoire of arguments into constitutional mobilization. Chapter 77's contribution is to explain the longer, less linear history of how that repertoire came to exist.

That is why the Constitutional Revolution was not inevitable. Reform ideas had circulated for decades without producing a constitution, and several possible outcomes existed: continued administrative reform under the monarchy, a revival of royal autocracy, deeper foreign control, or a different kind of reform movement. The reformers disagreed about means and goals, and their audience was limited. What happened in 1905–1906 depended on contingent events and on the actions of merchants, clerics, guild members, and others. Those developments are examined in Chapter 78, which begins where this chapter ends.

Cause and Effect

How Intellectual Reform Helped Create the Conditions for Constitutional Mobilization

Long-term causes

Military defeat and foreign pressure; the introduction of printing; the formation of a state-trained bureaucratic and translating class; the growth of merchant and clerical networks across borders; and an older tradition of discourse about just rule.

Short-term causes

The expansion of the exile press in the 1890s; the circulation of Qānūn and other periodicals; the growth of new schools; and the experience of the Tobacco Protest.

Immediate context

Financial strain, concession and loan politics, and growing resentment of arbitrary government in the early years of Mozaffar al-Din Shah’s reign, which began in 1896 (examined in Chapter 78).

Transformation

A political vocabulary of law, justice, nation, and constitution became available and plausible to a wider set of actors, including merchants and clerics, than had been the case in 1850.

Immediate effects

Demands for a house of justice and an assembly could be framed in familiar terms and justified in religious as well as rational language.

Long-term consequences

The same vocabulary became contested in the constitutional struggle (Chapter 79), as different groups gave the terms competing meanings.

Historical complexity: Intellectual reform was one factor among others, including economic grievances, foreign intervention, and political contingency, and it cannot by itself explain the revolution.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Iranians in the nineteenth century developed new ways of communicating, educating, criticizing government, and imagining political authority. It began from the premise that these developments occurred within an existing culture of manuscripts, sermons, oral performance, bureaucratic correspondence, and merchant networks, and that new media were absorbed into and altered that culture without replacing it.

Printing arrived under state patronage in the early nineteenth century, and the adoption of lithography from the 1830s allowed the reproduction of Persian calligraphy at lower cost, making books and periodicals more widely available. The earliest newspapers, including the official gazette begun in 1851, were instruments of state communication that nevertheless introduced new genres, new vocabulary, and the regular reporting of domestic and foreign affairs.

Because the state controlled publishing at home, much independent Persian-language journalism developed abroad, in Istanbul, Cairo, Calcutta, and London, and reached Iran through merchants, pilgrims, and diplomatic channels in spite of prohibitions. Estimating readership is difficult, because reading was often collective and circulation figures are unreliable. Translation, travel, and the activities of students and diplomats supplied new knowledge and new points of comparison, and translators shaped meaning through their choices of vocabulary.

Educational change involved the coexistence of maktabs and madrasas, missionary and minority schools, the state’s Dar al-Funun, and new-method private schools associated with Mirza Hasan Roshdieh and others. Traditional education was an active intellectual field, and the new institutions served a small elite and a limited number of pupils. Debates about women’s education reveal both the exceptional individuals who wrote and the limited institutional provision before 1906.

Reform thinkers developed diverse arguments. Akhundzadeh advocated rational criticism and script reform; Malkom Khan argued for law and accountability while being implicated in concession politics; Mostashar al-Dowleh argued that constitutional law was compatible with Islamic principles; and Jamal al-Din al-Afghani emphasized Muslim solidarity and resistance to imperialism. Their differences warn against treating “reformers” as a single group.

Political vocabulary changed through translation, argument, and experience. Terms such as qānūn, ʿadālat, āzādī, mellat, taraqqī, and mashrūṭeh acquired new meanings and remained contested. The debate over whether the ideas were imported or reworked from indigenous traditions suggests that both processes occurred together.

Religious authorities were not uniformly opposed to reform; positions among the ulama, religious reformers, and secular critics varied, and intellectual modernization was not necessarily secular. Conceptions of Iranian identity drew on pre-Islamic memory, Shi’i and Islamic heritage, Persian literature, and territory, and historians must distinguish dynastic loyalty, religious community, cultural belonging, and emerging nationalism.

The limits of reform were considerable: low literacy, censorship, repression, uneven access, elite concentration, and divisions among reformers. Whether the movement transformed society or remained largely elite is debated. The chapter concludes that these developments made constitutional claims more intelligible and usable without making the Constitutional Revolution inevitable, and it leaves the events of 1905–1906 to Chapter 78.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows political change as something made with tools: presses, schools, translations, journeys, and words. The reformers of the nineteenth century did not lead a revolution, and most of them did not live to see one. What they did was alter what could be said and how it could be justified, and that alteration shaped the possibilities open to later actors.

It also reveals the transregional character of Iranian intellectual life. Ideas moved among Tabriz, Tehran, Istanbul, Tiflis, Cairo, Calcutta, London, and Paris, and Iranian writers participated in conversations that included Ottoman, Russian, Arab, and Indian interlocutors. The history of the plateau, as this textbook has stressed since Chapter 1, cannot be confined to the modern map.

The chapter’s developments continue in later chapters. The vocabulary of law, justice, nation, and constitution became central to the mobilization of 1905–1906 and was then contested in the struggles of 1906–1911. The school movement expanded after the revolution, women’s organizing developed more fully, and debates about religion, authority, and national identity continued into the Pahlavi era and beyond.

Its larger lesson is methodological. To understand how ideas matter in history, one must ask not only what was said but how it was produced, who could read or hear it, what words meant to their users, and what limited their reach. Treated that way, intellectual history ceases to be a gallery of famous thinkers and becomes a study of how societies argue with themselves.
