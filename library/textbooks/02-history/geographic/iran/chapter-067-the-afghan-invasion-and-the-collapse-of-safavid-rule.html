The Afghan Invasion and the Collapse of Safavid Rule

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

How did Safavid Isfahan, the capital of an empire that had endured for more than two centuries, become unable to sustain itself against an invading army, and what happened to the people and territories that depended on its government?

An analytical reconstruction. The scene that follows is not the report of a single recorded day. It is assembled from the kinds of evidence this chapter uses, Dutch company diaries and letters, Armenian chronicles, European residents' narratives, and later Persian and scholarly reconstructions, to suggest how Isfahan may be pictured in October 1722, in the last days of a siege that had begun in March. No individual in it is named, and no conversation is reported.

In the autumn of 1722 Isfahan was still, by every outward measure, a great city. The royal square laid out under Shah Abbas I, the mosques and colleges that framed it, the covered bazaars, the bridges over the Zayandeh River, and the Armenian suburb of New Julfa across the water testified to a century of royal investment and commercial wealth. But the city's ordinary rhythm had stopped. The markets that normally received grain, fruit, and fodder from the surrounding districts stood largely bare. Households had spent savings, sold furnishings, and exhausted whatever stores they had. Officials of a government that still issued orders under the seal of a reigning shah argued about whether a negotiated settlement could be reached, and rumors about the shah's intentions passed through streets where the weakest inhabitants were already dying. Beyond the walls, the forces of an Afghan commander in his early twenties held the approaches to the capital.

The people who found themselves in this situation had not expected it. The revolt that had begun at Kandahar in 1709, described at the end of Chapter 66, had been a distant provincial conflict on the empire's eastern edge, fought by a Ghilzai leader against a Georgian governor. For thirteen years it had been one of several frontier problems, along with the Abdali revolt at Herat, raiding in the south, and the disintegration of order in Shirvan in 1721. That a force from Kandahar could cross the central plateau, defeat a much larger Safavid army outside the capital, and starve Isfahan into submission was not a conclusion that most contemporaries drew from the events of 1709. This chapter therefore treats the conquest as something that happened through a sequence of decisions, battles, and failures, each with alternatives, rather than as a destiny that the earlier crisis had already settled.

The chapter's argument can be stated in advance. The Afghan conquest resulted from the interaction of regional political grievances, capable Afghan military leadership, weaknesses in Safavid mobilization and command, tactical failure at Gulnabad in March 1722, and the collapse of the capital's capacity to feed itself under blockade. Yet the capture of Isfahan did not mean the disappearance of Safavid legitimacy, nor the subordination of Iran's provinces to Afghan authority. The surrender of the monarch, the fall of the capital, the collapse of effective central authority, and the survival of dynastic claims were separate developments, and the chapter keeps them separate. What followed the surrender was not a new, settled regime but a contest among Afghan rulers, a Safavid claimant, provincial leaders, and two neighboring empires that pursued their own interests in the wreckage.

The evidence for these events is unusually rich and unusually uneven. The Dutch East India Company's representatives in Isfahan kept a diary of the siege that survives in translation, and the company's correspondence offers one of the best continuous records of a besieged early modern city anywhere. The chronicle assembled by Petros di Sarkis Gilanentz preserves reports from Armenian correspondents outside the court, but its compiler was not an eyewitness to the siege of Isfahan. The Polish Jesuit Judasz Tadeusz Krusiński, resident in Isfahan, wrote a narrative that shaped European understanding of the crisis for generations, though he wrote after the event and with explanations of his own. Persian court histories are fewer and more guarded. The Afghan side is the hardest to recover, because almost everything known about Afghan motives and decisions was written by enemies, outsiders, or later authors. Each type of source is read in the chapter with attention to who produced it and what it could and could not know.

The chapter observes the boundaries set by the textbook's outline. The reasons the late Safavid state was vulnerable, including succession, finance, military organization, and the debate over decline, belong to Chapter 66. The recovery of political authority, the rise of Nader, the defeat of Afghan rule in 1729, and the Afsharid and Zand regimes belong to Chapter 68. This chapter follows events from the 1709 revolt through the death of Mahmud Hotak in April 1725, with brief references beyond that date only where needed to explain consequences. Five divisions of four sections each carry the narrative: the road from Kandahar to the plateau, the battle and siege, the surrender and the struggle over legitimate rule, the provinces and foreign powers, and the occupation and its consequences. Throughout, the experience of provincial and urban populations is treated as part of the history of the collapse and not as an afterthought to the story of kings and commanders.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how a local conflict at Kandahar in 1709 developed over thirteen years into an invasion of central Iran, distinguishing grievances, leadership, and opportunity.
  2. Distinguish the Ghilzai and Abdali political groupings and explain why neither Mir Wais Hotak's nor Mahmud Hotak's movement can be treated as representative of all Pashtuns or all Afghans.
  3. Trace the Afghan advance through Kerman and Yazd to Gulnabad, explaining the roles of roads, oases, supply, and provincial resistance.
  4. Analyze the battle of Gulnabad and the reasons Safavid numerical advantage did not produce victory, with attention to disputed figures and dates.
  5. Explain how blockade, interrupted supply, and administrative paralysis turned a military defeat into the starvation of Isfahan.
  6. Evaluate the Dutch siege diary and other sources for the civilian experience of the siege, distinguishing direct observation from hearsay.
  7. Describe the different experiences of Armenians, Muslims, merchants, officials, laborers, and other communities, without assigning uniform political responses.
  8. Separate the stages of Sultan Husayn's surrender, abdication, and Mahmud's entry into Isfahan, and interpret reported sums as evidence of bargaining, not payment.
  9. Explain why Safavid legitimacy survived the loss of the capital, using the career of Tahmasp II.
  10. Assess the Russian and Ottoman interventions of 1722–1724, including the status of the 1723 Treaty of Saint Petersburg and the 1724 Russo-Ottoman agreement.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the collapse, distinguishing the explanation of a battle from the explanation of an empire's fragmentation.
  12. Distinguish dynastic collapse, territorial conquest, social disruption, and the continuing struggle over legitimate rule.

Historical Context

The Safavid dynasty had ruled most of the Iranian plateau since 1501 and, by the early eighteenth century, governed a territory that stretched from the Caucasus and Shirvan in the northwest to the eastern borderlands around Herat and Kandahar, and from the Caspian provinces to the Persian Gulf. Chapters 61 through 66 explain how the dynasty's institutions were built and how they changed. The relevant background here is brief. The crown administered large provinces directly through appointed officials, while other regions were governed by provincial commanders, hereditary local dynasties, and tribal leaders whose obedience rested on negotiation as well as force. The capital, Isfahan, was the seat of court, treasury, secretariat, and the most important concentration of consumers, artisans, and merchants in the empire.

Chapter 66 concluded that the state in 1721 still possessed a capital, a treasury, a large share of its revenue, and many commanders and soldiers, but that the means of a coordinated response to simultaneous crises were under strain. It identified several overlapping pressures: the loss of Kandahar after 1709 and the failure of the expeditions of the following years; the Abdali revolt at Herat around 1716–17; the Omani seizure of Bahrain in 1717; raiding in the south and east; the dismissal and blinding of the experienced grand vizier Fath Ali Khan Daghestani around 1720; and the sack of Shamakhi by Lezgi and Daghestani forces in August 1721. This chapter begins from that situation and does not repeat the debate about whether “decline” describes the preceding century.

The eastern frontier had a distinctive political structure. Kandahar lay on the routes between the Iranian plateau and northern India, and the Mughal and Safavid empires had contested it for a century. The population of its hinterland included Pashtun tribal groupings, chiefly the Ghilzai and the Abdali, Persian-speaking townspeople, Baluch, Qizilbash settlers, and officials and garrison troops sent from elsewhere. Most Pashtuns were Sunni, while the Safavid state was officially Twelver Shi‘i, but religious difference was one of several factors in local relations with the state and not an automatic cause of conflict. Local authority was exercised through tribal chiefs, town notables, and the Safavid governor, and the balance among them depended on revenue demands, military service, and the personal conduct of those in office.

Beyond the plateau, the weakening of Safavid authority attracted attention. The Russian Empire under Peter I had been developing commercial and strategic interest in the Caspian and the Caucasus, and had sent an envoy, Artemii Volynskii, to Isfahan in the 1710s. The Ottoman Empire, with a frontier settled by the Peace of Zuhab in 1639 and a long period of relative quiet with its eastern neighbor, observed events in Shirvan, Georgia, and the northwest with its own interests. In the south, European trading companies, chiefly the Dutch and English East India Companies, maintained agents in Isfahan and at Bandar Abbas, and their records are among the principal sources for the chapter. Students should read the events that follow as the intersection of a domestic collapse with the strategic calculations of neighboring states, none of which was acting in coordination with the others.

Finally, the evidence problem deserves restatement. Almost no source in this period was written by a cultivator, laborer, or ordinary Afghan fighter. Dutch merchants, Armenian clerics and traders, a Catholic missionary, and Persian officials wrote about events from within the capital or from afar, and each had reasons to emphasize some things and omit others. Numbers of every kind, including army strengths, deaths, and sums of money, are unreliable and are given in this chapter only with attribution and qualification.

From Kandahar Revolt to Invasion: The Eastern Frontier Crisis

Provincial grievances, Afghan political leadership, and the road toward Isfahan, 1709–1722. The four sections that follow show that the transformation of a regional revolt into a campaign against the capital was neither instantaneous nor inevitable.
Section 67.1

The Safavid Empire on the Eve of the Afghan Invasion, 1709–1721

The political situation in the years after 1709 is easiest to misread from the vantage point of 1722. Isfahan was the capital of a state that still claimed sovereignty from the Caucasus to the eastern borderlands, and its government continued to appoint governors, issue decrees, receive foreign envoys, and collect revenue from the provinces nearest the center. Yet the difference between sovereignty asserted from Isfahan and the practical ability to collect revenue, maintain garrisons, and secure the cooperation of local communities was already wide in the empire's distant provinces. In Fars, Isfahan's hinterland, and the Caspian crown provinces, officials appointed by the shah collected taxes and kept order. At the frontiers, the presence of the state depended on governors with their own troops, local dynasties with hereditary standing, and tribal leaders whose obedience had to be bargained for, as Chapter 66 showed.

Kandahar illustrates the problem. The city sat on the route between the Iranian plateau and northern India, between the Safavid and Mughal worlds, and it had changed hands in 1622, 1638, and 1649. Its loss in 1709 was not simply the defection of a garrison town: it removed a commercial and military base on the eastern frontier from Safavid control and left the court dependent on Herat and Khurasan to shield the east. Those regions carried their own difficulties. Herat was held by the Abdali Afghans, whose own revolt around 1716–17 later added a second eastern challenge. Khurasan, with the shrine city of Mashhad, faced pressure from the Turkmen and Uzbek frontiers. Kerman, Yazd, and the southeastern oases lay along the roads by which any army from the east would have to approach the plateau, and their governors were responsible for local defense with limited resources and little direct support from the center.

The court was not unaware of the eastern problem. The expeditions of 1710 through about 1713 were costly attempts to retake Kandahar, and their failure, discussed in Chapter 66, damaged the court's credibility without making the Afghan leadership an obvious threat to the capital itself. In the following years the Safavid government turned to other matters, including the Gulf, where an Omani force seized Bahrain in 1717, the Caucasus, and the factional politics of the court. The dismissal of the experienced grand vizier Fath Ali Khan around 1720 removed one of the few ministers with substantial knowledge of the frontiers. Nothing about these developments marked the Afghan leadership as the decisive danger; they were simply among the problems that demanded attention, money, and commanders at once.

This is the point to stress about the situation on the eve of invasion: the empire's final crisis arose from several developments that were not necessarily connected in origin. The Kandahar revolt grew from a quarrel with a governor on the eastern frontier. The Abdali rising grew from the politics of Herat. The sack of Shamakhi in August 1721, treated in Section 67.14, arose from the politics of Shirvan and Daghestan and the local disorder there, and the Omani seizure of Bahrain from Gulf naval power. No single coordinating intelligence linked them. What connected them was the court's limited ability to respond to several simultaneous crises, and the fact that each success by a challenger weakened the credibility of the center and encouraged others.

That context shapes how the narrative should be read. When Mahmud Hotak marched toward central Iran in 1721–22, he was not carrying out a long-held plan to conquer the empire. The sources suggest an opportunistic expansion, building on earlier raids, successive tests of Safavid resistance, and an emerging recognition that provincial defenses were thin. The conquest of Isfahan was the result of a chain of choices by Afghan commanders and Safavid officials alike, a point the Timeline Study that follows is designed to make visible.

Section 67.2

Mir Wais Hotak and the Kandahar Revolt of 1709

Mir Wais Hotak belonged to the Hotak clan of the Ghilzai, a major Pashtun tribal grouping of the Kandahar region, and held the position of kalantar, a notable who represented the town and its communities in their dealings with the state. The office placed him between the people of Kandahar and the Safavid governor: he was responsible for helping to organize revenue and order, and his standing depended on his relations with both sides. Specialist scholarship, including the entries in Encyclopaedia Iranica, identifies his revolt as a turning point in the weakening of Safavid control over Kandahar. What cannot be recovered with confidence is the full range of his motives, because nearly everything known about them comes from Persian, European, and Armenian authors writing about an enemy of the Safavid state, or from later Afghan tradition shaped by the memory of the revolt's success.

The immediate background was the appointment, around 1704, of Gurgin Khan, the Georgian ruler Giorgi XI of Kartli, as governor of Kandahar and commander of its forces. Gurgin had entered Safavid service, converted to Islam, and been given high responsibilities. He arrived at Kandahar with a body of Georgian troops, a commander of the kind the court often trusted on difficult frontiers because his dependence on the shah was thought to make him reliable. Sources describe his rule as harsh. Accounts allege exactions by his officers and soldiers, conflicts over taxation, and mistreatment of local leaders, though allegations of this kind in hostile or later accounts must be read as claims and not as established facts, and their details vary from source to source.

The tradition that Gurgin suspected Mir Wais of disloyalty and had him sent to Isfahan is widely repeated, and it appears to reflect the real context of friction between a governor and a local notable. At the Safavid court, according to the accounts, Mir Wais was received, impressed some officials, and eventually was allowed to return to Kandahar. Later narratives add that he made the pilgrimage to Mecca and obtained a legal opinion sanctioning resistance to the governor. Historians treat that story with caution, since it is late and may reflect efforts to give the revolt religious legitimacy. What the sources generally support is that he spent time at the court, learned something about the state's weaknesses, and returned to Kandahar still holding standing among the Ghilzai.

The revolt itself took place in April 1709, when Mir Wais and his supporters killed Gurgin Khan and many of his Georgian troops and took control of Kandahar. Tradition places the killings at a gathering to which the governor and his officers were invited, but accounts differ, and the exact sequence is uncertain. The immediate result was clear: the Safavid governor was dead, much of his force destroyed, and the city in the hands of local leaders. What followed was less a proclamation of a new kingdom than an effort to preserve the local autonomy the revolt had won. Mir Wais does not appear to have claimed the title of shah, and later sources describe his rule over Kandahar in terms of local leadership, with relations with the Safavid state and with neighboring powers left deliberately ambiguous.

The revolt's causes are best treated as interacting. Local grievances against a coercive governor and his troops, disputes over taxation, resistance to external supervision of a frontier society accustomed to negotiating its relationship with distant rulers, and the ambitions of a leader who gained prestige from defying the governor all contributed. Religion also played a role, though a more limited one than some narratives suggest. Most Pashtuns of the region were Sunni, the Safavid state was officially Shi‘i, and the court's policies toward Sunnis in the provinces were a source of resentment in some places, as Chapter 66 noted. But Sunni identity does not explain why this particular revolt occurred when and where it did, and the sources do not show that all Pashtun or Sunni communities shared Mir Wais's goals. The Abdali of Herat, for instance, pursued their own interests and rivalries, and some Pashtun leaders served the Safavid state, as others had for generations.

Mir Wais then defended his position against the Safavid expeditions that followed. The first, under a nephew of Gurgin Khan, was destroyed in the fighting, and its commander was killed; the next major effort around 1712–13 also failed. The reasons historians give are those summarized in Chapter 66: difficulties of assembling and supplying forces over long distances, uncertain command, and the rebels' ability to use local terrain and local support. By the time of Mir Wais's death in 1715, Kandahar had been effectively lost to the Safavid state for six years, and the court showed no sign of being able to reverse that.

Ghilzai and Abdali. Definition: Two large Pashtun tribal groupings of the Kandahar–Herat region, each divided into clans and lineages. Mir Wais Hotak belonged to the Hotak clan of the Ghilzai. Historical context: The two groupings competed for pasture, revenue, and political standing and sometimes cooperated with, and sometimes resisted, the Safavid state. Why it matters: It prevents the error of treating “the Afghans” as a unified political actor. Potential misunderstanding: “Afghan” in this period is a label often applied by outsiders to Pashtun groups of the eastern borderlands, and it did not designate a nation or a state.

Revolt. Definition: Organized resistance to an existing authority. Historical context: The 1709 revolt began as resistance to a governor and to external supervision, not as a campaign to conquer the empire. Why it matters: A revolt may aim at autonomy, reform, or replacement of a ruler, and it should not be described as a conquest until its aims enlarge. Potential misunderstanding: The word does not by itself imply a religious war or a national movement.

Provincial autonomy. Definition: A high degree of local self-government within or alongside a larger state. Historical context: Frontier regions of the Safavid empire often had negotiated relationships with the crown, in which local leaders retained authority in return for tribute, troops, or recognition. Why it matters: The Kandahar revolt can be understood as a dispute over how much autonomy the region would keep. Potential misunderstanding: Autonomy was not necessarily independence; leaders such as Mir Wais at times preferred ambiguity about their relationship to the shah.

Section 67.3

After Mir Wais: Afghan Leadership, Rivalries, and the Rise of Mahmud Hotak

Mir Wais died in 1715, and the question of what Kandahar would become depended on who succeeded him. The leadership passed first to his brother Abd al-Aziz. Sources, mostly hostile or retrospective, describe Abd al-Aziz as inclined to seek an accommodation with the Safavid state, perhaps to submit in return for recognition, an option that would have preserved the local autonomy won in 1709 without the risks of continuing confrontation. Whether that was a considered political line or a story told by supporters of his rival is difficult to determine. What the sources agree on is that he was removed, and that he was replaced by Mir Wais's young son Mahmud, who is generally described as a teenager or young man in his late teens at that time. Several accounts say Abd al-Aziz was killed by Mahmud himself, around 1717, though the sequence is told in different ways.

Mahmud's rise was therefore a competition within the Hotak leadership and not a transfer of power decided by the Safavid state or by the Afghan population as a whole. His supporters included Ghilzai leaders who preferred confrontation, and his advantage lay in the loyalty of fighters who saw the Safavid court as weak. The change was important because it substituted a leader with an expansionist outlook for one who may have favored accommodation. It did not follow, however, that expansion into Iran was a settled aim, and the sources suggest that Mahmud's ambitions grew with successive opportunities.

The Afghan forces that Mahmud commanded were assembled through tribal coalitions and personal ties of loyalty. They relied heavily on cavalry, mobile and experienced in raiding, along with infantry armed with firearms, and they drew on revenue from Kandahar, tribute from nearby communities, trade taxes, and plunder from successful operations. Command relationships were personal and shifting, and a leader's authority depended on his ability to reward followers and win battles. These features gave Afghan forces mobility and cohesion in the short run, and they made it hard to garrison conquered regions or sustain a long administration. Historians emphasize that this force was not a modern army and that its leaders learned about Safavid weaknesses chiefly through raids and probing campaigns.

The Ghilzai groupings of Kandahar and the Abdali communities of Herat remained politically distinct. The Abdali revolt of about 1716–17 was directed at Safavid authority at Herat, but it was not coordinated with Mahmud, and the two groupings were rivals as well as occasional partners. Mahmud's relations with the Abdali were marked by conflict, and when the Afghan movement expanded into Iran it did so under Ghilzai leadership, with many Abdali and other Pashtun groups remaining outside it or opposing it. This matters for how the invasion is described: it was the enterprise of a particular Ghilzai political network, not the common act of a people.

The transformation of a movement concerned with Kandahar's autonomy into a campaign of territorial ambition happened in stages. First came consolidation of Mahmud's own position, including the contest with Abd al-Aziz and the struggle against rival Afghan leaders. Next came expeditions toward the west, testing Safavid defenses in Kerman and beyond. By 1721 Mahmud was committed to a campaign that went well beyond what Mir Wais had attempted, and by early 1722 he was in a position to march toward the capital. The evidence for his thinking is thin, and later accounts that attribute detailed plans to him should be treated with caution.

Section 67.4

The Road West: Kerman, Yazd, and the Afghan Advance toward Central Iran

The route from Kandahar to Isfahan crossed some of the least hospitable and least densely settled regions of the plateau. Between the eastern borderlands and the central cities lay deserts, salt basins, and mountain ranges, and the roads connecting them followed oases, wells, and river valleys where water and fodder could be found. For an army, this meant that movement was constrained by water supplies and the capacity of each oasis to feed men and animals. An army far from its base could not live off the land for long, and it depended on the cooperation or submission of the places it passed, or on plunder, which was a strategy that made enemies. These constraints cut both ways: they limited the size and speed of any invading force, and they made the cities along the route important strategic points.

Mahmud's incursions around Kerman developed during 1719–1722 rather than forming one uninterrupted conquest. Regional research places a particularly destructive Afghan occupation of Kerman in 1719–1720, followed by a withdrawal in spring 1720. Contemporary company records describe serious damage to bazaars, houses, and the Zoroastrian quarter, but also the return of merchants and the resumption of commercial life after the Afghans left. An Afghan force came back in 1721; after renewed attacks on the city and its citadel, it withdrew again in February 1722, apparently accepting payments in return for leaving. Thus Mahmud did not simply secure Kerman and march onward with a pacified province at his back. Local Zoroastrians appear in some reports as allies of the invaders, while other residents of that community suffered losses during the campaigns; neither experience should be generalized to all Zoroastrians. The uneven record is drawn chiefly from European company correspondence and later accounts, and the scale of devastation cannot be reduced to a single reliable total.

Distinguishing different outcomes matters for understanding what the Afghans actually achieved on this route. An attempted attack is not a conquest. A temporary occupation is not secure territorial control. A tribute demand, which a city might meet to save itself, is not an administration. The Afghan forces at times took cities or districts, at times extracted payments and moved on, and at times failed to take places that refused to submit. Yazd, to the northwest of Kerman along the route toward the central plateau, is the clearest case of resistance. The city, an oasis town with significant mercantile and artisan communities, declined to submit, and Mahmud, unwilling or unable to undertake a long siege, left it behind and continued toward Isfahan. That decision suggests how conscious he was of the logistical limits of his army.

One of the questions a student should ask is why the Afghan army could advance at all, given the Safavid state's apparent advantages in territory, resources, and population. Part of the answer lies in the character of the provincial defenses. Local governors commanded limited forces, were often at odds with each other or with the court, and could not easily draw reinforcements from the center. The state's central field army was assembled at Isfahan, far from the eastern oases, and it could not respond quickly to raids that came and went. A second part of the answer lies in the Afghan force itself: mobile, experienced in desert warfare, and led by a young commander who was willing to take risks. A third lies in the lack of coordinated Safavid strategy. The court knew of the threat, and some officials urged action, but the sources suggest that no clear plan existed for meeting an Afghan army in the field before it reached the central plateau.

The response of provincial officials varied. Some governors resisted, some negotiated, and some withdrew, depending on their resources, the strength of local fortifications, and the support of their populations. Ordinary inhabitants experienced the campaign as demands for supplies, the movement of armies through their fields, and the threat of violence. For cultivators in the path of an army, a single season of requisitioning could destroy a year's food and seed, and for merchants, the closing of roads halted trade. These effects were felt well before the siege of Isfahan began, and they set a pattern that would recur across the plateau in the following years.

Section 67.5

The Battle of Gulnabad, March 1722: Armies, Commanders, and Tactical Failure

The battle of Gulnabad was fought on the plain east of Isfahan, near the village from which it takes its name, on the road by which an army from Yazd and Kerman would approach the capital. Sources disagree about the day. Some date the battle to 8 March 1722, while the specialist biography of Sultan Husayn in Encyclopaedia Iranica gives 9 March. The difference is not trivial and should not be silently resolved: it reflects the use of different sources and the difficulty of converting between Islamic, Julian, and Gregorian calendars as well as variations in how accounts count the days of a multi-day encounter. This textbook gives both dates and uses “March 1722” as the safe designation.

The Safavid army that gathered to meet the Afghans was assembled at the capital in the weeks after it became clear that Mahmud's force was approaching. It was a composite army, as Chapter 66 described. It included the royal guard and corps of ghulam soldiers, musketeers and artillerymen paid by the crown, cavalry contingents raised by provincial governors and tribal leaders, and levies from regions near Isfahan, among them troops from the south and west. Georgian and other Caucasian soldiers had long formed part of the military establishment. A Georgian force under Rostam Khan did fight at Gulnabad, but the court's relationship with Vakhtang VI of Kartli was strained, and the much larger reinforcement that could have been raised from Kartli did not arrive. It is therefore incorrect to infer that Georgians as a whole were absent from the Safavid defense. Contemporary and later accounts differ about the exact distribution of commands, about how many men took the field, and about the order of battle. The supreme command was entrusted to leading court figures, and the army was divided into wings, with different commanders responsible for each.

The numbers reported for both armies are among the least reliable data of the siege. Different sources give the Safavid army at several times the size of the Afghan force, with figures ranging widely. Estimates for the Afghan army at Gulnabad run from roughly ten thousand to considerably more in different accounts. The textbook does not adopt any of these figures, because they come from observers and later writers who had no means of counting, and because military writers on both sides had reasons to exaggerate. The safest statement is that the Safavid army was substantially larger than the Afghan force and that its advantage in numbers is mentioned in nearly all the sources.

The terrain and the tactical situation mattered. The open ground near Gulnabad favored cavalry, and the Afghan force, mobile and led by commanders who had campaigned together, used its mobility. Accounts describe the Safavid army as arranged in a long line with artillery, some of it carried on camels, and with firearms among the infantry, but also as slow to coordinate its parts. The accounts suggest that the Afghan attack concentrated on parts of the Safavid line and that the failure of one wing spread to others, though the details differ. Safavid cavalry contingents were reportedly drawn from tribal and provincial leaders who had their own interests and who disagreed about positions and responsibilities. Reports describe a dispute over the disposition of forces on one flank, in which the commander of a contingent from the southwestern provinces was said to have withdrawn or refused to engage as ordered, but historians treat the exact circumstances of such reports with caution.

The outcome was a rout. The Safavid army broke, much of its artillery and baggage was lost, and the surviving commanders and troops fell back toward Isfahan. Contemporary sources report heavy Safavid losses and considerably lighter Afghan casualties, but give no reliable figures. Gulnabad should not be read as proof that Safavid military technology was universally inferior to that of the Afghans. Safavid musketeers and artillery were substantial, and the army had won campaigns in earlier generations. The explanation of the defeat turns on command, coordination, and decisions made on the day and in the weeks before, as the following section argues.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Field Army, Siege, Blockade, and Logistics

Field army. Definition: A force assembled to meet an enemy in open battle, as distinct from garrison troops tied to fortified places. Historical context: The Safavid field army at Gulnabad was assembled quickly from several sources and was not a permanently organized force. Why it matters: The loss of the field army left the capital without a mobile force to challenge the besiegers. Potential misunderstanding: A field army's defeat does not by itself destroy a state, though it removes the ability to defend open country.

Siege and blockade. Definition: A siege is a military operation to capture a fortified place, by assault, by starvation, or by negotiation. A blockade is the interruption of a place's supplies and communications. Historical context: Mahmud's decision to isolate Isfahan, not to storm it, made the blockade the principal instrument of conquest. Why it matters: A blockade can produce surrender without a decisive assault, but at the cost of prolonged suffering for the people inside. Potential misunderstanding: A blockade is not always complete; supplies may leak in, and the severity of shortage varied over time and by neighborhood.

Logistics. Definition: The organization of food, fodder, transport, equipment, and money for armies and cities. Historical context: Armies and large cities alike depended on grain moved by camels, mules, and carts from surrounding districts. Why it matters: The story of Isfahan is as much about logistics as about battle. Potential misunderstanding: Logistics is not a minor detail; it often determined outcomes more than tactics did.

Section 67.6

Why the Safavid Army Failed: Command, Coordination, and Political Decisions

Explanations of the defeat at Gulnabad have changed over time. The oldest, found in many European accounts and repeated by later writers, ascribed the disaster to the cowardice or decadence of the Safavid military class and the weakness of the shah. More recent scholarship, including Rudi Matthee's study of the crisis, gives greater weight to command arrangements, coordination, and the political circumstances in which the army was raised and led. The question is not whether Safavid soldiers were brave or timid, a matter on which the sources offer anecdotes and little else, but how an army of this composition was assembled, who commanded it, and how it responded to the pressures of a battle.

Command was divided. The supreme commander, a leading court figure, had to coordinate contingents under tribal and provincial leaders who had their own followings and their own rivalries, and who had little experience of fighting together. Court politics affected the choice of commanders: appointments reflected the balance of influence among ministers, household officials, and military leaders, and the court's factional struggles, which had removed an experienced grand vizier only two years earlier, weighed on the army's preparation. Troop discipline and training were uneven, and the long peace on the western frontier meant that few of the commanders had led a large army in a pitched battle. The shah, who remained in the capital, was reported by European observers to have been indecisive, and advice to him came from competing quarters.

Strategic and tactical decisions mattered too. The decision to fight in the open, east of the city, instead of preparing a defense that exploited the capital's resources or waiting for reinforcements from distant provinces, has been questioned by historians. A prompt battle may have reflected political pressure to demonstrate the state's strength and to protect the royal palace at Farahabad and the villages around the capital, or it may have reflected an assumption that numerical superiority would suffice. Another hypothesis is that the commanders feared that delay would allow the Afghan force to plunder the surrounding districts and weaken the capital's supplies. In each case the evidence is thin, and later narratives, written by authors who knew the outcome, may have assigned motives with the benefit of hindsight.

The limitations of the sources deserve emphasis. European eyewitness and retrospective accounts, including those by Krusiński and others, often describe the conflict through moral judgments about the shah's character, his piety, his reliance on eunuchs and clerics, and his supposed lack of resolve. Such judgments reflect real impressions, and they may contain truth, but they also reflect the preconceptions of European writers about Asian monarchy, and they take for granted a causal link between personal character and political outcome that must be shown, not assumed. Persian court histories, written under later regimes, tend to be more reserved. The Dutch records, written by merchants, concern the military events chiefly as they affected the capital and the company's interests, and do not offer a detailed account of the field of battle.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Gulnabad a Tactical Defeat or the Expression of Deeper Institutional Weakness?

The question: Was the defeat at Gulnabad primarily the result of immediate decisions and circumstances on and before the day of battle, or the military expression of deeper Safavid institutional weaknesses?

Interpretation A — Tactical and contingent: Some historians emphasize decisions made in the weeks before the battle and on the field: the choice to fight in the open, divided command, disputes among commanders, the failure of one wing, and the absence of the Georgian contingent. On this view a different set of choices, or a different sequence of events on the day, might have produced a Safavid victory or at least a limited defeat, and the larger Safavid army might have prevailed.
Interpretation B — Structural: Others argue that the defeat reflected the weaknesses Chapter 66 described: a composite army assembled hastily from contingents with different loyalties, officers with little combat experience after a long peace, a court whose decision-making was slow and factional, and fiscal and administrative difficulties that limited preparation. On this view the particular errors at Gulnabad were symptoms of conditions that made a defeat likely sooner or later.

Evidence: Both sides use the same limited evidence: Persian and European narratives, the Dutch and other company records, and later reconstructions. None gives a reliable order of battle or an account of the decisive moments.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation, not chronology: how much weight to give contingent choices as against long-term conditions. Both explanations may be partly right, and the evidence does not allow a decisive resolution.

Why it matters: If the defeat was contingent, the conquest of Isfahan was not foreordained, and the fall of the state hinged on events that might have gone differently. If it was structural, the collapse appears as the likely culmination of earlier weaknesses. The most careful accounts hold that both contributed without treating the result as inevitable.

Student task: Identify one reported decision and one structural condition that might have contributed to the defeat, and explain what evidence would help determine which mattered more.

Section 67.7

The Encirclement of Isfahan: Blockade, Supplies, Failed Relief, and Political Paralysis

After Gulnabad, Mahmud advanced to the capital. His forces occupied Farahabad, the shah's pleasure palace and gardens near the city, and positioned themselves in the districts around Isfahan, including the Armenian suburb of New Julfa across the Zayandeh River, where Mahmud also established a presence. The occupation of Julfa had its own consequences for the Armenian community, discussed in Section 67.9. The Afghan force did not attempt a full-scale assault on the city, a choice that is one of the central decisions of the campaign. Isfahan's urban defenses were modest by the standards of a major fortified city, and the capital still had thousands of defenders and a large population. A direct assault would have been costly and uncertain for a force that had won a battle but was still modest in number and far from its base. A blockade, by contrast, exploited the city's dependence on outside supplies.

That dependence was considerable. Isfahan was a metropolis of several hundred thousand people by some estimates, though population figures for the city are uncertain and the textbook does not adopt one. It relied on grain and other food produced in the surrounding districts and brought by road, along with fodder for animals, fuel, and goods for its markets. When the Afghan forces controlled the approaches to the capital and occupied the surrounding villages and agricultural land, the grain supply was disrupted. The markets, the places where ordinary households obtained their food, depended on merchants and carters willing to risk the roads. As the blockade tightened, these networks failed, prices rose, and the city began to draw down whatever stores it had.

The Safavid court attempted to organize a defense and to relieve the city, but efforts were frustrated by political difficulties. Calls went out to provincial governors and tribal leaders for troops. A contingent from the Bakhtiari region under a local leader is reported to have approached the capital and to have retired without breaking the blockade, but the details are told differently in different accounts. Other forces did not arrive, were delayed, or were held by their own provincial concerns, including threats at the frontiers. Within the capital, rival officials disagreed about whether to fight, negotiate, or evacuate the royal family. The shah, who had lost his main army at Gulnabad, lacked a mobile force with which to engage the besiegers, and the sources describe a government that was slow and divided in the face of a rapidly deteriorating situation.

The relationship between the loss of military initiative and the interruption of supply networks is the key to understanding the siege. Once the field army had been destroyed, the Safavid state could no longer protect the roads and districts that fed the capital. The loss of those districts put the city on a clock: stores would be exhausted, prices would rise, and public order would erode. This in turn weakened the bargaining position of the court, since each week of siege increased the pressure to accept terms. The Afghan commander had no need to risk an assault so long as time worked in his favor, though his own supplies and the patience of his followers imposed limits of their own.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Battlefield Defeat to the Collapse of Isfahan's Food Supply

Long-term causes

A capital dependent on outside grain, a composite army with divided command, limited urban fortification, and a court whose decision-making was slow and factional.

Short-term causes

The Afghan advance through Kerman and Yazd, hasty assembly of the army, disputes among commanders, and the absence of the Georgian contingent and other reinforcements.

Immediate trigger or context

The defeat at Gulnabad in March 1722, which destroyed the main field army near the capital.

Event or transformation

Afghan forces occupied the surrounding districts, Farahabad, and New Julfa, and cut the roads that carried grain and other supplies into Isfahan.

Immediate effects

Rising prices, unequal access to food, public disorder, failed attempts at relief, and a weaker bargaining position for the court.

Long-term consequences

Famine and disease inside the city, surrender of the shah in October, and the loss of effective Safavid government in the capital.

Historical complexity: No single link in this chain was inevitable. A different Safavid decision at Gulnabad, a successful relief effort, or an Afghan assault might have produced a different result. The chain shows how military, economic, and political events reinforced one another.

Section 67.8

Famine, Disease, and the Human Catastrophe inside Besieged Isfahan

The siege of Isfahan lasted from March until October 1722, and for much of that time the people inside experienced a steady deterioration of the conditions of life. The human catastrophe is well documented, but its scale is difficult to measure and some of its most extreme episodes are hard to verify. The evidence is an unusual combination: the detailed diary kept by representatives of the Dutch East India Company, the accounts of Armenian and European residents, and later narratives. Together they allow a reconstruction of the main stages of the crisis, though not of its exact statistics.

In the early weeks of the siege, shortages were uneven. Households with stored grain and funds could still obtain food, and markets continued to operate in some districts. As weeks passed, the price of grain and bread rose, the supply of meat and other foods dwindled, and the cost of fodder and fuel climbed. The Dutch records, written by merchants attentive to prices, track the escalation in the city's markets, and their sustained attention to the cost of staple foods is part of what makes them valuable. As the blockade tightened, grain became scarce even at very high prices, and the usual ways of obtaining food, buying it, borrowing, or receiving it as wages or rations, failed for ever larger numbers of people.

Access to food was unequal. Wealthy households and officials could draw on stores, on stocks held in palaces and mansions, on the royal household's supplies, and on personal networks, and they could pay prices that most could not. Merchants with stored goods faced both opportunity and danger: some profited from scarcity, while others were pressured by the court or by the Afghan commander for money. Artisans and shopkeepers lost their customers and their income as trade collapsed, and laborers who lived on daily wages had no earnings. The poor were the first to be unable to buy bread, and the sources describe them eating whatever they could find, including animal fodder, hides, and, according to some reports, much worse. The most extreme accounts, such as stories of cannibalism, come from a range of observers and may reflect both real events and rumor. Historians treat them as indicative of the severity of the crisis, but caution that individual episodes cannot be verified.

Disease followed hunger. Weakened populations are vulnerable to epidemic illness, and the siege, with its crowding and the breakdown of normal sanitation, produced a high death toll beyond the direct effects of starvation. Burial became difficult, and the sources describe corpses in the streets and the failure of normal urban services. The ordinary functions of the city, supervision of markets, maintenance of order, removal of the dead, and care for the sick, either ceased or functioned in a greatly reduced form. Public disorder increased, and authority within the city weakened even before the shah's surrender.

Estimates of deaths vary widely, and none is reliable. Reports range from tens of thousands to figures much higher, and several sources give round numbers without any apparent method of counting. The textbook adopts none of them. The most defensible statement is that the siege killed a very large number of people, that the great majority were civilians who died of hunger and disease, and that the toll was heaviest among the poor. The Afghan force was not the only cause of death, since disease and the failure of the city's own institutions also played a part, but the blockade was the central cause.

Its dated entries allow historians to trace the stages of the blockade and the escalation of prices, and its authors, as merchants, had practical reasons to record prices and supplies accurately. It shows the siege from within the city, and it provides a check on narratives written later.

Limitations: The authors were foreign merchants with their own interests and limited mobility, and they saw the parts of the city they could reach. The diary ends on 31 August 1722, so students must not assume that every event of October appears in it. It records what the authors observed or heard, and distinguishing direct observation from hearsay requires attention to each entry. It says relatively little about the experience of the poorest residents beyond what the authors saw.

Perspective: The perspective is that of European merchants resident in the capital. Missing are the voices of Persian officials, ordinary Muslim and Armenian residents, and the Afghan leadership.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life: Food Scarcity and Household Survival in Besieged Isfahan

Note: the examples below are analytical reconstructions of patterns documented in many sources. They are not accounts of named individuals or particular households.

A household with means. A merchant family or a household of officials might begin the siege with stored grain, dried foods, and funds. Its choices concerned how long to sustain its dependents, whether to share supplies with neighbors and kin, and whether to pay the escalating prices or to wait. Such households faced the risk of demands for money by the court or by the Afghan leaders, and of theft or violence as order deteriorated, and they weighed protecting their goods against the risk of leaving them unguarded.

A household of artisans or shopkeepers. An artisan family depended on market income. As customers vanished and trade ended, it sold tools, furnishings, and clothing to buy bread, and then faced the choice of selling the means of livelihood itself. Such a family might send members to relatives in villages where food was thought to be more available, if the roads were passable, and might split to improve its chances of survival.

A household of laborers or the poor. A family living on daily wages had no reserves. Its choices narrowed quickly: reduce meals, forage for whatever could be eaten, seek charity from neighbors or religious institutions, or leave the city. Children, the elderly, and the sick were the most vulnerable, and women bore the labor of finding and preparing what food there was.

Because most written sources were produced by officials, merchants, clerics, and foreign residents, the experience of the poor must be inferred from indirect evidence, including price records, descriptions of the dying and the dead, and the later memory of survivors.

Section 67.9

Armenians, Merchants, and Communities of the Capital: Who Experienced the Siege as What

Isfahan in 1722 was not a single community. It contained Twelver Shi'i Persian-speaking townspeople, Turkic-speaking soldiers and officials attached to the court, Georgian and other ghulam families, Sunni residents, Zoroastrians on the city's margins, Jews, Hindu and other Indian merchants, European residents, and the Armenian population of New Julfa, the suburb across the Zayanda River that Shah Abbas I had founded for Armenian merchant families transplanted from Julfa on the Araxes. Each of these groups had a different relationship to the court, to the markets, and to the Afghan force outside the walls, and the siege did not fall on them equally.

New Julfa deserves particular attention because it lay outside the city proper and was exposed early. As Afghan forces occupied the districts around Isfahan in spring 1722, the Armenian suburb came under their control. Its inhabitants included some of the wealthiest merchants in the Safavid world, men whose firms had traded in silk, textiles, and other goods across a network reaching from Venice and Amsterdam to India. The suburb was a ready target for demands for money, supplies, and billeting, and the sources describe exactions imposed on its leaders. They also describe a community that tried to negotiate, to hide property, and to find ways to supply the people of the neighboring city. The balance between coercion and negotiation cannot be reconstructed precisely.

An important Armenian source is the chronicle compiled by Petros di Sarkis Gilanentz, a writer from a New Julfan merchant family who spent much of his life in Astrakhan and traveled to Gilan in 1722. It is not a straightforward eyewitness diary of Isfahan. Rather, Gilanentz gathered dated intelligence reports and correspondence, some of it from people who had seen events in the capital. Particularly important was Joseph Apislamaian, an Armenian secretary and interpreter associated with the French representative Ange de Gardane, who supplied eyewitness reports about Isfahan. The compilation was addressed to Archbishop Minas Tigranian in Astrakhan and reflected Gilanentz's own connections to Armenian political initiatives and Russian intervention. Its surviving manuscript and printed transmission also raise textual questions. The reports illuminate New Julfa's efforts at negotiation and defense and the pressures imposed on Armenian merchants, but students must distinguish Apislamaian's reported eyewitness evidence from Gilanentz's editing, hearsay, and political perspective. A report is stronger when independent sources corroborate it; none should be mistaken for the voice of the whole Armenian population.

Muslim townspeople in the city experienced the siege through the markets, the neighborhood quarters, and the mosques and shrines that provided charity and shelter. Our evidence for their experience is thinner than for the Armenians or Europeans, because there is no diary comparable to the Dutch record from a Persian resident of a quarter, and much comes from later chronicles written by or for those who survived. It is clear that quarters of the city differed in wealth and in their access to stored grain, and that neighborhood leaders, guild heads, and religious figures carried burdens of negotiation, charity, and the maintenance of order. These local institutions did not stop functioning at once; they weakened as the siege continued, and the decline of the urban structures that normally provided justice, supervision of markets, and public safety was one of the siege's deepest social effects.

Merchants, whether Armenian, Muslim, Indian, or European, were both targets and resources. Stored goods could be requisitioned or bargained away; money could buy safe passage, or protection, or neutrality. Foreign companies such as the Dutch East India Company and the English and French agents tried to protect personnel and property through negotiation, and the Dutch records show them calculating risk, observing the shortages, and considering how long they could remain. Their position was not neutral in practice, since the Afghan leadership regarded all who resided in the capital as subject to demands, but it was different from that of the poor, who had no means of leaving or buying protection.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Closing of the Siege, 1722

An Afghan commander From the Ghilzai side, the campaign had begun as a provincial revolt and had become an unexpected military success. A commander's concerns were practical: how to feed and pay a force that had been in the field for months, how to hold the rural districts, how to prevent the army from dissolving after a victory, and how to extract resources from a city whose government was collapsing. Legitimacy was a secondary concern in the short term, and there is no evidence of a settled plan to replace the dynasty.

Many faced a choice between loyalty to a discredited shah, flight toward provincial centers, and negotiation with the new power for survival. Section 67.10

Negotiation, Abdication, and Entry: Three Distinct Stages, October 1722

The end of the siege is easily compressed into the statement that Shah Sultan Husayn surrendered the capital. The sources suggest a sequence of at least three distinct acts, and treating them separately clarifies what changed and what did not. The first was the decision to negotiate and to yield; the second was the transfer of the symbols of royal authority; and the third was the entry of Mahmud and his troops into the city. Sources date these events to the days between about 21 and 25 October 1722, and they differ in detail. The chapter follows that range rather than asserting a single day for each stage.

The first stage was a negotiation conducted while the city was starving. By October, the court had few options. Relief had failed, provisions were exhausted, and the death toll was large. Sources report that the court explored proposals through intermediaries, and that various figures, among them court officials and religious leaders, advised the shah. The terms of the negotiation are reported with uncertainty. Mahmud's side demanded the submission of the shah and the transfer of authority; the court sought to preserve lives and some arrangement of rank. What the shah gained or hoped to gain from yielding cannot be fully recovered.

The second stage was the abdication. According to the principal accounts, Sultan Husayn went to Mahmud's camp at Farahabad and, in a ceremony of transfer, relinquished the royal headdress and the signs of sovereignty, acknowledging Mahmud's right to rule. The details of the ceremony vary by source and may have been shaped by later retelling. What matters is the political meaning: the reigning Safavid shah, with the surviving institutions of court, publicly yielded. This was a decisive act, because it removed the reigning monarch from the contest. It did not, however, remove the dynasty, since other members of the Safavid house survived, and one of them, Tahmasp, was outside the city.

The third stage was Mahmud's entry into Isfahan, again within the 21–25 October range. His entry brought armed Afghan forces into a famished city, and the sources report that the first period of occupation involved the requisition of resources and the establishment of Afghan authority. Contemporary accounts also note that, at least initially, Mahmud moved to take control of the royal palace and treasury, and to bring the machinery of government under his direction. The occupation did not begin as an undifferentiated sack. It also did not mean safety. The situation was fluid, and different sources emphasize different degrees of violence in these first days.

Separating these stages avoids a common error. The entry of an army into a city, the abdication of a monarch, and the acceptance of a new ruler by a population and a political class are not the same thing. Mahmud held the capital and had the abdicated shah in his custody, but a great many of the institutions of the Safavid state, its provincial governors, its military contingents, its tax networks, and its religious and legal personnel, were scattered across the realm. Whether they would accept the new situation was not yet known.

Quantitative Study

Quantitative Study: Reading Reported Sums of Money in the Sources

Sources on the siege and its aftermath report sums of money demanded, offered, or extracted. These figures are valuable for understanding bargaining, but they require careful handling. The point of this study is to practice reading such numbers.

The currency. In the Safavid monetary system used in this textbook, one toman was a unit of account equal to 10,000 dinars, and was commonly reckoned as fifty abbasis, so that one abbasi was 200 dinars. A figure of 400,000 tomans, which some accounts associate with a proposed payment in the negotiations, would therefore equal 4,000,000,000 dinars, or 20,000,000 abbasis. The scale illustrates why such sums should be read skeptically: they are far larger than what any single household or even community could readily pay in coin.

What the figures are. Sums mentioned in narrative sources are often reported by writers who were not party to the negotiation, who may have heard them as rumor, and who may have rounded or exaggerated for effect. Demands made of commercial communities, such as the Armenian merchants of New Julfa, are better understood as opening bargaining claims than as proof of amounts actually paid. The records of what was actually delivered are fragmentary.

What can be said. It is reasonable to conclude that the Afghan leadership demanded very large sums from the court and from wealthy communities, and that these demands were a major source of hardship. It is not reasonable to treat any specific sum as established fact without corroboration.

  1. Why might a bargaining demand be much larger than a sum actually paid?
  2. What kinds of source would allow a historian to check a reported sum, and why are such records rare for this period?
  3. Why does the conversion from tomans to dinars help show that a figure is implausible as a literal payment?
Section 67.11

Mahmud in Isfahan: The Limits of Occupation, 1722–1723

When Mahmud took Isfahan he held the largest city of the realm and its palaces, but he did not hold the realm. The difference between possessing a capital and exercising sovereignty is central to this chapter. In the months after October 1722 Mahmud faced several problems at once. He had to feed and pay an army that had been drawn from tribal and regional followings, to govern a population devastated by famine, to secure revenue from a country whose administration was disrupted, and to deal with the claim of Tahmasp, who was outside his reach.

Mahmud's first measures were those of a conqueror securing a base. He took control of the palace and treasury, seized stores, and sought money from the city's wealthy residents and communities. He installed Afghan commanders and soldiers in the city, and he sought to employ at least some Persian officials, since the management of taxation and the markets required people with administrative knowledge. The sources suggest that he did not have a developed administrative organization of his own, and that the need to rely on existing officials created a pattern of accommodation and suspicion. Some Safavid officials served the new ruler; others were distrusted.

The relationship between the occupiers and the population was shaped by scarcity. A city that had been starved could not readily support an additional army, and the demands of the occupying force fell on a weakened population. Reports of seizures and violence by occupying troops appear in the sources, as do reports of efforts to restore order and to bring food into the city. These accounts are not easy to weigh. Armenian, Persian, and European narratives each have their own perspectives and interests, and each tends to emphasize the experience of its own community. The most defensible summary is that the first period of Afghan rule was marked by coercion, requisition, and instability, and that conditions for much of the population remained severe even after the end of the siege.

Mahmud's position was also constrained by the geography of control. Afghan forces held Isfahan and some of the surrounding districts, and later extended their reach to other places on the plateau, but much of the country lay outside their authority. In the following chapters of this division and the next, the response of provinces will show that the fall of the capital did not bring a general submission. Some towns and regions came to terms with the occupiers; others resisted; some pursued their own local strategies; and in the north and west, other powers moved in.

One of the most contested aspects of the period is the question of Mahmud's own condition and conduct. Sources describe a ruler whose behavior became increasingly erratic and violent in the later part of his rule, and the chapter will return to this in Division V. For the early occupation, the central fact is that his authority was more military than institutional, and that he lacked many of the means by which the Safavid state had governed: a reliable bureaucracy, a stable fiscal system, a source of religious legitimacy accepted by the Twelver Shi'i establishment, and a network of provincial loyalties.

Section 67.12

Tahmasp II and the Survival of the Safavid Claim

The abdication of Sultan Husayn did not end the dynasty, because the Safavid house had other members, and one of them had left the capital before its fall. Tahmasp, a son of Sultan Husayn, escaped from Isfahan during the crisis, and the sources differ on the exact date and circumstances of his departure. He made his way northwest and, in late 1722, was proclaimed shah, taking the regnal name Tahmasp II, at Qazvin. The exact date of the proclamation and the sequence of his movements are reported inconsistently, and this textbook refers to them only in general terms.

Tahmasp's claim was significant because it preserved the idea that the Safavid state continued. The legitimacy of a dynasty rested on more than military control. Safavid kingship drew on the dynasty's descent from the line of the Sufi order founded by Shaykh Safi al-Din, on its association with Twelver Shi'ism, and on the loyalty of the Qizilbash and of the regime's other supports. By proclaiming himself shah, Tahmasp could appeal to those who still recognized this legitimacy. For provincial governors, tribal leaders, and communities that did not wish to accept Afghan rule, he offered a rallying point.

But his position was weak. He had no capital, no secure treasury, no standing army, and limited authority over provincial leaders who had their own interests. His early years were spent moving among provincial bases, seeking support, and dealing with ambitious followers. Qazvin, where he was first proclaimed, came under pressure from Afghan forces, and he was forced to move on. Sources also show that the loyalty of those who recognized him was often conditional, and that local rulers could respect his title while acting independently.

The contest between Mahmud and Tahmasp should not be reduced to a simple rivalry between a usurper and a legitimate heir. Mahmud claimed authority by conquest and by the abdication of the previous shah, and he sought to establish himself as a ruler; Tahmasp claimed it by descent and by the dynasty's continuing institutions. Neither was able to command the whole realm. The persistence of both claims, together with the claims of local rulers and foreign powers, is what made the situation a contest over sovereignty rather than a replacement of one dynasty by another. Tahmasp's survival also meant that foreign powers, notably Russia and the Ottoman Empire, could deal with him, with Mahmud, or with local rulers as suited their interests.

A further aspect of Tahmasp's position needs emphasis. The Safavid dynasty's legitimacy outlived the loss of the capital. This is a reminder that political authority in early modern Iran was not located only in a city or a person, and that it could survive in a name, a lineage, and a set of expectations. It would be tracked in later years through the political maneuvering that eventually brought new figures to prominence, a story told in the next chapter. Here it is sufficient to say that the Afghan conquest of Isfahan left unresolved the question of who had the right to rule Iran, and that the question remained open.

Division III's outcome can now be stated. The surrender of the shah, the transfer of regalia, and the entry of the conqueror were three acts, not one, and none of them delivered the whole state. The Afghan occupation brought coercion and hardship to a devastated capital while leaving the realm divided, and a Safavid claimant, however weak, kept alive the idea of a dynasty that had lost its capital but not its claim.

Section 67.13

Provincial Responses: Qazvin, Shiraz, Yazd, and Kerman

The provinces did not respond to the fall of Isfahan in a uniform way. Different cities and regions had different rulers, resources, and exposure, and they made different choices. Understanding these choices requires abandoning the picture of a country waiting passively for a new master. Governors, notables, tribal leaders, and townspeople acted according to local calculation, and their actions together determined how far Afghan authority could in fact extend.

Kerman and Yazd lay along the Afghan line of advance but did not share one political fate. Kerman endured destructive Afghan attacks before 1722, yet its defenders repeatedly held the citadel and its authority did not simply pass permanently to Mahmud's officials. Yazd, an oasis center with commercial and textile communities and an important Zoroastrian population, resisted Mahmud's approach in 1722. Its refusal showed that local political institutions, fortifications, and urban coalitions could still impose limits on an invading force even after other Safavid provinces had lost effective protection. Reports of payment, requisition, and plunder must be distinguished from evidence of durable occupation. These contrasting experiences are clearer than any simple map depicting all territory traversed by the Afghan army as conquered.

Qazvin, in the north, was a former Safavid capital and an important center on the route between the interior and the Caspian and Caucasian regions. Tahmasp's proclamation there made it a political center in late 1722. The Afghan response was to send forces to compel its submission, and the city experienced occupation and conflict. Sources report that the Afghan occupation of Qazvin produced tension and resistance among the population, and that the occupiers were driven out at some stage, though the details of the sequence differ. Qazvin shows how a provincial city could be a stage on which the contest for legitimacy was played out, and how its population could suffer the consequences.

Shiraz and the province of Fars occupied a different position. Shiraz remained an important provincial center after the fall of Isfahan and resisted an Afghan force dispatched in 1723. A prolonged siege followed, and the city fell in 1724. Its endurance is an important counterexample to any assumption that the capital's surrender produced immediate submission elsewhere. Siege conditions inflicted hardship on townspeople, and the subsequent occupation strained economic and administrative life. The sources are less continuous than the Dutch diary for Isfahan, so exact casualty totals and individual reports require caution. Fars and neighboring districts did not mount a single coordinated defense: individual communities, military leaders, and officials negotiated, resisted, or retreated according to their circumstances.

Across these regions, three kinds of response recur. Some communities negotiated and paid to preserve themselves; some resisted, often at heavy cost; and some local powerholders used the crisis to expand their own authority or to establish independence from any central power. These responses were not mutually exclusive, and the same community might shift between them as circumstances changed. This variety is the evidence for the chapter's central claim, that the collapse of the Safavid center did not produce a single new order but a fragmented political landscape.

The provincial experience also had a social dimension. Towns that were attacked or occupied suffered loss of life and property; rural areas along the routes of armies suffered from requisition and disruption of cultivation; and the flight of populations to safer areas changed local demography. Because most evidence comes from urban chroniclers and foreign observers, the experience of villagers and nomadic communities is more difficult to recover. Historians infer it from reports of abandoned villages, disrupted harvests, and the later recovery of some districts.

Section 67.14

Shirvan, Daghestan, and the Caucasus: Local Revolt before Foreign Intervention

While the Afghan advance developed in the southeast, the Safavid northwestern frontier was also in crisis, and the two sets of events were largely independent. The Caucasus and its Caspian littoral were a zone of mixed populations and political arrangements: Georgian kingdoms under Safavid overlordship, Armenian communities, Muslim and Christian villages, Sunni and Shi'i communities, and mountain societies in Daghestan whose relationship to the Safavid state had long been loose. Chapter 66 explained how Safavid control of this frontier had weakened. The events described here took place within that context.

The best-known episode is the sack of Shamakhi, the main city of Shirvan, in August 1721. Forces associated with Daghestani leaders, among them the Lezgin leader Hajji Dawud and Surkhay of Ghazi Ghumuq, attacked the city, and the attack resulted in the death of many of its inhabitants, including Shi'i Muslims, and in the killing and plundering of Russian and other merchants resident there. The sources describe the attack as the culmination of local grievances and of the resentment felt by Sunni communities toward a Shi'i state, though motives were mixed and included plunder. The attack mattered because it was carried out by local actors, not by outside governments, and because it was the occasion Peter I's government used to justify Russian intervention.

Safavid authority in the region was by then too weak to respond effectively. The court's attention and resources were consumed by the crisis in the east and then by the siege of Isfahan, and local governors had limited means. The Georgian ruler of Kartli, Vakhtang VI, who had been a Safavid vassal and had been drawn into Safavid military plans, found himself in an increasingly isolated position. His efforts to coordinate with outside powers, particularly Russia, would later have consequences for Georgia, but the episode belongs primarily to the regional history of the Caucasus. In this chapter it is relevant for showing that the northwestern provinces were slipping out of Safavid control before the fall of the capital.

It is important not to attribute the Caucasian crisis to a single cause. Long-term factors included the weak Safavid hold on peripheral regions, religious and communal tensions, economic pressures on highland communities, and the autonomy of local leaders. Short-term factors included the failure of Safavid defense and the opportunities that this created. Foreign powers did not cause these tensions but exploited them. The Caucasian crisis, like the Afghan one, was thus a mixture of local initiative and external opportunity, and its victims were again ordinary people, notably townspeople in places like Shamakhi who had no part in the political decisions.

Section 67.15

Peter I and the Caspian Campaign, 1722–1723

Peter I of Russia had commercial and strategic reasons to take an interest in Iran before 1722. Russian merchants traded in Safavid lands, Peter hoped to gain access to Iranian silk and to routes to India, and he had sent an embassy to Isfahan in the 1710s under Artemy Volynsky to explore these possibilities. When Shamakhi was sacked and Russian merchants were killed in 1721, and when the Safavid state was evidently disintegrating, Peter saw an opportunity. In 1722, shortly after finishing his long war with Sweden, he led a campaign down the Volga and along the western coast of the Caspian.

The Russian campaign was a calculated intervention, and Russian officials presented it in terms of protecting trade and punishing those who had attacked Russian merchants, and in terms of assisting the Safavid shah against rebels. The sources show that the Russian government pursued its own interests, particularly the acquisition of Caspian territories, and that it was willing to use the language of assistance while doing so. Russian forces entered Derbent in late August 1722 by the Russian Julian calendar (early September by the Gregorian calendar); supply difficulties and illness then curtailed the main campaign. In 1723 the Russians occupied Baku, in July, after a bombardment and a negotiated surrender, and extended their control to parts of the Caspian provinces, including Gilan, where a Russian garrison established itself at Rasht in early 1723 despite opposition by provincial authorities and residents. These footholds did not give Russia effective control of every territory it claimed.

The campaign illustrates the limits of external intervention as well as its opportunism. Russian forces faced severe logistical difficulties, including disease in the Caspian lowlands, and suffered heavy losses from illness. The Caspian coast was difficult territory, and Russian occupation depended on naval supply and on the cooperation or acquiescence of local populations. These populations did not necessarily welcome the Russians; their responses ranged from resistance to accommodation, and the sources show friction between occupiers and residents.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Treaty, Ratification, Protectorate, and Cession

Treaty and ratification. Definition: A treaty is a formal agreement between states; ratification is the formal confirmation by the competent authority that makes it binding. Historical context: Envoys could sign a text, but it did not take full effect until confirmed by the sovereign. Why it matters: The 1723 agreement between Russia and Tahmasp's envoy was signed but, as the next section shows, Tahmasp II refused to ratify it. Potential misunderstanding: A signed text is not the same as a binding treaty.

Cession. Definition: The transfer of territory from one state to another by agreement. Why it matters: A cession by a ruler with little control over the territory in question raised the question of whether he had the power to give what he did not hold.

Protectorate. Definition: A relationship in which a stronger state assumes responsibility for the defense or foreign relations of a weaker one. Why it matters: Russia presented its intervention as assistance, but the terms it sought amounted to territorial gain, not protection in a strict sense.

The diplomatic result was the agreement known as the Treaty of Saint Petersburg, dated 12 September 1723 in the Julian calendar then used in Russia, which corresponds to 23 September in the Gregorian calendar. Its terms, as described in the source window below, ceded Caspian territories to Russia in exchange for a promise of military help against the Afghans. The agreement was negotiated by an envoy sent by Tahmasp and was not ratified by Tahmasp II, who refused to accept its terms. It therefore never took effect as a binding treaty between the two states. The Russian presence in the Caspian provinces continued as a military occupation based on Russian power and not on a validated agreement.

The Ottoman Advance and the Russo-Ottoman Agreement of 1724

The Ottoman Empire had its own history of rivalry with the Safavids, and the Safavid collapse offered it a chance to recover or extend its position on the eastern frontier. Ottoman leaders also had defensive reasons to respond: the prospect of Russian expansion in the Caucasus and the Caspian threatened Ottoman interests, as did the possibility that a chaotic Iran would produce instability along the border. The Ottoman decision to advance was therefore a product of opportunity and anxiety together.

Ottoman forces moved into the Caucasus and western Iran in 1723. They occupied Tiflis, the capital of Kartli, in the summer of 1723, and then continued into Armenia and Azerbaijan, taking Erivan in 1724 after a campaign, and extending Ottoman control over further districts of the western plateau in the following years. The Ottoman advance was a military occupation of territory that had been under nominal Safavid sovereignty, and in its justification Ottoman authorities drew on religious arguments about the Safavid state, as well as on political ones. Local populations again responded in varied ways: some resisted, some negotiated, and some, including communities with grievances against Safavid rule, accepted the change.

The two advances, Russian along the Caspian and Ottoman from the west, brought Istanbul and Saint Petersburg into potential conflict, since both were claiming territory formerly under Safavid authority. In June 1724 the two empires reached an agreement, concluded at Constantinople, that divided the contested lands between them in a general way: Russia would keep its Caspian gains, the Ottomans would hold the western and Caucasian territories they had taken or might take, and the two agreed on how to treat the Safavid claimant. The agreement is an instance of two powers arranging the disposition of another state's territory without that state's participation. Its details and the way each side interpreted it are the subject of specialist study, and the chapter relies on its general outline.

The agreement did not settle Iranian affairs. It addressed the claims of the two empires against each other, not the question of who would rule Iran, and it left Afghan-held Isfahan and the contest with Tahmasp unresolved. It also did not end Ottoman operations, which continued after 1724, and later events fall outside this chapter. What the agreement shows is that, by mid-1724, three separate sets of actors, the Afghans in the center, Russia on the Caspian, and the Ottomans in the west, were each pursuing their own aims over the body of the former Safavid state, and that none of them acted in concert.

The consequences for the people of the affected regions were serious. Occupation, military operations, requisition, and the dislocation of trade affected towns and villages in the Caucasus, Azerbaijan, and the western districts. Communities that had paid taxes to the Safavid state now faced new demands from new authorities, and some people fled. The Christian communities of the Caucasus and Armenia faced particular uncertainties as the Safavid, Russian, and Ottoman powers competed, and their leaders weighed foreign protection against its costs.

Division IV's outcome can now be stated. By 1724 Safavid sovereignty had fragmented along several lines at once. Provincial rulers acted for themselves; Caucasian communities and highland leaders had broken from Safavid control before and independently of the Afghan conquest; Russia held parts of the Caspian coast under a treaty that Tahmasp II had refused to ratify; and the Ottomans occupied the west. The Afghan conquest of Isfahan was one crisis among several, and the collapse of Safavid rule was the sum of these overlapping and largely uncoordinated pressures.

Section 67.17

Mahmud's Last Years: Violence, Suspicion, and the Succession of Ashraf, 1723–1725

The later years of Mahmud's rule are described in the sources as a period of growing violence and instability, but they must be read with care. Most of the surviving narratives were written by people who suffered under the occupation or who wrote afterward for regimes hostile to the Hotaks, and they naturally emphasize cruelty. At the same time, the evidence for political killings is substantial and comes from several independent traditions, Persian, Armenian, and European. The defensible statement is that Mahmud's government turned increasingly to repression against those it regarded as threats, that the victims included Safavid officials, soldiers of the former guard, and members of the Safavid family, and that precise figures are not recoverable.

The reasons for this turn are partly explicable from the structure of his position. He held the capital but not the country. He had to satisfy an army whose loyalty depended on pay and plunder, and the resources of the occupied region were shrinking. Safavid officials and guards, whose loyalty he could not assume, were a danger, and the existence of Tahmasp outside his control made every Safavid connection suspect. In the sources, killings of former officials and guardsmen are reported from 1722 and 1723 onward, and they culminated in the killing of numerous Safavid princes in the last months of his rule, in early 1725. Contemporary and later accounts also attribute to Mahmud severe mental disturbance in this period, and some describe it as madness. Students should treat such diagnoses as the language of hostile or distant narrators, not as clinical findings, since the evidence does not permit a medical conclusion.

The violence of these years should be distinguished from the earlier hardship of the siege. The siege killed mainly by hunger; the political killings of 1723–1725 were deliberate acts by an identifiable ruling group against identifiable targets. Responsibility lay with Mahmud and those who carried out his orders, not with Afghans as a whole. Many Afghan soldiers took no part in such acts, and some residents of Isfahan, whatever their origin, sheltered others. Collective blame of an entire population for the acts of a regime is not historically supportable.

Mahmud's death came in April 1725. The circumstances are disputed. Some sources report that he died of illness after a period of derangement; others suggest he was removed or killed by his cousin and rival Ashraf, who succeeded him. The question cannot be settled with the evidence available, and the textbook records both possibilities. What is clear is that Ashraf, a son of Abd al-Aziz, took power in Isfahan in the spring of 1725. His reign, including his efforts to consolidate Afghan rule, his dealings with the Ottomans, and his eventual defeat, belongs to Chapter 68 and is not narrated here.

The change of ruler shows something about the nature of the Hotak regime. It was a dynasty in a narrow sense, resting on the family of Mir Wais, but its succession was decided by violence and rivalry within the family, and it had not developed institutions to resolve them. That weakness mirrored in some ways the problem of the Safavids themselves, whose succession practices Chapter 66 examined, and it helps to explain why Afghan rule in Isfahan did not become a stable replacement for the Safavid state.

Section 67.18

Government, Displacement, and Economic Life under Occupation

The history of the occupation involves more than rulers and armies. It includes the question of how a devastated region was governed and fed, how people moved or were moved, and how the economy of a once-wealthy region functioned under strain. Here the evidence is scattered, and the picture is built from many fragments: Dutch and other European company records, Armenian narratives, Persian chronicles, and indirect indications such as price movements and the abandonment of settlements.

Government under the Hotaks was thin. Mahmud and Ashraf relied on Afghan military commanders and on those Persian officials who could be induced to serve. Taxation continued in some form, because an army had to be paid, but it was often irregular and shaped by emergency requisition. Where the Safavid system had relied on a hierarchy of provincial governors, local officials, and tax collectors, the occupiers could not fully reconstruct it. Many districts were governed by local notables or by commanders with their own followings, and large areas remained outside Afghan authority altogether. The result was not a new centralized state but a patchwork.

Displacement was a major consequence. People left besieged and occupied cities for villages, other towns, and regions thought to be safer, and some sought refuge outside the realm. Armenians and other commercial communities moved or sent families and capital abroad when they could, while the poor had fewer options. The movement of people to and from districts changed the composition and prosperity of both. It is not possible to give reliable numbers for these movements, and the textbook offers none. The qualitative evidence nevertheless shows that the occupation and the preceding siege broke many of the connections between town and countryside and between Isfahan and its hinterland.

The economy suffered in several ways. Trade routes were interrupted by war, by the insecurity of roads, and by the occupation of ports and frontier regions. Silk, one of the great commodities of the Safavid economy, depended on production in the Caspian provinces and on networks of merchants whose communities were disrupted. Foreign companies reduced or withdrew their activity, and the decline of European commercial presence at Isfahan reflected both danger and the collapse of its market. At the same time, it would be wrong to suggest that all economic life stopped. Local markets, agriculture, and crafts continued in many places, adapting to changed circumstances, and recovery in some districts began once the immediate violence receded. The uneven geography of damage, with some districts devastated and others less affected, is itself an important finding.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Isfahan and the Plateau Before and After 1722

What changed? The capital passed from a Safavid shah to an Afghan ruler; the royal court, with its patronage of artisans, merchants, and religious institutions, was disrupted; the city's population was reduced by famine, disease, and flight; the state's ability to collect revenue and to coordinate provincial governors broke down; and parts of the realm came under foreign occupation.

What remained continuous? Many local institutions survived, including neighborhood leadership, guilds, religious and legal personnel, and village communities. The Twelver Shi'i character of much of the population persisted. Agriculture, local markets, and kinship networks continued. The Safavid dynasty's claim to legitimacy survived in the person of Tahmasp II.

When did change occur? The sharpest ruptures came in 1722, with the defeat at Gulnabad, the siege, and the surrender, but the process of fragmentation had begun earlier in the Caucasus and on the frontier and continued through 1724 and beyond.

Was change gradual, uneven, abrupt, contested, or cyclical? It was uneven and contested. For people in Isfahan the change was abrupt and catastrophic; for villages in distant provinces it came more slowly or in different forms.

Who experienced change most directly? The residents of Isfahan and New Julfa, the officials and soldiers of the old regime, and the inhabitants of towns on the routes of armies.

Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural communities distant from the armies, and local institutions that could adapt to whoever held power.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the collapse of a central government is not the same as the disappearance of a society. Many structures persisted beneath the political rupture, and they formed the ground on which later rulers would rebuild.

Questions for students:

  1. Which of the changes described could be reversed once fighting ended, and which could not?
  2. Why did the experience of change differ between the capital and distant villages?
  3. How does the survival of local institutions complicate the description of 1722 as the end of an era?
Section 67.19

Why Safavid Rule Collapsed: Structure, Contingency, and Interpretation

The question of why Safavid rule collapsed has produced a long historiographical tradition. Early accounts, both Persian and European, tended to emphasize the personal failings of Shah Sultan Husayn, his devotion to religious observance and the pleasures of the court, and the influence of eunuchs, courtiers, and clerics. These accounts contained real evidence of poor decisions, but they also reflected the moral concerns of the writers and, in the case of later Persian histories, the interests of regimes that succeeded the Safavids. Modern scholarship has moved toward explanations that give weight to institutions, finance, military organization, and the pressures of the frontier, while still debating how far the collapse was determined by long-term weaknesses and how far by contingent events.

A useful way to organize the problem is to separate several different things that are often run together. The loss of the capital was a military and logistical event. The surrender of the monarch was a political and ceremonial act. The collapse of effective central authority was a process involving the breakdown of administration, finance, and command. The survival of the dynasty's claim was a matter of legitimacy. These four things occurred at different times and for different reasons, and an explanation adequate to one does not necessarily explain the others. The defeat at Gulnabad and the blockade explain the loss of the capital; the court's paralysis and the starvation of the city explain the surrender; the earlier weakening of the army, the revenue base, and provincial control explain the breakdown of authority; and the dynasty's religious and genealogical prestige explains why its claim outlived its capital.

The causes of the collapse, set out in Chapter 66, included succession practices that had produced shahs without experience of government, a system of finance that depended on resources the state could not easily mobilize, a military organization in which the old Qizilbash and the newer corps of ghulams no longer provided a ready fighting force, and a frontier in which Safavid authority rested on the cooperation of local rulers. To these long-term conditions the events of 1709–1722 added contingent elements: a successful revolt at Kandahar led by an able local leader, the failure of the expeditions sent to suppress it, the energy and luck of the Afghan commander, the decisions made on the field of Gulnabad, and the court's inability to relieve the city. Foreign powers then acted on the weakness they found. None of these causes alone suffices, and removing any one of them might have altered the outcome, though not necessarily in a way that would have saved the system.

It would be a mistake to treat the collapse as inevitable. The Safavid state in 1721 still held a large territory, a major capital, and substantial resources. Alternative paths are conceivable, including a more effective defense, earlier reinforcement from the provinces, or a different decision about where to fight. At the same time, it would be equally mistaken to treat the collapse as merely accidental. The weaknesses that made rapid failure possible were real, and they explain why a campaign by a relatively modest force could produce such an outcome. Historians therefore speak of structural vulnerability combined with contingent events.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was the Collapse of Safavid Rule the Result of Long-Term Decline or of Contingent Events?

The question: Did the Safavid state fall in 1722 because it had been in long decline, or because a specific combination of events and decisions overwhelmed a state that remained capable of surviving?

Interpretation A — Long-term decline: Some scholars have argued that the Safavid state had been weakened over decades by fiscal problems, the decay of its military institutions, the isolation of the shah from government, and the growing autonomy of peripheral regions. In this view, the Afghan campaign exposed rather than caused the collapse.
Interpretation B — Contingency and crisis: Other scholars have questioned the language of decline, pointing out that the state in the early 1700s was still wealthy and capable, that its weaknesses were not unusual among early modern states, and that its fall depended on specific events: the course of the Kandahar revolt, military decisions, and the failure of relief at Isfahan. In this view, a decline narrative reads the outcome back into the earlier period.

Evidence: Both sides use Persian and European narratives, administrative and company records, and later chronicles. Evidence for the condition of the state before 1709 is more difficult to interpret than evidence for the siege, because it is more scattered and because many sources were written after the fall, by writers who knew the outcome.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and causation: how to weigh structural conditions against contingent events, and whether the evidence of earlier weakness was visible at the time or appears significant only in hindsight. Chronology is not the issue.

Why it matters: If the collapse was the product of long-term decline, the state's end appears in some sense predictable; if it was contingent, other outcomes were possible, and the history becomes one of choices and chance as well as structure. The answer also affects how historians assess the Safavid period as a whole and its legacy.

Student task: Choose one piece of evidence that supports the long-term view and one that supports the contingent view, and explain why a writer who knew the outcome might have emphasized one over the other.

Section 67.20

A Rupture, Not a Replacement: Synthesis and the Road to Chapter 68

The preceding sections allow the chapter's governing argument to be stated directly. The events of 1722 were a rupture in the history of the Safavid state, but they did not produce a simple replacement of one dynasty by another. The fall of Isfahan transferred a capital and a palace; it did not transfer the realm. The surrender of the shah removed a monarch; it did not end the dynasty. The Afghan occupation created a regime; it did not create a state with the institutions needed to govern the plateau. Meanwhile, Russia and the Ottoman Empire occupied large frontier regions, and provincial and local actors pursued their own strategies. The result was a prolonged struggle over sovereignty, in which several claimants and powers contended without any one of them commanding the whole.

Several lessons follow for the way students should read this history. The first is that a capital can fall without a state being conquered; the distinction between possessing the center and controlling the periphery is crucial. The second is that the experience of collapse differed greatly by place and social position. The inhabitants of Isfahan suffered starvation and occupation; Armenian merchants faced exactions and uncertainty; villagers along the routes of armies faced requisition; and populations on the frontiers faced foreign occupation. The third is that the actors beyond the Afghans, including Tahmasp, provincial rulers, Caucasian leaders, Russia, and the Ottomans, were independent agents whose motives and timing were their own, not merely reactions to the Afghan conquest.

The evidence for this history is uneven, and students should keep its limits in mind. The Dutch siege diary and the Armenian and Persian narratives give a vivid picture of the capital but cover little of the provinces. Reported numbers, whether of armies, of the dead, or of sums of money, are frequently unreliable. Disputed matters include the date of Gulnabad, the details of the surrender, the circumstances of Mahmud's death, and the numbers killed in the political violence of the occupation. A responsible account states what is firmly known, what is probable, and what is uncertain.

The next chapter takes up the story from this point. The situation at the end of Chapter 67 is unresolved: an Afghan ruler held Isfahan, a Safavid claimant continued to assert the throne, Russian and Ottoman forces held parts of the former realm, and the question of who would rule Iran remained open. Chapter 68 traces how that question was answered, through the emergence of new military leadership and the later regimes that followed, and does not need to be anticipated here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter traced the collapse of effective Safavid rule between the revolt at Kandahar in 1709 and the death of Mahmud Hotak in 1725. Its central argument is that the Afghan conquest of Isfahan in 1722 was a rupture that became a prolonged struggle over sovereignty. The loss of the capital, the surrender of the monarch, the breakdown of central authority, and the survival of the dynastic claim were four separate things, and they occurred at different times and for different reasons.

The revolt began in the Kandahar region, where Ghilzai Pashtun leaders under Mir Wais Hotak resisted Safavid provincial rule. The Safavid expeditions sent to suppress it failed, and the revolt consolidated into a regional power. The Afghan groupings were not unified: the Ghilzai and the Abdali were distinct and often rivals, and students must distinguish them. Mahmud Hotak, who succeeded Mir Wais's immediate successors, turned from defending Kandahar to invading Iran.

The Afghan advance across Kerman and Yazd toward Isfahan culminated in the battle of Gulnabad in March 1722. The date is given as 8 or 9 March in different sources, and the sizes of the armies are unreliably reported. The Safavid defeat resulted from a combination of immediate decisions and structural weaknesses, a question historians continue to debate.

After Gulnabad, the siege and blockade of Isfahan from March to October 1722 produced a human catastrophe. The Dutch East India Company's diary, together with Armenian and Persian narratives, documents the escalation of prices, the spread of hunger and disease, and the paralysis of the court. Estimates of the dead vary widely and none is reliable, and accounts of extreme suffering require critical reading.

The end of the siege consisted of three distinct acts in the days between about 21 and 25 October 1722: negotiation, the abdication of Sultan Husayn and transfer of the signs of kingship, and Mahmud's entry into the city. Mahmud held the capital but not the realm, and his authority was more military than institutional. The urban populations of Isfahan, including Muslim townspeople, the Armenians of New Julfa, merchants, and foreign residents, experienced the siege and occupation in different ways.

The Safavid claim survived in Tahmasp II, who escaped the capital and was proclaimed shah in the northwest in late 1722. He lacked a capital, a treasury, and an army, but the legitimacy of the dynasty outlived the loss of Isfahan, and he provided a focus for those who did not accept Afghan rule.

Provincial responses varied widely. Kerman, Yazd, Qazvin, and Shiraz each reacted in ways shaped by local conditions, with negotiation, resistance, and independent local initiative all in evidence. In the Caucasus, local revolts, including the sack of Shamakhi in August 1721, preceded and were independent of the Afghan conquest.

Russia and the Ottoman Empire acted from their own interests. Peter I's Caspian campaign took Derbent in 1722 and Baku in 1723, and the Treaty of Saint Petersburg of September 1723 was never ratified by Tahmasp II. The Ottomans advanced into the Caucasus and western Iran, and in June 1724 Constantinople and Saint Petersburg reached an agreement dividing their claims.

Mahmud's later years were marked by repression and the killing of Safavid officials and princes, and he died in April 1725 in circumstances that remain disputed. Ashraf succeeded him, and his reign belongs to the next chapter. Government under the Hotaks was thin, displacement and economic dislocation were severe, but local institutions persisted beneath the political rupture.

The debate over whether the collapse reflected long-term decline or contingent events remains open. The best reading combines structural vulnerability with specific contingencies, and treats neither as sufficient alone. The chapter ends with Iran's sovereignty fragmented among several claimants and powers, a situation that Chapter 68 takes up.

Persia in Crisis: Safavid Decline and the Fall of Isfahan (2012). A modern reassessment of the Safavid collapse, its military context, and the problem of decline.
Laurence Lockhart
The Fall of the Safavi Dynasty and the Afghan Occupation of Persia (Cambridge, 1958). A major narrative reconstruction; evaluate its older interpretive framework critically.
Willem M. Floor, compiler, editor, and translator
The Afghan Occupation of Safavid Persia, 1721–1729 (Paris, 1998). Includes the translated Dutch VOC diary of the siege of Isfahan (1 March–31 August 1722) and related archival documents.
Petros di Sarkis Gilanentz; Caro Owen Minasian, translator
The Chronicle of Petros di Sarkis Gilanentz Concerning the Afghan Invasion of Persia in 1722, the Siege of Isfahan, and the Repercussions in Northern Persia, Russia, and Turkey (Lisbon, 1959), with an introduction and additional notes by Laurence Lockhart. A compilation of correspondence, not a single eyewitness diary.
Judasz Tadeusz Krusiński
The History of the Revolutions of Persia, 2 vols. (London, 1728). A near-contemporary European narrative; consult the specific edition before quoting wording.
Encyclopaedia Iranica
Scholarly entries on Shah Soltan Hosayn, the Gilanentz chronicle, Kerman in the Safavid period, the Safavid dynasty, and Russo-Iranian relations. Use these to cross-check dating and to trace the primary materials cited by specialists.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can be broken by a combination of structural weakness and specific events, and how the consequences unfold unevenly. The fall of Isfahan was a decisive moment, but it was not the end of the story. What followed was a long contest over who held authority, fought out by Afghans, Safavid claimants, provincial rulers, and foreign empires.

The chapter also reveals that political collapse is experienced differently by different people. For the starving residents of Isfahan, the exposed merchants of New Julfa, the villagers along the armies' routes, and the Caucasian townspeople, the same crisis meant different things. A textbook that followed only rulers would miss this.

The events described shaped what came later. The weakness of the Hotak regime, the survival of the Safavid claim, and the presence of Russian and Ottoman forces defined the problems that later leaders would face. Chapter 68 takes up the question of how Iran's sovereignty was reconstituted.

The chapter also contributes a habit of mind: distinguishing the fall of a capital from the end of a state, and a reported number from an established fact. These habits apply well beyond this period.
