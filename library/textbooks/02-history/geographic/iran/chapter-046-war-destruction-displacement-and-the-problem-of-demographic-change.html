War, Destruction, Displacement, and the Problem of Demographic Change

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Mongol conquest of the Iranian world left devastation that contemporaries struggled to describe, yet the numbers they used to describe it cannot be treated as a census. This chapter holds both facts together: the violence was real and, in some places, near-total; the demographic arithmetic of that violence is largely beyond recovery.

Around 1219, according to his own geographical writing and later biographical accounts, the scholar and geographer Yaqut al-Hamawi left Merv amid growing fear of the Mongol advance; he subsequently fled Khwarazm as the invasion spread. Born in Byzantine lands, brought up as a slave and educated in Baghdad, Yaqut had worked in the libraries of Merv, and in the years before his death in 1229 he compiled a vast geographical dictionary in which he described what he had seen there. His account of the city's collections of books is among the most valued testimonies to Merv's intellectual life before 1221. It is also, necessarily, an account written from exile. Yaqut survived because he left; the city he remembered was, within months of his departure, a place of mass killing and ruin. His career already displays the three themes of this chapter: the destruction of settled communities, the flight of the people who could escape, and the dependence of historians on witnesses who were frequently elsewhere.

Chapter 45 explained how the Mongol conquest unfolded, from the destruction of the Khwarazmian state through the campaigns of Chinggis Khan and his commanders to the establishment of occupation. This chapter asks a different question: what did that conquest do to the people, settlements, farms, markets, and families of the Iranian world, and how much can historians actually establish about it? The governing problem is easy to state and difficult to answer. Contemporary chronicles describe massacre, abandoned towns, ruined fields, and scattered refugees, and these descriptions are corroborated in their general outlines by archaeology and by later administrative texts. Yet the chronicles' casualty totals, which for individual cities run into figures that rival the population of whole kingdoms, cannot be verified and in most cases should not be believed as counts.

The chapter therefore argues two propositions at once. The first is that the invasions inflicted profound destruction, especially in Khurasan and the neighboring lands of Transoxiana and Khwarazm, where cities fell after sieges, populations were killed, enslaved, or driven out, and the networks of agriculture and trade that sustained urban life were badly disrupted. The second is that the scale of population loss cannot be calculated from medieval casualty claims, and that consequences varied sharply from region to region. Historians must distinguish deaths from flight, flight from captivity, captivity from the relocation of settlements, and all of these from the effects of later taxation, climate, and political change. Rejecting an impossible number is not the same as rejecting a massacre, and recognizing a massacre does not authorize quoting an impossible number.

The chapter also resists the popular tendency to attribute every later difficulty in the region to a single moment. Not all violence in this period was Mongol violence: the Khwarazmian ruler's own forces, the campaigns of his son Jalal al-Din in Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, and local warfare all added to the burdens on the population. Not all agricultural decline in the region was caused by war: long-term environmental change, discussed through the Otrar oasis in Section 46.14, may explain some abandoned irrigation systems better than any episode of conquest. And not every community suffered alike: some regions of western and southern Iran negotiated submission and avoided the heaviest destruction, while others were attacked repeatedly. The chapter's regional case studies are meant to show why a single story of devastation distorts the evidence.

The evidence is correspondingly varied. The chapter draws on Arabic and Persian chronicles, among them the works of Ibn al-Athir, al-Nasawi, Juvayni, and Juzjani; on later local histories such as the history of Herat by Sayf al-Harawi; on geographical writing and administrative literature; on archaeological surveys and geoarchaeological studies; and on the few surviving figures that record enumerations of people or revenues. Each class of source illuminates part of the problem and conceals another part. A chronicler writing in Mosul, a secretary serving the Mongol court, a refugee historian in Delhi, and an archaeologist measuring abandoned canals each answer different questions, and the chapter's method is to ask what each can and cannot tell us.

Following the commitment made in Chapter 1, the chapter treats the regions it examines as historical rather than modern spaces. Merv lies today in Turkmenistan, Herat and Balkh in Afghanistan, Bukhara, Samarkand, and Gurganj (Kunya-Urgench) in Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan, and the Caucasian borderlands in several modern states, yet all belonged to the Iranian and Persianate world of the early thirteenth century. The chapter is the principal social-history and demographic-methodology chapter of the early Mongol period. It does not retell the campaigns (Chapter 45), analyze Mongol government (Chapter 47), examine the reforms of Ghazan Khan (Chapter 48), or trace the later recovery of the Ilkhanate in depth (Chapter 50). Its task is to establish the magnitude, the mechanisms, and the evidentiary limits of disruption, and to prepare the ground for those later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why no reliable empire-wide census exists for Iran before or after the Mongol invasions, and what this means for any claim about population loss.
  2. Analyze the perspectives, literary conventions, and transmission histories of the principal chronicles of the Mongol conquest, including their casualty figures.
  3. Distinguish direct killing, indirect mortality, flight, captivity, forced relocation, and settlement abandonment as separate processes that cannot be added together or treated as interchangeable.
  4. Compare the experiences of Merv, Nishapur, Herat, Balkh, the cities of Transoxiana and Khwarazm, and the regions of western and southern Iran, explaining the sources of regional variation.
  5. Trace the movement of refugees, captives, skilled workers, and pastoral groups, and assess the effects of this mobility on sending and receiving communities.
  6. Assess what the sources permit historians to say about women, children, households, and religious communities under conquest, and what they conceal.
  7. Evaluate the claim that the Mongols destroyed the irrigation systems of Iran and Central Asia, using archaeological and environmental evidence, including the Otrar oasis, as a comparative case.
  8. Explain how requisitions, tribute, and overlapping levies prolonged the economic costs of conquest after armies had moved on.
  9. Interpret the evidence for the recovery of Herat and explain why a surviving population figure cannot be converted into a percentage of loss.
  10. Evaluate competing explanations for long-term agricultural and demographic decline, avoiding monocausal answers.
  11. Connect the social and demographic consequences of conquest to the formation of Mongol government examined in Chapter 47.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How should historians interpret a medieval author's claim that a million or more people died in a single city?
  2. What is the difference between demonstrating that a massacre occurred and demonstrating how many people it killed?
  3. Why did the consequences of the Mongol invasions differ so sharply between Khurasan, western Iran, Fars, and Kirman?
  4. How did displacement, captivity, and the forced movement of skilled workers change communities that were not directly destroyed?
  5. What can archaeology and environmental science tell us about irrigation and abandonment that written narratives cannot?
  6. In what ways did the economic burdens of occupation continue after the main campaigns had ended?
  7. How do we know what happened to women, children, and ordinary households when the chroniclers rarely wrote about them?
  8. Why is a surviving enumeration of a city's inhabitants valuable even when it cannot measure the scale of loss?
  9. How can a chapter on catastrophe avoid both minimizing violence and repeating unverifiable statistics?

Historical Context

By the early thirteenth century the Iranian world was an assemblage of regions bound together by trade, religion, language, and the shifting authority of several states. Chapter 44 described the Khwarazmian empire, whose rulers had assembled a polity extending across Khwarazm, Transoxiana, Khurasan, and much of western and central Iran, and whose military power rested on Turkic troops with a fragile relationship to the Persian-speaking, largely urban and agricultural population they governed. Other powers occupied the margins: the Abbasid caliphate in Iraq, the Ismaili strongholds of the Alborz and Quhistan, local dynasties such as the Atabegs of Fars, and rulers in Kirman, Azerbaijan, and the Caucasus whose submission to the Khwarazmshah was sometimes real and sometimes nominal. The period before 1219 was not one of peaceful stability. Rival states had fought over Khurasan, and Khwarazmian expansion had provoked resentments that Chapters 44 and 45 discuss.

The population of this world lived in three overlapping settings. In the great oasis cities, among them Merv on the Murghab river, Nishapur and Herat in districts watered by rivers and underground channels, Bukhara and Samarkand in the Zarafshan valley, and Gurganj in the delta of the Amu Darya, large urban communities depended on irrigated agriculture in their hinterlands. In rain-fed and mountain districts, villages practiced mixed farming and herding at lower densities and with less dependence on elaborate waterworks. In steppe margins, mountains, and desert edges, pastoral groups, among them Turkmen, Kurds, Lurs, and others, moved seasonally with their herds and traded with settled communities. As Chapter 1 explained, scarce and unevenly distributed water concentrated population into a discontinuous network of settled zones, a pattern that made the survival of irrigation infrastructure a central condition of urban life.

This was also a world already familiar with catastrophe. The sack of Merv and Nishapur by Oghuz Turkic forces in the middle of the twelfth century, the damage inflicted by earthquakes in several Khurasanian centers, and the wars between rival dynasties had all disrupted cities before 1219. These earlier shocks matter for two reasons. They remind historians that the Mongol invasions did not strike a society at a uniform peak of prosperity, and they warn against comparing a devastated post-invasion city with an imagined pre-invasion maximum. Medieval geographers described the great cities of Khurasan in admiring terms, but these descriptions are impressionistic and rarely give quantitative information on population.

The chapter's central evidentiary fact must be stated plainly at the outset: no reliable empire-wide census of the Iranian world survives for the period before the Mongol invasions, and none survives for the immediate aftermath. Tax assessments existed, but comprehensive registers have not been preserved, and the figures that later authors report cannot be tied to consistent geographic or administrative units. Population estimates for the medieval Iranian world, whether made by modern historians or by earlier scholars, rest on indirect inference and vary widely. Students should treat any precise number for the population of Iran before or after the invasions as an estimate whose uncertainty is large, and the chapter will not present one as established.

Section 46.1

Iranian Society before the Mongol Invasions: Population, Cities, and Rural Landscapes

Any assessment of loss requires a baseline, and the baseline for the Iranian world in 1219 is poorly known. The region contained some of the largest cities of the medieval Islamic world. Merv, Nishapur, Herat, and Balkh in Khurasan, Bukhara and Samarkand in Transoxiana, Gurganj in Khwarazm, and Isfahan, Rayy, Hamadan, and Tabriz in the west were centers of scholarship, crafts, and commerce, and medieval geographers described them with admiration. But description is not enumeration. The geographers of the tenth century, such as al-Istakhri and al-Muqaddasi, wrote about districts, products, and routes. Their works help locate cities and identify economic specializations; they do not supply population totals, and the later chroniclers who wrote about destruction did not either, at least not reliably.

The countryside around the great cities was as important as the cities themselves. In the Murghab oasis of Merv, in the valley of the Hari Rud around Herat, in the Zarafshan valley, and in the Amu Darya delta of Khwarazm, rivers were diverted through networks of canals that watered fields, orchards, and gardens. In many other districts of the plateau, groundwater was brought to the surface by the underground channels known as qanats, discussed in Section 46.13. These systems supported dense settlement but demanded continuous labor and organization. A canal silted up if it was not cleaned, and a qanat collapsed if its shafts were not maintained. The prosperity of a city therefore depended on the labor and obedience of the villages that fed it, and on a political order that could mobilize and protect that labor.

Not all of the population lived in or around irrigated oases. Highland villages practiced rain-fed agriculture and terrace farming, and substantial pastoral populations moved between summer and winter pastures. Turkmen and other Turkic groups, Kurds, Lurs, and others raised sheep and goats and exchanged animal products for grain and manufactured goods. These populations were less exposed than city dwellers to siege warfare, and they could sometimes move out of the path of armies, but they were not insulated from war: armies requisitioned animals, and pastoral groups were themselves drawn into military service and displacement, as Section 46.12 shows. The Iranian world's population was thus heterogeneous in settlement, livelihood, and exposure to violence from the start, and this heterogeneity is a precondition for understanding why the consequences of the invasions differed from place to place.

The evidence for population before 1219 is correspondingly thin. Modern historians have proposed estimates for the medieval Iranian world, but these rest on assumptions about urban area, household size, and agricultural yield, and they diverge widely. Archaeological surveys can establish the extent of settled areas and the number of sites occupied in particular periods, and in a few regions, such as the Murghab delta around Merv, such work has produced valuable evidence for the changing scale of settlement. These surveys are partial in coverage, and converting site counts into population figures is an exercise of inference rather than measurement. Students should therefore approach statements about the pre-invasion population as plausible orders of magnitude at best, never as figures against which post-invasion losses can be calculated precisely.

Historical Vocabulary
Demography and Depopulation

Definition: Demography is the study of the size, structure, and movement of populations, including births, deaths, and migration. Depopulation describes a reduction in the number of inhabitants of a place or region, regardless of how that reduction occurred.

Historical context: A town that has fewer inhabitants after a conquest may have lost people to killing, to starvation and disease, to flight, to enslavement, or to forced relocation, and it may later regain people through return or resettlement. Medieval chroniclers rarely distinguished these processes, and a single phrase such as "the city was emptied" may refer to several of them.

Why it matters: This chapter treats direct deaths, indirect deaths, displacement, and long-term population decline as separate categories. They cannot be added together into a single total, and a decline in the number of residents of a place is not evidence that all missing residents died.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "depopulation" means "mass death." A place may be depopulated through flight and resettlement elsewhere while the regional population remains largely intact.

Section 46.2

The Sources of Destruction: Chronicles, Numbers, and Historical Memory

Our knowledge of the Mongol conquest of the Iranian world comes overwhelmingly from narrative chronicles, and the first task of any student is to learn who wrote them and under what conditions. Ibn al-Athir (1160–1233), writing in Arabic in Mosul in northern Iraq, completed his universal chronicle al-Kamil fi'l-ta'rikh while the invasions were still within living memory. He was beyond the zone of destruction and depended on refugees, merchants, and correspondents who brought news westward. His account is early and emotionally charged, but it is a distant account: his information about Khurasan and Transoxiana was second-hand, and he shared the moral and political perspective of a Sunni Muslim scholar in the Abbasid world, for whom the invasions were an incomparable disaster for Islam.

Al-Nasawi, secretary to the Khwarazmian ruler Jalal al-Din, wrote a biography of his master in Arabic that records the last years of Khwarazmian resistance from the viewpoint of a participant. His testimony is valuable for events in western Iran, Azerbaijan, and the Caucasus in the 1220s, and it is important for showing Khwarazmian conduct toward the population, but it is a court biography written to commemorate its subject. Minhaj-i Siraj Juzjani, born in 1193 and trained in the Ghurid milieu of what is now Afghanistan, fled to India and wrote his Tabaqat-i Nasiri in Persian at Delhi, completing it around 1260. He knew the region before the invasions and spoke with survivors, but he too wrote at a distance in time and space and with partisan interests. Ata-Malik Juvayni (1226–1283), the author of the Persian Tarikh-i Jahangushay, was born after the main campaigns and wrote in the 1250s as a servant of the Mongol administration. His narrative, treated in Source Window II, is the fullest account of the conquest and of early Mongol government, and it is also the product of a man who had accepted, and was part of, the political order that followed.

Other sources add perspectives. Armenian and Georgian chroniclers, including Kirakos Gandzaketsi for Armenia, describe the Caucasian campaigns from the standpoint of Christian communities. The later universal history of Rashid al-Din, written under the Ilkhans and examined in Chapter 49, draws on Mongol traditions unavailable to earlier authors. Local histories such as Sayf al-Harawi's history of Herat preserve memories at a longer remove. Chinese and Mongol sources illuminate the conduct of the campaigns from the conquerors' side but say little about their local effects in Iran. No single source can be treated as neutral, and the historian's method is to compare them, ask how each author learned what he reports, and identify claims that are repeated because authors copied one another rather than because they were independently confirmed.

These authors' reports of killing frequently take numerical form, and some of the figures are staggering. For individual cities such as Merv, Nishapur, and Herat, the sources offer totals ranging from several hundred thousand to well over a million, with variation between authors and even between manuscripts of the same work. Historians have long recognized that these numbers cannot be literal counts. Medieval writers often used large round numbers to convey magnitude, to mark an event as exceptional, or to underline its moral meaning as divine punishment. Manuscript copyists introduced errors, particularly in numerals, and the same formulas appear in descriptions of different cities. A claim that a city's whole population was killed is also difficult to reconcile with the same authors' reports of survivors who later reappear in the narrative. The figures therefore tell historians how contemporaries perceived the scale of the disaster, a perception that is itself historical evidence, but they do not tell us how many people died.

Primary Source Window I

Ibn al-Athir, al-Kamil fi'l-ta'rikh (The Complete History), on the Mongol Invasions

Creator: Ibn al-Athir (1160–1233), historian of Mosul · Date: annals for the years of the invasion written around 1230, shortly before the author's death · Type: Arabic universal chronicle, organized year by year

Context: Ibn al-Athir wrote in northern Iraq, beyond the area of the first devastation but alarmed by the possibility that the invaders would advance further west. His chronicle reached the years of the Mongol invasions as news arrived from Khurasan, Transoxiana, and Iran through refugees and travelers. Because I have not checked a specific published translation for this edition, the passage below is an editorial paraphrase of the content of his introduction to the events, not a quotation.

Paraphrase: Ibn al-Athir states that he long hesitated to record the Mongol invasions because of the pain of describing them. He presents the destruction as an unparalleled calamity for the Muslim world, describes the invaders as moving from city to city and leaving ruin behind them, and frames the events as a catastrophe whose scale he finds difficult to convey.

Analysis: The passage shows how a contemporary scholar understood the meaning of the invasions: as a moral and religious catastrophe rather than merely a political reversal. His evident distress is itself evidence of the shock with which the Muslim world received the news. His account also shows how information about distant events reached Mosul, through refugees and rumor, and how that process could magnify or distort the reports of casualties.

Limitations: The author was not an eyewitness to the destruction of the cities of Khurasan and Transoxiana, and his figures cannot be independently verified. His grief and his theological framing shape how he selects and presents information. His chronicle cannot establish how many people died in a particular city, how quickly any city recovered, or what the experience of ordinary survivors was.

Perspective: The account represents an educated, Arabic-writing Sunni Muslim observer in a city outside the zone of conquest. It does not represent the Persian-speaking townspeople who experienced the sieges, the rural populations of the oases, or the Mongol and Turkic soldiers who conducted them.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say, show, or record about how the author understood the invasions?
  2. Who created it, and how did the author obtain his information?
  3. What purposes may have shaped how he framed and organized the account?
  4. What can historians learn from it about contemporary reactions to the invasions?
  5. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Quantitative Evidence Study

How to Evaluate a Medieval Casualty Claim without Treating It as a Census

Suppose a chronicle states that a city lost a number of people greater than any plausible population of the walled city. The following steps show how historians reason about such a claim. The steps contain no invented data; they are a method.

  1. Identify the author and his access. Was the author present, did he interview survivors, or did he copy an earlier writer? A number repeated by several authors may be a single claim transmitted several times.
  2. Identify the literary function. Does the number serve a rhetorical or moral purpose, such as marking an event as unparalleled or as divine judgment? Is it a round number?
  3. Check manuscript variation. If different manuscripts of the same work give different totals, the figure is unstable, and the variation itself is evidence for copying error or revision.
  4. Test physical plausibility. Compare the figure with what archaeology suggests about the size of the city and with what is logistically possible in the time the narrative allows, including the number of attackers available and the time needed.
  5. Compare with other evidence. Do later sources describe survivors, resettlement, or continued institutions in the same place? Do archaeological findings show continued occupation?
  6. Separate the categories. Even a credible casualty figure measures only deaths in one episode; it does not measure flight, captivity, or the change in a city's population over decades.
  7. State the result honestly. A defensible conclusion might be: the sources describe a massacre of very large proportions; the stated total cannot be verified and probably exaggerates; the loss to the city was severe.

Lesson: A large number in a chronicle is a signal about how contemporaries perceived magnitude. It is not an independently defensible population estimate, and rejecting the number does not mean rejecting the event.

Section 46.3

Sieges, Mass Killing, Reprisals, and the Destruction of Urban Communities

Chapter 45 narrates how Mongol armies took cities; this section examines what happened to urban communities once they did. The sources describe a range of outcomes rather than a single policy. Cities that submitted before assault were often spared mass killing, although they were required to hand over wealth, provisions, labor, and often skilled people, and to accept a garrison or a governor. Cities that resisted and were taken by storm commonly suffered massacre, plunder, and the destruction of fortifications and often of much else. Cities that had submitted and then rebelled, or that had killed Mongol envoys or commanders, were treated most severely, because reprisal was understood by the conquerors as a response to betrayal. The fate of Herat, which surrendered in 1221, rebelled, and was destroyed after a second siege in 1222, illustrates this pattern.

The procedure the narratives describe after a city fell was systematic. Inhabitants were commonly driven outside the walls and sorted: artisans and other people with useful skills were set aside for service or deportation, young people and women might be taken as captives, and the rest were often killed. In the sieges themselves, captured civilians were sometimes forced to labor in attacks, filling ditches or building siege works, under the levy the sources call hashar, a practice that turned noncombatants into instruments and casualties of the assault. After the killing, buildings might be burned or torn down, walls leveled, and in some cases the site was combed for survivors who had hidden in cellars, wells, or underground passages. The sources also report the deliberate use of terror, in which the destruction of one city encouraged others to submit.

Historians approach these narratives with a distinction between event and elaboration. That mass killing occurred at Merv, Nishapur, Herat, Gurganj, and other cities is established by multiple independent lines of testimony, by the later literary memory of the cities, and in some cases by archaeological signs of destruction. The more lurid elements, such as orders to kill every living thing, towers of heads, or precise body counts, often appear in several works in similar form and may be literary conventions that expressed horror rather than reporting verified facts. This is not a reason to dismiss the testimony. It is a reason to ask of each detail how the author could have known it and what it was meant to convey.

The conquerors' practices also varied by commander, circumstance, and period. Some commanders accepted submission readily; others exacted massacre after resistance of any kind; the same army could act differently in different cities. Mongol decisions depended on strategic needs, the speed of the campaign, the presence or absence of promising captives, and reactions to specific provocations. The question of whether the Mongol approach to Iranian cities amounted to a systematic policy of extermination is distinct from the question of whether they carried out massacres. The sources support the second proposition clearly and the first only in limited and regionally variable ways, a distinction that the chapter's regional sections develop.

Primary Source Window II

Ata-Malik Juvayni, Tarikh-i Jahangushay (History of the World Conqueror), on the Destruction of Cities and Early Mongol Administration

Creator: Ata-Malik Juvayni (1226–1283), from a family of secretaries and officials who served the Khwarazmian and then the Mongol administrations · Date: composed in the 1250s, completed around 1260 · Type: Persian court and administrative history

Context: Juvayni was born after the main campaigns in Khurasan and joined the Mongol administration as a young man, accompanying Mongol officials and later serving the Ilkhan Hülegü, who made him governor of Baghdad after its capture. His history was written to record the rise of the Mongol rulers and was composed at a time when he had access to administrative information and to survivors' accounts. Because this edition has not checked a particular translation of the passages in question, the content below is an editorial paraphrase.

Paraphrase: Juvayni describes the fall of cities in terms of sieges, assaults, and mass killing, sometimes giving numerical totals of the dead, and he also records how survivors later reemerged and how Mongol-appointed administrators attempted to restore cultivation and revive towns. His narrative combines lament for the destruction with justifications of Mongol rule and praise for officials who rebuilt.

Analysis: The source is indispensable because it combines narrative of destruction with testimony about early administration and recovery. Its dual character shows that destruction and reconstruction were closely connected processes in the experience of Iranian communities, and it provides the strongest textual evidence for the measures that ended the period of open slaughter.

Limitations: Juvayni was not an eyewitness to the 1220s, depended on informants, and wrote as a servant of the regime. His casualty totals are not verified counts. His accounts of Mongol benevolence and administrative skill may reflect his position, and his silence on matters unfavorable to the Mongol court is not evidence that those matters did not occur.

Perspective: The text represents a Persian-speaking official who had adapted to Mongol rule. It does not represent the peasants, craftspeople, and women who bore the costs of war and early exactions, nor the dynastic and clerical perspectives of those who resisted.

Section 46.4

The Wider Human Cost of War: Hunger, Flight, Insecurity, and Disrupted Livelihoods

Killing in the course of assault, however horrifying, was only one way that war reduced populations. Hunger, exposure, and insecurity could continue to kill and displace people long after armies had left. Armies living off the land seized grain, animals, and fodder from the districts through which they passed, and the plundering of storehouses removed the reserves on which villages relied to survive the winter and to sow the next crop. Where farming households were killed or fled before planting or harvest, the loss of a single season's crop could cause hardship that persisted even if the fields themselves were undamaged. The sources occasionally report famine and hardship in conquered districts, though they rarely quantify them.

Disruption of infrastructure compounded these effects. Cities that lost their walls, markets, and administrative buildings lost the institutions that organized exchange, taxation, and relief. Bridges, caravanserais, and roads were damaged or fell into disuse, and trade through the affected regions became riskier and more costly. Rural households that had lost animals, tools, or draft oxen faced a long struggle to resume production. The interaction of these pressures meant that the losses from an episode of war were multiplied through time: a village that survived the passage of an army but lost its seed grain and its labor might be abandoned a year or two later, with no chronicler recording the connection.

Insecurity was also a cost in itself. Populations that had experienced massacre or seen neighboring districts destroyed might flee on rumor of an advancing force, abandoning farms and shops in advance of any attack. Some fled to mountain refuges or distant cities; some died in flight. Others delayed returning even after peace was proclaimed because they could not be sure of protection. Armed bands and the remnants of defeated armies, including Khwarazmian troops, preyed on local populations in the aftermath. The effect of insecurity on farming and commerce is extremely difficult to measure, but it helps explain why the demographic and economic consequences of a conquest could continue to unfold over years.

Historians should also exercise caution here. The sources are silent about disease for most of the 1220s, and the great epidemic of the mid-fourteenth century, the Black Death, belongs to a different period; it cannot be projected backward onto the thirteenth century. Specific epidemics may be discussed where contemporary evidence supports them, but a general assumption that war in this period was accompanied by catastrophic plague has no secure foundation in the sources. The point is not to deny that malnutrition, exposure, and disease contributed to mortality, which is likely, but to avoid inventing evidence for it.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Assess the Scale of Mongol Violence When the Narrative Evidence Is Strong but the Numbers Are Unreliable?

The question: The chronicles agree that the conquest involved massive killing and destruction, but their numerical claims are implausible. How much weight should historians give to the descriptive testimony, how should they treat the figures, and what overall judgment about the scale of loss is defensible?

Interpretation A — The testimony establishes catastrophe of very large scale: Some historians stress the convergence of independent sources, the destruction of major cities, and the long-term decline of some regions, and they conclude that, even allowing for exaggeration, mortality and displacement in parts of Khurasan, Transoxiana, and Khwarazm were enormous.
Interpretation B — Rhetoric and transmission have inflated the picture: Other historians stress literary convention, copying errors, and the use of numbers to convey moral meaning, and they argue that population decline was more modest, more regionally limited, and more attributable to other causes than the traditional account suggests.
Interpretation C — The debate should shift from totals to mechanisms: A third approach holds that counting the dead is not possible and may not be the best question. It prefers to trace the processes that changed settlements and livelihoods, killing, flight, captivity, fiscal pressure, and ecological change, and to compare regions.

Evidence: All approaches draw on the same narrative sources, supplemented by archaeological surveys, later geographical and administrative texts, and the limited quantitative information discussed in Section 46.19. Disagreement concerns primarily scale and causation, and secondarily the proper interpretation of specific numerical claims, rather than whether massacres occurred.

Why it matters: How historians frame the question shapes the story they tell. A narrative of total annihilation and permanent decline supports one understanding of later Iranian history; a narrative of regional shocks followed by uneven recovery supports another. The chapter adopts the view that severe regional destruction is well established while aggregate totals are not.

Student task: Explain which kinds of evidence would be needed to move the debate beyond impressionistic judgments. Identify one claim that all three interpretations could accept, and one claim on which they would disagree.

Division II · Sections 46.5–46.8 Regional Patterns of Destruction and Survival
Section 46.5

Merv and Nishapur: Urban Destruction and the Memory of Catastrophe, 1221

Merv and Nishapur became in later memory the emblematic victims of the Mongol conquest, and their cases show how historical writing about catastrophe is made. Merv, the great city of the Murghab oasis, had been one of the intellectual and commercial capitals of Khurasan, with libraries, madrasas, and a long tradition of scholarship; Yaqut's testimony, discussed in the Chapter Opening, records the importance of its book collections. In early 1221 a Mongol army under Chinggis Khan's son Tolui took the city. The sources report that the governor surrendered, that the population was then driven out and killed over a period of days, and that Mongol forces searched for survivors. These reports establish that Merv suffered a massacre and the destruction of much of its urban fabric. They do not establish how many people died, and the totals offered by chroniclers cannot be accepted as verified counts.

Nishapur's fate in the same spring followed a siege in which a Mongol commander, a son-in-law of Chinggis Khan, was killed. The sources present the subsequent destruction as reprisal, and the narrative includes grisly details, such as the claim that even animals were killed, that scholars have long regarded as likely to include rhetorical elaboration. Nishapur had also suffered earlier devastation, including damage from earthquake and from the Oghuz attacks of the twelfth century, and it was not at the height of its fortunes in 1221. The destruction of the city nonetheless ended a long period of its prominence and scattered the learned families who had made it a center of religious scholarship.

The more difficult question is what survived. In neither city is there good evidence for total annihilation. Narrative sources themselves report that survivors emerged from hiding, and some report that the conquerors returned to kill those who had concealed themselves, a detail that implies both the presence of survivors and the conquerors' awareness of them. Archaeological work at Merv indicates substantially reduced but continuing occupation after the Mongol conquest; the great urban area of the older city did not recover its former extent, and the full history of the oasis after the invasion lies beyond this chapter, and should not be inferred simply from later accounts of regional warfare. Nishapur reappears in later geographical and biographical literature as a functioning though diminished town. These facts support a picture of devastation followed by partial survival and slow regrowth, not of permanent erasure.

The reasons these cities became symbols deserve attention. Their destruction came early, shocked contemporaries, and was reported by authors who were themselves refugees from the region or knew refugees. The cities were also centers of the scholarly and religious networks whose members wrote the chronicles, so the loss of their libraries and learned communities weighed heavily in the literature that survived. Historical memory therefore magnified the significance of these cities, not because the events were fabricated but because the people who recorded them had a personal stake. Students should hold two judgments together: that Merv and Nishapur were severely destroyed, and that the traditional casualty figures attached to them are not defensible statistics.

Section 46.6

Herat and Balkh: Siege, Depopulation, Survival, and Return

Herat and Balkh illustrate how two cities of Greater Khurasan could experience the same conquest and emerge with very different histories. Herat, in the valley of the Hari Rud, surrendered to the Mongols in 1221 and was left with a governor and a small garrison. After a Khwarazmian victory elsewhere encouraged resistance, Herat rebelled and killed the Mongol-appointed official, and in 1222 a Mongol army besieged the city for months and took it. The sources report mass killing and the destruction of the city's defenses, and the later narrative tradition contains formulaic claims that only a handful of people survived. Such statements are a recognized literary device. They also sit uneasily with evidence discussed in Section 46.17 that the city was being repopulated and its canals repaired within a couple of decades.

Recent specialist research on Herat has treated the city's destruction and revival as interconnected processes, and this is a helpful corrective to narratives that end with the sieges. Herat's geographic position, its fertile valley, and its role as a node of routes between Iran, Central Asia, and the Indian subcontinent gave the site advantages for resettlement. People who had fled returned, others arrived, and local notables reestablished administrative and religious institutions. The result was not a restoration of the city as it had been, but the survival of the urban community and the foundations on which later flourishing, examined in Chapters 53 and 54, was built.

Balkh's trajectory differed. Balkh surrendered in 1220 and was at first spared, but it was later destroyed in the course of the operations of 1221, when resistance and shifting military circumstances led the conquerors to treat it harshly. Unlike Herat, Balkh did not recover its earlier importance. Travelers in the early fourteenth century, among them Ibn Battuta in the 1330s, reported seeing a city in ruins, with remains of grand buildings visible. The contrast with Herat shows that the level of initial destruction does not alone determine later outcomes. Water supply, regional politics, the presence of patrons willing to invest in reconstruction, and the shifting priorities of the Mongol administration all shaped which cities revived.

The paired cases also demonstrate a methodological point. A historian who looked only at the narratives of siege and massacre would predict similar fates for the two cities. A historian who looked only at the later evidence would see one city thriving and the other diminished and might infer different original destruction. The two cities' histories become intelligible only when destruction, survival, and recovery are studied together and when the evidence from each phase is allowed to qualify the others.

Section 46.7

Khwarazm and Transoxiana: Gurganj, Bukhara, Samarkand, and Their Hinterlands

Transoxiana and Khwarazm, the lands beyond and around the Amu Darya, experienced the first and in some respects the most thoroughgoing impact of the invasion, and they are essential to any comparison with Khurasan. These regions lie largely outside the modern borders of Iran, but as Chapter 1 argued they belonged to the Persianate world, and Bukhara and Samarkand had been among its most important cultural centers. After the capture of Otrar in 1219–1220, the Mongols took Bukhara in early 1220. The narratives report that the city surrendered in part, that the citadel resisted and was stormed, and that fire destroyed much of the city. Samarkand fell shortly afterward; its garrison was large, resistance was brief, and the city suffered plunder and the removal of artisans, whom the sources report in large numbers, though the figures are not reliable.

The later histories of the two cities differ. Bukhara partially recovered and, under the administration of the Khwarazmian-born official Mahmud Yalawach and his family, became a center of Mongol administration in Transoxiana, though not without tension: the uprising of Mahmud Tarabi around 1238 shows popular discontent under early Mongol rule. At Samarkand, the old city on the mound known as Afrasiab seems not to have regained its former importance, and the later city grew on a neighboring site. These details, derived from textual and archaeological evidence, illustrate that survival often meant relocation rather than simple restoration.

Gurganj, the capital of Khwarazm in the delta of the Amu Darya, was besieged in the winter and spring of 1221 in a prolonged and bitter fight complicated by quarrels among the Mongol commanders. Juvayni and other writers report that the dam holding back the river was breached and the city flooded. The circumstances and extent of this water damage are disputed: whether it was deliberate, how much of the city it affected, and whether the account represents literal engineering or a dramatic elaboration of the destruction cannot be settled from the narrative evidence. What can be said is that the city was taken, its population was massacred or deported, and the Khwarazm oasis was badly disrupted. Yet Gurganj revived within the Mongol successor state of the Jochids, and the later city of Urgench was described in the fourteenth century by Ibn Battuta as large and busy, a reminder that a heavily damaged region could recover in a different political framework.

Transoxiana and Khwarazm thus offer a comparison with Khurasan that resists a single moral. Some of the worst reported destruction occurred there, and some of the most vigorous later recovery. The irrigated oases of Bukhara, Samarkand, and Khwarazm were more resilient than the narratives of destruction might suggest, but the human and cultural losses were severe, and the geographic distribution of urban life shifted. The Otrar study discussed in Section 46.14 is a reminder that environmental history can also shape what the narratives record as the consequences of the war.

Section 46.8

Western Iran, Azerbaijan, Fars, Kirman, and the Caucasian Borderlands: Unequal Experiences of Conquest

The regions west and south of Khurasan experienced the conquest quite differently, and their histories caution against treating the catastrophe as uniform. In 1220–1223 a Mongol force under Jebe and Subedei crossed northern and western Iran and the Caucasus in an extended raid. Some cities, among them Tabriz and Hamadan, bought off the invaders with tribute; others, including Qazvin and Maragha, were attacked, and the sources report massacres. The raiders moved on rather than occupying, and many districts experienced the episode as a brief but terrifying visitation, and as a financial burden rather than as destruction followed by occupation. The region then endured other pressures, notably the campaigns of Jalal al-Din Khwarazmshah in the 1220s, which brought exactions, warfare against the Georgians, and the capture of Tabriz and other cities. The population in this region thus suffered from several armies.

From the 1230s the Mongols returned in force. Under Chormaqan, Mongol troops established themselves in Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, took Ganja around 1235, and subdued Georgia and Armenia over the following years. Armenian chroniclers record the devastation of districts and the imposition of tribute and obligations. Isfahan, which had avoided the earlier campaigns, was taken and badly hurt in the late 1230s, and the sources suggest that internal quarrels weakened its resistance. Parts of western Iran therefore experienced destruction in a later phase than Khurasan, and the effects were intertwined with the beginnings of administration.

Fars and Kirman provide a counterpoint. According to the scholarship summarized in the Encyclopaedia Iranica, their rulers accepted tributary status early and thereby avoided the main devastation. The Atabegs of Fars submitted in the 1220s, preserving Shiraz and its institutions, and in Kirman the dynasty of the Qutlugh-khans, founded by officials of Qara Khitai origin, accepted Mongol suzerainty. These arrangements did not provide permanent protection from intervention or from the fiscal demands of the Mongols, but they spared the cities and countryside the experience of storm and massacre. The survival of intellectual life in Shiraz, where Sa'di composed the Bustan and the Gulistan in the late 1250s, is one sign of the difference.

The regional comparison should not be turned into a simple contrast between disaster and security. Submission carried costs, including tribute, the presence of Mongol officials, and later the exactions discussed in Section 46.15. Cities that avoided storm might still lose people to deportation and levies. The point is that conquest involved a range of outcomes, determined by the timing of Mongol contact, the decisions of local rulers, the distance from the main campaigns, and the strategic priorities of the invaders. The Caucasian frontier, Azerbaijan, and western Iran experienced a combination of raiding, resistance, and later direct occupation that cannot be equated with the early devastation of Khurasan.

Division III · Sections 46.9–46.12 Displacement, Families, and Social Reorganization
Section 46.9

Refugees, Flight, and the Movement of Displaced Populations

Many of the people who left their homes in these years did so before any army arrived. News of destruction traveled faster than armies, and families, scholars, officials, and merchants with the means and connections to leave did so. The destinations available depended on where they lived. Those in Khurasan and Transoxiana could move south and east toward the Ghurid lands and India, west toward Iraq and Syria, or northwest toward Anatolia, while those in western Iran might retreat into mountain districts or seek shelter in cities that had submitted. Flight was not random: refugees followed existing commercial and scholarly networks, going to places where relatives, correspondents, or fellow members of a religious or professional community might receive them.

Individual cases illustrate the pattern. The family of the preacher Baha al-Din Walad, including his son Jalal al-Din (the future poet Rumi), moved westward from the Khwarazmian sphere before or near the onset of the Mongol invasions and eventually settled in Anatolia. The date and motive are disputed: scholarly reconstructions place departure as early as c. 1214–1216, and tensions with Khwarazmian authorities may have mattered more than any immediate Mongol threat. The family cannot safely be counted as a documented refugee household fleeing the 1219 invasion. Yaqut left Merv, as the Chapter Opening describes. Juzjani went to India, where the sultanate of Delhi became a refuge for scholars and officials from Khurasan and its neighboring regions. Such movements helped to extend Persianate culture westward and southward, a process that later chapters examine. The dates and details of individual biographies are often supplied by later hagiographic literature and should be handled with caution, but the general pattern of scholarly emigration is well documented.

For most refugees, however, there were no networks to rely upon and no distant courts to welcome them. Rural families might hide in mountains, deserts, or marshes, return after the armies passed, and rebuild. Urban dwellers might shelter in cellars, wells, and underground passages, as sources report, with varying success. Mosques, Sufi lodges, shrines, and other religious institutions sometimes offered protection or relief, funded by endowments, though they were also targets of reprisal when cities resisted. The conquerors themselves sometimes spared religious figures or granted them exemptions, a pattern that became more formal in later decades.

Flight altered both the places left behind and those that received refugees. Abandoned districts lost labor and skills, and when the refugees were skilled or educated the loss was disproportionate. Receiving communities gained scholars, artisans, and labor but also faced demands for housing and food, and competition for patronage and employment. Evidence for these effects is mostly anecdotal. No source tabulates the number of refugees or their destinations, and any claim about the scale of flight is an inference. What the evidence does establish is that the population of the destroyed districts was not simply lost: a significant but unmeasurable portion moved, and some of it returned.

Section 46.10

Captivity, Forced Labor, Deportation, and the Movement of Skilled Workers

One of the most consequential Mongol practices was the sorting of populations after a city's capture. Artisans, engineers, physicians, scribes, astronomers, and other people with useful skills were set aside from those to be killed, and many were taken into the service of Mongol princes and commanders or sent to distant parts of the empire. The sources report that craftspeople from Bukhara and Samarkand and other cities were sent eastward, and that large numbers were involved, though precise figures are not trustworthy. This policy served the conquerors' needs for siege engineers, craftspeople, and administrators and reflects a broader Mongol approach to conquered peoples as resources.

Captives were also used as military labor. The practice of hashar forced civilians to participate in sieges, and captured soldiers and civilians might be drafted into Mongol armies or assigned as slaves to Mongol aristocrats. Others were distributed among the conquerors as booty, and the slave markets of the region received captives. For the captives, forced travel meant separation from family, exposure, and in many cases death on the road. For their communities, it meant the sudden loss of workers and, in the case of craft specialists, of knowledge that was transmitted within households and workshops.

The consequences of forced mobility are difficult to trace. Households were broken apart, with husbands, wives, and children sent to different destinations. Skills could be lost when the person who held them died or was relocated away from apprentices. Yet movement also created new connections. Artisans and scholars taken east encountered Chinese and Inner Asian traditions, and the Ilkhanate later drew upon East Asian physicians, astronomers, and artisans, a process examined in Chapters 47 through 50. Some deportees later returned, and some were absorbed into communities in their new homes. The historical record rarely allows us to follow individuals, and the evidence for the long-term effects of deportation is largely inferential.

The practice also reveals the conquerors' logic and its limits. Mongol policy distinguished sharply between people who were useful and people who were not, and this gave certain groups a measure of protection even as it condemned others. Yet the chroniclers who describe the practice were mostly members of the educated classes who were themselves among the people protected as skilled, and their accounts may overstate the prominence of artisans and scholars in the population of the displaced. The experiences of farmers, laborers, and servants, who left little record, are underrepresented in the sources and must be inferred.

Section 46.11

Women, Children, Households, and Religious Communities under Conquest

The sources on the Mongol conquest were written by men, nearly all of them members of the literate elite, and they say little about women and children except in the most general terms. They mention the capture of women and children as part of the spoils and describe the grief of survivors, but they give few details about individual experience and no systematic accounts of what happened to families. Historians must therefore reason carefully from indirect evidence and general patterns, and must acknowledge that much is unknowable. This is a limitation of the evidence, not an indication that these experiences were unimportant.

Several processes can nonetheless be identified. Women and children were taken as captives and distributed or sold, and the sorting of populations separated families. The death or capture of male heads of household left widows and orphans, with consequences for inheritance, property, and support that the legal and endowment institutions of the time could address only in part. Pious endowments (waqf) sometimes provided for the education and maintenance of orphans, but their revenues were disrupted by the destruction of the properties that supported them. Women who survived had to manage households, farms, and workshops, often without male relatives. Some Mongol women of high rank exercised political influence and patronage in the empire, but this was not the experience of the Iranian women of conquered towns.

Religious communities experienced the conquest in diverse ways, and generalizations about uniform Mongol policy are misleading. Muslim scholars, preachers, and Sufi shaykhs were killed in the sacks of cities, and tradition holds that the Sufi master Najm al-Din Kubra died in the fall of Gurganj, though biographical details of this kind come from later sources. Other religious figures were spared or granted exemptions, and over time the Mongol court's openness to a range of religious specialists became a feature of its rule. Christian communities of the Church of the East, with congregations in cities such as Merv and Herat, as well as Jewish and Zoroastrian communities in Khurasan, Isfahan, Yazd, Kirman, and elsewhere, suffered as the populations around them did, and in some places later gained a measure of favor under Mongol rulers who did not share the Islamic framework of earlier states. The evidence for these communities is thin and uneven, and it does not support a single narrative of persecution or privilege.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reasoning about a Displaced Household: What the Sources Allow and What They Do Not

No source describes the experience of a particular farming family from the Murghab or the Hari Rud valley, and this feature does not invent one. Instead it identifies the kinds of decisions and constraints that the evidence suggests such households faced, and the kinds of sources that allow us to reason about them.

A household in a district approached by an army faced choices that depended on resources and information. It could stay and hope the district would submit; it could hide nearby, in mountains or underground, and return; or it could leave for a distant town or region. Households with animals and portable goods could move more easily than those whose wealth lay in land, and households with kinsmen or religious connections elsewhere had places to go. For those who stayed, the risks were killing, capture, and the loss of crops and stock to requisition. For those who left, they were exposure, hunger, and the loss of land rights that might be difficult to reclaim.

After the immediate danger, the household faced the labor of rebuilding: replacing animals, recovering seed, repairing canals, and reestablishing claims to land. The early Mongol exactions discussed in Section 46.15 made this harder. Evidence for these processes comes from several sources: chronicle statements about the reappearance of survivors, endowment and legal documents where they survive, archaeological evidence of resettlement, and later administrative texts describing efforts to restore cultivation. All are indirect and come from the perspective of the elite or the state.

Study point: Distinguishing what the sources say from what plausible reasoning suggests is central to social history. When a historian writes that "families fled," the claim is supported by general testimony; when a historian writes about how a particular family felt, the claim is speculation.

Section 46.12

Pastoral Migration, Military Settlement, and Changes in Population Distribution

The Mongol conquest was not only a story of populations leaving. It also brought new populations into the Iranian world, and nowhere was this more significant than in the northwest. Mongol armies were accompanied by their families and herds, and they included large numbers of Turkic troops from the steppe. Pastoral armies required pasture, and the high plains and river valleys of Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, especially the Mughan plain and the highland pastures, offered it. From the 1230s, Mongol commanders such as Chormaqan stationed forces in this region, and garrison forces known as tamma were established in conquered territories. The settlement of these groups changed the balance of land use in places where their herds required grazing land that had previously been used for agriculture or by other pastoralists.

The consequences were gradual and complex. Competition for land and water between pastoralists and farmers could provoke conflict, and the levy on livestock known as qubchur reflected the importance of herds in the Mongol fiscal system. Over time some pastoral groups settled and adopted farming, while some villagers shifted toward pastoralism, and the relationships between the groups were negotiated in ways that varied by district. Turkic-speaking pastoral populations had been present on the plateau before the Mongols, a result of the Seljuk and Oghuz movements examined in Chapter 38, and the Mongol period added to them. The Turkmen confederations discussed in Chapter 55 emerged from this longer process.

It is essential not to read later linguistic outcomes back into this period. The Turkicization of Azerbaijan, in which Turkic languages became predominant in the region, was a process unfolding over several centuries and involving many groups and migrations; it was neither instantaneous nor attributable to the Mongols alone. In the thirteenth century the population of the region remained largely Iranian-speaking in many districts, with Kurdish, Armenian, Georgian, and other populations in neighboring areas. The Mongol-era settlement shifted the proportions and prepared the way for later change, but the notion of an immediate ethnic or linguistic replacement is not supported by the evidence.

The effect of pastoral settlement on the population map was thus one of redistribution, not simple replacement. Some districts lost cultivators and gained herders; others saw little change; and in the east the loss of settled populations was not offset by new settlement. The data do not permit us to trace the demographic changes with precision. What they allow is the recognition that the human geography of the Iranian world after the conquest differed from its predecessor not only in the number of people but in the kinds of livelihoods and the distribution of land use.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Early Mongol Occupation: Perspectives from Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives, based on patterns that historians infer from many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of a Mongol or Turkic commander and his troops: Conquered territory was a source of provisions, labor, and pasture, and the main strategic concern was maintaining military strength and communication. Settled populations were valuable as producers of revenue and goods, which gave commanders reasons both to exploit and, over time, to protect them.
From the perspective of a Persian-speaking secretary or administrator: Survival and influence depended on adapting to the new rulers and acting as intermediaries. Men in this position, among them the Juvayni family, could moderate demands and direct reconstruction, but they also depended on the conquerors and are open to the charge of collaboration.
From the perspective of a peasant household in an irrigated district: The central concerns were seed, animals, and the canal. Armies, levies, and officials all made claims on the harvest, and the household's survival depended on avoiding both violence and ruinous exactions. Households left almost no records of their own.
From the perspective of a refugee scholar: Exile meant the loss of libraries, students, and patrons, but it also created opportunities in distant courts. The scholars' accounts of the conquest shaped later memory disproportionately, because they were the people with the means and training to write.
From the perspective of a ruler who accepted tributary submission: Paying tribute and acknowledging Mongol authority could preserve a city and its institutions, at the price of obligations and of the risk that later demands would intensify. Rulers in Fars and Kirman made this calculation with imperfect knowledge of what would follow.
Division IV · Sections 46.13–46.16 Agriculture, Infrastructure, Commerce, and Economic Disruption
Section 46.13

Agriculture, Irrigation, and the Vulnerability of Rural Production

The agricultural economy of the Iranian world rested on water management. Rivers were tapped by canals and dams; groundwater was brought to the surface by qanats; reservoirs and cisterns stored water; and flood-control works protected fields. These systems were not static monuments. They were maintained by organized labor, financed by landowners, communities, and the state, and governed by customary and legal arrangements over water rights. When the labor force, the funds, or the political authority needed to sustain them weakened, canals silted, channels collapsed, and fields went out of cultivation.

This vulnerability helps explain how war could have effects far beyond its immediate violence. Direct physical damage was possible. Dams could be breached, canals deliberately blocked, and qanat shafts filled or destroyed, and the sources report some such acts. Yet equally significant was the interruption of maintenance. If the cultivators who cleaned the canals were killed, enslaved, or fled, and the local notables who organized the work were removed, the systems might deteriorate even if no one had attacked them. Taxation changes could also alter incentives: higher or more arbitrary demands discouraged the investment of labor and capital in long-term improvements. The interaction of these factors made the recovery of agricultural systems slow and uncertain.

Several features of the landscape shaped the vulnerability. Large river-fed systems such as those at Merv and in Khwarazm depended on the management of a single river across a wide area; if the central structures failed, the whole system could be affected. Qanat systems were more decentralized, with each channel serving a particular settlement, and their loss or survival depended more on local circumstances. Rain-fed upland farming was less exposed to infrastructure failure but more exposed to the loss of seed, animals, and households. The consequences of war for rural production therefore depended on the type of agriculture as well as the type of violence.

Landholding and fiscal institutions affected how losses were distributed. Mongol conquest transformed property relations: lands could be confiscated, granted to Mongol princes and officers, or placed under new administrative arrangements, while the old landholding families sometimes survived by adapting. These changes affected who controlled the water and who benefited from rebuilding. The sources do not allow a systematic account, but they indicate that the agricultural effects of the conquest were as much about control and incentives as about physical destruction.

Historical Vocabulary
Qanat and Karez

Definition: A qanat is an underground channel, with a gentle gradient and periodic vertical shafts, that carries groundwater from an aquifer to a settlement or field without pumping. Karez is the term used for similar systems in Afghanistan and parts of Central Asia.

Historical context: Qanats enabled settlement in places where surface water was scarce, and they required regular cleaning and repair by skilled specialists. They were often owned or managed by communities or families who held shares in the water.

Why it matters: Because qanats depend on ongoing labor and expertise, they are vulnerable to the loss of the people who maintain them, even when the tunnels are physically intact. This helps explain why war could reduce agricultural output without systematic destruction of the infrastructure.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that qanats were uniform or that all were damaged or destroyed in the Mongol period. The evidence for the fate of particular systems is uneven, and each case must be examined on its own terms.

Section 46.14

Did the Mongols Destroy the Irrigation Systems? Archaeology, Climate, and Historical Causation

One of the most widely repeated claims about the Mongol conquest is that it destroyed the irrigation systems of Iran and Central Asia and thereby caused a lasting agricultural collapse. The claim has some textual basis. Chroniclers report breached dams and ruined canals, and the narrative of Gurganj's flooding is the most famous example. It also has a persuasive logic, since the physical landscape of abandoned canals and ruined settlements seems to testify to a catastrophe. Yet modern research has made the question more complicated, and this section examines the evidence with the care that a claim of such breadth requires.

The central point is that abandoned irrigation does not by itself identify a cause. A canal system may be abandoned because enemies destroyed it, because its people left or died, because the river that fed it changed course, because the climate shifted, because the soil salinized, or because political and economic conditions made it unprofitable to maintain. The traditional narrative assumed the first explanation. Archaeologists and environmental scientists now test it by dating the abandonment of canals and settlements, reconstructing river histories, and examining climate records. In many cases the results do not match the narrative of a single episode of destruction.

Archaeology and Environment Study

The Otrar Oasis: Comparing Written Accounts with Geoarchaeological Evidence

The place: Otrar, near the confluence of the Syr Darya and the Arys river in what is now southern Kazakhstan, was an important city and irrigated oasis on the northeastern edge of the Iranian and Persianate world. Its governor's conduct toward a Mongol trading party in 1218 is traditionally treated as a trigger for the invasion of Khwarazm, and the city fell in 1219–1220.

The traditional explanation: The decline of Otrar's irrigated landscape was commonly attributed to Mongol destruction. Written accounts of the siege and the general reputation of the invasion supported the assumption.

The research: A geoarchaeological study of the Otrar oasis, which combined field survey, dating of canal systems, and analysis of river and sediment histories, concluded that major phases of irrigation abandonment were better explained by long-term changes in river behavior and climate than by a clearly identifiable episode of destruction during the Mongol invasion. The authors' findings link canal abandonment and settlement change to shifts in the rivers that fed the system and to environmental trends operating over longer periods. (Verified study: Willem H. J. Toonen and colleagues, “A hydromorphic reevaluation of the forgotten river civilizations of Central Asia,” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 117, no. 52 (2020): 32982–32988; doi:10.1073/pnas.2009553117.)

What the study does and does not show: The study concerns the sites and evidence examined at Otrar. It demonstrates that, in that oasis, the assumption of war-caused collapse is not supported as the principal explanation for the agricultural changes observed. It does not show that the Mongols caused no damage anywhere, nor that climate explains abandoned settlements at Merv, Herat, Gurganj, or in Iranian qanat districts. It is a comparative case that shows how the question can be tested, not a universal answer.

Implications for method: Where narrative sources report destruction, historians can now check for physical evidence of the timing of abandonment. Where the timing coincides with the invasion and a plausible mechanism exists, as at some besieged cities, the narrative gains support. Where it does not, the narrative of destruction may reflect the chroniclers' tendency to attribute observed ruin to the great catastrophe they knew.

Study questions:

  1. Why is the dating of canal abandonment important for testing a claim about war damage?
  2. How could a chronicler have attributed to the invasion a decline that began earlier or had other causes?
  3. Why would it be a mistake to generalize the Otrar finding to every irrigation system in the Iranian world?
  4. What kinds of evidence would you want from Merv or Herat before making a comparable claim?

The right conclusion is a disciplined middle position. The Mongol conquest did disrupt agriculture, through killing and displacement of cultivators, the loss of maintenance, requisition of animals and grain, and in some cases deliberate damage to waterworks. But the claim that the Mongols destroyed all or most of the irrigation systems of Iran and Central Asia, and thereby caused a uniform agricultural collapse, is not supported. Some systems declined for reasons unrelated to the invasion; some recovered quickly; some, like those of Khwarazm, revived under Mongol successor states. At Gurganj, the circumstances of the flooding remain disputed. The historian's task is to examine each case on its evidence, and to avoid both the blanket accusation and the blanket exoneration.

Section 46.15

Requisitioning, Tribute, Taxation, and the Continuing Economic Costs of Occupation

The cost of conquest did not end when the fighting did. The occupying forces and the administrators who followed needed grain, animals, fodder, labor, and money, and they took them from the conquered population. Early Mongol rule in Iran was marked by improvisation, with commanders, princes, and officials making overlapping demands. Scholarship identifies these overlapping obligations, along with requisitions and the burdens of maintaining Mongol military and communications needs, as significant pressures on communities in the decades after the conquest. This section examines them as social consequences and leaves the institutional history of Mongol government to Chapter 47 and the reforms of the 1290s to Chapter 48.

Several kinds of demand are attested. Armies and officials required provisions and animals, taken as requisition or under levies. The communications system, known as the yam, depended on post stations supplied with horses, food, and personnel by local communities, and travelers with Mongol authorization could demand mounts and supplies. Conquered peoples owed tribute, either as a lump sum negotiated at submission or as annual payments. Livestock taxes, poll taxes, and extraordinary levies, imposed on top of preexisting land taxes, multiplied the burdens on households. Corvée labor was demanded for construction and for military purposes. Because different officials could impose different levies, sometimes on the same people, the combined effect could be far heavier than any single demand.

Persian sources, including Juvayni, describe abuses and the efforts of some administrators to curb them. Mongol officials such as Korguz, who administered Khurasan in the 1230s and early 1240s, and Arghun Aqa, who governed it afterward, are credited in the sources with measures to regularize taxation and encourage cultivation, among them censuses for tax purposes. The evidence suggests a gradual movement from plunder toward systematic exploitation, in which the conquerors recognized that a ruined peasantry produced nothing. But the transition was uneven, and the communities in which it occurred left few records of their experience.

It is important not to attribute every later fiscal or commercial difficulty to the initial invasion. The economy of the region in the later thirteenth century was shaped by the policies of the Mongol rulers, by the rivalries among Mongol princes, by the weight of the military establishment, and by the way revenues were collected and spent. Chapter 48 examines the argument that excessive exactions and administrative disorder required major reform. The point for this chapter is that the economic costs of conquest were prolonged, layered, and mediated by institutions, and that they interacted with the effects of violence and displacement to slow recovery.

Historical Vocabulary
Hashar, Qubchur, Tamma, Yam, and Waqf

Definition: Hashar refers to forced labor or levies of civilians, including those used in sieges. Qubchur was a Mongol levy on livestock. Tamma designated a military garrison force stationed in conquered territory. Yam was the Mongol postal and relay system, supported by local communities. Waqf is a pious endowment that dedicates the revenue of property to religious, educational, or charitable purposes.

Historical context: The first four terms describe the machinery by which conquerors extracted labor, wealth, and support from conquered populations. Waqf describes an institution of the conquered society, which could be damaged by the loss of endowed properties but which also provided a framework for relief and for the continuity of learning.

Why it matters: These terms show that the burden of occupation was institutional as well as violent. They help explain why communities that avoided massacre could still suffer severely.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that these institutions were uniform across the empire or constant over time. Their application varied by region and period and was subject to later reform.

Section 46.16

Urban Economies, Trade Networks, Crafts, and the Survival of Intellectual Life

The destruction of cities, the removal of artisans, and the insecurity of roads reduced the productive capacity of urban economies and disrupted long-distance trade. Markets were looted and burned, workshops lost their craftsmen, and merchants lost goods and capital. The commercial networks that linked the Iranian world to China, India, and the Mediterranean were damaged in the zones of war. Yet the interruption was neither total nor permanent. The Mongols were not hostile to commerce and soon had interests in protecting merchants, and the same empire that destroyed cities created a vast zone of political connection across Eurasia. The commercial picture of the thirteenth century, discussed in Chapter 50, thus combines the loss of established centers with the opening of new routes.

The effects on craft production and urban life varied by city. In the worst-hit centers, the loss of skilled workers and the destruction of markets meant a long contraction. In others, the survival of workshops and the continuity of local elites allowed earlier activities to continue at lower levels. Some crafts benefited from new patronage: Mongol rulers valued metalwork, textiles, and architecture, and later Ilkhanid cities supported substantial production. But the evidence for specific industries in the early decades is limited, and the generalization that the conquest ended Iranian urban crafts is incorrect.

Intellectual life suffered severe losses but did not end. The destruction of libraries, as at Merv, the death or flight of scholars, and the disruption of madrasas and the endowments that supported them were grave blows, and manuscripts lost in these events cannot be recovered. At the same time, learning continued in the regions that were spared, such as Fars, where Shiraz sustained a vibrant literary culture in the age of Sa'di, and in the lands to which refugees moved, including Anatolia, Syria, Egypt, and India. Within the Mongol realm, Persian-speaking officials like Juvayni produced major works of history, and intellectual traditions introduced in Chapter 43 persisted in altered circumstances; Chapter 49 examines a major later project of Ilkhanid historical scholarship. The conquest broke the intellectual structures of some centers while redistributing the human carriers of learning across a wider world.

The historical judgment must therefore be careful. The conquest caused severe intellectual and economic disruption in Khurasan and Transoxiana, and the loss of knowledge and institutions was real. But it neither ended all learning nor all commerce in Iran, and the notion that Iranian urban civilization was permanently destroyed does not stand up to evidence of continuity, relocation, and revival. The more productive question is how the geography and character of intellectual and commercial life were reorganized, and who gained and lost in the process.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains decline. Violence, displacement, fiscal pressure, and environmental change acted together, and their relative weights varied by region. Where evidence is thin, historians should say that causation is uncertain rather than assign blame to a single cause.

Section 46.17

The Recovery of Herat: Repopulation, Waterworks, and Urban Restoration

Herat offers the best opportunity in this chapter to follow a city from destruction into reconstruction, and it does so because a local historical tradition and recent specialist research allow the story to be told in more than one register. Shivan Mahendrarajah's A History of Herat: From Chingiz Khan to Tamerlane, chapter 7, “Early Efforts to Revive Agriculture and Commerce” (2026), documents initiatives to restore the city's canals around 1236 and discusses a population enumeration of approximately 6,900 persons in 1240–1241 (chapter reference). These pieces of evidence indicate that the city was not abandoned but that it was reduced and that efforts to rebuild were under way within a generation.

The restoration of canals was central. Herat's agricultural base depended on water from the Hari Rud distributed through canals, and without their repair no substantial population could be fed. Accounts of the first restoration effort in 1236 identify a group of weavers permitted to return under Ögedei, alongside broader efforts by inhabitants and organizers working under Mongol sovereignty. Reconstruction depended not just on officials and notables but also on returning workers with the practical knowledge and labor needed to reopen canals. In the middle of the century, rulers of the local Kart family emerged as Mongol-recognized governors of Herat, and the city's later flourishing, examined in Chapters 53 and 54, grew from the institutional continuity that this local leadership provided. The recovery developed through unequal cooperation among imperial authorities, local leaders, and returning residents, who needed both permission to settle and practical access to water and labor.

The population figure of approximately 6,900 requires disciplined use. A historian must ask what the number counts: all inhabitants of the city, only adult men, taxable households, or the population of the city and its surrounding district. The edition of the sources and the categories used by the enumerators determine how the number can be interpreted, and the geographic coverage may not correspond to the pre-invasion city. The figure cannot be compared with an invented pre-invasion census to calculate a percentage of loss, because no reliable pre-invasion count exists. Its value lies elsewhere: it shows that a count was made, that the Mongol-era administration collected demographic information for taxation, that the number recorded in this particular enumeration was in the thousands, and that the community was undergoing reconstruction; it does not by itself prove the total number of city residents.

This case teaches a general lesson about historical evidence. A single surviving number is valuable when its source, date, and categories are known, even if it cannot support an empire-wide estimate. Used carefully, it informs the interpretation of the narrative sources, constraining claims about both destruction and survival: the chronicles' stories of a handful of survivors and of vast numbers killed are both difficult to reconcile with a city that could be enumerated and was repairing its canals within two decades. The reconstructed history of Herat therefore avoids both the image of annihilation and the image of rapid return to normal.

Section 46.18

Uneven Recovery: Regional Differences in Settlement, Agriculture, and Commerce

The regional histories examined in Division II suggest that recovery was as uneven as destruction. Khurasan, the region of the earliest and heaviest losses, saw some cities, like Herat, regain vitality, while others, like Balkh, remained diminished and Merv recovered only slowly and on a smaller scale. Khwarazm and parts of Transoxiana experienced renewed activity under Mongol successor polities: Gurganj, also known as Urgench (the historical site now called Kunya-Urgench), again became an important city, while Samarkand's old Afrasiab site did not regain its former urban position. Azerbaijan and the western regions, damaged in a later phase, gradually became the heartland of the Ilkhanate, with Tabriz rising to prominence. Fars and Kirman, which had avoided the early catastrophe, retained institutions and developed as relatively stable regions.

Several factors explain the differences. The severity of the initial destruction mattered, but so did the availability of water and the state of the irrigation system, the location relative to routes and to Mongol centers of power, the existence of local elites able to organize reconstruction, and the policies of Mongol rulers. A city that lay on a route valued by the Mongols and had patrons willing to invest could recover faster than a city that was out of the way. A region whose rulers had submitted early might enjoy continuity of institutions, but also bear tribute and exactions. Recovery also depended on timing: districts devastated in the 1220s had decades to rebuild before the reforms of the 1290s, while those struck in the 1230s or 1250s entered the Ilkhanid period with fresher losses.

Students should also distinguish early recovery from the later consolidation of Ilkhanid administrative policy. The reconstruction efforts of the 1230s and 1240s were local and partial, often driven by individual administrators, and they took place under a regime whose demands were erratic. The more comprehensive efforts to stabilize fiscal systems, restore cultivation, and promote commerce belong to the period after Hülegü's establishment of the Ilkhanate and especially to the reforms examined in Chapters 47, 48, and 50. This chapter's task is to establish the starting point of that later recovery: a landscape in which the damage was unevenly distributed, the human and economic resources were reduced and redistributed, and the beginnings of rebuilding were visible but fragile.

  • Skilled workers and scholars were redistributed through captivity, deportation, and flight across a much wider geography

  • Taxation and requisition became heavier, more layered, and administered by new officials

  • Trade routes across the region remained important and were later revived
  • Regions that submitted early, including Fars and Kirman, retained continuity of rule and institutions

When and how change occurred: Change was abrupt in the cities that were stormed and gradual in the regions that experienced occupation and fiscal pressure. Those who suffered most directly were the inhabitants of the destroyed cities and the cultivators of affected districts; those who experienced greater continuity included rulers who submitted, many rural communities in less exposed areas, and administrators who adapted.

Why the pattern matters: A narrative of total rupture and a narrative of continuity are both inaccurate. The pattern is one of regionally uneven rupture accompanied by significant continuity.

Historical Debate

Was Long-Term Agricultural Decline Caused Principally by Destruction, Displacement, Fiscal Extraction, or Environmental Change?

The question: Where cultivated land and settlement contracted in the century after the conquest, which causes were responsible, and in what proportions? Historians differ in the weight they give to the physical destruction of infrastructure, the loss and displacement of the people who maintained it, the economic burden of occupation, and environmental factors.

Emphasis on destruction and killing: Where narrative sources and archaeology point to the sacking of cities and the breaching of waterworks, this interpretation holds that war damage and the loss of cultivators were the principal causes of decline.
Emphasis on displacement and the loss of maintenance: This view stresses that labor-intensive systems failed when the people who sustained them fled, were captured, or were redirected, so the decline may have occurred even without deliberate demolition.
Emphasis on fiscal extraction: Here the burdens of overlapping levies, requisitions, and arbitrary exactions discouraged investment and drove cultivators from the land, a process that continued until reforms.
Emphasis on environmental change: Studies such as that of the Otrar oasis show that river behavior and climate can explain abandonment better than a single episode of war in particular places, and this view warns against attributing all decline to conquest.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on chronicles, administrative literature, archaeological surveys, and geoarchaeological studies. Few regions have been studied with all these methods, and the results cannot be generalized across the Iranian world.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation and relative weight, and it is complicated by regional variation and by the difficulty of dating abandonment precisely. Most historians accept that several factors combined, and the dispute is largely about proportions that the evidence may never settle.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the conquest is judged and how later Iranian economic history is understood. A monocausal story of destruction misses the roles of fiscal policy and environment, while an environmental story risks minimizing human violence.

Section 46.19

Can the Demographic Losses Be Measured? Chronicles, Archaeology, Tax Registers, and Historical Estimates

The question that has run through the chapter can now be posed directly: how many people did the Mongol invasions kill or displace, and by what proportion did they reduce the population of the Iranian world? A defensible answer begins with a statement of the evidence's limits. There is no reliable census of the region before the invasions or immediately afterward, so no direct before-and-after comparison is possible. The chronicles' casualty totals are unverified, appear to be inflated, and measure at best single episodes. Administrative boundaries changed, so figures from different periods often refer to different territories, and the rare enumerations that survive, like that of Herat, cover particular places and categories.

Other kinds of evidence provide partial constraints. Archaeological surveys can show how many settlements existed and were occupied in different periods and can detect the abandonment of sites and canals, but their coverage is incomplete, and converting settlement counts into population estimates requires assumptions. Later fiscal and geographical texts, such as the early fourteenth-century writings of Hamd Allah Mustawfi, report revenue figures for provinces and cities, and comparison with earlier figures has been used to infer economic decline or recovery. These comparisons are difficult because the units, the currency, the tax categories, and the rates of assessment differed, and revenue changes can reflect policy as much as population.

The categories of loss must also be kept separate. Direct deaths in sieges and massacres, indirect deaths from hunger and exposure, flight to other regions, enslavement and deportation, and the abandonment or relocation of settlements are different processes with different evidence, and they cannot be added into a single figure. A decline in the population of a city may reflect any or all of them. A settlement that disappears from the record may have been destroyed, abandoned, or renamed. Even if one could establish the number of deaths, it would not measure the long-term change in population, which depended also on births, return migration, and resettlement.

Given these constraints, a reasoned assessment is possible but qualitative. The evidence supports the conclusion that parts of Khurasan, Transoxiana, and Khwarazm suffered very heavy losses, with some cities severely depopulated and some districts abandoned; that other regions, such as Fars and Kirman, experienced much less direct destruction; that displacement and the redistribution of skilled people and pastoral populations were significant; and that recovery began within decades in some places and was slow or incomplete in others. Whether the total reduction in the population of the Iranian world was modest, large, or enormous is a question to which the sources do not permit a numerical answer, and the chapter does not offer an aggregate death toll. Students who encounter precise totals in popular histories should ask what source they derive from and whether that source can bear the weight.

Section 46.20

Chapter Synthesis and Review: Destruction, Survival, and the Problem of Demographic Change

This chapter has argued that the Mongol invasions caused profound destruction in important parts of the Iranian world while the scale of demographic loss remains beyond precise reconstruction. The evidence for massacre, ruin, and displacement is strong and comes from multiple independent traditions, and it is confirmed in general outline by archaeology and by later administrative evidence. The evidence for numbers is weak, and the casualty totals that appear in chronicles and popular histories cannot be treated as statistics. The two conclusions are not in tension; together they define the historian's task.

The regional comparisons have shown that the experience of conquest varied widely. The cities of Khurasan, Transoxiana, and Khwarazm bore the heaviest early destruction, but their later paths diverged: Herat recovered within a generation, Balkh remained diminished, Urgench revived under a successor state, and Merv slowly regained a fraction of its earlier scale. Western Iran and the Caucasus were raided, taxed, and later occupied; Fars and Kirman negotiated submission and preserved much of their institutional life. Displacement, captivity, and migration redistributed people and skills across a wide geography, and the settlement of pastoral populations in the northwest changed patterns of land use without producing instantaneous ethnic transformation.

The economic analysis has shown that the costs of conquest outlasted the fighting. Requisitions, tribute, and overlapping levies placed heavy burdens on communities, and the loss of maintenance for irrigation systems, as much as deliberate destruction, constrained agricultural recovery. The study of the Otrar oasis has shown that environmental change can explain some abandonment better than war, and the chapter has insisted that this finding applies to the places studied and is not a universal law. The intellectual consequences were severe in the centers that were destroyed, but learning and commerce continued in other regions and were redistributed across a wider world.

The central lesson is methodological as well as historical. Students have learned to distinguish an event from its elaboration, a casualty figure from a population estimate, deaths from displacement, and abandoned sites from destroyed ones. They have learned to ask of every source who wrote, when, from where, and for whom, and to recognize that a surviving number such as the approximately 6,900 persons associated with Herat in 1240–1241 is valuable for what it can show and dangerous when stretched beyond that.

These findings prepare the ground for the next chapter. The conquerors who had destroyed cities and levied heavy exactions came, within a few decades, to govern the same lands, and the realities of devastated populations, damaged waterworks, and a resentful but indispensable class of local administrators shaped the institutions they created. Chapter 47 examines how Mongol rule became an Iranian state, and Chapters 48 and 50 will return to the fiscal reforms and economic recovery that followed.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 46 has examined the human, social, economic, and environmental consequences of the Mongol invasions of the Iranian world and the evidentiary problems of measuring them. Its central argument is that the invasions caused profound destruction, especially in Khurasan, Transoxiana, and Khwarazm, but that the scale of population loss cannot be established from medieval casualty claims and that consequences varied sharply by region.

The chapter began by establishing that no reliable census of the pre-invasion or immediate post-invasion Iranian world survives. Medieval geographers described cities and districts but did not enumerate populations, and modern estimates rest on inference. Earlier shocks, including the Oghuz sacks of the twelfth century and earthquakes, mean that the pre-invasion baseline was not one of uniform prosperity.

The principal chroniclers, Ibn al-Athir, al-Nasawi, Juzjani, and Juvayni, wrote from different positions, at different distances from the events, and under different pressures. Their testimony establishes that massacre, plunder, and destruction occurred, but their numerical claims are literary and unstable and cannot be treated as counts. The chapter distinguished rejecting an implausible number from rejecting the event, and it distinguished direct deaths, indirect mortality, flight, captivity, forced relocation, and settlement abandonment.

Regional case studies showed that experiences differed. Merv and Nishapur suffered severe urban destruction in 1221, yet the evidence points to reduced survival and slow regrowth rather than annihilation. Herat was destroyed in 1222 but began reconstruction within a generation, while Balkh remained diminished. The cities of Transoxiana and Khwarazm suffered heavily and recovered unevenly, with Gurganj's flooding a disputed episode. Western Iran and the Caucasus were raided and later occupied, while Fars and Kirman accepted tributary submission and avoided the worst destruction.

Displacement and social reorganization were as important as killing. Refugees followed scholarly and commercial networks to Iraq, Anatolia, Syria, and India. Captives and skilled workers were sorted, deported, or employed by the conquerors, and families were broken. The sources say little about women and children, a limitation of elite male authorship that the chapter acknowledged. Religious communities experienced the conquest in varied ways. The settlement of Mongol and Turkic pastoral groups in the northwest redistributed land use without producing instantaneous ethnic or linguistic replacement.

The economic analysis emphasized the vulnerability of irrigated agriculture to the loss of maintenance and labor. The traditional claim that the Mongols destroyed the irrigation systems of Iran and Central Asia is not supported in its sweeping form. The Otrar study showed that river and climate changes can explain abandonment better than war at the sites examined, but it cannot be generalized to Merv, Herat, Gurganj, or Iranian qanat systems. Requisitions, tribute, and overlapping levies prolonged the costs of conquest, and the later fiscal reforms treated in Chapter 48 responded to this distress.

The chapter's treatment of Herat's recovery showed how a single enumeration, approximately 6,900 persons in 1240–1241, can be valuable without supporting a percentage of loss. Recovery was uneven, shaped by water, location, patronage, and policy, and the early reconstruction of the 1230s and 1240s must be distinguished from the later consolidation under the Ilkhans. The intellectual life of the region suffered severe losses but continued and was redistributed.

Finally, the chapter addressed the measurement problem directly. Chronicles, archaeology, tax registers, and later fiscal texts each provide partial and non-comparable evidence, and the categories of loss cannot be added. A reasoned qualitative assessment is possible: severe regional devastation, significant displacement, uneven recovery, and no defensible aggregate death toll. The chapter thus prepares for Chapter 47's account of how Mongol rule became an Iranian state and for Chapter 50's treatment of recovery.

Demography

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how difficult it is to write honestly about catastrophe. The Mongol invasions are among the best known disasters of premodern history, yet what is known is a pattern of violence, displacement, and disruption supported by strong testimony, not a precise ledger. Learning to hold these two truths together, to take suffering seriously without borrowing authority from unverifiable numbers, is a skill that applies far beyond medieval Iran.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The conquest redistributed people, skills, and wealth across Eurasia, linked the Iranian world more closely to Inner Asia and China, and shifted the political center of gravity toward the northwest. It exposed the dependence of settled society on water management and local administration, and it showed how the interests of conquerors and conquered could converge in reconstruction. These patterns shaped the Ilkhanate, the Timurid world, and the regional geography that preceded the Safavid state.

Continuities survived the catastrophe. Persian-speaking administrators and scholars remained indispensable, Islamic institutions endured and adapted, and the oases and river valleys that had supported earlier cities remained the foundations of settled life. The chapter has argued against both the image of permanent ruin and the image of seamless continuity, and in favor of a history of regionally uneven rupture and recovery.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the larger textbook by establishing a model of evidence-conscious social history. The questions raised here, about who produced the sources, what they could know, and what remains invisible, will recur in later chapters on famine, war, revolution, and repression. Chapter 47 now turns to the institutions through which the conquerors became rulers, and the legacy examined here, a damaged landscape and a population that had survived, formed the conditions with which those institutions had to work.
