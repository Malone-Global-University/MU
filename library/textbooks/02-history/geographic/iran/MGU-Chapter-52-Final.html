Timur and the Conquest of Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Timur defeated the rulers of Iran one by one between 1381 and 1393, yet winning battles and extracting submission was not the same as building a government. This chapter asks how a Central Asian commander assembled the military, political, and ideological means to conquer a fragmented Iran, and why the consequences of that conquest fell so unevenly on the cities, countrysides, and peoples he brought under his control.

In the spring of 1381 the city of Herat, one of the great urban centers of eastern Iran, submitted to an army that had come from Transoxiana. The principal narrative sources, written at Timur's court or under his descendants, report that the Kartid ruler Malik Ghiyath al-Din Pir ʿAli chose surrender over a siege, that tribute was paid, and that the city's fortifications were afterward dismantled. Herat was not sacked on this occasion. A little more than a decade later, by contrast, the Muzaffarid dynasty of Fars would be destroyed as an independent power, and in 1387 the people of Isfahan would suffer one of the most severe massacres of the period. The first campaign of conquest and the last were separated by only a short span of years, but their human consequences could hardly have been more different.

That contrast is the central problem of this chapter. Timur (Temür, known in European tradition as Tamerlane) conquered Iran in a series of campaigns concentrated between 1381 and 1393, but he did not do so in a single sweep, and he did not treat every region alike. Some rulers submitted and were absorbed into his system; others resisted and were destroyed. Some cities were spared in exchange for money and the removal of their walls; others were punished after rebelling. Skilled workers and scholars were seized from many places and sent to Samarqand, where they contributed to a building program of great splendor, while the communities they left behind lost people they could not easily replace. A responsible account must hold together both the effectiveness of Timurid military organization and the severity of its costs.

The chapter also tries to separate two things that are often fused in popular memory: conquest and government. Timur was extraordinarily successful at defeating regional rulers and imposing military supremacy. But supremacy gained through campaigning did not automatically produce stable administration, economic recovery, or an orderly succession. He spent much of his reign in the field, returning repeatedly to regions that had submitted and then risen again. The durable system of provincial rule, elite competition, and dynastic politics that the Timurids later attempted to build is the subject of Chapter 53. Here the emphasis falls on how the conquest was carried out, what it meant for those who lived through it, and why historians are cautious about calling it a reunification of Iran.

The evidence for these events requires particular care. The most detailed accounts of Timur's campaigns were written by men who worked for him or for his family. Nizam al-Din Shami composed his Zafarnama at Timur's order, completing it around 1404; Hafiz-i Abru and Sharaf al-Din Yazdi wrote under his son and grandson. These authors preserved dates, itineraries, and administrative detail that no other sources supply, but they also shaped events to flatter their patron and to justify his violence. Hostile or distant observers, notably the Syrian-born Ibn ʿArabshah, the great North African scholar Ibn Khaldun, and the Castilian envoy Ruy González de Clavijo, offer contrasting vantage points, though each has an agenda of his own. Numbers of dead, of deportees, and of soldiers are especially unreliable. The chapter treats such figures as reported claims, not as demographic data.

The chapter follows the geography of conquest. It begins with the political landscape inherited from the Ilkhanid collapse, examined in Chapter 51, and with Timur's rise in Transoxiana, his ideology, and his army (Division I). It then follows the invasion of eastern Iran from Herat to Sistan (Division II) and the campaigns across the northern, central, and southern plateau, culminating in Isfahan and Shiraz (Division III). Division IV places these campaigns within Timur's wider wars against the Jalayirids, the Golden Horde, the Mamluks, the Delhi Sultanate, and the Ottomans, while Division V turns to violence, deportation, uneven social consequences, and the question of durability.

Throughout, readers should keep in mind that the region discussed here does not map neatly onto the present-day state of Iran. Herat lies in modern Afghanistan, Samarqand in Uzbekistan, Tabriz and Isfahan in Iran, and Baghdad in Iraq; Timur's campaigns crossed all of these, and the chapter uses the historical regions (Khurasan, Azerbaijan, Fars, Iraq) rather than modern borders, in keeping with the principle established in Chapter 1.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the political fragmentation of Iran after the Ilkhans created opportunities for invasion without treating Timur's conquest as inevitable.
  2. Trace Timur's rise in Transoxiana and identify the resources that allowed him to campaign against Iranian successor states.
  3. Analyze why Timur ruled as amir and gürägän rather than as a Chinggisid khan, and how nominal Chinggisid rulers served his legitimacy.
  4. Describe the organization of the Timurid army, including cavalry, command, intelligence, logistics, and siege warfare.
  5. Reconstruct the chronological sequence of the Iranian conquest from Herat in 1381 to the destruction of Muzaffarid independence in 1393.
  6. Distinguish negotiated submission, punitive reprisal, and massacre as different outcomes of Timurid conquest, and explain what shaped each.
  7. Evaluate casualty figures and other numerical claims in court chronicles with appropriate caution.
  8. Assess the forced transfer of artisans, scholars, and others to Samarqand, separating its cultural results from the suffering of those removed.
  9. Compare the regional consequences of conquest for Iranian society, avoiding claims of uniform collapse or uniform recovery.
  10. Connect Iranian campaigns to Timur's wars in the Caucasus, Iraq, Syria, India, and Anatolia.
  11. Interpret court chronicles, hostile accounts, and later illustrated manuscripts as evidence, identifying the perspective each represents.
  12. Evaluate whether Timur reunified Iran or subjected it to another conquest empire whose durability remained unresolved at his death.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why could Timur defeat the Iranian successor states one after another, and what did their rivalries contribute to that outcome?
  2. What was the difference between conquering a territory and governing it, and how does that difference apply to Timur's Iran?
  3. Why did Timur claim legitimacy as an amir and imperial son-in-law, and how did he combine Chinggisid precedent with Islamic royal language?
  4. How much of Timur's success can be attributed to terror, and how much to organization, intelligence, logistics, and diplomacy?
  5. Why did some cities obtain terms of submission while others suffered catastrophic punishment?
  6. How can historians use sources written under Timur's patronage, and what does a hostile source add or distort?
  7. What did the removal of skilled workers to Samarqand mean for the receiving city and for the communities that lost them?
  8. In what ways did the consequences of conquest differ among Khurasan, Sistan, Mazandaran, Azerbaijan, Fars, and Iraq?
  9. Did Timur reunify Iran, or did he superimpose a conquest empire on a politically divided land?

Historical Context

The political background to Timur's invasions was the collapse of Ilkhanid authority in Iran after the death of Abu Saʿid in 1335, a process examined in Chapter 51. In the following decades, no single power controlled the plateau. In Khurasan the Kartid dynasty ruled from Herat; further west the Sarbadars, a movement with a strong Shiʿi coloration and a base at Sabzavar, governed parts of northwestern Khurasan until internal rivalry weakened them. In Fars, Kerman, and Isfahan the Muzaffarids held sway, while the Jalayirids, a Mongol-descended dynasty, controlled Iraq and much of Azerbaijan from Baghdad and Tabriz. In the Caspian lands and the Alborz foothills, local houses, including sayyid lineages in Mazandaran, ruled with a degree of autonomy that the geography of the region encouraged.

These states were not uniformly weak. Several possessed strong fortified cities, experienced administrators, and significant armies, and some commanded loyalty among their subjects. Their weakness was relative and political: they were divided from one another by dynastic ambition, by rival claims over the same territories, and in some cases by internal disputes over succession. No framework existed in which they could readily coordinate a common defense. At the same time, the fragmentation did not mean chaos. Cities continued to trade, scholars continued to teach, and local administrators continued to collect revenue; a conqueror entering such a landscape confronted real institutions, not a vacuum.

To the northeast lay Transoxiana, the land between the Amu Darya (Oxus) and Syr Darya rivers, where the old Chaghatayid realm, one of the Mongol successor states, had fragmented into rival commands. Its political life was dominated by military leaders of Turkic and Mongol background who commanded steppe confederations and sedentary populations alike. This was the milieu from which Timur emerged. It gave him access to mounted soldiers, commanders experienced in steppe warfare, and a tradition of Mongol-style military organization, but it also exposed him to constant competition from rivals who commanded similar resources. Samarqand and Bukhara, with their long urban and scholarly traditions, provided the wealth and administrative expertise that distinguished a successful ruler from a steppe raider.

The military setting also mattered. Mongol conquests in the thirteenth century had demonstrated the power of mobile cavalry combined with siege techniques drawn from sedentary societies, and the Mongol states that followed had inherited a tradition of campaign organization, communication, and provisioning. Timur's army drew on that inheritance. At the same time, the Iranian plateau presented real obstacles: mountain passes, desert margins, and fortified settlements that could not simply be bypassed. Understanding Timur's conquest means understanding how a force built on steppe cavalry adapted to a country of walled cities, irrigated oases, and long supply lines. That adaptation, and the political relationships that accompanied it, are the subject of the sections that follow.

Timur's Rise, Imperial Ambitions, and Military Power

Establishing the political landscape Timur entered, his origins in Transoxiana, the claims by which he justified rule, and the military organization that made conquest possible.

Section 52.1

Iran after the Ilkhans: Fragmentation and the Opportunity for Conquest

When Timur turned his attention to Iran in the 1380s, he did not enter a political vacuum or a single decaying state. He entered a landscape of regional powers that had grown out of the Ilkhanid collapse examined in Chapter 51. In Khurasan, the Kartid dynasty governed from Herat, a city with strong walls, fertile irrigated districts, and a long tradition of scholarship and craft production. In western Khurasan, around Sabzavar, a regime associated with the Sarbadar movement survived, although its internal rivalries had by then eroded its coherence. Across the Alborz, in the Caspian lands, Marʿashi sayyid families and other local lords held mountainous and forested territory. In Fars, Kerman, and Isfahan the Muzaffarids ruled; and in Iraq and Azerbaijan the Jalayirids held Baghdad and Tabriz, the two great commercial and political centers of the west.

Historians often describe this configuration as fragmentation, but the word needs care. Each of these states had a functioning apparatus: revenue collectors, armies, fortresses, and networks of local notables. They were not helpless. What they lacked was a framework in which to combine. They competed with one another over territory, sometimes fought dynastic wars among their own princes, and had no mechanism for common action against an outside power. A ruler whose neighbors would likely welcome his defeat had little reason to trust that any appeal for help would be answered. Timur, whose campaigns proceeded region by region, could exploit this isolation by confronting each state separately.

Four regions mattered especially. Khurasan, with Herat and Nishapur and the routes running toward Transoxiana, was the closest to Timur's base and the natural first target. Azerbaijan, centered on Tabriz, controlled commercial routes toward Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Black Sea, and also offered rich pasturage for the horses of a mounted army. Fars, with Shiraz and its hinterland, was a prosperous agricultural and urban province in the south. Iraq, with Baghdad, linked the plateau to Syria and the Persian Gulf. Control of these regions gave a conqueror revenue, manpower, and strategic security against rivals; each also contained a political authority that would either submit or be removed.

It would be a mistake to read this situation as one in which Timur's conquest was waiting to happen. Nothing in the political geography made it certain that an outside conqueror would succeed, or that he would succeed so quickly. Timur's invasions depended on contingent factors: the defeat of his rivals in Transoxiana, the availability of a large and experienced army, his willingness to commit that army to repeated campaigns far from home, and the particular decisions of the Iranian rulers he confronted. Some chose submission, others resistance, and some changed their minds. The story that follows is one of choices under pressure, not of a foreordained reunification of the plateau under a single hand.

Conquest depended in part on the political information available to the invader. A commander approaching a province could learn which prince was estranged from his relatives, which city feared the demands of its current government, and which roads offered access to pasture and grain. Such knowledge did not erase local resistance; it allowed Timur to choose the order in which opponents would be confronted. The political divisions described in Chapter 51 therefore mattered operationally. The fall of one court could deprive another of a potential ally, and a ruler displaced from a city might continue campaigning elsewhere. The relevant unit of analysis is not merely a colored territory on a retrospective map but the shifting relationship among armies, fortifications, households, and urban communities.

Section 52.2

Timur's Origins and the Struggle for Transoxiana

Timur was born, according to the traditional date, around 1336 in the vicinity of Kish (modern Shahr-i Sabz) in Transoxiana. He belonged to the Barlas, a Turkicized Mongol group whose members had settled in the region after the Mongol conquests and become part of the military establishment of the Chaghatayid realm. The Barlas were not Chinggisids; they were a tribal military elite of considerable standing but not of royal descent. The distinction mattered a great deal for how Timur later presented himself, as Section 52.3 explains. Sources composed for Timur's family sometimes embellish his genealogy, and the details of his early life are known chiefly from later, favorable narratives, which should be read with caution.

His world was shaped by the decline of Chaghatayid authority. Throughout the mid-fourteenth century, the Chaghatayid khans of Transoxiana had lost control to powerful commanders who ruled districts and kept rival nominal khans. Such leaders controlled mounted followers and fortified bases and fought one another for supremacy. Timur emerged as one of these figures, building a following among Barlas relatives and other military groups. Accounts of his youth include periods of exile, small-scale raiding, and shifting alliances, but the specific episodes are hard to verify.

A decisive relationship in his rise was his partnership and then rivalry with Amir Husayn, a prominent regional commander and, according to the sources, a member of an important family of the region and a relative by marriage. The two cooperated for a time and then fell out, and the conflict between them occupied much of the 1360s. In 1370, Husayn was defeated and killed near Balkh, and Timur was acknowledged as the leading power in Transoxiana. He made Samarqand his principal seat. The city, a major commercial and cultural center, supplied revenue, craftsmen, and a symbolic setting for his rule, and Timur would devote enormous resources to its adornment in the years that followed.

From this base, Timur consolidated his position through campaigns against neighboring powers: in Khwarazm, in Moghulistan to the northeast, and elsewhere, over a period of years. These operations served several purposes. They eliminated rivals, secured the borders of his core territory, and gave his commanders experience and booty. They also built the coalition on which his later campaigns depended. His army was composed of commanders and followers drawn from many groups, whose loyalty depended on success, rewards, and the personal authority he cultivated. The essential point for this chapter is that by the late 1370s Timur controlled enough resources, and enough military experience, to look beyond Transoxiana. The decision to turn toward Iran came after the securing of his base, not before it, and it was part of a long pattern in which every successful campaign provided the means for the next.

The settlement of 1370 should not be pictured as the sudden creation of a bureaucratically unified empire. Timur had to manage the interests of men who had followed him through a decade of unstable alliances. Victories supplied horses, captives, and movable wealth, but a campaign also required commanders to accept assignments, remain loyal during absences, and return with enough rewards to sustain their followers. The attraction of the next conquest was therefore entangled with the demands of the military coalition already assembled. Samarqand served as a symbolic center and a place where extracted goods and expertise could be concentrated, but Timur repeatedly left it for long expeditions. That recurrent movement was a source of his strength and a constraint upon the kind of continuous provincial supervision his successors would later seek.

Section 52.3

Amir, Gürägän, and the Legitimacy of Imperial Ambition

Timur never took the title of khan. Under the Mongol imperial tradition, as it was understood in the fourteenth century, the title of khan, the sovereign ruler, was restricted to descendants of Chinggis Khan. Timur was not one. He therefore ruled principally as amir, a title meaning commander or lord, and he governed through a Chinggisid figurehead: first Suyurghatmish, installed in 1370, and after Suyurghatmish's death, Sultan Mahmud, whose death is dated approximately 1402–03 in modern reconstructions. Coins and official documents could carry the khan's name alongside Timur's. The arrangement allowed Timur to exercise real power while preserving the principle that legitimate sovereignty belonged to the Chinggisid house.

Timur also strengthened his position through marriage. He married Saray Mulk Khanum, a woman of Chinggisid descent, and acquired the title gürägän, usually rendered "imperial son-in-law." The title appears in Timur's titulature and in the chronicles written under his patronage. It did not make him a Chinggisid, but it tied him to the family and gave him a recognized place within the Mongol imperial order. The Barlas commander thus presented himself as a participant in an existing system of legitimacy, not as the founder of a rival one.

Historical Vocabulary
Gürägän (imperial son-in-law)

Definition: A Mongol-derived title meaning "son-in-law," applied to men who had married into the Chinggisid family and thereby gained a recognized relationship to the ruling house.

Historical context: Timur used the title throughout his reign to indicate his connection to the Chinggisids through his marriage. It appears in the chronicles composed under his patronage and in the formal designations used by his court.

Why it matters: The title shows how Timur justified a power he could not claim by descent. It linked him to the Mongol imperial order without requiring him to claim the title of khan.

Potential misunderstanding: Gürägän did not mean that Timur was himself a Chinggisid or a khan, and it should not be read as a mere honorific. It gave real, if indirect, standing within a political culture that valued association with the house of Chinggis Khan.

Timur's legitimacy also had an Islamic dimension. He presented himself as a Muslim ruler, patronized religious scholars and sayyids, supported shrines, and invoked the duty to maintain order and justice. His court chroniclers described his campaigns in terms of divine favor and the suppression of disorder. Timur also directed resources toward religious building, notably the major shrine complex of Ahmad Yasavi at Yasi (modern Turkistan). Such patronage helped to present conquest as the work of a pious ruler. At the same time, Timur attacked Muslim cities, and the religious rhetoric of his chroniclers coexisted with the violence of his armies. Rulers of the period often described their wars in Islamic language, and the question for historians is how far such language reflected sincere conviction, how far political convenience, and how far the preferences of the scholars who wrote it.

A further layer of legitimation concerned order. Chroniclers represented Timur as a restorer of stability in a region divided by competing rulers. This claim had some plausibility to audiences who had experienced the instability of the post-Ilkhanid decades, but it was also a way of justifying invasion. Distinguishing between what Timur claimed about himself, what his chroniclers claimed on his behalf, and what he actually did is one of the main tasks of the historian, and later sections return to it repeatedly. Some of the most elaborate idealization of Timur belongs to accounts written after his death, under his successors, who had their own reasons to portray the founder in glowing terms.

The Chinggisid claim was more than a matter of etiquette. Timur maintained titular khans, first Suyurghatmish and then Sultan Mahmud, both generally identified as descendants of Ögedei in modern scholarship, while exercising command himself. A ruler's name on coinage or in public ceremonial language could assert dynastic propriety, but political initiative lay with the amir who disposed of troops and appointments. The arrangement made it possible to honor the inherited conventions of the Chaghatayid world while pursuing ambitions that extended far beyond it. Nor should the relationship be described as though every titular khan were necessarily a powerless object: scholarship has drawn attention to the continued military and symbolic activity of particular figures. The historian should ask which kinds of authority each participant exercised rather than assume that royal title and actual command always coincided.

Timur's marriage connections and his religious patronage provided additional registers of legitimacy. His claims could be presented differently to Mongol military households, urban Muslim jurists, provincial landholders, and conquered rulers. The resulting language was adaptable rather than logically seamless. A chronicle describing conquest as restoration of justice is evidence about the court's desired public image; it is not independent proof that an occupied district experienced justice. This interpretive distinction is especially important when the same author recounts the protection of a submitting town and the punishment of another after an uprising.

Zafarnama

Definition: "Book of Victory," a title used for several Persian-language chronicles of Timur's campaigns.

Historical context: Nizam al-Din Shami composed the earliest, begun at Timur's order and completed around 1404. A later work with the same title was written by Sharaf al-Din Yazdi in the 1420s, drawing on earlier sources including Shami. Hafiz-i Abru's historical writings, produced under Timur's son Shah Rukh, are a further major source.

Why it matters: These works supply the chronology, itineraries, and administrative detail of the campaigns. Their authors, however, wrote for the ruling family, and they emphasize victory, divine favor, and legitimacy.

Potential misunderstanding: The shared title does not mean that the works are identical or equally close to the events. Shami wrote within Timur's lifetime, Yazdi after his death, and each reflects its patron's interests.

Section 52.4

The Timurid Army: Cavalry, Command, Logistics, and Siege Warfare

Timur's conquests rested on a formidable military organization. At its center was mounted cavalry, drawn from Turkic and Mongol groups of Transoxiana and its borderlands, who fought as mobile archers able to cover long distances. This cavalry was organized according to traditions inherited from the Mongol empire, in which units were nominally structured by the decimal system, with larger formations conventionally called tümen (units of ten thousand), though the figures should not be taken literally and the system had evolved considerably by Timur's time. Commanders, usually called amirs, led contingents composed of their own followers, and the loyalty of these men depended on the reputation, rewards, and success of the leader at the top.

The army also relied on a network of command relationships anchored in the ruling family. Timur gave important commands to his sons and grandsons, and to trusted officers, and he assigned territories and revenues to commanders in return for military service. The force was not a single homogeneous body. As the campaigns proceeded, it incorporated soldiers from conquered regions, including infantry and specialists drawn from sedentary populations in Khurasan and elsewhere. Historians such as Beatrice Forbes Manz have stressed that Timur's army combined steppe military traditions with the resources of settled societies, and that this combination gave him flexibility that purely nomadic armies could lack.

Siege warfare was essential, because Iran was a land of walled cities and fortresses that could not be ignored. Timur's forces employed siege engines, mining, and the building of earthworks, and they could use engineers and laborers drawn from the sedentary world. The sources describe operations against fortifications in detail, though specific claims about technology should be treated cautiously. Equally important was the ability to coordinate large forces across mountains and deserts, which required scouts, messengers, and officers able to organize supplies of fodder, water, and food. Timur's campaigns often began in spring or autumn, timed to the availability of pasture, and he sometimes wintered in regions with grazing.

Intelligence and diplomacy supported force. The sources show Timur sending envoys, soliciting defections, and exploiting divisions among his enemies. Rulers who were at odds with their own relatives or officials might find that Timur offered terms that appealed to some of their followers. The reputation for severity that Timur cultivated also worked in his favor, since cities that expected harsh punishment for resistance had reason to consider submission. But it would be a distortion to attribute every victory to terror. Organization, supply, information, and the exploitation of political divisions all contributed, and the ability to keep a large army together over years of campaigning was itself a notable achievement.

The army also had costs. It required constant feeding and payment, which meant that campaigns were partly sustained by plunder, tribute, and the extraction of resources from conquered territories. This dependence on booty helps explain both the severity of Timurid exactions and the pressure to keep campaigning. An army that was not winning, and not rewarding its commanders, risked losing the loyalty that held it together. The relationship between military organization and the demands of conquest therefore shaped the experience of the populations discussed in the following divisions.

Campaign planning joined military and political calculation. Steppe cavalry could move rapidly, but a large mounted force required seasonal access to forage and dependable intelligence about river crossings, valleys, and fortified roads. When confronting a walled city, commanders needed to isolate its defenders, protect their own communications, and find means of attacking or negotiating past fortifications. Experienced soldiers alone could not produce victory without engineers, interpreters, messengers, and a supply apparatus. Captives and coerced local labor might be pressed into these tasks, distributing the costs of a campaign well beyond the men counted as combatants.

Division II · Sections 52.5–52.8

The Conquest of Eastern Iran, 1381–1385

The invasion of Khurasan, the fate of established regional governments, and the first major consequences of Timurid conquest.

Section 52.5

The Campaign against Herat and the Kartids, 1381

The conquest of Iran began in Khurasan. Timur had been drawn into the region's politics through contacts with its rulers and through his earlier campaigns on its borders, and in 1380 he began the advance that led, in the spring of 1381, to Herat. The Kartid ruler, Malik Ghiyath al-Din Pir ʿAli, faced a choice. Herat possessed strong fortifications and a population accustomed to defending itself, but the Kartids were unlikely to receive reinforcement from neighboring states, and the cost of resistance against a large army could be heavy. According to the principal narratives, the ruler and the notables of Herat submitted in April 1381.

Submission was not simply surrender. It involved a negotiated settlement in which the city received a promise of protection in return for tribute and recognition of Timur's overlordship. The chronicles report the payment of substantial sums and the dismantling of Herat's fortifications, a measure that removed the city's ability to resist a future change of mind. The walls that had made Herat a formidable place were thus turned into a guarantee of its subordination. Herat was not sacked on this occasion, and its population was spared the massacre that would later strike other cities.

Historical Vocabulary
Amān (safe-conduct, quarter)

Definition: In Islamic political and legal practice, a promise of safety given to individuals, communities, or cities, typically in exchange for submission.

Historical context: Timur's chronicles frequently describe cities as receiving amān after surrendering and paying tribute. The promise could be honored or violated depending on circumstances, and later rebellion could cancel its protection in the view of the conqueror.

Why it matters: The concept helps explain the structure of Timurid conquest: submission offered a path that could limit immediate destruction, while resistance exposed a population to punishment.

Potential misunderstanding: An amān was not a guarantee of autonomy, and it did not prevent heavy financial demands, the removal of fortifications, or later deportation of skilled people.

Submission also left certain local institutions in place for a time. The Kartid ruler, local officials, and the city's elites continued to play a role, but within a new framework of dependence. Timurid representatives and troops were stationed in the region, and the authority exercised in the name of local rulers was ultimately subject to Timur's command. This pattern, in which existing structures were retained but subordinated, recurs throughout the conquest and illustrates the difference between nominal continuity and real political change.

It is important to distinguish the submission of Herat in 1381 from the final elimination of the Kartids. Disturbances in Herat in the following years led to further Timurid intervention, discussed in Section 52.7, and the Kartid ruler Pir ʿAli was ultimately removed. The sources place his execution in 1389. For students, the chronology is a reminder that the apparently decisive moment of surrender in 1381 was only the first stage of a process, and that a negotiated submission could be followed by rebellion and harsher treatment. The people of Herat experienced conquest not as a single event but as a sequence of demands, reprisals, and adjustments extending over almost a decade.

Herat's surrender was shaped by conflicts inside the Kartid political order. The historian of Herat records tensions between the ruler Ghiyath al-Din II Pir ʿAli and his brother, while sections of the landed and administrative elite maintained ties to Transoxiana. The Kartid vizier Muʿin al-Din Jami is specifically associated with a faction favorable to Timur's intervention. These were not merely personal intrigues: they influenced whether townspeople and commanders judged resistance viable. The advancing army had first attacked Bushanj, and Herat's decision followed some initial fighting rather than an entirely unopposed arrival. A calculation that preserved lives during the first occupation nevertheless surrendered significant political freedom. The city accepted the payment known as mal-i aman, a substantial levy connected with the promised safety of inhabitants who had not fought.

Timur ordered the demolition of the city's fortifications, and the surviving account places Kartid treasures and the iron gates of Herat among the objects sent to Shahr-i Sabz in Transoxiana. These details give a more precise meaning to submission than the simple phrase 'peaceful conquest.' Fortifications were a means of collective defense and could also enable independent negotiation in a future crisis; removing them altered the city's political options. Miran Shah was installed as Timur's deputy while important Kartid officials remained. The arrangement preserved administrative capacity but did so within a hierarchy whose ultimate coercive power lay outside Herat. When the city experienced renewed revolt in 1383, that earlier settlement did not prevent punitive intervention, renewed exactions, or the deportation of skilled inhabitants. Thus Herat illustrates successive phases of conquest: negotiation, supervision, resistance, and extraction.

Section 52.6

Sabzavar, the Sarbadars, and the Submission of Western Khurasan

From Herat, Timur's forces moved into the territories of western Khurasan, where the surviving Sarbadar government was centered on Sabzavar. The Sarbadars, whose movement had emerged in the 1330s and 1340s amid the breakdown of Ilkhanid power, had a history of internal conflict, with factions contending for control, and by the early 1380s their regime was a shadow of its earlier self. The leader during these years was Khwaja ʿAli Muʾayyad, who confronted a decision similar to that of the Kartid ruler.

The sources indicate that ʿAli Muʾayyad submitted to Timur and, for a period, cooperated with him, accompanying the Timurid army in operations in the region. The Sarbadars' internal divisions shaped their choices. A leader whose authority was contested at home, and whose resources were limited, had little capacity to withstand a large invading force. Submission offered a means of surviving politically, at least for a time. The result was that the Sarbadar state ceased to exist as an independent power, even if its former leader retained a position under Timurid overlordship.

Comparing Sabzavar with Herat is instructive. In both cases rulers submitted without a prolonged siege, and in both cases the submission preserved elements of local authority while ending sovereign independence. The conquest of eastern Iran thus involved negotiated surrender as well as coercion. Local rulers could choose among submission, resistance, and conditional cooperation, and each choice carried risks. The advantage of submission was that it might limit immediate violence; the disadvantage was that it exposed the ruler and the region to the exactions of the conqueror and left them vulnerable to later accusations of disloyalty. Resistance could succeed only if the defenders could hold out until the invader withdrew or was distracted, which was uncertain given Timur's resources.

The later history of Sabzavar and its region shows the limits of settlement. Reports of later disturbances and Timurid punitive operations in western Khurasan, which belong to the events of 1383 discussed next, indicate that submission in 1381 did not secure permanent peace. Narrative sources written under the Timurids sometimes describe harsh punishments in Sabzavar, but these accounts, which employ conventional language of spectacular violence, require critical reading. What can be said with confidence is that the Sarbadar regime was absorbed into the Timurid system and that the region continued to experience Timurid military pressure.

The Sarbadar case also shows that a conqueror's success could depend upon the survival of a faction rather than upon the defeat of an entire society. By the 1380s the political movement founded decades earlier at Sabzavar had outgrown its original local uprising, and its rulers had to balance military supporters, city notables, and religious networks. Khwaja ʿAli Muʾayyad was not simply an impersonal representative of the Sarbadars: the decision to accommodate Timur concerned his own survival and the rivalries that threatened his position. Acknowledgment of outside supremacy might secure support against immediate domestic opponents, even while it exposed the ruler to new demands for money and armed service.

The terms used by later historians can conceal the practical choices of ordinary residents. A city whose ruling household agreed to submit might avoid storming, yet still supply food, housing, transport, and military manpower. Merchants could retain their shops but face extraordinary levies; religious leaders might be asked to legitimate the new settlement; soldiers formerly responsible to a local dynasty could be summoned to a campaign beyond their district. Describing such a city as 'spared' is defensible only when the comparison is with immediate physical destruction, not when it implies that the population experienced no coercion.

Section 52.7

Rebellion, Isfizar, and the Devastation of Sistan

The year 1383 brought renewed disturbances in Khurasan and a series of punitive operations that showed the harsher side of Timurid conquest. Resistance emerged in places that had submitted, including Herat, whose population and ruler were accused of disloyalty. Timur's response was to send forces to suppress the disturbances, and the Kartid ruler Pir ʿAli, whose position was already compromised, was removed from effective power in this period. The final execution in 1389 belongs to a later phase.

One of the notable episodes of these operations was the siege of Isfizar, a fortified place in the Herat region (near modern Shindand in Afghanistan). Isfizar was taken after resistance, and the sources describe severe treatment of its defenders and population. Here the historian faces the conventions of medieval conquest narratives. Court chroniclers described the punishment of rebels in stereotyped terms, with reports of mass executions and displays of heads, and such descriptions served to demonstrate the consequences of defiance. The reliability of specific details, including numbers, is uncertain, but the fact that Isfizar was stormed and its population punished is generally accepted.

From Khurasan Timur proceeded to Sistan, the region around the Hilmand river, whose capital was Zaranj. Sistan was a region whose prosperity depended on irrigation works drawing water from the Hilmand delta. Timur's forces captured Zaranj in 1383 and devastated the region. The sources report the destruction of irrigation infrastructure and the killing of many inhabitants, and modern historians generally accept that Sistan suffered severely. Because Sistan's agriculture depended on a delicate system of canals and dikes, damage to this infrastructure could have lasting consequences for the region's economy. The extent and permanence of that damage, however, are difficult to measure from the available evidence, which consists largely of chronicle statements.

These operations illustrate several mechanisms by which conquest affected local societies. Resistance could lead to siege, which meant direct violence against defenders and, if the city fell, against civilians. Punitive taxation took money from communities that had survived. Destruction of infrastructure, whether deliberate or incidental, harmed agricultural production, and forced displacement removed people from their homes. These effects accumulated: a district that suffered several of them in succession could be reduced to a fraction of its former prosperity, while a neighboring region that had submitted early might suffer little. The variety of experience is one of the chapter's central themes.

Herat's second crisis reveals the fragility of the original settlement. The 1383 rising is associated in the chronicle tradition with a figure remembered as Ghuribacha, or 'the boy from Ghur,' and with support from the Ghur region. Miran Shah took part in restoring Timurid control. Even when later sources disagree about individual responsibility, the sequence is clear enough to demonstrate that the occupation had not extinguished independent military networks in the hinterland. The punitive aftermath included new demands upon the city and the removal of craftsmen, artists, and religious scholars. A political decision made by rulers was thus experienced as taxation, insecurity, and family separation by people who had no comparable freedom to negotiate.

Sistan presents a different kind of vulnerability. Its agricultural productivity rested on channels and settlements around the Helmand basin; destructive military operations could damage an interlocking system of waterways, embankments, labor arrangements, and cultivation. Narratives of ruined canals cannot by themselves specify the percentage of land that ceased to be farmed, but they identify the mechanism by which a short military campaign could have longer economic effects. The damaged system might require not merely funds but the restoration of labor, expertise, and agreements about water. A city could therefore return to formal political obedience while the surrounding countryside continued to bear the consequences of conquest.

Section 52.8

The Eastern Conquest: Local Elites, Garrisons, and Forced Transfers

By the mid-1380s, eastern Iran had been brought under Timurid control, and the character of that control can now be assessed. Timur did not replace the existing administrative apparatus wholesale. He relied on selected local officials, notables, and religious figures who could collect revenue and maintain order, while appointing military commanders and, increasingly, members of his own family to supervise the region. Khurasan was entrusted to his son Miran Shah in the 1380s, as the principal Timurid authority, although the arrangements of provincial rule belong to the subject of Chapter 53.

The terms of submission typically included the payment of tribute, the provision of troops and supplies, and sometimes the surrender of hostages. Timur also confiscated property and took wealth from those who resisted, and garrisons were placed in strategic locations. These measures extracted military and material resources from the region, but they also required cooperation from local elites, who understood local conditions and could mediate between the conqueror and the population. A ruler who destroyed all local institutions would have found it difficult to collect taxes or secure supplies, and the survival of some existing officials was therefore a practical necessity as well as a form of accommodation.

Local elites faced difficult choices. Some profited from cooperation, retaining wealth and status under the new regime; others lost power, property, or their lives. Religious scholars and sayyids, whom Timur patronized in line with his image as a Muslim ruler, sometimes found their position strengthened, while military and bureaucratic families linked to the displaced dynasties were more vulnerable. The experience of different groups varied with their relationship to the previous government and their usefulness to the conqueror.

Specialist research distinguishes the Jaʾun-i Qurbani Mongols transferred from Tus and Kalat in 1383 from the scholars and craftsmen later removed from Tabriz in 1386. The different social groups and potential uses of deportation are examined in Section 52.18. Both types of forced transfer linked conquered Iran to the demands of the imperial center, but neither should be represented as a voluntary relocation.

The most important distinction in the evidence about population transfer is between different kinds of deportees. The group removed from Tus and Kalat in 1383 is identified in specialist scholarship as the Jaʾun-i Qurbani Mongols, a population whose military and pastoral affiliations made its forced movement different in purpose from the later transfer of individual urban craftsmen. In 1386, by contrast, the sources specifically identify scholars, artisans, and craftsmen removed from Tabriz to Samarqand. Both episodes belong to a policy of treating people as movable imperial resources, but they were not identical in social composition, likely work, or relationship to military security. To describe all the 1383 deportees as builders or craft specialists would obscure this evidence.

The material exactions made during conquest likewise need separating. A tribute settlement transferred wealth but might leave many local institutions in place; the confiscation of a rival family's estates redistributed the fiscal base of government; the deportation of a trained worker removed a person whose skills and relationships were embedded in a household and city. These measures could be combined, and their effects could accumulate. Historians should resist drawing an overall provincial recovery rate from individual episodes of submission or destruction. What survives is a more uneven record of specific political bargains and their local consequences.

Division III · Sections 52.9–52.12

The Conquest of Northern, Central, and Southern Iran

The decisive campaigns against regional Iranian powers in the 1380s and early 1390s, from the Caspian approaches and Tabriz to Isfahan and Shiraz.

Section 52.9

Mazandaran, Astarabad, Rayy, and the Central Plateau

From Khurasan, Timur's forces advanced westward along the corridor between the Alborz Mountains and the desert interior, through Damghan and toward Rayy and Sultaniyya, and northward toward the Caspian lands of Astarabad and Mazandaran. The geography of this region shaped the campaigns. The Alborz range separates the dry plateau from the humid Caspian coast and complicates movement; passes are few, forests dense, and fortified settlements can be defended with difficulty to attack. Routes linking Khurasan with western Iran passed along the southern flank of the mountains, making cities such as Rayy and Damghan strategic points.

Timurid operations in the 1380s extended Timur's influence across this region. Astarabad was among the places entered in the middle of the decade, and Timurid forces moved toward Rayy and the Sultaniyya region, an important center that had been a favored city of the Ilkhans. These campaigns met local resistance and produced shifting alliances: local rulers who submitted might later revolt, and those who resisted might eventually be forced to terms. The advance was thus a gradual extension of influence, not a single conquest of the central plateau.

Mazandaran, with its Sayyid-led local authorities, posed a different challenge. The Marʿashi sayyids, descendants of the Prophet who ruled the region's mountains and coastal plains, had maintained a measure of independence. In the 1380s Timur's influence reached the region, but its full subjugation came later. The sources describe campaigns in Mazandaran in the early 1390s, during which the region's leaders were defeated and their authority ended, and some of the sayyids were removed from the region. The sequence illustrates the distinction between the initial extension of Timurid influence in the 1380s and the later subjugation of remaining opposition during the campaigns of the 1390s, a distinction that students should keep in mind when reading summaries that compress many years of campaigning into a single narrative.

The conquest of the central plateau and the Caspian lands also had strategic motives. Control of the routes between Khurasan and Azerbaijan secured communication between Timur's eastern gains and his intended western targets. It also deprived rivals of potential bases. The region's mountainous character meant that local populations could resist for long periods, and Timurid forces had to adapt, using forces that could operate in difficult terrain and relying on the cooperation of local guides and allies.

The final conquest of Mazandaran gives names and decisions to the regional struggle. The Marʿashi sayyid rulers had secured authority in the Caspian districts through religious and military networks that were not readily displaced by the occupation of plateau towns. Eskandar Shaykhi, a descendant of the earlier Afrasiyabid political house who had entered Timur's service, became a principal local ally of the invader. According to the specialist reconstruction, the Baduspanid ruler Saʿd al-Dawla Tus initially appeared to offer the Marʿashis a counterweight to Eskandar but later joined Timur's forces at Astarabad in 1392. Such reversals explain why the political geography of the region cannot be reduced to a contest between an external army and an undifferentiated local population.

Timur's campaign of 1393 removed the Marʿashi government from its former position; Amol and Sari were attacked, and captured sayyid leaders were deported after intervention on their behalf rather than universally executed. Eskandar Shaykhi received authority in Amol. This outcome illustrates the multiple techniques of subjugation: seizure of towns, forced removal of a ruling network, and appointment of a local rival dependent upon the conqueror. It also demonstrates why control could remain unstable. The appointee's interests were not identical to those of Timur, and later conflicts testified to the continuing capacity of Caspian groups to maneuver. The longer history belongs to the next chapter; for the conquest narrative, the point is that regional alliances shaped both the violence and the settlement.

Section 52.10

Azerbaijan, Tabriz, and the Struggle for Northwestern Iran

Northwestern Iran presented Timur with both opportunity and rivalry. Azerbaijan, centered on Tabriz, was among the richest regions of the plateau. Tabriz had been a major Ilkhanid capital and commercial city, and the region's pastures supported the horses on which armies depended. Its routes led toward Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Black Sea. For the Jalayirid ruler Sultan Ahmad, Tabriz was a principal seat, but his authority in the region was contested by local military groups and by outside powers, most notably the Golden Horde under Tokhtamysh.

The involvement of Tokhtamysh complicates the story. Timur had once supported Tokhtamysh's rise to power in the Golden Horde, but their relationship turned to rivalry. In 1385 Tokhtamysh invaded Azerbaijan and attacked Tabriz, a development that both demonstrated the weakness of Sultan Ahmad's position and threatened Timur's own interests. The region thus became a contested frontier among the Jalayirids, the Golden Horde, and Timur, with local military groups pursuing their own agendas.

In 1386, Timur occupied Tabriz. Sultan Ahmad, whose authority was already weakened, withdrew, and the city came under Timurid control. The sources suggest that Tabriz was not subjected to the sort of massacre inflicted on some other cities, but it suffered heavy exactions, and skilled workers were removed from it, as Section 52.18 discusses. The fall of Tabriz exposed the difficulty of securing a contested frontier: Timur could take the city, but holding Azerbaijan against rivals required continuing military effort, and the pastures and routes that made the region valuable also made it a magnet for competing powers.

Timur's operations in the northwest in these years also extended into the Caucasus, with campaigns toward Georgia, discussed in Section 52.14. The histories of the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu Turkmen confederations, which also contended for the region, belong to Chapter 55 and are not narrated here. The point for present purposes is that the northwest was both a prize and a point of vulnerability, linking Timur's Iranian conquests to a wider Eurasian struggle.

Tabriz was a strategic prize but also a vulnerable commercial center. Control of its markets could provide customs income, access to caravan brokers, and an established network of artisans; control of the surrounding grazing lands offered logistical advantages to a mounted army. Those benefits were inseparable from the city's exposure to competing powers. A raid by the Golden Horde in 1385 and Timur's occupation in the following year did not represent two steps in a single orderly succession: they were interventions by rivals with different claims and military objectives. Local merchants, officials, and neighborhoods had to respond to authority that could change more rapidly than trading connections or agricultural seasons.

The absence of an Isfahan-style massacre in 1386 did not mean that Tabriz escaped the costs of conquest. Its position made it a source of tribute and coerced transfer. Contemporary reports, as discussed in the scholarly literature, specifically describe the removal of scholars, artisans, and craftsmen toward Samarqand. Because the city's productive capacity depended in part on skilled workers and commercial relationships, the removal of people could have effects that are not captured by a list of buildings left standing. Timur could exploit the city as an imperial resource while leaving its later defense and administration to subordinates who themselves confronted rival military coalitions.

Section 52.11

The Capture of Isfahan and the Massacre of 1387

The events at Isfahan in 1387 are among the most severe of the Iranian conquest. After campaigning in the northwest and the Caucasus, Timur advanced into central and southern Iran in 1387, and Isfahan, a major city under Muzaffarid authority, came into his hands. According to the chronicles, the city submitted and was subjected to the demands of the conqueror, including the payment of a heavy levy. Timurid agents, and troops placed in the city, were charged with collecting the sums owed. Isfahan thus entered the Timurid system through submission, in the same pattern as Herat.

The pattern then broke. The sources report that the people of Isfahan rose against Timur's agents and killed a number of them. The chronicles vary in their accounts of how the uprising began, who led it, and how many Timurid soldiers or officials died, and the reasons they supply reflect their own purposes. Some emphasize the burden of the exactions; others emphasize the disorder or disloyalty of the population. What the sources agree on is that a rising took place and that Timur responded with extreme retaliation.

The reprisal was a massacre. Timur ordered the punishment of the city, and his troops killed a large number of its inhabitants. The sources describe the killing of civilians, the destruction of neighborhoods, and the displacement of survivors, together with the confiscation of property. Some accounts add that certain groups, including religious figures, were spared, and that skilled workers were taken to Samarqand, though these reports should be weighed with the other evidence. The attack ended livelihoods as well as lives: the city's markets, workshops, and households were disrupted, and the recovery of Isfahan was a longer story.

The casualty figures need to be handled with great care. Contemporary and later sources report a death toll of 70,000. This figure appears in the tradition, but it cannot be accepted as an independently verified demographic total. Medieval chroniclers used large numbers to convey magnitude and to demonstrate the consequences of rebellion, and the figure may reflect conventions of reporting, such as the counting of heads set in towers or the repetition of a number from earlier accounts, rather than an actual census. It is also uncertain how large Isfahan's population was at the time. The appropriate conclusion is not that the massacre was exaggerated into existence. Multiple sources document it, and it is generally recognized as a historically documented atrocity. The point is that its scale cannot be quantified reliably, and a responsible account reports the number as a claim.

It is necessary to reconstruct the sequence carefully because the events have often been reduced to an image of indiscriminate savagery detached from its political setting. Initial surrender placed Isfahan under a new occupying authority, with demands for money and supplies. The killing of officials and soldiers associated with that authority then made the city a target of collective punishment. The courtly narratives explain the response by emphasizing rebellion, but their account does not establish that every person killed had taken part in the uprising or had accepted the decisions of those who led it. The unequal relation between a ruling army and a civilian population makes the distinction essential. Collective retaliation cannot be understood merely as the battlefield consequence of combat between armies.

The impact extended beyond the immediately dead. A massacre disrupted households, markets, urban religious institutions, and the links between Isfahan and cultivated districts that supplied it. People who escaped violence could still lose property, relatives, professional associations, or the capacity to resume their former occupations. An account written for Timur might describe a restoration of control as the conclusion of the episode; a social history must ask what it meant to survive that conclusion. The city remained on important routes and did not vanish, but the destruction contributed to the vulnerability of its urban economy in subsequent decades. Modern historians can affirm the enormity of the event without pretending that a medieval round number gives a census of the dead.

Section 52.12

Shiraz, Shah Mansur, and the Destruction of Muzaffarid Independence

Fars, with its capital at Shiraz, was the heartland of the Muzaffarid dynasty. The dynasty had been weakened by succession disputes after the death of the ruler Shah Shuja in 1384, which left princes of the house contending for power. When Timur approached in 1387, the Muzaffarids could not agree on a common response. According to the sources, the principal ruler left Shiraz while other members of the dynasty and the city's officials chose submission. Shiraz accepted Timur's authority in 1387, thereby avoiding the fate of Isfahan.

This initial submission must be distinguished from the later destruction of the dynasty. Timur did not remain in Fars, and in 1388 he was called back to Transoxiana by the invasion of Tokhtamysh and a rebellion in Khwarazm. In his absence, the Muzaffarid princes resumed their struggles, and the dynasty's fortunes revived under Shah Mansur, a vigorous commander who emerged as the main figure of resistance. Shah Mansur established control over important parts of Fars and the surrounding regions and prepared to fight a Timurid army.

Timur returned in the early 1390s, after dealing with Tokhtamysh and the Caspian lands, and in 1393 the decisive confrontation occurred near Shiraz. The sources portray Shah Mansur as fighting with great determination, and the battle is described as hard-fought. He was defeated and killed, and the Muzaffarid dynasty's independence was destroyed. Captured members of the dynasty were treated severely, and many were killed, though some were dispersed. The elimination of Muzaffarid rule ended the last major independent Iranian dynasty of the post-Ilkhanid period in the south.

The political significance of Fars and Shiraz lay in their wealth, their strategic position between the plateau and the Persian Gulf, and their symbolic importance as a center of Iranian dynastic tradition. Provincial officials and urban elites faced the same difficult choices seen elsewhere: to cooperate with the conqueror, to support a resisting prince, or to preserve themselves by neutrality. After 1393, members of Timur's family were assigned to Fars as governors, beginning a period of Timurid rule whose organization belongs to Chapter 53. Together, Divisions II and III establish the principal sequence of the conquest of Iran: negotiated submissions, punitive reprisals, and the destruction of independent dynasties, extending over more than a decade and with outcomes that differed sharply from place to place.

The Muzaffarid crisis is best understood as both foreign invasion and dynastic conflict. After Shah Shuja's death, competing princes struggled for Shiraz, Isfahan, and neighboring districts. Timur's first campaign in 1387 did not sweep them all away; some received authority as dependents under his superior command. During his absence rivalries resumed, and Shah Mansur emerged by 1391 as the strongest Muzaffarid contender. His control of Shiraz and Isfahan did not make him secure, for rival kinsmen continued to maneuver and Timur now regarded renewed warfare among them as disobedience to his settlement.

The final confrontation in 1393 followed the capture of the fortress of Qalʿa-yi Safid. The sources say Shah Mansur initially left Shiraz but returned to fight when his retreat appeared dishonorable to the city's inhabitants. He died in battle against Timurid forces. The other princes' attempts to submit did not preserve the ruling house. On 23 May 1393, most surviving Muzaffarid princes were executed on Timur's orders. The blind Zayn al-ʿAbidin and his brother Sultan Shibli were exceptions and were sent to Samarqand. Timur distributed authority to his own trusted appointees, including Edigu Barlas in Kerman and his son ʿUmar Shaykh in Fars and Iraq-i Ajam. The conclusion was not merely the replacement of one man on the throne of Shiraz. It was the deliberate elimination of a dynastic network and its substitution by governors whose security depended on Timur. That political rupture helps explain why the post-conquest government needs separate treatment in Chapter 53.

Cause and Effect

Why the Iranian Successor States Fell to Timur

Long-term causes

Political fragmentation after the Ilkhanid collapse, dynastic rivalry among regional powers, and the absence of any framework for common defense.

Short-term causes

Succession disputes within ruling houses, such as that among the Muzaffarids after 1384; the weakening of Sarbadar authority; the exposure of the Jalayirids to Tokhtamysh.

Immediate context

Timur's consolidation of Transoxiana and the availability of an experienced army, combined with a willingness to campaign year after year.

Event or transformation

Between 1381 and 1393, the Kartids, Sarbadars, Muzaffarids, and in practice the Jalayirids in Iran lost independence through submission, defeat, or flight.

Immediate effects

Tribute, garrisons, deportations, and in places massacre; the dismantling of fortifications; removal of dynastic rulers.

Long-term consequences

A Timurid supremacy over much of Iran, resting on continuing military dependence and requiring a system of provincial rule that his successors would have to build.

Historical complexity: The fall of these states cannot be reduced to one cause. Timur's military organization, the political divisions of his opponents, the choices of individual rulers, and the demands of the conquest economy all contributed, and the outcome might have been different had rulers chosen differently or had Timur been distracted elsewhere.

Section 52.13

The Zagros, Baghdad, and the Jalayirid Challenge

After the defeat of Shah Mansur in 1393, Timur's forces moved across western Iran and into Iraq. The route lay through the Zagros Mountains and the lands of Luristan and Kurdistan, regions with fortified strongholds and mobile local populations whose cooperation or resistance affected the advance. Passes through the Zagros were narrow and defensible, and Timurid forces needed local guides and the submission of local chiefs to move large bodies of troops. The sources describe operations against fortresses and the reduction of local resistance along the way.

The target was Baghdad, the Jalayirid capital in Iraq and a city of great commercial and symbolic importance. Sultan Ahmad Jalayir, who had lost Tabriz in 1386, held Baghdad with limited resources. In 1393, as Timur approached, Ahmad fled, and Baghdad fell to the Timurids. According to the sources, the city did not suffer the devastation that would strike it a few years later; Timur installed his own representative and moved on to other operations in the region.

The seizure of the city did not eliminate the displaced dynasty. Ahmad escaped to the west and found refuge with the Mamluk state in Syria and Egypt, and later with other allies, preserving his claim and a base from which he could return. This illustrates a recurring difficulty of Timur's conquests: a defeated ruler who survived and retained allies could reappear when Timur's attention was elsewhere. Timur's campaigns therefore often involved returning to the same regions, and territory that had been conquered once could be lost and reconquered.

Baghdad's strategic importance explains why the contest continued. The city commanded routes linking the plateau, the Persian Gulf, and Syria, and its possession gave access to revenue from trade. For Timur, controlling Baghdad secured his western flank and removed a potential staging point for rivals. For the Mamluks and other powers, a Timurid presence in Iraq represented a threat to their own frontiers. The struggle over Iraq thus connected the Iranian conquests to a wider regional contest, which would escalate in the following years.

The Jalayirid experience shows that territorial occupation and dynastic extinction were different questions. Sultan Ahmad could retreat from a threatened city, make alliances, and seek opportunities to return. From the perspective of Baghdad's inhabitants, however, this meant repeated demands from successive armed powers rather than the reassuring continuity implied by the survival of a dynasty's name. Baghdad's position at the meeting point of Iraqi river routes, Iranian approaches through the Zagros, and roads westward made it valuable to more than one ruler. Occupation could therefore produce a garrison and a fiscal claim without finally resolving who would command the city after the main Timurid army departed.

The same geography made warfare expensive. Moving troops through the Zagros required knowledge of passes, water, and seasonal conditions; controlling the approaches to Baghdad demanded garrisons and cooperation from regional commanders. Those who submitted might remain as intermediaries, and those displaced could exploit weak points in the new arrangement. This helps explain the need to distinguish the 1393 capture from the violent reconquest of 1401. A campaign could win a capital without constructing a permanent system capable of eliminating renewed challenges.

Section 52.14

The Caucasus and the Wars against Tokhtamysh

While Timur campaigned in Iran, a second war pressed on his northern frontier. Tokhtamysh, who ruled the Golden Horde, had been aided by Timur in the 1370s in his struggle for power in the steppe, and for a time the two were on good terms. The relationship reversed as Tokhtamysh sought to control Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, regions that he regarded as within the Golden Horde's sphere, and as Timur's expansion brought him into the same territory. The attack on Tabriz in 1385 marked a turning point toward open rivalry.

The strategic geography of the conflict connected Azerbaijan, Shirvan, Georgia, and the Caucasus. Passes through the Caucasus, particularly along the Caspian coast, allowed forces from the steppe to move into the plateau and threaten Tabriz and the northern cities. Securing this frontier was therefore a condition of holding Iran. Timur campaigned in Georgia in 1386, taking Tiflis, and conducted further operations in the Caucasus in the following years. These expeditions served to secure the region and to extract resources, and Georgia suffered repeated raids in the period.

The wars with Tokhtamysh unfolded in several major campaigns. In 1388 Tokhtamysh invaded Transoxiana while Timur was in Iran, which led to Timur's recall from Fars. After further fighting, Timur's forces defeated Tokhtamysh in 1391 at the Kunduzcha river, in a campaign that took them far into the steppe. The conflict resumed, and in 1395 Timur won a decisive victory at the Terek river in the Caucasus region, followed by operations that devastated centers of the Golden Horde's power. These victories substantially reduced the threat from the north.

For the purposes of this chapter, the wars with Tokhtamysh matter because they shaped the pace and sequence of the Iranian conquest. Timur's absences from Iran, caused by threats in the north, gave regional rulers opportunities to recover and required his return. At the same time, the victories secured the Caucasian approaches to Azerbaijan and northern Iran. This should remain an Iranian-centered account of a wider Eurasian war: the history of the Golden Horde itself lies outside the scope of this chapter, but its interaction with Timur's campaigns helps explain why conquest in Iran was neither continuous nor easy to consolidate.

The rivalry with Tokhtamysh also complicates any account that calls Timur's campaigns simply the 'conquest of Iran.' Timur had earlier assisted Tokhtamysh's emergence as a ruler in the Jochid world, but the resulting political relationship did not remain one of obedience. Both men claimed influence over routes, pastures, tribute, and diplomatic partners extending across the Caucasus and northern Iran. The attack on Azerbaijan in 1385 gave Timur reason to regard his northwestern flank as insecure, even after he occupied Tabriz. Battles far to the north consequently affected how securely the Iranian territories could be held.

The victories of 1391 and 1395 were operational successes against a rival mounted power, but they also imposed demands on the conquered Iranian provinces through which armies moved or from which supplies were drawn. The destruction associated with warfare in the steppe and Caucasus did not translate automatically into undisturbed prosperity in Azerbaijan. Frontier security was contingent on continuing military strength, the cooperation of local commanders, and the changing ambitions of other powers. This geopolitical setting is why the northern campaigns belong in an Iranian history even though much of the fighting occurred beyond Iran's present boundaries.

Section 52.15

Syria, Baghdad in 1401, and the Escalation of Imperial Warfare

In 1399 Timur set out on a major campaign in the west, often called the Seven-Year campaign in the older scholarly literature, directed against the Mamluks of Egypt and Syria and their allies. The cause of the war lay in the continuing conflicts over Iraq and Azerbaijan, including the sheltering of Sultan Ahmad Jalayir and others hostile to Timur by the Mamluks. Timur advanced into Syria, taking Aleppo in 1400 and Damascus in early 1401. The sources describe the seizure of wealth, the killing of inhabitants, and the removal of craftsmen and others from the captured cities. The scholar Ibn Khaldun, who was in Damascus, met Timur and left an account of the encounter, which offers an outside perspective on the conqueror, though one shaped by Ibn Khaldun's own situation as a petitioner.

Timur then turned to Baghdad, which Ahmad Jalayir had reoccupied after the first Timurid capture. In 1401 the Timurids retook the city, and the result was far more destructive than the capture of 1393. The sources report a sack of great severity, with extensive killing and destruction, and later narratives describe towers of skulls. As with Isfahan, numerical claims should be treated as reports rather than totals, but the event is well attested and is generally regarded as one of the most destructive episodes of Timur's campaigns. The distinction between the relatively limited first capture and the devastating second capture is important: the same city could experience very different treatment depending on the circumstances, in this case the renewed resistance and the perception that the previous settlement had been betrayed.

The strategic logic of these campaigns connected Iranian territory, Iraq, Syria, and the commercial routes that linked them. Iraq and Syria were important for trade and for the movement of armies, and rival states, notably the Mamluks, sought to protect their frontiers. Timur's campaigns can be understood as an effort to secure his western borders by removing or weakening these rivals, but they also served the aims of plunder and demonstration of power. The seizure of wealth, the punishment of cities that resisted, and the removal of skilled workers reinforced Timur's imperial supremacy and fed the resources of his capital.

The escalation of violence in these years deserves explicit attention. By the early 1400s Timur's campaigns had grown larger and, in some cases, more destructive, and the punishment of resisting cities became a more prominent feature of his warfare. Whether this reflected a change in strategy, the accumulation of grievances, or the logic of an imperial system that depended on intimidation is a question historians continue to discuss, as the debate in Section 52.17 shows.

The renewed sack of Baghdad in 1401 was not an incidental continuation of the 1393 occupation. It demonstrated the limits of Timurid control in a city where the Jalayirid political claim had survived. Chronicles of the siege and assault emphasize the deliberate punitive character of the operation; medieval reports of the numbers killed should be handled with the same caution applied to Isfahan. The existence of a large and ancient urban population, its institutions, and its economic life must not disappear behind sensational depictions of victory. The imposition of punishment affected people who were not themselves commanders of opposing armies.

Timur's Syrian campaign likewise connected imperial competition with the capture of people and portable resources. The experience of the young Ibn ʿArabshah, removed from Damascus with his family and later educated in the Timurid world, supplies a rare identifiable life trajectory within the larger story of forced migration. His later account was strongly hostile to Timur and cannot serve as an unfiltered transcript of events, but his biography confirms that a deportee could be both a victim of coercion and an active participant in the intellectual networks created through displacement. The imperial concentration of learning was therefore inseparable from the unequal circumstances in which some of its participants arrived.

Section 52.16

India, Anatolia, and the Outer Limits of Timur's Ambitions

The Iranian conquests were part of a wider imperial project that extended far beyond the plateau. In 1398 and 1399 Timur campaigned in India, crossing the Indus and defeating the forces of the Delhi Sultanate under Sultan Nasir al-Din Mahmud Tughluq. Delhi was taken and sacked in December 1398, and a great deal of booty and many captives, including skilled workers, were taken back to Timur's realm. The campaign shows the scale of Timurid military capacity and the way booty from campaigns far from Iran supported the empire's wealth. It also illustrates the demands on manpower and the pressures on commanders to keep campaigning.

In the west, Timur's campaigns culminated in 1402 at Ankara, where he defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I and took him captive. The Ottoman state, expanding in Anatolia and the Balkans, had come into conflict with Timur over the allegiance of Anatolian rulers and over frontier regions. The battle demonstrated the reach of Timur's army and had major consequences for Ottoman history, but its significance for this chapter lies in what it reveals about Timur's system: an army capable of campaigning at great distances, and an ambition that went beyond the conquest of Iran. Timur did not seek to occupy Anatolia permanently; he withdrew after the victory, leaving the Ottoman state in disarray.

Timur's plans for further expansion eventually looked eastward. In his final years he prepared a campaign against Ming China, gathering forces in Transoxiana and setting out in late 1404. The reasons for this plan are not fully clear from the sources, and historians differ on how far it was a serious conquest project, but it shows that the process of expansion had not reached a limit when Timur died.

These campaigns should not dominate a chapter on Iran, and their purpose here is to clarify the scale of Timurid military demands. An army that campaigned in India, Syria, and Anatolia required enormous resources, and those resources were drawn in part from the territories conquered earlier. The Iranian provinces thus served as sources of revenue, soldiers, and skilled labor for a project whose center lay in Transoxiana. Understanding this relationship helps explain the pattern of extraction that characterized Timurid rule and the burdens that fell on Iranian society.

Timur's departure from Iran for campaigns on several frontiers illustrates a recurrent tension. Successful expansion brought booty and prestige that could sustain the loyalty of commanders, but absence increased the difficulty of monitoring governors and local allies in territories already subdued. News traveled through couriers and politically interested reports; an expedition far from Samarqand or Tabriz could not respond instantly to every provincial quarrel. The more extensive the imperial field of operations became, the more important the delegated command of relatives and military households grew.

The prospective expedition against Ming China makes this tension visible at the end of Timur's life. The project expressed ambitions on the scale of earlier Mongol universal empire, yet his death in 1405 halted it before its intended objective was reached. It would be misleading to infer from his army's earlier successes that a Chinese campaign was destined to succeed. The historian can identify resources assembled and intentions announced, but not outcomes that never occurred. Timur left behind territories joined by conquest and allegiance, but no tested mechanism that could prevent rival heirs from contesting those resources when the founding commander was gone.

Section 52.17

Siege Violence, Massacres, and the Politics of Terror

Violence was integral to Timur's campaigns, but it was not uniform, and distinguishing its forms is essential. The sources describe sieges, in which defenders and attackers fought over fortifications; negotiated submissions, in which cities paid and were spared; punitive reprisals, directed at places that had rebelled after submitting; plunder, which accompanied almost any military success; executions of rulers, officials, and captives; civilian massacres, such as those at Isfahan and Baghdad in 1401; and deliberate intimidation, including the display of heads. These categories overlap, but they are not identical, and treating all Timurid violence as one phenomenon obscures the choices that produced different outcomes.

Why did some cities receive protection while others faced catastrophe? Several factors appear in the evidence. Voluntary and early submission usually brought terms, while resistance, especially prolonged resistance, brought assault. Rebellion after submission was treated with particular severity, because it was regarded as a breach of the settlement. Military circumstances mattered: a city that had killed Timurid soldiers or officials, or that delayed the army, risked retaliation. Wealth and strategic value also played a part, since a city that could pay was worth preserving, while one that could not might be plundered. Promises could be violated, and circumstances varied, so that generalizations must remain cautious.

Casualty narratives require critical evaluation. Medieval chroniclers reported large numbers of dead, often in round figures, and used descriptions of towers of skulls and similar images. These conventions served to magnify the power of the ruler and to warn others, and later writers repeated and sometimes enlarged them. The issue is not whether violence occurred: it demonstrably did, and the massacres at Isfahan and Baghdad in 1401 are well attested. The issue is how its scale, causes, and purpose can be reconstructed from politically interested sources. Numbers of dead, deportees, and soldiers must never be presented as independently established totals merely because they appear in a chronicle.

Responsibility should also be stated precisely. Timur and his commanders ordered and carried out the operations, and the chronicles attribute decisions to Timur personally in major cases. Yet violence was carried out by an army composed of many groups, and the experience of the conquered varied according to which units and commanders were involved. Avoiding euphemism does not require collective condemnation of a population or of every soldier; it requires attributing orders and actions to those who gave and executed them where the evidence allows.

Violence also had an informational function. News of massacres traveled with merchants, fugitives, soldiers, and envoys; the knowledge that a previous city had been punished could influence whether the next ruler or council chose resistance. Yet intimidation was never a perfectly reliable instrument. Submission could encourage rulers to wait for Timur's departure before rebelling, while extreme reprisals could destroy resources the conqueror wanted to tax. The relationship between coercion and obedience therefore changed over time and from place to place. A military victory might silence immediate opposition while making future administration harder.

The sources differ not only in political loyalty but also in what their genres were designed to accomplish. A court chronicle could represent violence as the just consequence of treachery; a hostile biography could emphasize cruelty to expose the conqueror's moral failure; a later illustrated manuscript might turn an event into a symbolic spectacle. Neither convergence on dramatic details nor the use of a precise-looking number makes an account equivalent to independent demographic measurement. Cross-checking allows historians to distinguish the basic sequence—submission, uprising, reprisal—from uncertain totals and disputed personal motives. Care with numbers should never be mistaken for uncertainty that the underlying massacres occurred.

Historical Debate

Was Timur's Violence Principally Military Strategy or Imperial Ideology?

The question: Did Timur's extreme violence serve primarily practical military ends, such as deterring resistance and extracting resources, or was it an expression of an imperial ideology that demanded the punishment of defiance and the display of power?

Strategic and material interpretations: Some historians emphasize the practical functions of violence. Intimidation could encourage surrender and thereby save military effort; punishment of rebels deterred others; plunder and tribute sustained the army and the treasury; and the removal of walls or people reduced the capacity for future resistance.
Ideological and political interpretations: Others stress the political and ideological dimension. Violence expressed the claim of a ruler who saw himself as the legitimate holder of imperial authority and viewed resistance as rebellion; it was a means of demonstrating sovereignty, and it fitted the conventions of Mongol and Islamic statecraft.

Evidence: The debate draws on chronicles by Timur's court historians, on hostile accounts such as that of Ibn ʿArabshah, and on comparison with earlier Mongol campaigns. The sources also reflect the conventions of conquest narratives, which complicates their use.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and, to some extent, evidence: how far the chroniclers' descriptions reflect reality, and how far literary convention. It also concerns scale, since numbers are uncertain.

Why it matters: The answer affects how Timur's rule is understood: as a calculated system of coercion, as an ideological project, or as a combination. It also affects how the conquests are compared with those of other conquerors.

Student task: The same action could serve several purposes, and the reliability of reports must be assessed separately from the moral significance of documented violence. Explain how a single event, such as Isfahan, could be read under each interpretation, and identify evidence that could help distinguish them.

Section 52.18

Deportation of Artisans, Scholars, and Skilled Workers

One of the most consequential practices of Timurid conquest was the forced transfer of people from conquered cities to Samarqand and other imperial centers. Specialist research documents deportations from Tus and Kalat in 1383, from Tabriz in 1386, and from other conquered territories, and the practice continued through the Syrian and Indian campaigns. Those removed included craftsmen, builders, artists, and scholars, as well as other skilled workers, and in some cases pastoral groups. The sources are Timurid chronicles and later narratives, and exact numbers are not reliably known.

The practice served several purposes at once. It supplied labor and expertise for Timur's building projects and workshops, and thus enhanced the capital. It extracted human capital from conquered regions, weakening their capacity to recover and revolt. It also had a display function: the presence of skilled people from famous cities enhanced Samarqand's prestige and testified to Timur's reach. For those removed, however, deportation was a coerced displacement. Families were separated from their homes, communities lost members whose skills sustained local economies, and the journey and resettlement imposed hardships. Demographic disruption followed in the places they left.

The chapter must hold two facts together. Samarqand became a city of great monumental building under Timur, including projects such as the Bibi Khanum mosque begun around the end of the 1390s, and the concentration of artisans contributed to this activity. The cultural achievements of the receiving city are real. But they were built in part on forced labor and on the loss of skilled people from other communities, and the suffering of those removed should not be minimized by the splendor of the result. Accounts such as that of Clavijo, who visited Samarqand in the early 1400s, describe the city's activity and the presence of foreign craftsmen, but they report the scene from the perspective of a visitor, not of the deportees.

The later cultural consequences, including the development of manuscript production and other arts in the Timurid world, belong to Chapter 54. The relevant point here is that deportation was part of the conquest economy, one of the means by which Timur transferred resources from Iran to Transoxiana.

The transfer of people was selective, not simply an indiscriminate movement of everyone captured. Rulers sought skills, labor, and military usefulness. A trained mason, metalworker, or manuscript specialist represented years of acquired expertise and ties to a workshop; a pastoral fighting group could be resettled for strategic and military reasons. At the imperial center, such people might contribute to buildings, textiles, ornament, or the supply of armies. At their places of origin, removal could interrupt apprenticeship, separate families, deprive patrons of specialists, and weaken the networks that sustained urban production. These losses do not always appear as spectacular events in chronicles, but they are historically consequential.

Two cases show why precision matters. In 1383 the Jaʾun-i Qurbani Mongols were transplanted from Tus and Kalat to Samarqand, an example involving a distinct Mongol population rather than an identified guild of artisans. The movement from Tabriz in 1386, by contrast, is explicitly described in specialist scholarship as including scholars, artisans, and craftsmen. Herat also experienced removals of craftsmen, artists, and religious scholars after the 1383 unrest. These cases should be placed on a map with their dates and stated social composition. They support the existence of a policy of forced transfer without allowing historians to infer precise numbers of deportees for every town.

For many deportees, survival after removal did not erase the initial coercion. The individual history of Ibn ʿArabshah, taken from Damascus as a child around 1400–01 and later trained among scholars in Central Asia, shows the possibility of intellectual development within a life shaped by forced displacement. It would be a distortion to convert that development into evidence that deportation was benevolent. The court could take pride in Samarqand's splendor while former home communities suffered losses, and historians must hold both perspectives in view. Cultural concentration and the involuntary transfer of people were not competing interpretations of the same policy; they were connected consequences.

Section 52.19

The Uneven Consequences of Conquest for Iranian Society

The consequences of conquest differed greatly from region to region. Some areas suffered severe devastation: Sistan, where irrigation systems were damaged; Isfahan, where the massacre and its aftermath disrupted the city; places such as Isfizar and Baghdad in 1401 that were stormed or sacked. Other places, such as Herat in 1381 and Shiraz in 1387, entered the Timurid system through submission and avoided the worst immediate destruction, although they paid tribute, lost fortifications, or later suffered punishment. Still others, such as Tabriz, were spared massacre but lost skilled workers and wealth.

The effects on society spanned many areas. Rural production could be damaged by the destruction of irrigation, by the flight or killing of cultivators, and by the demands for supplies, since armies lived off the land. Taxation and tribute extracted resources and could be applied punitively. Trade was disrupted by campaigns and insecurity, although commercial routes remained important and merchants continued to operate. Settlement patterns shifted as people fled, were deported, or moved to safer places. Local elites were affected in varied ways, with some losing position and others retaining it, and religious institutions, which Timur patronized, often fared better than military or bureaucratic families of defeated dynasties. Urban life in conquered cities depended on whether the city had been spared or destroyed.

Historians caution against asserting uniform demographic collapse or rapid restoration. The evidence consists largely of chronicle statements, which are not statistical, and the recovery of a region depended on local circumstances, such as whether irrigation could be repaired, whether skilled people returned, and whether later rulers provided stability. The research of Maria Eva Subtelny on the economy of the Timurid period is an important foundation for assessing these consequences, and it stresses regional variation and the difficulty of generalizing. A responsible account must therefore avoid both a picture of total ruin and a picture of swift recovery.

The redistribution of resources and population toward Transoxiana affected the Iranian provinces in a further way. Wealth, booty, and skilled workers flowed to Samarqand, enriching the capital at the expense of the provinces. This was not an incidental effect but a structural feature of a conquest empire centered outside the conquered region. The Iranian provinces served as sources of revenue and labor for a ruler whose capital lay elsewhere. How far the later Timurid order, examined in Chapters 53 and 54, changed this relationship is a question for those chapters.

A useful comparison separates the different mechanisms of damage. In the Helmand basin, interference with irrigation and cultivation could have effects continuing beyond the departure of troops. In Isfahan, large-scale killing and flight affected the composition of neighborhoods, the capacity of workshops, and confidence in urban security. In Herat, negotiated submission initially preserved many inhabitants, but further rebellion led to renewed tribute and deportation. In Tabriz, the city's commercial importance could coexist with the extraction of skilled persons. A single measure of 'recovery' would conceal these different histories; even within one region, court offices and merchants might recover more quickly than vulnerable households.

The capacity to rebuild also depended on who retained access to water, land, capital, and political protection. Local religious foundations or administrative families could preserve records and some assets, while other institutions lost their patrons. Military commanders might benefit from reassigned estates at the same time that tenants or village cultivators faced new demands. Where chronicles report restored order, the historian should ask whether that means regular tax collection, safe travel for officials, profitable commerce for a limited elite, or improved conditions for the population. These possibilities overlap but are not equivalent. Evidence about the earliest Timurid decades is strongest for rulers, officials, urban monuments, and spectacular violence; the everyday recovery of villages is much less well documented.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iran before and after the Timurid Conquest

What changed? The independent regional dynasties, Kartid, Sarbadar, and Muzaffarid, were eliminated, and much of Iran came under Timurid supremacy. Fortifications were dismantled, wealth and people were transferred to Transoxiana, and military garrisons and appointments tied provinces to a new center.

What remained continuous? Local administrative practice, agricultural and commercial life, and religious institutions continued in many places, often through the same local officials. Persian remained the language of administration and culture, and the political tradition of Mongol-style legitimacy persisted.

When did change occur, and what was its pace? Change came in stages between 1381 and 1393, with rebellions and reconquests, so it was uneven and contested.

Who experienced change most directly? Rulers who were removed, cities that were stormed, deported artisans and scholars, and cultivators of damaged districts.

Who experienced greater continuity? Communities that submitted early and whose institutions were left in place, and rural populations in untouched districts.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that conquest transformed political structures faster than it transformed social and economic life, and that the effects differed by place and group.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary People and the Timurid Conquest

The chronicles dwell on rulers and battles, but the conquest was experienced by farmers, townspeople, artisans, and families. Cultivators faced the burden of supplying armies and the risk that fields and canals would be damaged. Urban households paid levies, hid or lost property, and in the worst cases lost members to massacre or deportation. Craftsmen of valued trades were at special risk of being taken, while others might find opportunities in new demand. Pastoral groups, some of whom were moved or recruited, lived by their own calculations of risk and reward.

Because most written sources were produced by court historians and learned men, the experience of such people is largely indirect, and historians must infer it from fiscal descriptions, accounts of damage, and the patterns of recovery. This limitation should be kept in mind: the absence of testimony from ordinary people is a feature of the evidence, not evidence that they were unaffected.

Section 52.20

Historical Synthesis: Timur's Conquests and the Problem of Imperial Durability

Timur established his supremacy over Iran through a combination of means. His military organization, combining steppe cavalry with siege capacity and the resources of settled societies, gave him the ability to defeat opponents; political alliances and the exploitation of divisions among his enemies reduced their capacity to resist; inherited Mongol legitimacy, expressed through the title of gürägän and the use of Chinggisid figureheads, and Islamic royal language supported his claims; and coercion, including punishment, plunder, and deportation, extracted resources and discouraged defiance. None of these explains the conquest alone, and the chapter's examples show that outcomes varied with local circumstances.

The difference between winning campaigns and establishing a lasting political order is the chapter's central lesson. Timur repeatedly had to return to regions that had submitted and risen again, and the need for constant campaigning is itself evidence that conquest was not the same as government. The supremacy he established depended on his personal command, on the army's loyalty, and on a flow of resources to Transoxiana. It rested on appointments of family members and commanders in the provinces, but the institutions that could turn such appointments into stable regional government were still to be developed.

Timur died in February 1405 at Otrar, while preparing a campaign against Ming China. His death left the question of succession unresolved, and the empire passed to his heirs without a clearly established mechanism for transferring authority. Whether his successors could transform a conquest empire into a functioning system of regional government is the question with which Chapter 53 begins, and the answer would prove to be complex and contested. This chapter does not narrate the succession struggles, but it has established the conditions: a vast territory held by military supremacy, provinces affected unevenly by conquest, and a political center whose authority rested heavily on one person.

The chapter's conclusions must therefore remain double-sided. Timur's army was an effective instrument of conquest: it combined mobile military organization with siege resources, intelligence, diplomatic bargaining, and a willingness to use extreme coercion. But successful campaigns did not abolish regional loyalties, produce a shared political community, or ensure that displaced ruling houses had no remaining supporters. The empire depended upon repeated movement by its founder, delegation to relatives, the redistribution of spoils, and the continued cooperation of military elites. Each of those arrangements presented a problem when the commanding figure could no longer intervene.

The meaning of conquest also depended on the standpoint from which it was described. A court historian might emphasize the restoration of a ruler's authority; an urban survivor might remember loss of kin, property, and security; a deported artisan might experience the imperial capital as both a place of opportunity and the destination of involuntary removal. No one perspective should erase the others. The chapter's narrative ends in 1405 with the problem of political durability unresolved. Chapter 53 begins where military supremacy ceased to be a sufficient explanation: with the institutions, succession contests, and regional accommodations through which Timur's descendants attempted to govern what he had taken.

Historical Debate

Did Timur Reunify Iran or Simply Subject It to Another Conquest Empire?

The question: Did Timur's conquests restore a unified Iranian political space after the Ilkhanid collapse, or did they impose another conquest empire on a politically divided land?

Reunification interpretation: Timur ended the independence of the regional dynasties and brought most of the plateau under one authority, in territorial terms the first such unification since the Ilkhans, and thus ended the period of fragmentation.
Conquest-empire interpretation: The empire's center lay in Samarqand, not in Iran; provinces were held by military appointments and tribute, resources flowed toward Transoxiana, local institutions persisted, and Timur's repeated returns show that control remained unstable. On this view the conquest created supremacy, not an Iranian state.

Evidence: Territorial results of the campaigns, military appointments, evidence of rebellion and reconquest, the extraction of resources and people, and the continuing role of local institutions.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation more than the basic facts: what counts as reunification, and whether an empire centered outside Iran can be said to unify it.

Why it matters: It affects how the Timurid period is placed in Iranian history, and whether it is seen as a restoration, an interruption, or a foundation for later developments.

Student task: Evaluate each interpretation, identify evidence that could favor one or the other, and consider whether the two are mutually exclusive.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Timur conquered Iran between 1381 and 1393 and what the conquest meant for the people and institutions he brought under his control. Its central argument is that Timur was highly successful at defeating regional rulers and imposing military supremacy, but that conquest did not automatically produce stable government, economic recovery, or a secure succession. The chapter has also argued that the consequences of conquest were severe but regionally varied, and that Timur cannot be understood either as a mere destroyer or as a mere patron of culture.

The political setting was the fragmentation of Iran after the Ilkhans. The Kartids of Herat, the Sarbadars of Sabzavar, the Muzaffarids of Fars, the Jalayirids of Iraq and Azerbaijan, and local lords in Mazandaran held power in competition with one another. These states were not helpless, but they lacked a means of common defense, and their rivalries offered an invader opportunities. The conquest was contingent: it depended on Timur's resources and on the choices of Iranian rulers, not on any inevitable reunification.

Timur emerged from the Barlas milieu of Transoxiana amid the decline of Chaghatayid authority. After defeating Amir Husayn in 1370, he established supremacy in Transoxiana, with Samarqand as his base. He ruled as amir, not khan, since he was not a Chinggisid, and he strengthened his position through marriage as gürägän and through nominal Chinggisid rulers. He also presented himself as a Muslim ruler and a restorer of order. Court chronicles such as Shami's Zafarnama expressed these claims, and they must be read as sources shaped by patronage.

His army combined mounted steppe forces with siege capacity and the resources of sedentary populations, and it was organized through commanders, family members, and military followers. Intelligence, logistics, and diplomacy supplemented force. The army's needs also drove a conquest economy dependent on tribute, plunder, and the extraction of resources, which shaped the experience of conquered populations.

The conquest of eastern Iran began with the negotiated submission of Herat in April 1381 and of the Sarbadar regime. Submission could preserve elements of local authority while ending independence, but rebellion in 1383 brought punitive operations at Isfizar and the devastation of Sistan. Timur relied on selected local elites, garrisons, tribute, and the first removals of skilled workers to Transoxiana. The Kartid ruler's final elimination in 1389 came years after the first submission.

In the north and center, campaigns extended Timurid influence across the plateau and toward the Caspian, though Mazandaran was fully subdued only in the early 1390s. Tabriz was taken in 1386, within a contest involving the Jalayirids and Tokhtamysh. The Isfahan massacre of 1387, following a rising against Timurid agents, was one of the most severe episodes; the reported death toll of 70,000 must be treated as a claim, not as a verified total. Shiraz submitted in 1387, but the Muzaffarid dynasty was destroyed after the defeat of Shah Mansur in 1393.

The conquest of Iran formed part of wider wars. Baghdad was captured in 1393 without the destruction of its later sack in 1401, and Sultan Ahmad Jalayir survived to return. The wars against Tokhtamysh, with victories in 1391 and 1395, secured the Caucasian frontier. The campaigns in Syria, India, and Anatolia, including the capture of Delhi in 1398 and the defeat of Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402, show the scale of the Timurid military system and the demands it placed on the conquered provinces.

Violence took many forms: sieges, punitive reprisals, plunder, executions, massacres, and intimidation. Why some cities were spared and others destroyed depended on the timing of submission, resistance, rebellion, wealth, and circumstance. Casualty figures in chronicles are unreliable as totals, though the occurrence of massacres is well attested. The debate over whether violence was principally strategic or ideological shows that a single action could serve several purposes.

Deportation of artisans, scholars, and other skilled workers, documented from Tus and Kalat in 1383 and Tabriz in 1386 among other places, transferred human capital to Samarqand. The cultural achievements of the receiving city must be distinguished from the suffering and displacement of those removed. The consequences for Iranian society varied by region: Sistan and Isfahan suffered severely, while places that submitted, such as Herat and Shiraz at first, avoided the worst, and historians avoid claims of uniform collapse or rapid recovery.

Timur died in 1405 while preparing a campaign against Ming China. The question of whether he reunified Iran or subjected it to another conquest empire remains open to interpretation, depending on how reunification is defined. What is clear is that the supremacy he established depended on military command and a flow of resources to Transoxiana. Whether his successors could turn it into a functioning system of regional government is the subject of Chapter 53.

  • Prompt 2 (Comparative): Compare the experience of two regions or cities, such as Herat and Isfahan, or Sistan and Shiraz, under Timurid conquest, and explain what accounts for the differences.
  • Prompt 3 (Evidence-based argument): Using the primary source windows, argue how far court chronicles can be trusted for the Isfahan massacre, and identify what additional evidence would help.
  • Prompt 4 (Connecting developments): Explain how the distinction between conquest and government helps connect Timur's death in 1405 with the problems his successors faced, using evidence from this chapter.

Constructing a Source Dossier on One City's Encounter with Timur

This project asks you to assemble a dossier on a single city or region, such as Herat, Isfahan, Tabriz, Shiraz, Baghdad, or Sistan, and to evaluate how historians reconstruct its experience of Timurid conquest.

  1. Choose a city or region discussed in this chapter and state the dates of its first submission or occupation and of any later rebellion or reconquest.
  2. Collect sources. Using library resources, identify at least three kinds of evidence: a court chronicle (such as Shami, Hafiz-i Abru, or Sharaf al-Din Yazdi, in translation or in scholarly discussion), a non-court or hostile account (such as Ibn ʿArabshah or Ibn Khaldun, where relevant), and modern scholarship such as H. R. Roemer's "Timur in Iran" or Beatrice Forbes Manz's The Rise and Rule of Tamerlane.
  3. Evaluate each source. For each, identify author, date, patron, purpose, and perspective, and note what it cannot show.
  4. Reconstruct the outcome. Explain what happened to the city's rulers, population, fortifications, and skilled workers, distinguishing what is well attested from reported numbers and conventional descriptions.
  5. Write your findings in a 3–5 page essay or annotated dossier, using careful language such as "the chronicles report" where evidence is uncertain.

Library resources to use: historical atlases, the Cambridge History of Iran volume on the Timurid and Safavid periods, Encyclopaedia Iranica articles on Timurid historiography, deportations, and the Timurid economy, and translated primary sources held by the MGU History Library.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a conquest can transform a political landscape without by that fact creating a stable order. Timur removed the independent dynasties of the post-Ilkhanid world and brought most of the plateau under his supremacy, but the means by which he did so, repeated campaigning, tribute, garrisons, deportation, and punitive violence, were those of a conquest empire, and they placed heavy and uneven burdens on the societies of Iran.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the history of the region: the continuing influence of Mongol models of legitimacy, the interaction of steppe military organization with sedentary resources, and the pull of Transoxiana as a center toward which wealth and people flowed. It shows the plateau once again as part of a wider system linking Central Asia, the Caucasus, Iraq, Syria, and India, with consequences that cannot be understood within the borders of any modern state.

Later developments grew from these foundations. The Timurid successors would try to organize provinces, elites, and dynastic politics in the territories Timur conquered, the subject of Chapter 53, and the cultural life of the Timurid world, especially in Herat, would draw on resources and people gathered in the age of conquest, examined in Chapter 54. The political vacuum left by the destruction of local dynasties also formed part of the setting in which the Turkmen confederations rose, as Chapter 55 shows.

The chapter's methods matter as much as its narrative. Students have seen how to read court chronicles critically, how to treat numerical claims as reports, how to separate documented violence from literary convention, and how to avoid both celebrating and caricaturing a conqueror. These habits of mind apply throughout the study of conquest empires and will be needed again in the chapters that follow.
