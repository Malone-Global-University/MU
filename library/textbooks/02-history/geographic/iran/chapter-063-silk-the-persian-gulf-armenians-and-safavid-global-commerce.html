Chapter Opening

A bale of raw silk reeled in a village of Gilan could end its journey in a weaving workshop in Aleppo, Livorno, or Amsterdam. Between the reeling basin and the loom lay mule trains, tax collectors, brokers, written credit, and merchants who answered to several rulers at once. Safavid Iran's participation in the early modern world economy was assembled from these ordinary links, not from the decrees of a shah alone.

Consider a transaction of the kind that occupied the merchants of New Julfa, the Armenian quarter on the south bank of the Zayandeh River across from Isfahan, in the early seventeenth century. The following account is an illustrative composite reconstructed from patterns that historians have documented. It describes no single surviving contract, and no named person in it is a real individual. A merchant house in the quarter has resolved to send a consignment of Gilan silk toward the Mediterranean. Its agent has first to secure the silk itself, buying from a broker who in turn has bought from reelers, or from cultivators who sold their cocoons or reeled silk to local dealers. Part of the crop has already been claimed by the crown or by the landowners and officials who took a share of the harvest. The house has to pay for the silk in coin, or on credit, or in a mixture of the two. It has to find a caravan operator whose mules and drivers will carry the bales over the Alborz and across the plateau, and it has to pay the tolls, customs dues, and brokerage fees that accumulate along each route.

That description already shows why this chapter treats commerce as a system and not as a procession of luxury goods. The shipment depended on peasants and women who raised silkworms in the Caspian provinces, on reelers who unwound the cocoons, on muleteers and camel drivers, and on caravanserai keepers and customs officials. It also depended on a trading house in Isfahan that had to trust an agent it might not see for years, on a Mediterranean buyer whose willingness to pay depended on European fashion and on the price of silver, and on a Safavid state that taxed, regulated, and sometimes tried to monopolize the commodity that mattered most to its cash revenue. The silk on the bale was a product of village labor, but its value was created by the whole chain of transport, finance, and demand.

This chapter follows that chain. Chapters 61 and 62 explained how Shah Abbas I consolidated Safavid power and how Isfahan was built as an imperial capital. Chapter 63 asks what economic networks sustained that state and tied it to the larger world. It begins with silk and the royal revenue derived from it, turns to the Armenian merchants whose organization of long-distance trade became one of the most studied commercial networks of the age, and follows trade to the Persian Gulf, where Portuguese, English, Dutch, Indian, Arab, and Iranian actors competed and cooperated. It then examines the overland routes through the Ottoman, Russian, Mughal, and Central Asian worlds, the financial systems that moved value across them, and finally the laborers, religious communities, and legal arrangements on which commerce rested.

The central question is how silk, Armenian merchant networks, international routes, maritime commerce, and royal fiscal policy connected Safavid Iran to the global economy, and who benefited from or bore the costs of those connections. The answer varies by social position. Some Julfan merchant families accumulated great wealth, while the Armenians of Old Julfa had been forcibly displaced to make that story possible. Royal revenues rose with silk, but the rural households that produced it were not the principal beneficiaries. European companies sought privileges at Safavid ports, but they often depended on Asian merchants and on the goodwill of Safavid officials who had their own interests.

The evidence for this history is unusually plural. It includes Safavid chronicles and administrative texts, Armenian chronicles and merchant letters, the records of the English and Dutch East India Companies, the accounts of European travelers such as Jean Chardin and Engelbert Kaempfer, Ottoman and Russian records, and surviving textiles. Each source has its own biases and silences. Company records over-represent European concerns, travelers described what they saw in a few cities and were prone to generalization, and Safavid chronicles concentrate on kings and courts. Historians therefore reconstruct the commercial system by comparing sources that were never intended to describe it as a whole.

The chapter also resists two simple stories. One is that Iran was a closed land that opened to the world only when Europeans arrived. The commercial traditions examined in this chapter were much older than the European companies, and Asian merchants carried most of the trade that Europeans sought to enter. The other is that royal monopoly and royal sponsorship made Safavid commerce a purely state-directed enterprise. The crown mattered enormously, but its control was always partial, and merchants, brokers, and carriers adapted, evaded, and bargained. The chapter's governing argument is that Safavid Iran was an active participant in a complex early modern commercial system linking the Iranian plateau to Europe, Russia, Central Asia, India, and the Indian Ocean, and that this participation was built on the work of producers, carriers, merchants, and the state together.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how silk production, from mulberry cultivation to reeling, linked rural households to state revenue and export markets.
  2. Distinguish raw silk exports from finished textile manufacture and explain where value was added in each.
  3. Analyze the aims, mechanisms, and limits of Shah Abbas I's silk monopoly and the gap between royal policy and enforcement.
  4. Trace the origins of New Julfa in the deportations of 1604–1605, distinguishing the human costs of forced migration from the later commercial success of selected families.
  5. Describe how Julfan trading houses used kinship, agents, credit, correspondence, and community institutions to sustain long-distance trade.
  6. Explain the commercial consequences of the Safavid recovery of Hormuz in 1622 and the rise of Bandar Abbas.
  7. Evaluate the roles of Asian and European actors in Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean trade without assuming European dominance.
  8. Compare the overland routes through Ottoman, Russian, Mughal, and Central Asian lands and explain why trade persisted across hostile frontiers.
  9. Interpret the financial mechanics of long-distance exchange, including currency, bullion, credit, and customs duties.
  10. Identify the laborers and households whose work sustained commerce, and the unequal protection afforded to different religious and legal communities.
  11. Assess historians' competing interpretations of later Safavid commerce as decline or restructuring.
  12. Analyze primary sources, recognizing their authorship, purposes, and limitations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a commodity produced by rural households in a few provinces come to underwrite state finance and international exchange?
  2. What could the Safavid crown control in the commercial economy, and what escaped its control?
  3. How should historians weigh the forced origins of New Julfa against the later prosperity of its merchants?
  4. How did Julfan merchants maintain trust across oceans and empires without a shared state to enforce contracts?
  5. Why did the Persian Gulf become more important to Iran's commerce after 1622, and who gained from the change?
  6. In what ways did European companies depend on Asian commercial networks rather than replace them?
  7. Why did trade continue across frontiers separating states at war?
  8. Who bore the costs of Safavid commercial integration, and how are those costs recoverable from the sources?
  9. Does later Safavid commercial change represent decline, adaptation, or both, and what evidence would distinguish them?

Historical Context

By 1598, when Shah Abbas I moved his capital to Isfahan, the Safavid state had survived the crises of its first century. Shah Ismail I had founded it in 1501 on the strength of Qizilbash military support and a charismatic Sufi order (Chapters 56 and 57). The Ottoman defeat of Safavid forces at Chaldiran in 1514 and the long confrontation with the Ottomans that followed (Chapter 59) shaped the empire's western frontier, while Shah Tahmasp's long reign consolidated administration (Chapter 60). Abbas's reforms, examined in Chapter 61, reduced the crown's dependence on Qizilbash military leaders by developing a standing force of ghulams and by expanding the crown lands (khassa) whose revenue went directly to the royal treasury. Chapter 62 showed how Isfahan became the stage for that political order.

An empire that wished to pay salaried troops, maintain a capital, and sustain diplomacy needed cash. Much of Iran's agricultural revenue was assigned to officeholders and military commanders or was collected in kind, and the crown looked for sources of revenue it could use directly. Silk was a leading candidate. Iran possessed one of the largest silk-producing regions of the early modern world in the Caspian provinces and the Caucasus, and European, Ottoman, and Indian markets demanded it. Control of silk production and silk exports promised cash, and it also promised leverage over the Ottomans, who needed Iranian silk for their own textile industries.

The commercial setting was no empty field. The plateau had been a passage between Mesopotamia, Central Asia, and India since antiquity (Chapter 1), and earlier chapters traced its caravan cities under the Parthians (Chapter 20) and the commercial recovery of Tabriz and other cities under the Ilkhans (Chapter 50). Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, and Indian merchants were active long before Shah Abbas, and the Portuguese had held Hormuz since the early sixteenth century. What the seventeenth century added was a more integrated set of connections: the crown's active interest in silk revenue, the founding of New Julfa, the shift of Gulf commerce from Hormuz to Bandar Abbas, and the arrival of the English and Dutch East India Companies, which brought organized maritime competition to the Persian Gulf.

The study of this commerce also carries a historiographical background. Earlier writing often described Safavid trade through European travelers' eyes and concentrated on the Europeans' diplomatic and commercial overtures. More recent scholarship, drawing on Armenian, Dutch, English, Persian, and Ottoman sources, has stressed that Asian merchants and Asian institutions organized the larger share of the trade, and that the Safavid state was neither simply a passive host to foreign merchants nor an omnipotent economic planner. That scholarship, which includes the works listed in the bibliography at the end of this chapter, underlies the account that follows.

Section 63.1

Safavid Iran in the Early Modern Commercial World

Safavid Iran lay at a junction of several commercial zones. To its west lay the Ottoman Empire, whose cities of Bursa, Aleppo, and Istanbul were centers of textile manufacture and of Mediterranean trade. To the northwest and north lay the Caucasus, the Caspian Sea, and the Russian state that had absorbed Astrakhan in 1556. To the northeast lay Central Asia, with the oasis cities of Bukhara and Balkh, and to the east and southeast lay Mughal India, one of the largest manufacturing and consuming economies of the age. To the south lay the Persian Gulf and, beyond it, the Indian Ocean networks linking Arabia, East Africa, India, and Southeast Asia. The plateau did not simply sit between these regions. Iranian goods, merchants, and ports tied them together.

Historians find it useful to distinguish three scales of exchange. Local markets supplied towns with food, fuel, and everyday crafts from surrounding villages. Regional exchange moved goods such as textiles, grain, dried fruit, and livestock among provinces, often by seasonal fairs and caravans. Long-distance commerce moved higher-value, durable goods over thousands of kilometers, and its participants needed capital, credit, and information that most local traders lacked. The three scales were connected, because a village that sold cocoons to a local dealer was feeding a chain that might end in a European workshop, but they were organized differently and involved different people.

Safavid global trade was therefore not an invention of Shah Abbas I. The Silk Roads of earlier ages had passed through Iranian cities, as earlier chapters showed, and Tabriz had been a major commercial hub under the Ilkhans and their successors (Chapter 50). In the sixteenth century, before Abbas, silk from Gilan and Shirvan already reached Ottoman and Venetian buyers, and the Portuguese had made Hormuz a customs port. Even European attempts to reach Iran by new routes began before 1588: English merchants of the Muscovy Company, for example, tried to open a Russian and Caspian route to Iran in the 1560s and 1570s. What Abbas's reign changed was not the existence of the trade but the crown's active attention to it, and the creation of the Julfan network and the Gulf port system that the following sections describe.

The attraction of a high-value cargo must not be confused with the volume of commerce. Silk could pay for a caravan because an expensive material occupied comparatively little space and withstood a long journey if it was carefully packed. Grain, livestock, timber, salt, and fuel moved over shorter distances in much larger quantities but seldom generated the records left by international merchants. A city might therefore depend for its subsistence on nearby farmers while its wealthiest traders dealt in goods brought from hundreds of miles away. Fiscal evidence similarly favors transactions that officials could tax. An export duty records a taxable crossing, not every exchange that had occurred between producer and customer. This documentary imbalance helps explain why global commerce is sometimes easier to reconstruct than the domestic trade on which urban populations depended.

The word global is useful only if it is applied precisely. A Julfan agent in a European port and a Gujarati broker in Bandar Abbas did not necessarily know or deal with one another; they worked within several connected but imperfectly integrated markets. Information about a crop failure, a war, or the arrival of an East India Company ship traveled more slowly than most modern readers expect and often reached competing traders at different times. Price differences between cities could represent profit, but they also reflected distance, exchange rates, risks, and intermediaries' payments. The history of Safavid commerce must therefore ask how particular links operated, not assume a single world market governed by identical conditions everywhere.

This chapter therefore begins with the producers. A commercial history that opened with merchants or with the court would leave the origin of the goods unexplained, and the next section turns to the silk regions themselves.

Section 63.2

The Caspian Silk Regions: Gilan, Mazandaran, Shirvan, and the Organization of Production

The best-known silk-producing zone of Safavid Iran was the Caspian littoral. Gilan, Mazandaran, and in the Caucasus Shirvan and Karabagh combined the moist climate that mulberry trees require with long traditions of household silk raising. Silk was also produced in other regions of the empire, including parts of Khurasan, Kashan, and Yazd, and these supplied domestic weavers. The Caspian provinces, however, were the principal source of silk for export. The humid forested environment of this coast, which Chapter 1 described as ecologically distinct from the arid interior, was the basis of the entire system.

The production cycle was seasonal and labor-intensive. Mulberry trees were cultivated, often on small holdings, and their leaves fed the silkworms, which hatched in spring and had to be fed repeatedly over several weeks. When the larvae spun their cocoons, the cocoons were harvested, and the silk was reeled by unwinding the filament from softened cocoons into skeins. The result, raw silk, was graded by quality, bundled, and sold. Each stage required close attention, and the timing of the harvest was fixed by the lives of the insects, so a household's labor had to be concentrated into a few weeks. The sources, which are mostly European travelers' accounts, indicate that women and children took a substantial part in rearing and reeling, though few documents record their work directly, and the details varied across regions.

Historical Vocabulary
Sericulture

Definition: The cultivation of mulberry trees and the raising of silkworms to produce silk, including the harvesting of cocoons and, in many systems, the reeling of the filament into raw silk.

Historical context: In Safavid Iran sericulture was practiced mainly by rural households on small plots, in the Caspian provinces and the Caucasus. The activity fitted into the agricultural year alongside other crops, and its output was taxed, traded, and exported.

Why it matters: Because silk entered Safavid revenue and export trade, ordinary household labor was linked to the empire's finances and to foreign markets. Understanding sericulture shows that Safavid global commerce had a rural base.

Potential misunderstanding: Sericulture is not the same as silk weaving. Raw silk was an agricultural and semi-processed product, whereas weaving, dyeing, and finishing were urban crafts that added further value.

The social relationships of production were not uniform. Some cultivators held land as tenants or smallholders, others worked land belonging to large landowners, religious endowments, or the crown. After Abbas I ended the autonomy of the rulers of Gilan in 1592, fiscal authority was subsequently reorganized and crown control over its silk revenues increased, and a large share of its revenue went to the royal treasury. Provincial governors and local officials, in turn, oversaw collection of dues, and the sources indicate that officials, tax farmers, and brokers all took profit from the silk economy. Understanding who actually received the revenue from a given bale requires attention to these intermediaries.

The relationship between agricultural taxation and export demand is central to this system. When demand and prices were high, cultivators had incentives to expand mulberry planting and silk raising, but the crown's claims on the crop and the terms on which dealers bought it limited how much of the gain reached households. Conversely, when prices fell or harvests failed, producers were exposed, since they could not quickly shift to another crop that suited the same land and household labor. The profits of the silk trade were therefore unevenly distributed: merchants and officials who handled the silk captured much more than the households that raised it, though the sources do not permit precise measurement of that gap.

Geography divided the productive landscape into distinctive districts. Gilan's coastal lowlands combined abundant moisture with difficult access across the Alborz, making transport from producing villages to the Iranian interior expensive and seasonally uncertain. Mazandaran had its own ecology and administrative history; Shirvan and Qarabagh lay in a borderland repeatedly contested by Safavid and Ottoman forces. A harvest in one district could be interrupted by military movement while another continued to produce. Commercial correspondents consequently cared about the quality and provenance of silk, not merely its undifferentiated quantity. Variations in reeling, cleaning, and sorting affected the price that merchants could obtain at distant destinations.

The tax collector encountered a product whose form changed repeatedly. Obligations could be levied on cultivated land, on production, or at points where merchandise moved, and successive claims were not always consistent. Direct crown administration could allow the treasury to obtain revenue previously passing through a provincial ruler, but it did not remove village intermediaries or guarantee that a cultivator was treated more favorably. The 1592 conquest of Gilan ended local political autonomy; incorporation and regulation of the territory's revenue developed through subsequent administrative changes. Distinguishing conquest from fiscal consolidation is important because a silkworm crop could be produced under one political arrangement and marketed under another. Moreover, the economic year followed the agricultural cycle, not the shah's political timetable.

Historians also caution against assuming that silk reshaped the entire Iranian economy. Silk was exceptionally important to the crown's cash revenue and to export earnings, but most Iranians worked in grain agriculture and pastoralism, and the wider economy rested on those activities, as Chapter 64 will examine. Silk was a leading commodity in Iran's global trade without being the whole of Iran's economy.

Section 63.3

From Raw Silk to Luxury Textiles: Weaving, Dyeing, Workshops, and Commercial Value

Safavid Iran exported both raw silk and, in smaller quantities, finished textiles. The distinction matters because the greater part of the value added in textile production occurred after the silk left the reeling basin. Raw silk could be sold to foreign weavers, who then turned it into cloth in Italian, French, or Ottoman workshops, capturing the manufacturing profit abroad. Iranian workshops that wove, dyed, and finished silk cloth kept more of that value within the empire. Which form of silk a merchant could sell depended on demand: European buyers wanted raw silk for their own looms, while many Asian customers wanted finished Iranian cloth.

Iranian textile production involved a range of specialized techniques and products. Weavers produced plain silks, patterned silks, brocades with gold and silver thread, and velvets, some with figural and floral designs. Dyers worked with plant and insect dyes, whose preparation required skill and access to materials, some of which were imported. Cities such as Kashan, Isfahan, Yazd, and Tabriz were noted for textiles, and the sources record specialized workshops, guild-like associations of craftsmen, and a royal workshop system (karkhaneh) that produced goods for the court. Royal patronage encouraged high-quality production and also supplied diplomatic gifts, which circulated Safavid textiles as far as European courts. Willem Floor's study of the Persian textile industry remains a principal survey of these crafts.

The value of a finished textile reflected skilled labor as much as material. A designer, a weaver, a dyer, and a finisher each contributed, and the most elaborate fabrics could occupy skilled workers for long periods. This explains why royal workshops and wealthy patrons prized them, and also why they were poorly suited to mass export: they were expensive to make and depended on a limited pool of expertise. Raw silk, by contrast, was easier to ship in volume and to sell in large markets.

Students should examine a documented Safavid silk textile as a piece of material evidence. Museums such as the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Victoria and Albert Museum, and the Museum of Islamic Art in Berlin hold Safavid velvets, brocades, and silk-and-metal-thread fabrics, many with online catalog entries giving dimensions, materials, and provenance. When examining such a piece, students should ask: What fibers and techniques does it use? What does its design suggest about the workshop and its patrons? How did the silk and the metal thread reach the weaver? What does the piece's later history, such as a European church or collection, reveal about the channels through which Safavid goods traveled? A single textile can connect a Gilan village, an Isfahan or Kashan workshop, a royal or merchant patron, and an overseas owner, and for that reason textiles are among the most direct material records of the production chain described in this chapter.

A workshop was simultaneously an economic unit and a site where skills were transmitted. An apprentice could learn through years of repeated tasks before being entrusted with complex designs, while experienced dyers guarded recipes and knew how a change in water or treatment affected color. Merchants commissioning cloth needed to judge whether patterns, thread quality, and dimensions suited the preferences of the intended buyer. This required information from markets as well as knowledge of materials. Royal purchases might stabilize demand for a prized design, but they could also divert skilled labor into objects that ordinary consumers could never afford. Hence the fame of Safavid luxury weaving tells us something about elite patronage, not the average clothing available to urban residents.

The historian's evidence is particularly vulnerable to survivorship bias. A museum displays textiles that were preserved by unusual circumstances: a court gift, a carefully stored furnishing, or a fabric later reused in a European ecclesiastical setting. Most garments and household cloth were worn out, altered, or discarded. Provenance is not always known, and attribution to a Safavid workshop sometimes rests on technique or style rather than a surviving purchase document. A fabric's physical weave can be examined directly; the identity of the person who raised its silkworms usually cannot. The material object is thus strong evidence for craftsmanship and possible patterns of consumption, but much weaker evidence for the wages or bargaining power of the workers who created it.

The chain was not one-directional. Iranian weavers also used imported materials, such as dyes and, in some cases, metal for thread, and Iranian designs were influenced by Chinese, Indian, and European patterns. The commerce that carried silk abroad also brought ideas and materials to Iranian workshops. Textile production therefore links the rural economy of Division I with the merchant networks of Division II, and connects the village to the diaspora.

Section 63.4

Shah Abbas I, Crown Revenues, and the Royal Silk Monopoly

Shah Abbas I's expansion of the crown lands and his interest in cash revenue gave silk special importance. Chapter 61 described the military reorganization that required regular payment of ghulam troops, the court that Chapter 62 showed in its new capital, and the diplomatic and building programs that all demanded funds. Control over silk offered the crown a source of income that did not depend on the assignment of land revenue to military commanders. Following the conquest of Gilan in 1592, royal control over the province's revenue was expanded through subsequent administrative measures, and silk grown in the other crown provinces strengthened the same arrangement.

The crown also sought to regulate silk exports. Scholars generally date the assertion of a royal monopoly over the export of raw silk to about 1619 or a little earlier, although the details and chronology are debated. In its fullest form the policy would have required that silk destined for export be sold to the crown or to merchants acting as its agents, which would have allowed the shah to capture the difference between the purchase price and the price at which silk could be sold to foreign buyers. The shah also used his position to negotiate directly with European representatives, hoping to exchange silk for silver, for goods, or for diplomatic support against the Ottomans. Part of the logic was fiscal and part was strategic: redirecting silk away from the Ottoman routes toward the Gulf or Russia would deprive the Ottomans of customs and of the silk their industries needed, though historians debate how central that motive was.

Crown policy must be understood within a sequence of decisions rather than as a single fixed ordinance. Gilan became subject to Abbas after the suppression of its independent rulers in 1592, while later royal revenue arrangements extended the shah's control over particular crops and trading channels. Privileges granted to merchants could secure safe movement and exemption from interference even when other officials attempted to extract payments. A February 1593 order protecting Khwaja Nazar, a merchant associated with Old Julfa, is particularly revealing: the monarchy wanted commercially capable agents while recognizing that governors and tax collectors could obstruct them. Such an order represented an intention to protect trade; whether it was honored on every road was a different question.

In the last decade of Abbas's rule, especially around 1619, he sought to make the export of raw silk a royal prerogative, either through compulsory purchases from the crown or through expensive duties imposed upon independent traders. The policy's effectiveness cannot be assessed only through court declarations. European companies complained when silk could not be obtained on expected terms; private merchants searched for profitable alternatives; and officials who had to reconcile the demands of the treasury with the realities of trade sometimes compromised. The monopoly disappeared as a formal policy after Abbas's death, while privileged royal merchants and export duties remained. Historians should therefore resist labeling the whole seventeenth-century Safavid economy a permanent state monopoly. It was a shifting mixture of direct control, negotiated privilege, and private enterprise.

The monopoly was never a simple, uniform, or fully enforced system. The Safavid administration lacked the means to supervise every producer, caravan, and port, and the sources show merchants continuing to trade silk privately, to evade the crown's agents, and to bargain over terms. Armenian merchants, who were useful intermediaries, served at times as royal agents and at other times as independent traders. Foreign companies negotiated contracts and complained when the crown did not honor them. After Abbas's death in 1629 later shahs altered, relaxed, or applied the arrangements unevenly. Historians therefore distinguish carefully between a policy announced by the monarchy and the commercial reality it met. The limits of royal economic control are as important to understand as its ambitions.

Section 63.5

Roads, Caravanserais, Customs, and the Infrastructure of Overland Commerce

Silk and other goods reached markets by road. The principal arteries of Safavid overland commerce linked the silk-producing north with the capital and the south: routes ran from Gilan through Qazvin to Kashan and Isfahan, from Tabriz across the northwest toward Anatolia and Aleppo, and from Isfahan southward through Shiraz or Yazd and Kerman toward Bandar Abbas. Other routes led eastward toward Herat, Kandahar, and Mughal India. These roads were not paved highways but tracks and established stages, and their usability varied with the season, the terrain, and the state of local security.

Caravans were the standard means of transport. Muleteers, camel drivers, and the owners of pack animals organized trains of animals under the supervision of caravan leaders. The animal chosen depended on terrain and load: mules and horses were favored in mountainous districts, and camels on the long, drier stretches. Along the main roads stood caravanserais, fortified inns that provided shelter, fodder, and storage for travelers and goods. Many were built or restored by rulers, officials, and wealthy patrons, and Abbas I and his successors are credited with substantial building and repair. They also housed markets and, in some cases, customs officials. Brokers (dallals) arranged sales, and translators assisted foreign merchants.

Taxation shaped the economics of the road. Tolls and customs duties were levied at checkpoints, bridges, and city gates, sometimes by officials of the crown and sometimes by those who had purchased the right to collect them. Terms such as rahdari (road toll) and tamgha (a commercial levy) appear in the sources, and the rates and practices varied by place and period. A merchant crossing several jurisdictions faced successive payments, and goods that crossed into Ottoman, Russian, or Mughal territory met a fresh set of duties and rules.

A caravan's economic organization was more complicated than the image of a continuous line of loaded animals. Goods were collected from separate producers, stored until a suitable departure, repacked at transfer points, and sometimes sold or pledged before reaching their ultimate destination. Merchants might entrust merchandise to hired carriers without accompanying it, while their own agents followed market information by letter. At stops a caravanserai supplied some combination of shelter, storage, fodder, and opportunities to exchange news; the institution was not automatically a free public service. Travelers also had to plan for changes in weather, availability of water, political control, and the availability of pack animals after a military mobilization.

Different routes served different commercial strategies. A shipment heading northwest might cross through Tabriz toward Anatolia and Mediterranean ports, while a northern consignment might move by Shirvan and the Caspian toward Astrakhan. From Isfahan, goods could travel south to the Gulf or east toward Mughal markets. No route was reliably superior for all seasons and destinations. A war could make a shorter road more expensive than a lengthy detour; a merchant's trusted agent in a distant city could offset higher carriage costs by reducing fraud or improving the sale price. Customs charges, road tolls, and payments demanded by individual authorities further complicated calculations. The commercial route was therefore a negotiated chain of jurisdictions, not a straight line drawn between two modern map pins.

Transport costs and risk decided which goods were worth carrying. High-value, low-weight goods such as silk and pearls could bear long overland journeys, while bulky goods such as grain generally moved only short distances. Bandits, theft, bad weather, animal deaths, and war could all destroy a shipment or force a detour, and merchants responded by traveling in convoy, hiring guards, purchasing the protection of local authorities, and using intermediaries who knew the ground. Safe passage also depended on politics, since conflicts between the Safavids and the Ottomans, or unrest along the Caucasus and eastern frontiers, could close or divert routes. The next sections turn to the merchants who organized such journeys, starting with the founding of the community that came to dominate Safavid silk trade.

Section 63.6

Old Julfa, Forced Migration, and the Establishment of New Julfa

Julfa (Jugha) was a town on the Aras river, in the borderland between Iran and Armenia, whose Armenian inhabitants had a reputation as merchants well before the seventeenth century. Its location on routes leading from Tabriz toward Anatolia and the Caucasus gave its traders access to several markets, and its merchants participated in the silk trade and in regional exchange. In the late sixteenth century this area was repeatedly contested between the Safavids and the Ottomans (Chapter 59), and it passed back and forth during the wars of the period.

In 1603, Abbas I opened a campaign to recover territories lost to the Ottomans. During the campaign of 1604, as Safavid forces moved through the Armenian and Caucasian borderlands, the shah ordered the removal of large numbers of people from Julfa and neighboring districts, both to deny the advancing Ottoman army resources and to resettle productive populations in the Iranian interior. Armenian chroniclers, notably Arakel of Tabriz, recorded the event as a disaster, and European observers also noted it. Estimates of the number of people deported vary widely, and no reliable exact figure exists; the numbers cited in older works are often large and should be treated with caution. What is not in doubt is that the deportation was coerced, that it brought hardship and loss of life, and that families were separated from homes and property.

In 1605 a settlement for the deportees from Julfa was established across the Zayandeh river from Isfahan and named New Julfa. The Safavid crown granted the community privileges. The Armenians were permitted to maintain their own religious life, to build churches, and to be governed internally through a headman (kalantar) who dealt with the state. Some leading merchants received royal favor, and later sources suggest that the crown supported certain families with capital or commercial advantages, although details are hard to verify. New Julfa subsequently grew into a substantial community with churches, a cathedral begun in the mid-seventeenth century, and merchants' houses.

The circumstances of forced settlement were not uniform. In the frontier campaigns that began in 1604, the Safavid monarchy sought to deny the Ottoman army local provisions and to bring valuable human resources under its own authority. The treatment of displaced people depended on location, social standing, and their usefulness to the state. Certain Julfan merchant households eventually obtained commercial and communal privileges, whereas many other Armenian deportees, and other communities moved in the same operations, endured harsher labor and settlement conditions. A later record of a successful merchant family cannot be projected backward onto every person who survived the journey. Distinguishing those experiences prevents economic history from turning coerced migration into an uncomplicated story of opportunity.

New Julfa's emergence involved several kinds of negotiation. Merchant families needed security for their goods and persons, the ability to appoint representatives, places for religious worship, and some predictability in their obligations to officials. The shah needed their contacts, experience, capital, and access to western markets. Such complementary interests could produce favorable agreements, but they did not put both parties on equal footing. The crown retained coercive power and could revise privileges, while merchant households had varying capacities to relocate assets or appeal to other authorities. The relationship was consequently durable in some periods and deeply insecure in others. This ambiguity should remain visible when the narrative moves from the deportations to commercial success.

The history of New Julfa must hold two facts together. The merchants who built international networks from the quarter were descendants of people forced from their homes, and many deportees suffered severe hardship, including hardship in the course of the journey and during resettlement. At the same time, a number of Julfan families used the opportunities available in Isfahan to build commercial houses of great reach. The commercial success of some families should not be allowed to obscure the violence of the origin, and the violence should not be used to flatten the complexity of what followed. Later Armenian memory has regarded the deportation as a trauma, while royal and later European accounts often treated it as a favor to the Armenians, and historians evaluate such claims against the evidence of the displacement itself.

Section 63.7

Armenian Merchant Houses: Family Firms, Credit, Partnership, and Commercial Trust

The commercial organization of New Julfa has been the subject of detailed modern study, notably by Sebouh Aslanian, who used a large body of Julfan merchant correspondence. The basic unit was the family firm: a trading house led by a senior merchant, supported by sons, brothers, nephews, and other relatives, and extended by agents placed in distant markets. Young men were trained within the house, often beginning as junior agents or clerks who learned commerce, accounting, and the languages needed for trade through practice abroad. Education combined apprenticeship with literacy in Armenian and, for many, in other languages relevant to their destinations.

Historical Vocabulary
Commercial Factors, Agents, and Partnerships (the commenda)

Definition: A factor or agent is a person who conducts trade on behalf of another. The commenda is a long-standing form of commercial partnership in which a capital-providing merchant entrusts funds or goods to an agent who travels and trades, with profits shared on agreed terms. Scholars use the term for the arrangements by which Julfan houses sent capital to agents abroad.

Historical context: Julfan merchants relied on agents in Mediterranean, Indian, Russian, and Southeast Asian markets. The arrangement allowed a house in Isfahan to operate in many places without traveling there itself, but it depended on the agent's honesty and judgment.

Why it matters: The agency system is how a community of limited size could operate across a vast area. It also created the problem of trust, which the community addressed through kinship, reputation, correspondence, and shared institutions.

Potential misunderstanding: A modern reader may picture a salaried employee. In practice, the relationship usually combined elements of partnership, employment, and kinship, and its terms varied.

The key difficulty of long-distance trade was trust. An agent in Venice or Madras could not be supervised from Isfahan. The sources suggest that Julfan merchants managed this problem through several overlapping mechanisms. Agents were frequently kin or members of the same community, so that misbehavior risked damage to family and reputation. Merchants wrote to each other regularly, exchanging price information, instructions, and accounts, and the circulation of this correspondence helped both to coordinate transactions and to track agents' conduct. Community institutions, including the church and the authority of the kalantar, offered arbitration of disputes within the community, which reduced reliance on foreign courts. Merchants also kept written accounts and used bookkeeping to monitor partners.

A long-distance merchant firm solved the problem of absence. Its owner might purchase silk in Iran, authorize an agent to sell it in Aleppo, instruct another correspondent to convert proceeds into silver or cloth, and arrange for the return of funds through an exchange of accounts rather than through the physical carriage of all the coins. Each instruction created opportunities for misunderstanding, delayed delivery, dishonest reporting, or loss at sea. Partnership agreements and obligations among relatives could reduce certain risks, but kinship did not eliminate conflict. The mechanisms mattered because merchants operated in places where a Safavid court order was unenforceable and where local legal institutions applied different rules.

Commercial training therefore involved skills beyond arithmetic. Agents had to learn names of weights and measures in several markets, recognize differences in currency and quality, interpret letters, maintain accounts, and negotiate with brokers and authorities. The Julfan educator Kostand Varzapet is associated with an instructional compilation that described rules of trade and the measures used in many cities. This is evidence that commercial knowledge could be deliberately taught within a community; it does not mean every apprentice attended a formal school. Religious networks and traveling clergy also conveyed news and helped maintain contacts among distant settlements. The history of the merchant house thus combines family strategy, education, community institutions, and practical calculation.

The working papers of a firm can be misleading if read as a transparent narrative. Letters generally record disagreements, information needs, and instructions precisely because business was uncertain; successful routine exchanges may leave little trace. A merchant recording a complaint about a negligent agent tells us that a dispute existed, but not that most agents were dishonest. Even a detailed account may omit the contributions of servants, carriers, or women who helped maintain the household. Historians reconstruct the network by comparing correspondence with contracts, legal proceedings, religious records, and records kept by other communities.

Disputes, debts, and losses were inevitable. A shipment might be lost at sea or seized, prices might fall between purchase and sale, or an agent might be accused of misappropriating funds. The correspondence shows merchants complaining of delays, disputing accounts, and negotiating compensation, and it shows that trust was maintained partly by reputation and partly by the possibility of exclusion from the network. The system was not frictionless, and individual houses failed. What the evidence suggests is that its institutions made repeated cooperation more attractive than defection.

Section 63.8

The New Julfa Commercial Diaspora: From the Mediterranean to India and Southeast Asia

Julfan merchants and their agents were active across a large part of Eurasia. In the Mediterranean world, they traded through Aleppo and other Levantine cities, where Iranian silk was sold to European buyers, and in Italian ports such as Venice and Livorno and, later, in Amsterdam and Marseille. In the north, they were active in Astrakhan and sought access to Russian routes. In India, they operated in Surat and other cities and, in the later seventeenth century, in Madras, where their settlement and the 1688 agreement with the English East India Company show a durable presence. Julfan merchants and their agents also reached Southeast Asian centers such as Manila, Batavia, and Siam. This geographic range is well documented in the sources.

The network's institutions were as important as its reach. Kinship and shared community membership gave the network its basic cohesion. Community institutions, including churches and the authority of respected merchants, provided a framework for settling disputes and for preserving the identity of far-flung communities. Multilingual communication allowed merchants to deal with Italian, Ottoman, Russian, Indian, and Dutch partners. Agents in each major port also gathered commercial intelligence, information about prices, shipping, and political developments, which gave the network an advantage in timing purchases and sales.

The extent of the diaspora was created incrementally. A few agents established links at one commercial center, whose success attracted associates and relatives; other settlements remained small or temporary. Firms with agents in both Mediterranean and Indian ports could exploit price differences and divert shipments when war or bad weather disrupted a familiar route. Yet travel to a faraway city was not itself evidence that large volumes of silk passed through that city. Some settlements served mainly as information points, debt-collection centers, or places to obtain alternative products. Describing a network by listing its distant endpoints can exaggerate the concentration of actual trade along those extremities.

The seventeenth-century expansion also reveals the difference between an Armenian commercial community and a universal Armenian corporate organization. Merchant houses competed with one another even when they shared churches, language, marriage ties, and practices of communal arbitration. The wealthy merchant negotiating in London did not necessarily speak for poor artisans or for all families in New Julfa. Collective petitions or privileged agreements could nonetheless present a unified community when doing so offered advantage. The 1688 agreement negotiated with the English East India Company in London is an instructive case: an Armenian representative claimed authority on behalf of a wider group, and the company sought access to networks it could not itself construct. Its relevance lies in the negotiation of commercial rights across empires, not in a claim that every Julfan trader used the same routes or accepted the same bargain.

New Julfa thus became part of what historians call a transimperial trading network, a system of exchange that crossed the boundaries of several states and that depended on none of them completely. Its members lived under Safavid, Ottoman, Russian, Mughal, and European jurisdictions, and negotiated privileges with each. Historians distinguish, however, between the network's geographical reach, which is well documented, and the volume of its transactions, which is much harder to measure. Claims that Armenians handled most of Iran's silk exports or most of its foreign trade should therefore be treated with care. The surviving evidence supports the conclusion that Julfans were major participants, particularly in silk exports to the Levant and Europe, but it does not allow a precise calculation of their share, and other merchants, including Iranian Muslims, Indians, and Europeans, also traded.

Historical Debate

Merchant Autonomy or Royal Sponsorship?

The question: Was the Julfan commercial network chiefly the product of royal sponsorship, in which Shah Abbas and his successors used the Armenians as instruments of state commercial policy, or did it develop largely through the independent initiative and organization of the merchants themselves?

Interpretation A — Royal sponsorship: Scholarship such as that of Ina Baghdiantz McCabe has emphasized the shah's role in creating and supporting the Julfan community, including the grant of privileges, the use of Armenians as agents in the silk trade, and the connection between royal silk policy and Armenian commerce.
Interpretation B — Merchant autonomy: Sebouh Aslanian and others have stressed the independent organization of the Julfan network, arguing that its institutions of kinship, correspondence, and agency developed largely from the merchants' own practices and that its operations extended far beyond the reach of the Safavid state.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on Armenian chronicles, merchant correspondence, European company records, and Safavid and European descriptions of the shah's dealings with Armenian merchants.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the existence of particular facts. Historians agree that the crown granted privileges and that merchants built their own networks. They differ about which was primary and about how much of the network's later development depended on its Safavid origins.

Why it matters: The answer affects how we describe Safavid economic power. If sponsorship was decisive, the Safavid state looks like a deliberate builder of commercial capacity. If autonomy was decisive, the network looks like a private enterprise that the state tolerated, taxed, and used, but did not control.

Student task: Identify what evidence would best distinguish the two interpretations, for example, evidence on the sources of merchant capital or on the network's activity outside Safavid territory, and explain how the evidence might support a combined view.

Section 63.9

Hormuz, Portuguese Maritime Power, and the Persian Gulf before 1622

The Persian Gulf is a semi-enclosed sea connected to the Indian Ocean through the Strait of Hormuz, and its shores have long been commercial meeting points. On the Iranian side, ports such as Siraf in earlier centuries and the harbors of the Fars and Kerman coasts handled seaborne trade. On the Arabian side, Bahrain was famed for its pearl banks, and Muscat controlled access from the Gulf of Oman. At the head of the Gulf, Basra connected the sea to Mesopotamia, and under the Ottomans after 1546 it became an outlet of Ottoman commerce. Hormuz, a small island near the strait, became a leading entrepôt in the late medieval and early modern periods, serving as a point where goods from India and the Gulf were exchanged with those from the Iranian hinterland.

The Portuguese came to the Gulf as part of their expansion across the Indian Ocean. Alfonso de Albuquerque attacked Hormuz in 1507, and after later campaigns the Portuguese established firm control of the island by 1515, holding it through a system of fortification, a customs house, and an arrangement under which a local ruler remained nominally in place under Portuguese oversight. The Portuguese also held Muscat and had a presence on Bahrain for periods, giving them positions at key points around the Gulf. Their aim was less to rule a territorial empire than to command strategic ports and levy customs on shipping, and they issued passes (cartazes) which Asian vessels were expected to carry and to pay for.

Historians caution, however, against overstating Portuguese control. The Estado da Índia could dominate particular ports and fortresses and could intercept ships, but it never controlled every merchant or vessel in the Gulf. Asian merchants continued to use other ports, to evade Portuguese customs, and to trade by routes that bypassed Portuguese posts, and the Ottomans and Safavids each had interests that limited Portuguese freedom of action. Portuguese influence over strategic ports was real, but it coexisted with a large volume of Asian shipping that operated within, around, and sometimes against it.

Hormuz's political significance arose from its position at an important maritime chokepoint, but its commercial activity relied on a far larger region. Vessels brought goods from India and Arab ports, while caravan routes connected the island's customs system to the Iranian hinterland. Maritime commerce passed through many smaller harbors and anchorages as well, and shipowners chose where to call based on duties, water supplies, weather, and security. A fortress could pressure traders and provide protection in some circumstances, yet customs officials could not know every transaction occurring beyond the harbor. This gap between political domination and commercial reality is crucial to understanding the Portuguese presence.

Portuguese ambitions also met local resistance and accommodation. Some merchants valued access to armed escorts or predictable passage agreements; others objected to fees, inspections, and interference with established trade. A ship's captain might acquire a Portuguese pass while pursuing business with merchants who did not support Portuguese supremacy. Political categories therefore obscure a complex system of bargaining among port rulers, armed powers, and commercial intermediaries. Moreover, Portuguese records commonly emphasize naval operations and taxation, offering a biased picture of the much larger Asian merchant population. To reconstruct the Gulf economy, students must pair such records with Persian, Arabic, and Indian evidence wherever possible.

For Safavid Iran, the Portuguese hold on Hormuz was a problem. The Portuguese collected customs on goods passing through the island, which reduced the revenue that Iranian authorities obtained from maritime trade, and Portuguese control of the strait limited the crown's ability to direct Gulf commerce. Abbas I's search for ways to redirect trade, to secure revenue, and to challenge Portuguese power set the stage for the events of the early 1620s, which the next section examines.

Section 63.10

The Safavid Recovery of Hormuz, 1622, and the Redistribution of Maritime Power

In 1622 a Safavid force under the governor of Fars, Imam-Quli Khan, took Hormuz from the Portuguese with the naval assistance of the English East India Company. Chapter 61 treats the territorial and military aspects of the conquest, and this section considers its commercial consequences. The English contributed ships, which the Safavids lacked in sufficient number to blockade the island and to prevent Portuguese relief, and in return the Company was promised a share of the customs revenue of the port that replaced Hormuz and certain commercial privileges. The Portuguese position at Hormuz collapsed in the spring of 1622.

The capture of the island did not simply transfer a customs house from one ruler to another. The Safavids did not rebuild Hormuz as the center of Gulf trade. The island's economy had depended on imported water and food, and its role as an entrepôt on the strait was tied to the Portuguese system, which had now been destroyed. Commerce shifted instead to a mainland port, Bandar Abbas, which was closer to the Iranian interior and more easily supplied. Hormuz declined, and the Safavid crown now had a more direct claim over customs from the Gulf's principal Iranian outlet.

The redistribution of maritime power was partial. The Portuguese lost Hormuz but retained Muscat until the Omanis expelled them in 1650, and they remained significant elsewhere in the Indian Ocean. The English gained privileges but found that the shah's officials did not always honor the promised customs share. The Safavids gained a port and a larger fiscal stake in maritime trade, but they did not become a naval power, and they depended on others for sea power. The Dutch, who were establishing their own presence in the region, benefited from a more open field. For Iranian and Indian merchants, the end of Portuguese control altered the cost and risk of using Gulf ports, though they continued to face the dues and uncertainties of any port.

The expedition of 1622 exposed the unequal capabilities of partners. Safavid land forces could besiege a fortified position, while the English Company's ships contributed naval power without which the operation would have taken a different course. Imam Quli Khan, the governor of Fars, was central to the Safavid campaign. Yet neither the agreement to cooperate nor the fall of the fortress created a joint sovereign authority over the Gulf. Following victory, each party argued over the meaning of promised customs shares and privileges. English factors described frustrating delays and imperfect payments; Safavid officials viewed the port as part of the shah's territory and revenue system. This divergence explains why cooperation in war was followed by persistent commercial bargaining.

The transfer of importance to the mainland did not happen because an entire maritime economy could be moved by a proclamation. Traders had reasons to prefer a port with more direct access to caravan routes and a royal customs administration, but they also had existing networks, warehouses, and shipping practices elsewhere. Hormuz's earlier position depended on more than a fortress, and merchants continued to use several ports around the Gulf. The long-term effect of the conquest was a redistribution of political leverage and revenue opportunities. It did not end Portuguese activity elsewhere in the Indian Ocean, nor did it give the English Company uncontested dominance of Persian Gulf trade.

The 1622 events are therefore best understood as a commercial restructuring. They strengthened Safavid influence over a key maritime gateway, drew European companies into direct relations with Safavid officials, and opened the way for the commercial development of Bandar Abbas. They did not produce a Safavid maritime empire, and the benefits were unevenly shared among the crown, the English, and the merchants who actually carried the trade.

Section 63.11

Bandar Abbas and the Commercial Transformation of the Persian Gulf

Bandar Abbas, on the mainland coast opposite Hormuz, had been a small harbor before 1622. Afterward, it became Iran's principal maritime port. Its growth reflected both the Safavid crown's interest in the revenue it could generate and the practical advantages of a mainland site. It was linked by caravan routes to Kerman, Shiraz, Yazd, and Isfahan, and through those to the rest of the empire. Goods arriving by sea from India and elsewhere were unloaded, stored in warehouses, assessed for duty, and sent inland, while goods from the interior, such as silk, carpets, and dried fruit, were gathered for shipment.

Customs Duties and Trading Factories

Definition: Customs duties are charges levied on goods crossing a border or passing through a port. A trading factory was a fixed base, comprising residences, storage, and offices, established in a foreign city or port by a merchant company or community, where its agents (factors) conducted business.

Historical context: At Bandar Abbas, the Safavid crown collected customs, and the English and Dutch companies maintained factories where their agents negotiated, stored goods, and kept accounts. Their privileges, such as exemptions or shares of customs, were matters of negotiation and dispute.

Why it matters: Customs were a principal link between commerce and state revenue, and factories were the points where foreign companies met Safavid administration. Disputes over customs reveal the practical limits of each side's power.

Potential misunderstanding: A "factory" in this period was not a manufacturing plant. The word comes from "factor," meaning an agent.

The port was administered by officials appointed by the crown, who supervised customs, and the revenue could be assigned or leased in various ways. European companies maintained representatives and warehouses there, as did Indian, Armenian, and Iranian merchants. Brokers and translators facilitated deals, and local labor loaded and unloaded ships, carried goods, and tended animals. The sources, including company records and European travelers, describe Bandar Abbas as a busy but unhealthy place: its summer heat was severe, many European visitors reported illness, and merchants often left the coast for cooler inland towns during the hottest months.

Bandar Abbas linked commercial actors who worked at very different scales. Provincial officials and customs personnel sought receipts for the royal treasury; ship agents required pilots, port labor, storage, and services; and inland carriers coordinated departures with maritime arrivals. A delay in unloading a vessel could cause a merchant to miss a caravan season, while a disruption on the road from Shiraz might leave a warehouse crowded with goods that could not reach their intended market. The port's importance lay partly in making these kinds of transfer possible. Its commercial success also required officials to negotiate with foreign companies and local traders rather than simply assess a fixed rate on all incoming cargo.

The privileges of a European trading company could confer important advantages while generating disputes with other merchants. Customs exemptions sometimes made company shipping attractive to Asian traders who paid freight to use its ships, while revenue farmers and harbor officials attempted to protect their own income. These arrangements blurred the line between a company's private merchandise and goods belonging to independent customers. Competition among English, Dutch, and Asian merchants involved shipping capacity, credit, customs expectations, and the timing of demand, not merely the possession of cannon. Bandar Abbas accordingly provides a useful case study in how a Safavid port could be both an instrument of royal policy and a place where many different authorities and commercial interests had to cooperate.

The port's practical difficulties were considerable. The long caravan journey to Isfahan took several weeks, and transport costs, tolls, and the risk of theft all affected profits. The Safavid court, far inland, sometimes took time to respond to disputes, and local officials sometimes pursued their own interests. Relations among the crown, the merchants, and the port administration were therefore a constant negotiation. Royal revenue from the port was substantial but variable, depending on the volume of shipping, on the arrangements for collecting duty, and on the degree to which merchants could evade it. The transformation of the Gulf economy after 1622 gave Iran a more active maritime outlet, but it did not remove these frictions.

Section 63.12

Indian Ocean Shipping: Surat, Gujarat, Arabian Ports, and Maritime Exchange

The most important sea link for the Gulf ran to Mughal India, especially to Surat and the Gujarat coast. Indian ships carried cotton textiles, indigo and other dyes, spices, sugar, and other goods to Gulf ports, and returned with Iranian and Gulf products. Among the goods that sailed toward India were silk, carpets, dried fruit, rose water, and wine, along with pearls from the Gulf's banks. Horses were an especially notable item: Iranian and Arabian horses were exported in large numbers to India, where the Mughal military and elite demanded them, and the horse trade was a major component of Gulf commerce. Precious metals also moved, as merchants settled balances in silver and gold.

The participants in this trade were diverse. Indian merchants, including Gujarati and other traders who established communities in Iranian ports and cities, were particularly prominent, and their banking and credit operations were widely used. Iranian and Armenian merchants sailed or sent goods and agents to India. Arab merchants and shipowners operated vessels along the Arabian coasts, and Arab ports such as Muscat served as stops in the network. European companies, first the English and Dutch, entered the trade with armed ships and organized fleets. Historians have emphasized that European shipping did not dominate every part of the network: Asian merchants and shipowners carried much of the cargo, and the Europeans were one group of participants among others, though their naval strength gave them leverage in certain places and times.

The trade was seasonal, structured by the monsoon winds, which determined when ships could sail between India and the Gulf. Merchants planned shipments around these cycles, and the timing of arrivals and departures affected prices and credit. Port cities coordinated the exchange of goods between the interior and the sea. Surat's links with Bandar Abbas made the Gulf one of the principal places where the Safavid economy met the Mughal one, and the exchange between them was one of the largest commercial relationships of Safavid Iran.

The Indian Ocean cannot be reduced to a route between two states. Cargoes moved through a web of ports, and ownership could change at successive stops. Gujarati shipowners and brokers participated in established commercial communities whose contacts were often older and wider than the Safavid or English presence at Bandar Abbas. Goods from the Deccan, Bengal, or Southeast Asia could appear at western Indian ports before being shipped to the Gulf, just as Iranian products might reach an Indian consumer through several intermediaries. This explains why a document recording an English ship sailing from Surat tells us only part of the history of its cargo: the merchants financing it and the markets supplying it may have been predominantly Asian.

The composition of cargoes also shaped commercial decisions. Raw silk and precious materials could justify expensive transport, while horses required space, feed, and attention and were vulnerable to loss in a different way. Cotton cloth existed in many grades and served markets ranging from everyday clothing to luxury consumption. Silver and other precious metals helped settle balances when the goods offered by one market did not match the demands of another. Merchants often combined cargoes to spread risk, and some used their profits in one commodity to finance purchases in another. These practices created an economic geography that did not coincide neatly with either political frontiers or the maritime routes visible on a single voyage.

This maritime world connected Iran to a broader Indian Ocean system without making Iran subordinate to it. Iranian silk, horses, and other goods were wanted overseas, and Iran's demand for Indian textiles and for spices and dyes was substantial. The Gulf became a commercial bridge in which Iranians, Indians, Armenians, Arabs, and Europeans all participated, and the shape of that bridge depended on all of them.

Division III outcome: Students should understand how the Persian Gulf became an important commercial bridge between Safavid Iran and the Indian Ocean world, and why that development involved several interacting powers.

Section 63.13

The English and Dutch East India Companies: Privileges, Competition, and Commercial Negotiation

The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, and the Dutch East India Company (VOC), chartered in 1602, were joint-stock enterprises that combined trade with armed shipping and with diplomacy. Both sought access to Persian silk, and both established trading factories at Bandar Abbas, with further factories or representatives in Isfahan and other cities, from the 1620s. Their relationships with Safavid officials were negotiated through agreements, gifts, and farmans (royal decrees), and the privileges they obtained, such as customs exemptions or shares of port revenue, were subject to repeated dispute.

The companies did not arrive in an empty market. They competed with each other, and also with Armenian, Indian, Iranian, and Ottoman merchants who already handled much of the silk trade. A company could offer capital, shipping, and, in the case of the Dutch, access to the spices and other goods of Southeast Asia, but it also had to deal with Safavid officials and brokers who knew local conditions far better than its servants did. Local intermediaries, interpreters, and brokers were indispensable, and the companies often depended on them for access to silk and for information about prices. In many respects the Europeans entered an existing Asian system as new participants rather than replacing it.

The English and Dutch companies were organized as chartered commercial enterprises, but their institutions did not operate in Iran as miniature sovereign states. Company factors had to obtain access, defend credit arrangements, employ local brokers, and settle disputes with officials who interpreted privileges differently. Their instructions from Europe could be based on prices and political conditions that had changed by the time a ship arrived. Moreover, English and Dutch expectations were not identical. Each sought goods that would sell profitably elsewhere in its network and sometimes used the threat of moving its business as leverage, even when withdrawal would be costly. Differences among officials in Isfahan, Bandar Abbas, and provincial towns could make a nominally clear agreement difficult to execute.

The documentary record reflects these practical tensions. Company letters commonly emphasize unfulfilled promises, shortages of suitable merchandise, and the need for more cash or marketable textiles. Such complaints should be treated as evidence of commercial difficulties, not as neutral judgments on the Safavid economy as a whole. Local merchants could benefit from working with the companies while also resisting their attempts to secure favorable prices. Persian and Indian brokers sometimes mediated transactions and provided knowledge that foreign factors lacked. This evidence helps students see why European chartered companies mattered without adopting a European-centered explanation of commerce. It also makes commercial negotiation, rather than inevitable foreign dominance, the focus of the section.

The companies' fortunes in Iran were mixed. The English hoped for a large silk trade but found the shah's control of silk and the preferences of his officials frustrating, and their share of the promised customs revenue was often disputed. The Dutch secured contracts and a substantial presence, and in the course of the century the VOC became a leading European buyer of Iranian silk, though from the mid-seventeenth century it increasingly turned to other sources, as Section 63.19 discusses. The companies also traded in other goods, importing spices, sugar, and textiles and exporting silk, carpets, and other Iranian products. Their presence added competition and capital to the Gulf, and it influenced prices and the terms offered to Iranian suppliers.

Section 63.14

Mughal India, Central Asia, and Asian Commercial Connections

The overland trade with the east and northeast ran through a set of corridors in the territories later associated with Afghanistan and Central Asia. Herat, Kandahar, and Kabul were key points. Caravans from Iran moved through Herat and Kandahar toward Multan and Lahore in Mughal India, or through Kabul toward the Punjab, and in the opposite direction toward Balkh and Bukhara. These routes carried horses from Central Asia and Iran into India, Indian cotton textiles and indigo toward the west, and a variety of goods such as precious stones, dyes, manufactured items, and tea and other products reaching Iran from still farther afield.

The merchants who used these routes included Iranians, Indians, and Central Asians. Indian merchants, particularly from Multan, formed communities in Iranian and Central Asian cities and played a prominent role in credit and trade. Stephen Dale's study of Indian merchants in Eurasian trade has shown how widely they operated. Safavid, Mughal, and Central Asian merchants and officials all had interests in the movement of goods and in the revenue that it produced.

Kandahar was a strategic crossroads, but the profitability of its trade depended on more than which ruler held the fortress. Caravans needed agreements with local authorities, reliable water and provisioning, and access to markets beyond the military frontier. The city changed hands in 1622, 1638, and 1649; each transfer could alter duties, garrisons, and the perceived security of a route. A merchant house might prefer to divert goods during a siege rather than lose them altogether. Political change could therefore affect the direction and timing of caravans without making the movement of goods permanently impossible. The distinction between territorial sovereignty and commercial continuity is especially important in a region where Persianate diplomatic and cultural practices were shared across imperial lines.

Iranian and Indian merchant communities also handled finance and procurement in ways that connected several distant markets. Indian merchants from Gujarat, Multan, and other centers appeared in Iranian towns as traders, lenders, and intermediaries. Central Asian routes brought livestock, textiles, and other merchandise through the oasis cities of Transoxiana; changes in military control or the customs regime influenced which routes were favored. These connections were diverse rather than uniform. The merchant selling cloth in Isfahan, the horse trader operating near Kandahar, and the broker sending silver toward a port faced different risks and held different kinds of information. Their businesses nonetheless intersected through credit, transport services, and markets for imported goods.

Because the sources are uneven, historians should not estimate the size of all Asian trade from the accounts of one European factory. Company records often describe commodities and partners visible to their agents, while omitting transactions conducted privately by Indian and Iranian merchants. When such records do identify local financial intermediaries, the very fact that a company needed them reveals something important about the scale of existing networks. Students should therefore read statistics and reports as samples of particular businesses, not as complete surveys of an imperial economy.

Politics affected but did not end this trade. Kandahar, a strategic city between the Safavid and Mughal realms, changed hands several times in the seventeenth century: the Safavids took it in 1622, the Mughals secured it in 1638, and the Safavids regained it in 1649. Wars and changes of frontier raised the risk and cost of travel and sometimes disrupted caravans, but merchants adapted by changing routes, paying new tolls, and waiting for conditions to improve. The sources indicate that commercial exchange continued across periods of conflict, because the traders, officials, and rulers involved all gained from it. Frontiers changed the terms of trade without necessarily closing it.

Section 63.15

The Ottoman, Caspian, and Russian Routes: Commerce across Political Frontiers

For much of the Safavid period, the principal route for silk to Europe ran west. Caravans carried silk from Iran across the northwest through Tabriz and into Ottoman Anatolia, and from there to Aleppo and the Mediterranean coast, or to Bursa and Istanbul. The Ottoman textile industries needed Iranian silk, and Ottoman customs on its passage were a significant source of revenue. This created an awkward fact: the Ottomans and Safavids were rivals who fought repeated wars, yet they were also trading partners with a mutual stake in the traffic. At various times Ottoman rulers prohibited or restricted trade in Iranian silk during wartime, and Safavid rulers sought to divert silk away from Ottoman territory, but enforcement was uneven, and trade often continued.

Peace in 1639 (Chapter 59 treats the earlier conflicts) reduced the risk on the western routes and helped to stabilize the Levant trade, which Armenian merchants used heavily. In Aleppo, they sold silk to European merchants, including those of English, French, and Italian origin, and bought imports for the Iranian market. The system depended on the cooperation of Ottoman officials and on the tolerance of Ottoman customs.

Northern routes offered alternatives. Caravans and boats carried silk and other goods across the Caspian Sea and up the Volga through Astrakhan, which had been in Russian hands since 1556, toward Russia and, in principle, toward northern European ports such as Archangel. In 1667 the Russian government concluded an agreement with representatives of Julfan merchants concerning the transit of Persian silk across Russia. The northern route faced difficulties: the long and risky journey, Russian customs and regulations, and disorder on the Caspian and Volga in the late 1660s associated with the Cossack rebellion of Stenka Razin. The sources suggest that the route did not replace the Levantine or Gulf trades, but it provided a way to diversify.

Political frontiers were expensive but porous. A caravan moving west from Iran might pass through districts where Safavid and Ottoman authorities competed for influence, then reach cities where merchants had well-established relationships despite the rivalry of the two imperial courts. Warfare could prompt seizure of goods, interrupt correspondence, or raise tolls, but commercial demand and the interests of local officials sometimes encouraged continued exchange. Ottoman textile producers valued Iranian silk; merchants and brokers on both sides benefited from its passage. Thus even a period of intense political hostility could contain substantial economic interdependence. The route through Aleppo mattered partly because of the Mediterranean buyers and commercial institutions concentrated there, not simply because it was geographically direct.

The northern route offered different opportunities and dangers. After Russia acquired Astrakhan in 1556, merchants used the Volga-Caspian corridor to connect Iranian products to Russian and ultimately European markets. The privileges granted to Julfan Armenian merchants in 1667 were the result of negotiations between merchant representatives and the Russian government. The agreement allowed special transit rights, including an important position in silk transport, but conflict, the Cossack raids of the late 1660s, charges at transfer points, and the interests of merchants committed to Ottoman routes limited its immediate impact. Later revisions to privileges and Russian restrictions altered the scheme again. The agreement therefore illustrates state sponsorship of an alternative route, not evidence that an entire trade network promptly abandoned Anatolia.

These routes show why commerce continued across frontiers that separated hostile states. Merchants, officials, and rulers all benefited from customs, from access to goods, and from relations that could be restored after wars. The ability of traders such as the Julfans to move between jurisdictions was itself a commercial asset, since they could shift their goods among routes as conditions required. Trade thus connected states that were otherwise rivals.

Section 63.16

Bullion, Currency, Prices, Credit, and the Economic Logic of Global Exchange

International commerce required financial systems as well as caravans and ships. Iran's silk exports were paid for largely in silver and gold, in coin and bullion brought by European and Asian buyers, and the importation of precious metals was a significant feature of the trade, as Rudi Matthee's study of silk and silver emphasizes. Iran possessed limited domestic silver, so the flow of metal from abroad supported the coinage and the crown's ability to pay its troops and officials. At the same time, Iranian demand for Indian textiles, spices, and other imports sent value back out, and the balance of payments shifted over time.

The monetary system was complex. The sources refer to units of account such as the toman, which was worth 10,000 dinars, and to coins such as the silver abbasi, valued at 200 dinars, so that a toman equaled fifty abbasis. Foreign coins, including European and Spanish silver coins, circulated and were valued by weight and fineness, and coin-changers (sarrafs) assessed and exchanged them. The Safavid crown periodically recoined and sometimes debased coinage, and exchange rates and metal contents varied. For the detailed monetary history, students may consult the study by Matthee, Floor, and Clawson listed in the bibliography.

A merchant calculating profit confronted at least three distinct quantities: a price quoted in the unit used at the place of purchase, a transport and taxation bill payable along the route, and a sale price denominated in the market's own currency. Before comparing them, the merchant had to determine what each unit represented. The Safavid toman was a unit of account conventionally valued at 10,000 dinars, while an abbasi was a coin name associated with a nominal accounting value of 200 dinars in the relevant system. Coins were not modern tokens with an unchanging stamped face value; their weight, metal content, local valuations, and acceptance varied over time. A numerical equivalence in a textbook is therefore a guide to accounting terminology, not permission to convert every recorded payment at a fixed universal exchange rate.

Credit arrangements helped overcome the difficulty of carrying money between distant cities, but they created new obligations. A merchant could receive an advance, use funds entrusted by a partner, pledge goods, or arrange settlement through accounts kept by correspondents. Each method shifted the distribution of risk. If a ship was lost, responsibility might depend on the contract; if a sale was delayed, the cost of capital could continue to accumulate. Foreign companies sometimes wanted to pay in goods that Iranian merchants did not wish to buy, leaving silver as a more acceptable settlement medium. Shortages or restrictions on specie could thus disrupt trade even when there was demand for textiles or silk. Accounting and commercial law belonged at the center of economic history because they determined whether a profitable shipment could actually be financed.

The hypothetical exercise in this section deliberately uses invented teaching numbers. It is valuable for showing how margins shrink when costs rise, but it does not measure historical profitability. To reconstruct an actual merchant's return, students would need a recorded purchase quantity and quality, dates of payment and sale, transport and customs charges, coin standards, credit terms, and an account of losses. Even then the result would describe one transaction, not the Safavid export sector as a whole.

Credit was equally important. Merchants rarely carried all of their capital in coin; they used bills of exchange, loans, partnership contracts, and accounts that could be settled over time. Indian bankers and merchants provided credit in Iranian cities and ports, and Julfan houses used correspondents to transfer funds. Customs duties, tolls, and brokerage were further costs that entered the calculation. A merchant considering a shipment had to estimate purchase price, transport costs, duties, risk, interest on borrowed capital, and the sale price in a distant market that might change before the goods arrived.

Section 63.17

The People behind Commerce: Producers, Carriers, Sailors, Brokers, and Commercial Households

A history that stops with merchants and royal officials misses most of the people who made commerce possible. At the base of the silk economy stood agricultural workers: those who planted and tended mulberry trees, fed the silkworms, harvested the cocoons, and reeled the silk. Beyond them were weavers and dyers in urban workshops, apprentices who learned their crafts, and the laborers who prepared materials and carried goods. Their work is poorly documented because the surviving sources were written mainly by officials, merchants, and travelers, and they are most often visible in passing or through aggregate descriptions.

Transport depended on a distinct set of workers. Muleteers and camel drivers, many of them from pastoral or rural communities, supplied animals and labor to the caravans. Caravanserai keepers, guides, and guards provided services along the roads. At the ports, sailors and shipowners operated vessels, and porters and laborers loaded and unloaded goods in the heat. Brokers (dallals) arranged transactions and earned commissions, and translators bridged languages. Servants and household dependents supported merchants' families. Some of these workers were wage earners, some were dependent or enslaved, and others were independent small operators, and their conditions differed widely, as Chapter 64 will examine in more detail.

Women's economic roles are documented unevenly but are visible in several areas. Women took part in rearing silkworms and in reeling, and in spinning, embroidery, and other textile work. In merchant households, women managed domestic economies and could hold property, and some Armenian women are documented in commercial or property matters, although the evidence is thin and the legal position of women differed among Muslim, Armenian, and other communities. Historians should therefore avoid both ignoring women and generalizing from isolated examples.

Everyday Life and Social History

Producers and Laborers in the Silk Economy

Imagine the lives behind a single bale of silk, again as a composite drawn from the patterns in the sources and not from a particular documented individual. In a village of the Caspian provinces, a household's year was organized around the silkworms' cycle. In spring, the family prepared the eggs and tended the larvae, feeding them mulberry leaves several times a day for weeks, with the household's labor concentrated on this task. When the cocoons were formed they were gathered and reeled, a skilled and tiring job. The silk was sold to a local dealer or broker, from whom the household often received less than the silk would fetch in the city, and a portion of the crop or its value went to the landowner, the tax collector, or the crown.

Down the chain, a caravan driver spent weeks on the road with his animals, depending on the weather, the state of the track, and the safety of the stages. At a caravanserai, he and his animals found shelter and fodder. At Bandar Abbas or another port, laborers moved goods in heat that European visitors found oppressive. In Isfahan, urban weavers and dyers worked in workshops in which skill was learned over years, and wealthy merchants' households, such as those of New Julfa, relied on servants and dependents. The people who stood in these places belonged to different religious communities, social ranks, and legal conditions, and their relationships to the merchants and officials who profited from the trade varied accordingly.

Using the sources: Because producers and carriers rarely wrote, historians reconstruct their lives from travelers' descriptions, tax and legal records, archaeological and material evidence, and comparison with better-documented regions. Students should ask who is missing from each source and what that absence means.

  1. Which groups in the silk economy are best documented, and which least? Why?
  2. How might the experiences of a silk-reeling household differ from those of a Julfan merchant family, or of a wage-earning porter?
  3. What kinds of evidence might reveal women's economic work more fully?

The journey of a commercial object can hide several kinds of labor. In a silk-producing village, feeding silkworms and reeling cocoons demanded concentrated seasonal work often performed within households. At a workshop, experienced artisans and apprentices added further value. Carriers transported bales along mountain routes, but the goods could also require packers, guards, stable workers, and people who supplied fodder. At the Gulf, porters, boat crews, pilots, and shipboard labor made maritime departure possible. Each stage exposed people to a different set of risks, and wages or earnings were not necessarily tied to the final price at a distant market. A dramatic increase in export prices might benefit a merchant already holding stock much more immediately than a reeler who had sold her output months earlier.

Family and gender were central even when the records do not enumerate women's wages. Commercial households depended on food preparation, child care, property administration, and the transmission of knowledge. Some women owned assets or acted in inheritance matters; others performed demanding productive or domestic work whose economic contribution was not separately counted. Evidence that a woman or child worked in sericulture should not be generalized into a single Safavid labor regime. The circumstances of a rural cultivator, an enslaved household servant, and a wealthy Julfan merchant's daughter differed sharply. By comparing these social positions, students can understand why a commercially thriving empire was not necessarily prosperous or secure for most of the people whose labor sustained it.

Commercial households and wage laborers lived in different worlds. Wealthy Julfan families built houses and churches and sent sons abroad, while the muleteer, the reeler, and the port laborer lived close to subsistence and bore the physical risks of the work. The prosperity of long-distance commerce did not automatically spread to those who sustained it, and the distribution of its rewards is a central question for the chapter's conclusion.

Section 63.18

Religion, Community Privilege, Commercial Law, and the Limits of Merchant Security

Commerce in Safavid Iran crossed religious and legal boundaries. Muslim, Armenian Christian, Jewish, Hindu, and European merchants all participated, and each belonged to communities with their own institutions and customs. Safavid officials dealt with them through a mixture of Islamic law, customary practice, royal decree, and negotiation. Disputes among members of the same community were often settled within the community, as in the case of the Armenians, whose kalantar and clergy arbitrated, while disputes across communities could go before Safavid officials or judges, whose decisions depended on the parties' status and connections.

The Armenians of New Julfa enjoyed notable privileges, including the right to practice their religion and to organize their own community. Contracts could be enforced through community arbitration, written agreements, and royal favor. Their position was nevertheless precarious. The privileges derived from royal grant and could be restricted, and Armenians, like other non-Muslim subjects, were subject to discriminatory rules and to periodic pressure. Their origins in forced displacement were never forgotten, and later in the seventeenth century restrictions affecting Armenians and other non-Muslims increased in some periods, a subject taken up in Section 63.19. Catholic missionary activity in Isfahan, which sought to bring Armenians into communion with Rome, added further religious tension within the community and between it and the Safavid authorities.

Other communities occupied different positions. Iranian Muslim merchants had the advantage of shared religion and legal standing with the ruling elite, yet they competed with Armenians and foreigners and could not always match the Julfans' networks. Indian merchants, many of them Hindu, were tolerated and valued for their capital and credit, and formed communities in Iranian and Central Asian cities. Jewish merchants and craftsmen lived in several Iranian cities and are documented in trade, and the Jewish community suffered periods of persecution, including pressure to convert in the middle of the seventeenth century. European merchants enjoyed privileges that they negotiated, which were in turn limited by the willingness of Safavid officials to honor them.

Commercial law was not a single universally recognized set of rules. The same merchant might rely on community arbitration for a dispute with a relative, seek a local official's help with a customs demand, and petition a foreign company when cargo traveled on one of its ships. The existence of Armenian communal courts and trusted elders could increase the predictability of dealings within a network; it did not eliminate conflicts with outsiders or the power of Safavid authorities to change the terms of residence and trade. Nor did the privileges enjoyed by some merchants automatically extend to all members of an Armenian congregation. Political favor could be withdrawn, and even a successful family had to consider the security of property left at home while its agents traveled abroad.

The legal position of other communities varied. Muslim merchants could use institutions and connections unavailable to some non-Muslim traders; Indian merchants organized financing and partnerships through their own commercial practices; Jews and Christians confronted particular vulnerabilities in matters of conversion, taxation, and inheritance. These differences are not evidence that commerce operated within permanently isolated communities. Transactions crossed religious boundaries, and merchants often depended on partners who followed different legal traditions. The critical question is how traders managed such pluralism, especially when courts or officials disagreed. Their solutions included reputation, witnesses, written agreements, collateral, reciprocal relationships, and at times the protection of a powerful patron.

Economic opportunity could coexist with discrimination, insecurity, and coercion. A merchant's wealth did not guarantee protection, because privileges could be withdrawn, officials could demand gifts or loans, and legal remedies depended on the relative power of the parties. Safavid commercial law offered some security, and the sources show that merchants used courts and arbitration, but security was never uniform or complete. The history of the communities that sustained Safavid commerce is therefore both a history of enterprise and a history of vulnerability. Chapter 65 considers the religious authorities and institutions that shaped the wider environment in which these communities lived.

Section 63.19

Commercial Change in the Later Safavid Period: Restrictions, Adaptation, and the Problem of Decline

After Abbas I's death in 1629, Safavid commercial institutions changed. The royal silk monopoly was modified over time, applied unevenly, and in various respects relaxed, and the relationship between the crown and merchants was renegotiated under successive shahs. Foreign companies adjusted their strategies. The Dutch, who had been major buyers of Iranian silk, found from the middle of the seventeenth century that silk from Bengal could be obtained at competitive cost, and they bought less from Iran; the English and others also had alternatives. Silk remained important, but it was no longer the only or always the leading option for European buyers.

Other pressures accumulated. The sources report a severe famine around the turn of the 1670s, which strained rural production and the state's revenues. Currency problems, including recoinage and debasement, affected prices and confidence. Restrictions affecting some commercial communities appeared at times, as religious pressures increased under certain rulers in the later seventeenth century, and some merchants relocated their activities or their capital elsewhere, including to India. The Julfan network, for its part, adapted to shifting conditions by developing its presence in India and elsewhere. At the Gulf, Omani naval power rose in the early eighteenth century and disturbed shipping, as Chapter 66 and Chapter 67 will further note. Meanwhile, trade did not stop: the routes, communities, and institutions described in this chapter continued to operate, and some sectors, such as carpet production and certain regional exchanges, remained active.

Historical Debate

Economic Decline or Commercial Restructuring?

The question: Do the commercial changes of the later Safavid period demonstrate a general economic decline that contributed to the dynasty's fall, or a more complicated process of adaptation and regional change?

Interpretation A — Decline: Some historians point to declining European purchases of Iranian silk, monetary problems, famine, restrictions affecting commercial communities, and fiscal difficulties as signs of a weakening economy that left the state vulnerable.
Interpretation B — Restructuring: Others emphasize adaptation, such as the diversification of exports, the continued activity of merchant networks, the redirection of trade, and the difficulty of generalizing from limited sources, and they caution against reading the dynasty's military collapse in 1722 back into the economy of earlier decades.

Evidence: The debate draws on company records, travelers' accounts, monetary evidence, Armenian and Persian sources, and later narratives of decline. Quantitative evidence is limited and often indirect.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over chronology, since changes might have begun at different times in different sectors, over causation, since some factors were external, such as European sourcing decisions, and over interpretation, since evidence of change can be read as decline or as adjustment.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians explain the end of Safavid rule, which Chapters 66 and 67 examine. If commerce was in broad decline, the fall in 1722 appears more predictable, whereas a restructuring reading emphasizes contingency and the role of political and military events.

Student task: List the evidence in this chapter that supports each interpretation, identify what additional evidence would help, and explain why the fall of the dynasty in 1722 should not be treated as the inevitable result of commercial change.

Commercial change was uneven across commodities and routes. A decline in a company's silk purchases might reflect poorer terms in Iran, greater profitability in India, fluctuations in the price of silver, or a strategic decision to use ships for different goods. It cannot by itself establish that Iranian production or all Safavid trade contracted. Likewise, the relocation of a merchant house from New Julfa to Madras or Amsterdam could demonstrate insecurity within Iran, the attraction of an overseas market, or both. Historians must distinguish what happens to commercial activity in one port from what happens to an entire kingdom. The most useful evidence compares a range of records over time rather than treating one disappointed European factor as a representative observer.

The later seventeenth century brought real strains. Safavid rulers imposed taxes and sometimes restrictions on merchants, while political instability and disputes over property rights made some non-Muslim households less secure. Policies favoring converts in inheritance disputes could affect the property held by Armenian families. International disruptions, monetary problems, and changing commercial opportunities further complicated calculations. Yet merchants adapted by developing overseas bases, using alternative routes, and diversifying their goods. The movement of business abroad might reduce the benefit that the Safavid treasury obtained from commercial networks even as the networks themselves survived. This is a more precise historical proposition than saying commerce simply declined.

The severe political crisis that culminated in the siege and fall of Isfahan in 1722 disrupted the conditions in which merchants operated, but it should not determine the interpretation of every earlier decade. Chapter 66 considers the broader arguments about late Safavid state power; Chapter 67 treats the military collapse. Here the task is to identify economic changes and the evidence for them without assuming that every difficulty was a step toward a known endpoint.

Whatever the balance, it would be a mistake to treat the fall of the dynasty as the necessary outcome of commercial developments. The events of 1722 involved military, political, and regional factors, and historians have emphasized contingency. Commercial change in the later seventeenth century was real, but its meaning remains contested, and the evidence supports both difficulty and resilience.

Section 63.20

Historical Synthesis: Safavid Iran and the Early Modern World Economy

The preceding sections show that silk, royal finance, Armenian merchant networks, Gulf ports, and Eurasian trade formed an interconnected system. Households in the Caspian provinces produced silk, which the crown taxed and, from Abbas's time, sought to control. Merchants, especially the Julfan Armenians, organized its sale across Eurasia, supported by agents, credit, and shared institutions. Caravans and ships carried it along overland and maritime routes, passing through customs posts, caravanserais, and ports such as Bandar Abbas. European companies, Indian merchants, and Ottoman, Russian, and Central Asian partners all took part, and silver and credit moved in the opposite direction. The Safavid state benefited through revenue and through the connections that commerce created, but it did not direct the system.

The benefits of commercial integration were unevenly shared. The crown gained cash and a stake in maritime customs. Some merchant families gained wealth and influence. European companies gained access to silk and Gulf ports, although not on the terms they often wanted. Producers, carriers, and laborers, who made the system work, received a much smaller portion and bore many of its risks. For communities such as the deportees from Julfa, integration into the commercial world began in displacement. The system's vulnerabilities included dependence on a few commodities, exposure to European purchasing decisions, the limits of royal control, and the insecurity of non-Muslim merchants.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Safavid Commerce across the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries

What Changed

  • The crown took direct control of the principal silk provinces and sought to regulate silk exports
  • New Julfa created a new merchant community with a worldwide network
  • The Gulf's commercial center moved from Hormuz to Bandar Abbas after 1622
  • The English and Dutch companies entered the Safavid commercial world
  • European demand for Iranian silk shifted as alternative sources developed

What Stayed the Same

  • Silk remained produced largely by rural households using household labor
  • Caravan routes, caravanserais, and the taxation of passage continued to structure overland trade
  • Asian merchants carried much of the trade
  • Commerce continued across hostile frontiers
  • Merchants relied on brokers, credit, and negotiated relations with officials

When and how: Change occurred unevenly: the crown's policies and the Julfa deportation were abrupt acts, while shifts in European demand and in community conditions developed gradually. Who experienced it: Merchants and officials most directly felt changes in policy and ports, while rural producers experienced continuity in labor and change mainly through prices and taxes. Why it matters: The pattern shows a commercial system with enduring structures and shifting surfaces, which cautions against both narratives of sudden transformation and of timeless tradition.

The Iranian silk economy brought together activities usually studied separately. A rural household chose how to use its labor and mulberry trees; a merchant evaluated the quality of raw silk and planned a shipment; officials decided what duties to assess; an Armenian partner in a distant city advanced funds; and a buyer in another empire decided which commodity was worth purchasing. The shah could alter taxes and privileges, conquer ports, or require merchants to sell to royal agents, but he could not directly control the crop, the international price, the timing of a ship, or the trust between business partners. Economic integration expanded the monarchy's opportunities precisely because it extended far beyond the monarchy's administrative reach.

The most useful comparison is therefore between political control and commercial capacity. Safavid officials could extract revenue from silk lands and customs posts, while independent traders possessed information, credit relationships, and logistical skills that the crown needed. European chartered companies had armed ships and financial backing, but depended on Indian and Iranian intermediaries; Armenian firms reached many markets, yet their households remained exposed to the policies of rulers under whom they lived. These connections made the economy adaptable in some respects and vulnerable in others. Geography shaped the routes, labor sustained the commodities, and institutions determined how risks and returns were shared.

The chapter also shows why histories centered only on luxury trade are incomplete. The silks and precious goods that crossed empires occupied a prominent place in surviving accounts because rulers and wealthy merchants recorded them. The majority of the population relied more directly on ordinary agriculture, local crafts, and markets. Integrating the two scales provides a bridge to Chapter 64, which examines the wider society in which commerce operated, and to subsequent chapters on religious institutions and the political pressures of the late Safavid state.

The chapter's governing conclusion is that Safavid Iran was not an isolated empire occasionally visited by foreign merchants, but an active participant in a complex early modern commercial system linking the Iranian plateau to Europe, Russia, Central Asia, India, and the Indian Ocean. That participation rested on the work of producers, carriers, merchants, and the state together. It brought wealth and connections, and it also brought inequality, coercion, and exposure to forces beyond the crown's control. The next chapter, Chapter 64, turns from commerce to the broader social order that underlay it, examining pastoral tribes, settled communities, enslaved and captive labor, gender relations, minorities, and the hierarchies that structured life under Safavid rule.

Division V outcome: Students should be able to explain both the achievements of Safavid international commerce and the social, political, and economic vulnerabilities of the system.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Safavid Iran participated actively in an early modern commercial system that stretched from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean, and that this participation depended on a chain of producers, carriers, merchants, and state institutions. It began with a transaction in New Julfa to show that a luxury commodity consumed far away rested on village labor, credit, taxation, and transport across many jurisdictions.

Silk formed the foundation. Households in the Caspian provinces and the Caucasus raised silkworms and reeled silk, and their production was taxed and traded. Abbas I's conquest of Gilan in 1592 began the process of bringing its silk revenues under closer royal control, and from about 1619 he sought to regulate silk exports. The policy aimed at cash revenue and, plausibly, at strategic advantage against the Ottomans, but the crown lacked the capacity to enforce it fully, and merchants evaded, bargained, and adapted. Raw silk and finished textiles added value at different stages, and skilled urban workshops kept more of that value in Iran.

The second division showed how overland commerce depended on roads, caravanserais, tolls, and transport animals, and how transport costs and risk shaped what could profitably be carried. The Julfan Armenians emerged from the forced deportation of 1604–1605, an event that brought suffering to many even as selected families later prospered. In New Julfa, family firms, agents, credit, correspondence, and community institutions allowed merchants to operate from the Mediterranean to Southeast Asia. Historians debate whether sponsorship by the crown or the merchants' own initiative was more decisive, and they distinguish the network's documented reach from the less certain volume of its trade.

The third division turned to the Persian Gulf. The Portuguese had controlled Hormuz from the early sixteenth century, and the Safavid capture of the island in 1622, with English naval help, ended Portuguese control there. The commercial consequence was the rise of Bandar Abbas as a mainland port linked to the interior, where customs, warehouses, and trading factories joined crown revenue to merchant activity. Trade with Surat and Gujarat connected the Gulf to Mughal India, and Iranian, Armenian, Indian, Arab, and European participants shared a maritime world that Europeans did not dominate.

The fourth division examined the English and Dutch East India Companies, whose privileges were repeatedly negotiated and disputed, and which depended on local intermediaries and competed with established Asian networks. Overland routes through Herat, Kandahar, and Kabul linked Iran with Mughal India and Central Asia, and the Ottoman, Caspian, and Russian routes carried silk across hostile frontiers. Trade persisted because merchants, officials, and rulers benefited from it. Underlying all of these exchanges were financial mechanisms: bullion flows, currencies, credit, and customs, which the quantitative study illustrated with hypothetical figures.

The fifth division looked at those who sustained commerce and at the limits of their security. Producers, caravan workers, sailors, port laborers, brokers, servants, and merchant households formed a structure in which wealth and vulnerability coexisted. Religious and legal differences among Armenian, Iranian Muslim, Indian, Jewish, and European participants shaped their opportunities and exposed them to discrimination and insecurity, and privileges depended on royal favor.

After Abbas I, the royal monopoly was modified, European purchasing strategies diversified among Iranian and other Asian suppliers, and famine, currency problems, and restrictions on commercial communities affected the economy. Historians disagree over whether these changes constitute general decline or restructuring. The chapter has stressed that the fall of the dynasty in 1722 should not be treated as the inevitable result of commercial developments, and that evidence supports both strain and adaptation.

Continuity and change coexisted. The crown's direct control of silk provinces, the Julfan community, the shift of the Gulf's center to Bandar Abbas, and the entry of European companies were important changes. Rural household production, caravan routes, Asian merchants' leading role, and the practice of trading across hostile frontiers were important continuities. Change was often uneven and experienced differently by merchants, officials, and producers.

Evidence for this history is plural, drawing on Safavid and Armenian chronicles, merchant letters, company records, travelers' accounts, and surviving textiles, each with limitations. The chapter's primary source windows illustrated the importance of asking who created a source, why, and whose voices are missing. The next chapter, Chapter 64, extends the analysis from commerce to the broader social order, examining tribes, slavery, gender, minorities, and hierarchy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Safavid state, so often described through its shahs, armies, and monuments, rested on an economic world that extended far beyond the court. The bale of silk with which the chapter began connected a Caspian village to distant markets, and following it revealed producers, carriers, merchants, brokers, officials, and foreign companies as parts of one system. Understanding that system corrects the image of Safavid Iran as either isolated or wholly directed by the crown.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in early modern history: the importance of diasporic merchant networks that operated across empires, the persistence of Asian commercial systems alongside European expansion, and the tension between state ambitions and the capacity to enforce them. The history of New Julfa in particular joins commerce to coercion, and it cautions students against separating economic achievement from the human costs that sometimes accompany it.

These developments shaped what followed. The commercial networks, ports, and communities of the seventeenth century formed part of the setting in which later Safavid rulers governed and in which the crises of the early eighteenth century unfolded, a story told in Chapters 66 and 67. Chapter 64 turns to the wider social order of pastoral tribes, settled communities, enslaved labor, gender relations, and minorities, which supported commerce and which commerce in turn affected.

The chapter's lasting lesson is methodological as well as substantive. A history of commerce has to be built from sources that were written by a few and that record the work of many, and its conclusions are strongest where they acknowledge uncertainty about volume, enforcement, and the experiences of those who left few records. Students who carry that habit forward will read the rest of the textbook with a fuller sense of how Iran's history was made.
