Shah Abbas I: Royal Power, Military Reform, and Imperial Expansion

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

On 1 October 1588, a seventeen-year-old prince was enthroned at Qazvin as shah of Iran. He held the title, the dynastic name, and the loyalty of the men who had brought him there, and little else: Tabriz was in Ottoman hands, Herat was besieged by Uzbek forces, and the commander who had made him king could dictate whom he might trust. This chapter is about how that ruler, within a generation, became the most formidable monarch of the Safavid dynasty, and about what his consolidation cost the people who lived under it.

Abbas I (born 1571, formally enthroned 1588, d. 1629) has long been remembered as the greatest of the Safavid shahs, and the reputation is not without foundation. By the end of his reign Safavid armies had expelled the Uzbeks from Khurasan, recovered Tabriz, Yerevan, and the Caucasus lands lost to the Ottomans, retaken Kandahar from the Mughals, driven the Portuguese from Hormuz, and entered Baghdad. A new capital at Isfahan housed a court that European envoys described with admiration, and a reorganized army and treasury supported a degree of royal control that his predecessors had not achieved. The retrospective image, found in Persian chronicles, European travel narratives, and later national memory alike, is of a sovereign who restored order and made Iran great.

That image is incomplete in two directions. First, it understates how weak the starting position was. Abbas began his reign amid territorial losses and the tutelage of factional commanders, and for the first decade his priorities were survival and the rebuilding of his means of action rather than expansion. Second, the image tends to omit the instruments through which the transformation was carried out. Military reorganization depended on the recruitment, often through capture, of Caucasian soldiers and administrators who were converted and trained as the shah's personal servants. Financial reorganization expanded the share of the realm whose revenues went directly to the crown. Strategic security in the northwest was pursued through the devastation and depopulation of whole districts, the deportation of Armenians and Georgians, and the settlement of captives across Iran. And the concentration of authority in the person of the shah produced a suspicion of his own sons that left the dynasty with a dangerous problem of succession.

The central question of the chapter is therefore how Shah Abbas I transformed a divided and militarily vulnerable Safavid state into a more centralized and expansionist monarchy, and what political, social, and human costs accompanied that transformation. The argument developed in the following twenty sections is that Abbas's successes resulted from a combination of inherited institutions, strategic compromise, military reorganization, new sources of royal revenue, territorial campaigning, and coercive political control. Those successes were substantial. They did not, however, remove the monarchy's dependence on powerful elites, and they did not secure a stable succession.

The chapter's evidence is rich but uneven in perspective. The central Persian narrative is the Tarikh-e Alam-ara-ye Abbasi of Iskandar Beg Munshi, a court secretary who wrote in the shah's service and whose account is indispensable and openly favorable to its patron. Persian court histories are supplemented by Armenian, Georgian, Ottoman, Russian, and Mughal narratives, by the records of the English East India Company and other European trading companies, by the reports of missionaries and diplomats, and by travelers such as Pietro Della Valle. These sources pursue different interests, and none of them gives a voice to most of the cultivators, pastoralists, captives, and townspeople whose lives the reign altered. Where the chapter reconstructs such lives it says so, and it identifies analytical composites as composites.

The chapter fits among its neighbors as follows. Chapter 60 treated the reign of Shah Tahmasp I and the institutions that Abbas inherited; this chapter begins with the crisis that followed Tahmasp's death only to the extent needed to explain the accession. Chapter 62 will treat Isfahan, its buildings, markets, and urban life in detail; Chapter 63 will treat silk, the Persian Gulf, and Armenian merchant networks; and Chapter 64 will treat tribes, slavery, gender, and social hierarchy as broader subjects. Here, Isfahan, silk, merchants, and captives appear because they were instruments of Abbas's government. As Chapter 1 argued, the regions involved in this story, from the Caucasus to Kandahar and Baghdad, cannot be read within modern national borders, and no frontier of the modern kind existed in 1600.

A note on dates. Some general histories label the reign 1587–1629, but the detailed chronology presented by Roger Savory in Encyclopaedia Iranica dates the actual enthronement at Qazvin to 1 October 1588. The political maneuvering, Uzbek offensive, and negotiations that made the accession possible began in 1587. This chapter distinguishes the events leading to the accession from the dated transfer of power, rather than treating 1587 and 1588 as two equally established dates for one ceremony. Dates in the Common Era are given throughout; where specialist sources diverge, notably on the sieges of Herat and Mashhad, the disagreement is explicitly identified.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and military conditions Abbas inherited in 1587/1588 and why he accepted a peace with the Ottomans on unfavorable terms in 1590.
  2. Trace the recovery of Khurasan from the Uzbeks and its importance for Abbas's later offensives.
  3. Analyze how Abbas rebalanced, without eliminating, Qizilbash military power through the qurchi corps, new commands, and new provincial appointments.
  4. Describe the ghulam system, distinguishing its Tahmasp-era beginnings from its expansion under Abbas, and assess both the opportunities and the coercion it involved.
  5. Evaluate competing explanations of the military reforms, including the claim that English visitors created them.
  6. Explain the relationship among crown lands, provincial lands, royal revenue, and the financing of a salaried army.
  7. Reconstruct the Ottoman-Safavid wars of 1603–1618 and distinguish battlefield victory, occupation, treaty recognition, and durable incorporation.
  8. Assess the deportations and scorched-earth policies in Armenia and Georgia, including the founding of New Julfa, as mechanisms of imperial power.
  9. Interpret Safavid diplomacy toward Europe, Russia, the Ottomans, and the Mughals, and the Gulf and Kandahar campaigns as parts of one international system.
  10. Evaluate Abbas's treatment of princes and courtiers and the question of whether personal strength produced institutional strength.
  11. Critique Persian, Armenian, Georgian, European, and Ottoman sources for the reign, identifying author, date, and purpose.
  12. Judge the reign's legacy without assuming either inevitable greatness or inevitable decline.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What does it mean to say that Abbas "rebalanced" rather than destroyed the Qizilbash?
  3. How did new forms of military service, new revenue, and new loyalties reinforce one another?
  4. To what extent was the transformation of the army a Safavid initiative, and what part did foreign visitors actually play?
  5. What is the difference between a battle won, a city occupied, a treaty signed, and a province governed?
  6. How should historians weigh the strategic purposes of deportation against its human consequences?
  7. In what ways did Abbas's international relations serve security, revenue, and prestige, and where did they fall short?
  8. What did royal consolidation mean for cultivators, captives, merchants, governors, and members of the royal family?
  9. Does strengthening a reigning monarch necessarily strengthen the monarchy as an institution?
  10. How reliable are court chronicles, and what voices do they leave out?

Historical Context

The Safavid state that Abbas inherited had been built by his great-grandfather Ismail I from the devotion of Turkmen tribal followers, the Qizilbash, and from the administrative expertise of Persian-speaking officials, as Chapters 57 and 58 showed. Defeat at Chaldiran in 1514 (Chapter 59) had demonstrated the limits of a military system based on tribal cavalry, but it had not toppled the dynasty. Under Shah Tahmasp I (r. 1524–1576), examined in Chapter 60, the monarchy survived a long struggle with the Ottomans, concluded the Peace of Amasya in 1555, shifted its capital from Tabriz to Qazvin, and began to recruit captives from the Caucasus as royal servants. Yet Tahmasp never resolved the underlying tension between a shah whose legitimacy rested on sacred and dynastic claims and a military elite whose tribal commanders held provinces, followers, and revenue of their own.

That tension broke open after Tahmasp's death in 1576. Over the following eleven years rival Qizilbash groups, royal women, and princes struggled over the throne while Ottoman armies attacked from the west and Uzbek armies from the northeast. Section 61.1 narrates this crisis briefly. For the present context the essential point is that the Safavid state had survived Chaldiran because its provincial system and bureaucracy continued to function, and that after 1576 the same system became a source of fragmentation: commanders defended their own districts and followings, and the central treasury could not reliably finance a royal army.

The regional environment had also changed. The Ottoman Empire, at the height of its sixteenth-century power, ruled Anatolia, the Balkans, Syria, Egypt, and much of Iraq, but by the 1590s it faced a long war with the Habsburgs in Hungary and the destabilizing Celali rebellions in Anatolia. The Uzbek Shaybanids, under Abdullah Khan II (r. 1583–1598), were consolidating Transoxiana and pushing into Khurasan. The Mughal empire of Akbar held Kabul and, from 1595, Kandahar. Portuguese power in the Indian Ocean rested on a chain of fortified ports including Hormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, while English and Dutch trading companies were beginning to seek Asian markets. A Safavid ruler could therefore hope for opportunities created by his neighbors' difficulties, but he also faced rivals on every frontier.

Finally, the Iranian plateau's geography and economy set the terms of the problem. As Chapter 1 explained, state power on the plateau has depended on integrating discontinuous regions, each with its own agricultural base, and on moving armies across mountains, deserts, and high passes. Safavid revenues came from agricultural taxation, customs, and above all from the production of raw silk in Gilan, Shirvan, and neighboring districts, which linked Iran to markets in the Ottoman lands, Russia, and the Indian Ocean. Control of such resources, and of the routes that carried them, was what the reorganization of Abbas's reign was largely about.

Division I — Dynastic Crisis, Accession, and the Recovery of Royal Authority Section 61.1

The Safavid Crisis after Shah Tahmasp: Civil War, Invasion, and Dynastic Instability, 1576–1587

Shah Tahmasp I died in May 1576 after a reign of more than fifty years, leaving a large family, several competing Qizilbash coalitions, and no settled arrangement for the succession. Chapter 60 examined the institutions he built and the factional management he practiced; what mattered after his death was that the system had worked chiefly because an old and experienced shah stood at its center. Once he was gone, the same coalitions that had supported him turned against one another, and the competing interests of Turkmen tribal groups, Persian officials, Georgian and Circassian household servants, and the women of the royal household moved into the open.

The first successor, Ismail II (r. 1576–1577), had been confined for many years in the fortress of Qahqaha on his father's orders. His brief reign is remembered chiefly for the elimination of rival princes and their supporters, and the sources describe a determined effort to remove potential competitors. His sudden death in November 1577, which some contemporaries attributed to poison, ended the experiment before it could consolidate. The chroniclers who wrote under later rulers had reasons to dramatize these events, and the circumstances of his death remain unresolved.

His half-brother, Muhammad Khodabanda (r. 1578–1588), came to the throne with impaired eyesight, which reduced his ability to command in person, and with a strong-willed wife, Khayr al-Nisa Begum, known by the title Mahd-e Olya. She took an active part in court politics and in appointments, and her influence aroused the hostility of many Qizilbash commanders, who resented direction from a woman and from the Persian officials and relatives who surrounded her. In 1579 she was murdered by a group of commanders. The episode shows how thoroughly the court had become an arena in which tribal factions, not the throne, set the limits of what was possible.

Conflict among the Qizilbash continued throughout the decade. Groups such as the Ustajlu, Shamlu, Takkalu, and Turkman fought for governorships and for the guardianship of princes, and provincial governors often acted as autonomous rulers. In 1586 the eldest and most capable of Muhammad Khodabanda's sons, Hamza Mirza, was assassinated in his camp during the campaign against the Ottomans. The killing deprived the dynasty of its most experienced prince and deepened the sense of a monarchy that could not protect its own heir.

These divisions coincided with foreign invasion. In 1578 an Ottoman army advanced into the Caucasus and occupied Shirvan and parts of Georgia, taking advantage of the instability in Qazvin to repudiate the Peace of Amasya of 1555. Over the following years Ottoman forces built fortifications in the region and extended their control. In 1585 an Ottoman army took Tabriz, the former Safavid capital and the commercial center of the northwest, and in the same period the Safavid position in Georgia, Karabagh, and Shirvan deteriorated. Reports reaching Qazvin also described Ottoman garrisons and the dependence of local rulers on the Ottoman side. The loss of Tabriz was not only strategic but symbolic, since it was the city where Ismail I had proclaimed the dynasty in 1501.

On the northeastern frontier the Uzbek Shaybanids under Abdullah Khan II (r. 1583–1598) exploited the same weakness. Khurasan had long been governed by Qizilbash commanders, and its principal centers, Herat and Mashhad, depended on local forces that were distracted by court politics. The Uzbeks began a major siege of Herat in late 1587 and captured the city in 1588–1589 according to differing specialist chronologies; Mashhad was captured in 1589–1590. Safavid authority across much of Khurasan then collapsed, as Section 61.4 explains. The Uzbek conquest of Mashhad also placed the shrine of the eighth imam in non-Safavid hands, a blow to the dynasty's religious standing.

The Safavid state of the mid-1580s therefore faced simultaneous threats on two fronts and a monarchy that could not command a united army. The treasury, dependent on provincial revenue controlled by governors, could not finance a force loyal to the crown, and the officers who might have led one were rivals of one another. Students should avoid the temptation to read this crisis as proof that the dynasty was doomed. The Safavid administrative system continued to function, many provinces remained under Safavid governors, and the Qizilbash still had every interest in a monarchy that justified their positions. What the crisis did was to create the conditions under which a new ruler could attempt a more thoroughgoing reconstruction of royal power, and in which compromises that would have been unthinkable under Tahmasp became unavoidable.

Section 61.2

Abbas Mirza, Murshid Quli Khan, and the Seizure of the Throne, 1587–1588

Abbas was born in Herat on 27 January 1571 (the sources give the date by the Hijri calendar), the son of Muhammad Khodabanda, who was then governor of Khurasan. As a child he grew up in the region he would later recover, under the guardianship of Qizilbash tutors. The principal guardian was Ali Quli Khan Shamlu, a commander of the Shamlu tribal group who held Herat and treated the young prince as a means of securing his own position. The prince's boyhood was therefore spent as a pawn in the rivalry between Shamlu and Ustajlu commanders, each of whom wanted to control him and, through him, a future monarchy.

In 1581 a coalition of Shamlu and Ustajlu commanders, led prominently by Ali Quli Khan Shamlu, proclaimed the ten-year-old prince as shah in Khurasan, hoping to supplant the reigning Muhammad Khodabanda. The rebels had coins struck and the khutba recited in Abbas’s name. The attempt failed when a royal army approached and the coalition broke apart; Ali Quli Khan reaffirmed his allegiance to the reigning shah. This episode should not be credited principally to Murshid Quli Khan Ustajlu. In 1585 Murshid Quli took Mashhad from rival commanders and seized the prince from Ali Quli Khan during their struggle for power. Control of Abbas’s person thereafter gave the Ustajlu chief leverage over the succession. The sequence reveals how a prince’s name could legitimate a provincial revolt even while the prince himself had little independence.

The great Uzbek invasion launched in December 1587 transformed the succession struggle. Murshid Quli Khan already controlled the prince, but feared that continued fighting in Khurasan might cost him his political asset. He brought Abbas westward, negotiated support from several Qizilbash groups, and ultimately reached Qazvin in 1588. On 1 October 1588, Muhammad Khodabanda handed the royal insignia to his seventeen-year-old son, who was enthroned as Shah Abbas I. The court’s public assent did not mean that the decision was freely made: armed factional power had determined the outcome. Murshid Quli Khan received the office of vakil-e divan-e aʿla and remained the most powerful minister and the shah’s effective guardian.

The question of dates deserves attention. The historical chronology must distinguish the succession campaign from the ceremony. Broad accounts sometimes date Abbas’s reign from 1587, when the Uzbek advance made his installation urgent, but the detailed reconstruction in Encyclopaedia Iranica specifies 1 October 1588 (10 Dhu’l-Qaʿda 996) for the handing over of the royal insignia. This chapter therefore dates the enthronement to 1588 and uses “1587–1588” for the process culminating in it. Kalender conventions alone should not be made to explain away a genuine discrepancy among secondary chronologies.

Context: Iskandar Beg wrote as an official in the shah's secretariat, drawing on archival documents, his own experience of the court, and the reports of participants. His chronicle is organized by year and by the regnal sequence and is the principal Persian narrative of Abbas's reign. It was written for a royal audience and under a patron who was still living.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The chronicle presents Abbas's emergence as the fulfillment of a providential design. It describes the arrival of Murshid Quli Khan with the prince at Qazvin, the abdication or withdrawal of the old shah, and the enthronement of the young ruler amid the acclamation of the commanders. It treats the confusion of the previous decade as a period of misrule from which the dynasty was rescued, and it portrays Abbas as destined to restore justice.

Analysis: The source is essential for the sequence of political events, for the names of commanders and officials, and for the vocabulary in which the court wished the reign to be understood. It also shows how the new regime framed its legitimacy: the new shah was a corrective to disorder, and the narrative devalues the reigns of his immediate predecessors.

Limitations: The chronicle is partisan and retrospective. Its account of an enthronement in which a regent held real power is shaped by the later fact that Abbas eliminated that regent, and its treatment of Murshid Quli Khan reflects that outcome. It says little about the views of ordinary soldiers, of the displaced shah, or of the provinces being lost.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a loyal court official writing for the ruling house. Ottoman, Uzbek, European, and non-courtly Persian voices are absent from the passage.

The enthroned shah was initially in a weak position. Murshid Quli Khan appointed his own supporters to offices and treated the young ruler as a figurehead, and the commanders around him expected to continue distributing governorships among themselves. Abbas's early compliance, which the sources record, reflected the fact that he had no independent armed force with which to resist. Yet he was also observing. Within about two years he had assembled a small group of loyal servants and moved against the regent: in 1589 Murshid Quli Khan was removed from power and killed, in an operation that the chroniclers present as the shah's decisive assertion of authority. The episode set a pattern that recurred throughout the reign, in which Abbas tolerated powerful supporters only as long as they were useful and removed them when they ceased to be.

Section 61.3

The Ottoman Peace of 1590: Territorial Concessions and Strategic Survival

The new shah's first major decision was to make peace with the Ottomans on terms that would have been unacceptable to most of his predecessors. In 1590 an agreement, usually called the Peace of Istanbul, was concluded. Under its terms the Safavids recognized Ottoman control of Tabriz and of a large part of the northwest, including Azerbaijan, Shirvan, Karabagh, and territories in Georgia, together with other districts that the Ottomans had occupied in the war that began in 1578. Abbas also agreed to give up the public cursing of the first three caliphs, a practice that had been a mark of Safavid Shi'i identity and an irritant to the Sunni Ottomans, and sent his nephew Haydar Mirza to Istanbul as a hostage to the agreement. The Ottomans, for their part, gained recognition of what they had won.

A modern reader might call these terms humiliating, and many Safavid contemporaries evidently did. The loss of Tabriz meant ceding the commercial capital of the northwest and the place where the dynasty had been founded. The loss of the Caucasus districts cut Safavid access to the Georgian and Armenian populations from which the household military servants were later drawn. And the concession on the ritual cursing, however limited, touched on the dynasty's religious claims. It is therefore worth asking why a ruler who would later be celebrated for conquest began his effective reign by accepting such terms.

The answer lies in the distinction between capitulation and strategic retreat. Abbas could not fight on two fronts. The Uzbeks had overrun Khurasan, including the shrine city of Mashhad and the great center of Herat, and their ruler Abdullah Khan II was among the most capable Central Asian commanders of the age. The shah's army was divided, his treasury weak, and his hold over the Qizilbash commanders uncertain. An Ottoman war, which had already cost the Safavids most of their northwestern territory, could not be won from this position. Peace with one enemy would allow the dynasty to concentrate against the other, and would also give Abbas the time he needed to reorganize.

Nor was the peace a permanent renunciation. A treaty in this period recorded a balance of power at a moment in time, not a legal settlement of sovereignty in the modern sense. Neither side regarded the boundary it defined as a fixed line, and both understood that conditions could change. Ottoman commanders, for their part, were preoccupied with the Habsburg war in Hungary, which began in 1593, and had reasons of their own to accept a quiet eastern frontier. The agreement suited both parties for the time being.

The cost, however, was real, and it fell on specific groups. For the inhabitants of the districts ceded to the Ottomans, the change of sovereignty meant new tax collectors, new garrisons, and new obligations. Local rulers in Georgia and Shirvan, who had hoped for Safavid support, found themselves dealing with Ottoman governors or seeking accommodation with whichever power could reach them. The peace also distinguished claims of sovereignty from actual military control. Ottoman recognition of Abbas's rule in the remaining Safavid lands did not make the ceded territories Ottoman in any thorough administrative sense, since Ottoman authority there depended on garrisons and local cooperation, and the situation remained open to renegotiation.

The decision therefore tells us about the way Abbas understood power. He was willing to accept a humiliating position in the short term in order to secure the capacity for a more favorable position later. Many rulers in his situation would have been unable to make that calculation, because their legitimacy depended on recovering lost territory at once. Abbas's legitimacy, at this stage, depended on his survival, and on his ability to show that the monarchy could still defend what it held. The sequel, in the northeast, was the first test of that ability.

Section 61.4

The Uzbek Threat and the Recovery of Khurasan, 1590–1598

The Uzbek Shaybanids under Abdullah Khan II exploited the weakening of Safavid authority in Khurasan during the late 1580s. The prolonged siege of Herat began in late 1587; one Encyclopaedia Iranica account dates its capture to February 1588, while a specialist study of Ali Quli Khan Shamlu dates the surrender to February 1589. Mashhad fell later, placed in late 1589 by one scholarly chronology and spring 1590 by another. Neither city should be described as having fallen to the Uzbeks in 1587. From these bases the Uzbeks extended their raids and occupation across the northeast, and Safavid authority in the region was reduced to scattered fortresses and local commanders. For a dynasty that identified itself with the shrines of the Shi'i imams, the occupation of Mashhad was a particular humiliation, and the plundering of the city was long remembered.

Abbas's response in the 1590s was limited and cautious. He did not at first take the field in strength, partly because he was occupied with consolidating his own authority, partly because the Uzbek ruler's forces were formidable, and partly because the army was not yet capable of a major offensive. He seems to have used diplomacy and indirect pressure, including embassies to the Uzbek court, while he rebuilt his forces. This period of restraint, from 1590 to 1598, is the least dramatic of the reign, but the rebuilding of the army and the court that took place in it made the later campaigns possible. The principal institutional developments are examined in Division II.

The turning point came in early 1598, when Abdullah Khan II died. His death produced a succession crisis among the Uzbeks, as his son Abd al-Mu'min was killed within months and the Shaybanid leadership fragmented among rival claimants. Abbas, who had been waiting for such an opportunity, assembled an army and marched toward Khurasan in 1598. Persian sources describe a campaign marked by rapid movement and the support of local populations and commanders who were ready to return to Safavid allegiance. Near Herat, at a place called Rabat-i Pariyan, the Safavid army defeated an Uzbek force under the leadership of Din Muhammad Khan, and Herat was recovered. Mashhad and other districts followed.

The victory depended on several conditions. Abbas's cavalry gave him the mobility needed to cover the long distances of the plateau and to exploit the collapse of Uzbek coordination. His newly strengthened royal troops, including firearms units that Section 61.7 describes, supplemented the tribal cavalry and gave the army a core that did not depend wholly on the loyalty of regional chiefs. The logistics of the campaign relied on provincial support, with local governors supplying provisions and men, and on the willingness of Khurasani communities, who had experienced Uzbek rule, to side with the Safavids. The Uzbek disarray after the death of their ruler was the decisive circumstance, and the chroniclers' emphasis on the shah's leadership should not obscure it.

The political consequences were large. The recovery of Mashhad restored the dynasty's custody of the shrine of the eighth imam, and the shah's later pilgrimage to the shrine, which the sources describe as undertaken on foot, became a part of the royal image. Herat returned to Safavid hands, although Khurasan would remain exposed to Uzbek raids and the frontier was not permanently secured. Importantly, the eastern success freed Abbas to turn against the Ottomans: with Khurasan stabilized and his army reorganized, he could contemplate the war that he had deferred in 1590.

Division II — Restructuring Military Power, Government, and Royal Finances Section 61.5

The Qizilbash, Royal Household Cavalry, and the Redistribution of Military Authority

The chief obstacle to royal authority in 1588 was not a foreign enemy but the Qizilbash themselves. The tribal groups that had carried the dynasty to power held provincial governorships, controlled large cavalry contingents recruited from their own followers, and regarded the throne as a partner whose decisions they could shape. Abbas's experience as a boy and as a puppet ruler made him acutely aware of this dependence. His task, however, was not to destroy the Qizilbash, who remained the backbone of the Safavid cavalry and the main source of experienced provincial commanders. It was to alter the balance so that the shah's own forces and servants could outweigh any combination of tribal leaders.

The first instrument was the strengthening of the royal household cavalry, the qurchi corps. The qurchis had existed under earlier shahs as a royal guard recruited from Qizilbash families, and under Abbas they were reorganized and enlarged. Their commanders, the qurchi-bashi and other officers, were chosen by the shah and rewarded with offices that depended on his favor. The corps provided a body of mounted troops who were paid and supplied by the crown and whose advancement was tied to royal service rather than tribal leadership.

Historical Vocabulary
Qurchi

Definition: A Turkic-derived term for a member of the royal quiver-bearing guard, the household cavalry of the Safavid shahs, commanded by the qurchi-bashi.

Historical context: In the early Safavid period the corps consisted mainly of men from Qizilbash tribal families who served in the shah's retinue. Under Abbas it was expanded and given a central role in the military hierarchy, and its senior officers rose to high rank. In the seventeenth century the qurchi-bashi was among the principal figures of the army.

Why it matters: The qurchis were a means of building a royal force alongside the tribal contingents, one whose officers owed their position to the shah. They show how Abbas worked through existing institutions, enlarging a familiar body rather than inventing a wholly new one.

Potential misunderstanding: The qurchis were not the same as the ghulams, who were a different category of royal servant largely recruited from Caucasian captives, though both groups served the shah directly and the lines between them could be blurred. Nor were the qurchis a wholly new creation of Abbas's reign.

The second instrument was the redistribution of provincial offices. Abbas moved Qizilbash governors from their tribal bases, reassigned governorships to other officials, and increasingly appointed to major posts men who owed their careers to him rather than to a tribal following. In some regions the crown took over administration directly (Section 61.8), and in others the shah placed trusted ghulam commanders in positions that had formerly belonged to Qizilbash chiefs. The result was a gradual reduction in the number of provinces ruled by hereditary tribal leaders, although the process was uneven and some governorships remained in tribal hands throughout the reign.

The third instrument was political violence. Abbas eliminated or sidelined powerful commanders when they threatened his control. The removal of Murshid Quli Khan in 1589 was the first case. In 1598 Farhad Khan Qaramanlu, a commander who had risen with Abbas and had been one of the most powerful men of the early reign, fell from favor and was killed on the shah's orders, in circumstances that the sources attribute to suspicion of his ambitions. Such episodes illustrate the connection between administrative consolidation and coercion: the creation of a more centralized system required the removal of individuals whose independent power was incompatible with it, and the removal was often lethal.

It would nonetheless be wrong to describe these developments as the end of Qizilbash power. The tribal groups continued to supply a large share of the cavalry throughout the reign, and Qizilbash families continued to hold major offices, including governorships and posts at court. What changed was the balance. By the later years of Abbas's reign the shah possessed forces and officials who did not depend on tribal loyalty, and the Qizilbash chiefs could no longer dictate royal decisions as they had in the 1580s. The system that emerged was a rebalancing in which the crown held the stronger position, not a replacement of one military elite by another. Later chapters will show how the Qizilbash remained a political force, and how the shifting equilibrium between them and the ghulams shaped Safavid politics in the following decades.

Section 61.6

The Ghulam System: Caucasian Recruitment, Enslavement, and Royal Service

The most consequential new element in Abbas's army and government was the large-scale recruitment of ghulams, royal servants of mainly Caucasian origin who were converted to Islam, educated in the royal household, and trained for military and administrative careers. The practice was not new. Tahmasp had taken Georgian, Circassian, and Armenian captives in his campaigns of the 1540s and 1550s and had placed some of them in royal service, and a small number had risen to positions of responsibility. What Abbas did was to expand the system dramatically and to give it a political purpose it had not previously had: the creation of a body of commanders and officials whose loyalty would be to the shah alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghulam

Definition: In Safavid usage, a royal servant, usually of Caucasian origin and captive or purchased status, who was converted to Islam and trained for service to the shah. The Arabic word literally means "boy" or "servant" and has been used for many forms of dependent military and household service across the Islamic world.

Historical context: Under Tahmasp the ghulams were a modest group. Under Abbas they became a major element of the army and administration, and some rose to the highest offices. The term covers a spectrum of statuses, from captives and slaves in the legal sense to officials whose power was considerable, and the experience of individuals varied accordingly.

Why it matters: The ghulam system explains how Abbas could build a force and a bureaucracy independent of tribal leaders. It also reveals the coercive foundations of the reorganization, since many ghulams were obtained through warfare, capture, and the tribute or purchase of children.

Potential misunderstanding: Ghulam status did not mean that individuals were powerless. Some wielded extraordinary authority. But their position derived from the shah's favor and could be reversed, and the recruitment process involved real loss and violence for the families and communities affected. The Ottoman devshirme and the Mamluk systems are sometimes compared with it, though each had distinct institutions and the comparisons should be made with care.

Recruits came from the Caucasus, particularly from Georgia, Circassia, and Armenian districts, through several channels: capture in war, the demand for boys from subject rulers, purchase in slave markets, and the removal of populations during campaigns. They were converted to Islam, a step that was sometimes coerced and sometimes accepted as a condition of advancement, and were placed in the royal household where they were educated, trained in horsemanship and arms, and assigned to units or to administrative posts. Their dependence on the shah for their status, and their lack of family or tribal connections within Iran, made them appear to Abbas a more reliable base for government than the Qizilbash. Contemporary European visitors remarked on the prominence of such men at court.

Two careers illustrate the potential of the system. Allahverdi Khan, a Georgian by origin, rose from household service to become one of the principal commanders of the reign. By the 1590s he held the governorship of Fars, an important province in the south, and he became the supreme commander of the army, the highest military position, commanding the reorganized forces in campaigns against the Uzbeks and the Ottomans. He died in 1613, leaving a reputation as the shah's most trusted servant. His son Imam Quli Khan, born into the new elite, succeeded to the governorship of Fars and to a position of great wealth and influence, and led the campaigns in the Persian Gulf discussed in Section 61.15. Both men also became patrons, and Allahverdi Khan's name is attached to major structures in Isfahan that Chapter 62 will examine.

These careers show how far a ghulam could rise. They should not, however, be taken as representative. Most ghulams served in more modest capacities as soldiers, grooms, officials, and household attendants, and many of those taken in war were not elevated at all but settled as laborers, domestic servants, or cultivators, as Chapter 64 will discuss more broadly. The prominence of a few individuals was, moreover, built on a system that involved enslavement and displacement on a large scale. Captured families were separated, children were removed from their communities and converted, and the demand for recruits contributed to the pressure on the Caucasian populations discussed in Section 61.12. The contemporary sources, written by the beneficiaries or by observers fascinated by the phenomenon, rarely recorded the experience of those who were taken.

The political effect of the system was nonetheless substantial. The ghulam commanders formed a counterweight to the Qizilbash, and by the end of the reign they held a significant share of senior military and administrative posts. The reliance on a group of men who owed their position to the monarch strengthened Abbas's personal authority but also tied the stability of the system to the shah's continued capacity to manage them, a vulnerability that became evident after his death.

Section 61.7

Firearms, Musketeers, Artillery, and the Reorganization of the Safavid Army

The Safavid army that fought at Chaldiran in 1514 was dominated by tribal cavalry and made limited use of firearms, a limitation that Chapter 59 identified as one factor in the defeat. In the century that followed, gunpowder weapons became more widespread in Iran, as elsewhere in western and southern Asia. Tahmasp's forces used artillery and handguns in sieges and campaigns, and his reign saw the development of musketeer units, although the Qizilbash cavalry remained the main striking force. Abbas inherited this experience and enlarged it. The reforms of his reign built upon a foundation that already existed.

The reorganized army of the early seventeenth century combined several elements. The tofangchi, musketeers armed with matchlock firearms, formed a large body of infantry paid by the crown. The tupchi, specialists in artillery, operated and maintained cannon and were responsible for siege guns, which were needed for the capture of fortified towns such as those of the Caucasus and the Gulf. These gunpowder forces were supplemented by the royal household cavalry, the ghulam units, and the tribal contingents, which continued to supply many of the mounted troops. The army was therefore a combined force, not one that replaced cavalry with infantry. Contemporary writers give large figures for these new units, in the low tens of thousands, but such numbers are approximate and sometimes rhetorical, and the chapter does not adopt a single figure.

Recruitment drew on several groups. Tofangchis came largely from the settled population, including peasants and townspeople in various provinces, who were levied or recruited and paid, with the expectation that they would serve under arms in the campaigns. The ghulams provided additional troops, as did captives and converts. Payment in cash or in kind from the royal treasury was essential: a force paid by the crown depended on the crown's revenue, which is why the fiscal reforms of Section 61.8 were inseparable from the military changes. The supply of such an army required the collection of grain, fodder, and munitions, the maintenance of gunpowder production, and the organization of transport, requirements that put heavy demands on the provinces through which the army passed.

Compared with the army of Chaldiran, the transformed force was more heavily armed with firearms, more dependent on royal finance, and more capable of siege warfare. It was also more flexible. The combination of mounted troops able to move rapidly with infantry and artillery able to take fortresses allowed Abbas to conduct the campaigns of the 1600s and 1620s, in which fortified towns such as Yerevan, Kandahar, and Hormuz were captured. Students should resist the temptation to describe this as the abandonment of an outdated system for a modern one; it was an adaptation in which older and newer elements coexisted.

Historical Debate

Were the Military Reforms Primarily Safavid Initiatives or the Result of European Intervention?

The question: A long-standing narrative, found in many European accounts and repeated in popular histories, credits two English adventurers, Anthony and Robert Sherley, who reached the Safavid court in 1598, with creating Abbas's artillery and reorganized army. To what extent is that claim defensible?

Interpretation A — European help was decisive: Some older accounts, relying on the testimony of the Sherleys and their companions and on European travel literature, describe Robert Sherley as having trained Persian gunners and organized the artillery, and treat the reforms as the introduction of European military science.
Interpretation B — The reforms were primarily Safavid and had earlier roots: Most modern scholars emphasize that Safavid forces had used gunpowder weapons before the Sherleys arrived, that Tahmasp had already developed firearms units, that the principal reorganizing figures were Iranian and ghulam commanders such as Allahverdi Khan, and that the Persian chronicles give the Englishmen at most a modest role in training. On this view the Sherleys were participants and publicists whose own accounts exaggerated their importance.

Evidence: The sources are the Persian chronicles, which are largely silent about European technical contributions and focus on the shah's own decisions; the narratives produced by or for the Sherleys, which were written to promote the brothers in Europe; and the reports of other European visitors. Each has an interest. The Persian sources serve a patron who wished to credit the shah, and the English sources serve men seeking reputation and employment.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the weight to assign to foreign advisers relative to indigenous development and existing institutions, and the reliability of self-interested European accounts. It is not a dispute about whether firearms and gunners increased in importance, which is agreed.

Why it matters: If the reforms were imported, the story is one of technological transfer to a backward state. If they were Safavid, the story is one of institutional adaptation by a state already engaged with gunpowder warfare. The evidence favors the second view, with the first retaining limited value for understanding diplomatic and technical contacts. The debate also illustrates how the self-presentation of foreign visitors can shape historical memory.

Section 61.8

Crown Lands, Provincial Government, Taxation, and the Financing of Royal Power

An army paid by the crown required revenue under the crown's control. In the Safavid state of the sixteenth century, much of the land revenue was assigned to provincial governors and military commanders in return for service, so that the central treasury had direct access to only a portion of the state's income. Abbas altered this balance by expanding the share of territory administered directly on behalf of the shah, a change that supplied the funds on which the salaried forces of Section 61.7 depended.

Historical Vocabulary
Khassa and Mamalek

Definition: Khassa (also khalisa) designated crown land, whose revenues were collected by officials of the royal administration and paid to the royal treasury. Mamalek (plural of mamlaka) designated provinces governed by appointed governors, often Qizilbash commanders, who retained much of the revenue to support themselves and their troops and forwarded the balance.

Historical context: The distinction was well established in Safavid administration before Abbas. During his reign a number of provinces, including Gilan and Mazandaran in the north, and areas around Isfahan, Yazd, and Kashan, were shifted wholly or partly into the crown category, as the shah took over administration of regions whose governors he replaced or whose territory he annexed. Contemporary and later writers describe this as a significant expansion, although the exact extent and timing are matters on which sources vary.

Why it matters: The shift altered who benefited from provincial revenue, and gave the shah the resources to pay troops and officials directly rather than relying on assignments to tribal commanders.

Potential misunderstanding: The categories were not fixed or exclusive. Crown lands might be administered by officials, assigned, or leased, and provinces under governors still contributed to the treasury. The division was a matter of degree.

The conquest of Gilan in the early 1590s illustrates the process. This humid Caspian province, as Chapter 1 explained, was the principal source of raw silk, and its former ruler was removed when Abbas brought it under direct administration. Its revenues, and the profits of the silk trade, passed substantially to the crown. Other territories followed, so that by the later reign a larger proportion of the Safavid domain was administered through royal officials. These officials, many of them Persian-speaking administrators who belonged to the long-established bureaucratic families of the Safavid state, collected taxes, managed royal estates, and handled the accounts. The chancery and treasury departments they staffed continued practices inherited from earlier reigns and expanded under the demands of the new system.

Revenue came from several sources: taxes on agricultural production, assessed on the produce or the land and collected in cash and kind; customs duties on trade at ports and along routes; and royal monopolies, notably on silk, whose trade the shah sought to control and in which he participated directly through agents. Later in the reign the crown contracted with merchants, including Armenian merchants of New Julfa, to handle the sale of silk, a subject that Chapter 63 treats in depth. Expenditure included the pay of the army, the maintenance of the court and the household, building projects, gifts and diplomatic expenses, and the upkeep of religious endowments and shrines.

It would be wrong to equate this increase in royal revenue with increased prosperity. For cultivators, the shift from a tribal governor to a royal official did not necessarily lighten the burden, and the demands of campaigning, including requisitions of grain, fodder, and labor, fell heavily on the districts through which armies passed. For merchants, royal monopolies and the shah's direct participation in trade could limit opportunities even as they provided protection. Provincial elites who lost their governorships or revenue assignments lost income and status. The limits of royal oversight were also real: the administration depended on a large body of officials whose honesty and competence varied, and on local intermediaries whose cooperation was needed to collect taxes. Contemporary European observers sometimes remarked on both the shah's wealth and the exactions of his officials.

Cause and Effect

Revenue Centralization, Military Financing, and Royal Authority

Long-term causes

A state dependent on tribal commanders who held provincial revenue and followings; the growing importance of firearms and siege warfare, which required paid, trained specialists; a long-standing distinction between crown and provincial lands that could be adjusted.

Short-term causes

The crisis of 1576–1590, which exposed the weakness of a monarchy without its own forces; the need to confront the Uzbeks and Ottomans; the opportunity provided by the removal of rival commanders and governors.

Immediate trigger or context

Abbas's decision, from the early 1590s, to place provinces under direct administration and to build a paid royal army, beginning with Gilan and extending through the reign.

Event or transformation

The expansion of crown-administered lands and the creation of salaried musketeer, artillery, and household cavalry units.

Immediate effects

A shah with forces and revenue not controlled by tribal chiefs; greater royal capacity for campaigning; the displacement of some governors and elites.

Long-term consequences

Increased administrative capacity and a more centralized monarchy, together with heavier burdens on provincial society, dependence on the shah's personal supervision, and a financial structure that later rulers found difficult to sustain.

Historical complexity: Military reform, fiscal reform, and political consolidation reinforced one another, and none can be treated as the single cause of the others. Each depended on the cooperation of provincial society and on the coercion of those who resisted.

Fiscal centralization, military autonomy, and administrative capacity thus grew together. The shah's ability to pay troops freed him from reliance on tribal levies; the troops, in turn, enforced the collection of revenue and the removal of rivals. The relationship was reciprocal and self-reinforcing, but it also made the system dependent on the continued vigor of the ruler and on the revenues that sustained it, a point to which the chapter returns in its conclusion.

Division III — War with the Ottomans and the Reordering of the Caucasus, 1603–1625 Section 61.9

The Ottoman War Resumes: Tabriz, Yerevan, and the Northwestern Frontier, 1603–1604

By 1603 Abbas had recovered Khurasan, reorganized his forces, and moved his court toward Isfahan. The Ottoman Empire, meanwhile, was committed to the long war against the Habsburgs in Hungary, which had begun in 1593, and was troubled by the Celali revolts, a series of rebellions in Anatolia that drained Ottoman resources and attention. These circumstances did not cause the Safavid offensive, but they made it feasible. Abbas chose his moment in the autumn of 1603, when his army marched northwest toward Azerbaijan. Persian chronicles present the campaign as the restoration of lost territory; Ottoman sources treat it as an act of aggression that broke the Peace of 1590.

Tabriz was the first objective. The city had been under Ottoman control since 1585, and the Ottoman garrison and administration there were not deeply rooted. According to the chronicles, much of the local population welcomed or at least accommodated the Safavid return, and the city was recovered in the autumn of 1603 with limited fighting. The recovery restored the commercial center of the northwest to Safavid hands and reopened the routes toward Anatolia and the Caucasus. Nakhchivan, Julfa, and other districts of the Araxes valley followed in the same campaign.

The major fortress of the region was Yerevan, a strongly built citadel commanding the Armenian highland and the road between Azerbaijan and Georgia. Abbas besieged it in 1603 and returned in 1604 after an Ottoman relief attempt failed to dislodge his forces. The siege lasted months and required the artillery and engineers described in Section 61.7. In 1604 an Ottoman army under a senior commander approached to relieve the fortress, and Abbas withdrew his army from the immediate vicinity while pursuing a different policy, described in Section 61.10, that denied the advancing army supplies. Yerevan surrendered later in the year, and the Safavids secured control of most of the former Ottoman districts of the northwest.

The war was not yet over. The Ottomans retained forces in eastern Anatolia and the Caucasus, and the Safavid gains had to be defended. Nonetheless, the campaigns of 1603–1604 reversed much of the settlement of 1590 within two years, and in doing so shifted the strategic position of the empire. They also showed how the reorganized Safavid army could now besiege and hold fortified places, something earlier armies had struggled to do.

Military victory on the frontier also depended on cooperation in places where the Safavid court could not simply issue an order and expect it to be obeyed. Local rulers in eastern Armenia, Azerbaijan, and the upland Caucasus faced competing claims from both empires; their calculations reflected the security of towns, access to pasture, the fortunes of particular families, and the prospect of retaining office. A Safavid governor might accept a local magnate’s submission in return for hostages or assurances, only to discover that obedience weakened when an Ottoman column approached. Describing the frontier as “recovered” therefore explains an imperial claim, not the immediate elimination of local political choices.

The logistical scale mattered as much as the map. The movement of a Safavid army toward Yerevan required the concentration of grain, animals, siege equipment, skilled labor, and transport across districts repeatedly traversed by armed forces. The capture of a fortress did not instantly reopen cultivated land or restore commerce; garrisons needed supplies, bridges and roads required repair, and cultivators who had fled could not always return. These constraints help explain why the campaign of 1603–1604 was followed by further conflict rather than a single conclusive settlement.

Section 61.10

Scorched Earth, Armenian Deportations, and the Founding of New Julfa

The recovery of the northwest was accompanied by one of the most severe measures of the reign. When the Ottoman relief army advanced in 1604, Abbas ordered the population of a large area of Armenian and other settlements, including the district of Julfa on the Araxes, to be evacuated, and the land they left to be stripped of supplies. The aim was to deny the Ottoman forces food, fodder, and shelter, and to make the region useless to any occupier. Alongside the military rationale, the deportations also served the shah's economic purposes, since the people removed were resettled in the interior of Iran, where their skills and labor could be used.

The scale of the removals is uncertain. Contemporary and later sources give figures that vary widely and that cannot be verified, and modern historians generally speak of many thousands of people, and in some accounts a few tens of thousands or more, from Armenian communities of the Araxes valley and neighboring districts. Whatever the total, the process was coercive. Families were driven from their homes, forced to march in difficult conditions, and subject to losses along the way from hardship, disease, and violence. Some perished before reaching the destinations, and some were taken as captives. Others were able to take part of their property.

The deportees were settled in several regions. The best known destination was Isfahan, where a suburb on the south bank of the Zayandeh River, named New Julfa after the community's original home on the Araxes, was established for the Armenian merchants and craftsmen. Others were settled in districts of Gilan, Mazandaran, Fars, and elsewhere, where they worked as cultivators and laborers, and some of the deported were enslaved or distributed among officials and soldiers. The merchants of Julfa, who had been central to the silk trade, were treated differently from the rest: the shah gave them privileges, including the right to practice their religion, to maintain churches, and to organize their community under their own leaders, in return for their commercial services to the crown. Chapter 63 will examine the economic role of New Julfa and the Armenian trading network.

Bournoutian (Costa Mesa: Mazda Publishers, 2010), especially chapters 4–5, on deportations from the Ararat region and Old Julfa; paraphrase below is a thematic summary, not a translated passage

Context: Arakel belonged to the Armenian Church and wrote as a member of a community that experienced the removals and their consequences. His history covers the events of the seventeenth century as understood by the Armenian clergy, and it draws on memory, oral reports, and ecclesiastical sources. He wrote after Abbas's death, when the founding of New Julfa could be assessed in hindsight.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The narrative describes the forced departure of Armenian families from their homes, the hardships of the journey, the deaths that occurred along the way, and the grief of the survivors. It also records the eventual resettlement and the building of churches in the new settlements, and it interprets these events in a religious framework as both a trial and a providential preservation of the community.

Analysis: The source gives the perspective of those who suffered and survived, a perspective largely absent from the Persian court chronicles. It shows that the deportations were experienced as catastrophe, and it documents the efforts of the community to preserve its institutions and its religion.

Limitations: Written decades later by a cleric, it reflects memory, religious interpretation, and communal concerns, and its descriptions and numbers cannot be treated as precise. It says little about the shah's calculations, the experience of non-Armenian deportees, or the economic benefits some Armenian merchants later enjoyed.

Perspective: The Armenian clerical perspective. Missing are the voices of lay villagers, women, the enslaved, Muslim inhabitants of the region, and Safavid administrators who carried out the orders.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the experience of deportation?
  2. Who created it, when, and how far removed was the author from the events?
  3. How might the author's religious position and the later prosperity of New Julfa have shaped the narrative?
  4. What can historians learn from the account that Persian chronicles do not provide?
  5. What can they not establish from it alone, and what corroborating evidence would be needed?

Historians have long debated how to weigh the military, economic, and administrative motives of the deportations, and this textbook does not reduce them to one. The scorched-earth policy had a plain strategic logic, since it deprived the advancing Ottoman army of supplies. The resettlement of artisans and merchants served the shah's economic interests. But none of these purposes alters the fact that the measures involved forced displacement on a large scale, with heavy suffering for the people affected. The creation of a prosperous merchant community in Isfahan was a result for some of the deportees and their descendants; it was not the experience of all, and it should not be allowed to obscure the cost.

Section 61.11

Sufiyan, the Ottoman Counterattacks, and the Treaties of 1612 and 1618

The Ottomans did not accept the loss of the northwest. In 1605 a Safavid army decisively defeated an Ottoman force near Lake Urmia, at a place called Sufiyan, near Tabriz. The battle is traditionally regarded as one of the major Safavid victories of the reign, and Persian sources celebrate it as a demonstration of the reorganized army. The Safavid forces used their combined cavalry and firearms units and benefited from Ottoman difficulties in supplying and coordinating a large force in a devastated region. The Ottoman defeat did not end the war, but it demonstrated that the Safavid position in the northwest could be defended.

The following years brought further fighting, interrupted by negotiation. The Ottomans were still engaged in Hungary, where the Peace of Zsitvatorok in 1606 ended the long war with the Habsburgs, and they confronted the Celali rebellions at home. In 1611 and 1612 the conflict was reopened, and a settlement was reached through negotiations associated with the Ottoman commander Nasuh Pasha, who concluded an agreement with Abbas in 1612. The terms recognized the Safavid possession of the territories recovered in the northwest, with the boundary roughly following the lines established in 1555, and provided for the payment by the Safavids of an annual tribute in silk, a feature that the Ottoman side presented as a sign of Safavid deference.

The arrangement did not hold. The silk tribute was resented, and in Istanbul the settlement was criticized as having been made on unfavorable terms. Ottoman officials sought to revise it, and Abbas, who had agreed to it chiefly in order to secure his gains, was unwilling to comply fully. In 1615 hostilities resumed, and Ottoman armies campaigned in the northwest and in Georgia. Abbas again used mobility and denial, avoiding a set battle against a larger force when the circumstances were unfavorable, and the Ottomans could not secure a decisive success. The war ended with the agreement of 1618, associated with the town of Serav (Sarab), which broadly confirmed the territorial settlement while reducing, not eliminating, the Safavid annual silk obligation: the commonly reported figure fell from 200 to 100 loads. Whether deliveries were made regularly was a separate issue from the written diplomatic demand. It also left the Safavids in control of Azerbaijan, Shirvan, and most of the Caucasian districts they had recovered.

Students should be careful about the terms treaty and peace. These agreements recorded a balance of military capacity and diplomatic calculation at a given moment, and neither the Safavid nor the Ottoman court regarded them as permanent. They were also negotiated in the language of the day, which included gestures of submission and tribute that did not always correspond to actual relations of power. The 1612 and 1618 settlements show a pattern in which each side accepted a lasting stalemate on the northwestern frontier while retaining claims that could be revived. The next major phase of Safavid-Ottoman conflict, over Baghdad and Iraq, began in the 1620s and is discussed in Section 61.16.

The wars of this period also fell heavily on the populations of the frontier districts. Armies requisitioned grain and animals, fortified towns endured sieges, and civilians in contested districts faced displacement and violence from whichever force passed through. The Safavid victories were not experienced in the same way across the region: many Armenian, Georgian, and Muslim villagers experienced them as additional levies and destruction. A history that focuses only on treaties and battles risks omitting these experiences, which are poorly documented but certainly real.

Section 61.12

Georgia, Kartli, and Kakheti: Vassalage, Revolt, Repression, and Limits of Control

Georgia was not a single political unit in this period. The territory comprised several kingdoms and principalities, of which Kartli (in the center) and Kakheti (in the east) were the most important for Safavid relations, together with western Georgian polities, such as Imereti, that were more closely linked to the Ottomans. The rulers of Kartli and Kakheti, who were Orthodox Christians, had for decades been tributaries or clients of the Safavids or the Ottomans according to the shifting balance of power, and they sought to preserve as much autonomy as possible by playing one empire against the other. Georgian nobles and princes also served the Safavid state, and, as Section 61.6 explains, Georgian captives and converts supplied many of the ghulams.

Abbas's approach to Georgia combined patronage, coercion, and the use of Georgian princes as instruments. Some Georgian nobles converted to Islam and rose in Safavid service, and the shah favored rulers who accepted his authority. Kakheti's king Alexander II (r. 1574–1605) was among those who sought accommodation; his son, who later became known in Safavid service, and other relatives were drawn into the Safavid orbit. Over time, tension grew between the shah's expectations of obedience and the Georgian rulers' desire for independence, and between rival Georgian factions, some of whom looked to the Safavids and others to the Ottomans or Russia.

The conflict intensified in the 1610s. A series of Safavid campaigns brought Georgian lands under heavy pressure, and Abbas undertook large-scale removals of Georgian populations, as he had done in Armenia. Persian sources describe the movement of large numbers of people from Kakheti and Kartli to the interior of Iran, and later historians generally agree that the deportations and enslavement affected many thousands, with varying estimates. Many were settled in regions such as Fars, Isfahan, and especially Mazandaran and Farahabad, where Georgian communities persisted for generations. In 1614 and again in 1616–1617 Safavid forces campaigned in Kakheti, and the region suffered severe devastation. Resistance continued, and the Georgian rulers and nobles who had initially submitted frequently rebelled.

A further phase came in the early 1620s. A major coordinated Georgian uprising erupted in 1625, associated with Giorgi Saakadze, a Georgian noble who had served in the Safavid world and then turned against it. The rebels won an initial success at Martqopi before a larger Safavid force defeated Georgian troops at Marabda later that year; resistance nevertheless continued. The role of Luarsab II of Kartli, whom Abbas imprisoned and had killed in 1622, belongs to the earlier phase of this conflict and must not be confused with the sixteenth-century Luarsab I. The sources describing these events include Persian chronicles, which emphasize the restoration of order, and Georgian chronicles, which emphasize the suffering and heroism of the population. Neither can be treated as neutral, and their numbers and interpretations differ.

Multiple Perspectives

The Safavid Campaigns in Georgia

The Safavid court

Court chroniclers presented the campaigns as the restoration of royal authority over disobedient vassals and the punishment of rebellion. For the shah and his officials, Georgia was both a frontier to secure against the Ottomans and a source of troops and servants for the ghulam system. The sources emphasize order, obedience, and the shah's justice.

Georgian rulers and nobles

Georgian princes sought to preserve their dynastic authority and autonomy between two empires. Some chose accommodation, conversion, and service as the route to survival and advancement, while others rebelled. The Georgian nobility was divided, and its choices were shaped by rivalries within Georgia as much as by imperial pressure.

Georgian villagers, townspeople, and clergy

Peasants and townspeople bore the destruction of campaigns, requisitions, and deportations. Orthodox clergy saw the removals and conversions as threats to the Church and community. Georgian narratives preserve a memory of loss and martyrdom, although they were written by literate elites and clergy and reflect their concerns.

Georgian captives and converts in Iran

For deported Georgians the experience ranged from enslavement and hardship to integration and, for a few, high rank. Communities of descendants preserved Georgian identity in parts of Iran over generations, although their members' own accounts from this period are scarce.

The Ottomans

The Ottoman court regarded western and parts of central Georgia as within its sphere and treated Safavid advances in the region as a challenge, using local rulers and military pressure to contest them.

These groups did not possess equal power. The Safavid state could compel, while Georgian communities could resist, flee, or negotiate within narrow limits. Comparing perspectives reveals the human consequences of imperial expansion without implying that all positions were equivalent.

The Georgian case shows the limits of Safavid conquest. Military force could defeat Georgian armies, remove populations, and install loyal rulers, but it could not secure durable, uncontested authority. Georgia remained a region of recurrent revolt and competing claims, and Safavid control depended on the continued presence of garrisons and on cooperation from some local elites. The deportations also removed productive population from the Caucasus without making the region quiet. The distinction between territory gained and control secured is central to the whole chapter and recurs in the later sections.

Division IV — Diplomacy, Frontier Expansion, and the Limits of Safavid Reach Section 61.13

Diplomacy: The Sherleys, Husayn Ali Beg Bayat, and Relations with Europe, Russia, and the Mughals

Abbas conducted foreign relations on several fronts at once. The Ottomans and Uzbeks were the immediate military rivals, but the Safavid court also maintained relations with the Mughal Empire to the east, with Muscovy to the north, with European monarchies and merchants, and with the Portuguese and, increasingly, the English and Dutch in the Indian Ocean. These contacts served varied purposes: securing allies against the Ottomans, obtaining trade and technology, and displaying the shah's standing among the rulers of the age.

The best-known episode involves the English brothers Anthony and Robert Sherley, who arrived at the Safavid court in 1598 as part of a small English party. Anthony Sherley was sent by Abbas in 1599 as an envoy to European courts, together with a Safavid official named Husayn Ali Beg Bayat, to seek an anti-Ottoman alliance. The mission traveled through Russia and various European states, and it was marked by quarrels between the Englishman and his Persian colleague, by Anthony's pursuit of his own interests, and by a lack of concrete results. Robert Sherley later remained in Safavid service for years, married a Circassian woman of the court circle, and was sent on further missions to Europe in the 1600s and 1620s. As Section 61.7 emphasized, claims that the Sherleys created the Safavid army are not supported by the evidence; their importance lies chiefly in diplomacy and the transmission of information.

Husayn Ali Beg Bayat, who accompanied Anthony Sherley and traveled in Europe, left or inspired a narrative of the mission, and some later European writing about Persia drew on such accounts. His role reminds students that Safavid diplomacy was conducted by Iranian officials who made their own judgments, and that the Persian envoy was an agent in the exchange, not merely an object of European curiosity. European courts, for their part, received Safavid embassies with interest but offered little practical help. The Habsburgs and the Papacy viewed Safavid Persia as a potential ally against the Ottomans, but geography, distrust, and competing priorities prevented effective cooperation.

Relations with Russia and the Mughals illustrate other dimensions. Muscovy and Iran exchanged embassies concerning the Caucasus and the silk and fur trades, with Russian interest in the northern route to Persia growing in the seventeenth century. With the Mughal court, the Safavids shared a frontier at Kandahar, a point of contention discussed in Section 61.14, and the two empires exchanged envoys and gifts while competing for influence in the region. Diplomatic correspondence between the courts used elaborate forms of address and conveyed claims of precedence that did not necessarily reflect the practical balance of power.

Trade was inseparable from diplomacy. Abbas encouraged European merchants, offered privileges to the English East India Company and the Dutch East India Company, and sought markets for Persian silk. He also sought to reduce Portuguese control of the Gulf trade, a matter that led directly to the campaign of 1622 described in Section 61.15. European observers who visited the court, including merchants, missionaries, and travelers, left accounts that are a valuable evidence source and that also reflect the preconceptions and interests of their authors. Chapter 63 will examine the commercial dimension in detail.

Section 61.14

Kandahar, 1622: Safavid-Mughal Rivalry in the East

Kandahar, in southern Afghanistan, was a strategic fortress and a commercial hub on the routes connecting Iran, Central Asia, and the Indian subcontinent. Its control was contested among the Safavids, the Mughals, and the Uzbeks. The Mughals had held Kandahar since 1595, when it was surrendered by a Safavid governor during the period of Safavid weakness, and its loss remained a Safavid grievance. The city's position made it a gateway: whoever controlled it commanded access between the Iranian plateau and the Indus valley.

In the early 1620s the circumstances favored a Safavid move. The Mughal court was distracted by a succession conflict involving the emperor Jahangir's sons, including the future Shah Jahan, whose rebellion against his father diverted Mughal resources and attention. Abbas, who had maintained friendly diplomatic relations with Jahangir, sent an army under his commanders to besiege Kandahar in 1622. The Mughal garrison held out for a period but the city fell, and the Safavids secured it after a siege of some weeks, with Mughal reinforcements unable to arrive in time.

The conquest illustrates the capacity of the reorganized army to besiege a major fortress at a distance from the center of the empire, and the exploitation of rivals' difficulties. It also demonstrates the diplomatic approach: the shah had treated the Mughal ruler as a friend until the moment of the attack, and he later explained the action in terms of the restoration of what had been Safavid territory. Mughal reaction was resentful but limited, and attempts to recover the city were not immediately successful. Kandahar remained a point of contention in the following decades, but the subsequent changes in control belong to later Safavid and Mughal political history, not Chapter 62’s study of Isfahan.

The significance of Kandahar lies partly in what it did not change. The Safavid hold on the city required a garrison and continued attention, and it remained exposed to Mughal counterattack and Uzbek pressure. Its acquisition extended the territorial reach of the empire but did not transform the regional balance, since the Mughal Empire remained larger and richer. Students should therefore distinguish between the capture of a strategic point and the establishment of lasting regional dominance. The Safavid position in the east, as in the northwest, rested on a combination of military capability, rivals' distraction, and the difficulty of defending distant holdings.

The fortress also tested the resources needed to keep a conquest. The Safavids had to appoint commanders, arrange pay and provisions for a garrison, monitor approaches from the Indian side, and negotiate with local households whose livelihoods depended upon caravan traffic. A defended gateway city could be valuable even without a broad territorial advance beyond it: armies and merchants alike depended upon passes, wells, pack animals, and controlled points of passage. Yet the same geography made the garrison expensive and exposed it to renewed siege whenever relations with the Mughals deteriorated.

The diplomatic record counsels against measuring Abbas’s policy solely in terms of deception or personal friendship with Jahangir. Letters between royal courts expressed mutual esteem within a language of imperial hierarchy even as military officers assessed the frontier in practical terms. Ambassadors might be received courteously while governors strengthened walls or gathered supplies. For students, Kandahar is therefore a case of overlapping sovereignties and strategic claims rather than proof that diplomatic language accurately described intentions at the border.

Section 61.15

The Persian Gulf and Hormuz, 1622: Portuguese Power, English Support, and Safavid Maritime Limits

The Persian Gulf was a commercial zone of great importance, linking Iran to India, Arabia, East Africa, and the wider Indian Ocean. Since 1515 the Portuguese had held the island of Hormuz, at the mouth of the Gulf, and from this fortified base they taxed and regulated shipping through a system of licenses and customs. Safavid Iran, in the sixteenth century, was not a naval power and could not dislodge them. The coast was held by local Arab and Persian rulers, and the Safavid state's principal maritime interests lay in the export of silk and other goods through Gulf ports, in which the Portuguese were intermediaries and competitors.

By the early seventeenth century conditions changed. The English East India Company, established in 1600, sought access to Persian silk and a share of the Gulf trade, and its ships were engaged in conflict with the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean. Abbas, for his part, wished to reduce Portuguese influence and to redirect trade to routes under his control. In 1622 a combined operation brought these interests together: a Safavid land force commanded by Imam Quli Khan, governor of Fars, attacked the Portuguese on the island of Qeshm and then at Hormuz, while English ships blockaded the Portuguese by sea and carried some of the supporting forces. The Portuguese garrison at Hormuz surrendered in the spring of 1622 after a siege.

Several points need emphasis. The land campaign was Safavid, led by the governor of Fars, whose army and logistics made the assault possible. The English contribution was naval and was motivated by the company's own commercial and strategic aims, and the English expected a share of the spoils and of customs revenue, an expectation the Safavids only partly honored. The operation was therefore a temporary alliance of convenience between two parties with different goals, not a European conquest on the shah's behalf and not a purely Safavid triumph either. The earlier practice of attributing the success to Allahverdi Khan, who died in 1613, is incorrect; Imam Quli Khan led the operation.

After the fall of Hormuz, the town was largely destroyed, and trade shifted to a mainland port, Bandar Abbas (Gombroon in English usage), which the shah promoted as the principal Gulf outlet. The Safavids did not become a major naval power. They controlled coasts and ports but lacked the fleet to dominate the sea, and the Gulf trade of the following decades was increasingly shaped by the English and Dutch companies, which established factories at Bandar Abbas and negotiated privileges with the crown. The expulsion of the Portuguese thus altered who held the strategic island but did not give Iran command of the maritime economy.

Section 61.16

Baghdad, 1623–1629: Conquest, Shrine Cities, and Contested Control of Iraq

Baghdad was the old capital of the Abbasid caliphate and, for the Ottomans, a major provincial center that they had held since 1534. For Shi'i Iran the surrounding region of Iraq contained the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala and the shrines of the imams at Kazimayn and Samarra, which gave Safavid rulers a strong religious interest in its control. The Safavids had briefly held Baghdad earlier, and its loss during Tahmasp’s own reign was a long-standing grievance.

An opportunity arose in 1623 through the internal politics of the Ottoman province. A local commander, Bakr Subashi, who had seized power in Baghdad amid disorder in the Ottoman Empire, found himself threatened by an Ottoman force sent against him and appealed to the Safavids for help. Abbas marched with his army in 1623, and the city, after a short siege, passed to Safavid control in the winter of 1623–1624. The role of Bakr Subashi's overture and subsequent events is variously reported, and the sources reflect the interests of their authors, but the essential sequence is clear: Ottoman provincial disorder created a chance that the shah exploited.

The Safavids went on to occupy much of Iraq, including the shrine cities, and Abbas visited the shrines in a display of piety and sovereignty. The Ottoman response was slow but determined. A major Ottoman siege of Baghdad in 1625 was repulsed after a prolonged effort, and fighting continued in the following years. Abbas died in 1629 with Baghdad still in Safavid hands. The city and the region later returned to Ottoman control in 1638, when Sultan Murad IV recaptured it, and the Treaty of Zuhab in 1639 confirmed a frontier that endured, with modifications, for a long period. Those events belong to the reign of Abbas’s successor and to the wider history of Ottoman–Safavid relations, not to Chapter 62’s urban history of Isfahan. They matter here only as consequences: the Safavid hold on Iraq lasted about fifteen years and proved temporary.

The experience of Baghdad's inhabitants is important. A largely Sunni city was brought under a Shi'i dynasty, and the sources record measures against Sunni religious institutions and communities, as well as the movement of troops and the burden of garrisons. Ottoman-era Sunni sources describe the occupation as oppressive, while Safavid sources emphasize the reverence shown to the shrines. The religious dimension of the conflict, though real, was not the only factor: strategic position, trade routes, and imperial prestige all contributed. The case again shows the difference between territorial gain and lasting control. The Safavids could seize and temporarily hold a distant city with the help of local circumstances, but sustaining the position against an Ottoman Empire that had recovered from its internal troubles proved beyond them.

Division V — Royal Power, Human Costs, and Historical Assessment Section 61.17

Isfahan as Royal Capital: Court, City, and the Representation of Monarchy

The transfer of the court from Qazvin to Isfahan, which began in the late 1590s, was one of the defining decisions of the reign. Qazvin had been the capital since the 1550s, but it lay close to the northwestern frontier and to the Ottoman threat, and it was associated with the factional politics of the Qizilbash. Isfahan, in the central plateau, was more secure, closer to the crown lands and the south, and distant from the old tribal power bases. The move served strategic, fiscal, and political purposes at once.

The city's transformation under Abbas involved the construction of a vast central square, the Maydan-e Naqsh-e Jahan, surrounded by the royal mosque, the mosque of Shaykh Lutfallah, the palace gateway of Ali Qapu, and the royal bazaar, as well as the garden avenue of Chahar Bagh and the New Julfa suburb. Chapter 62 treats the architecture, urban planning, and artistic culture of Isfahan in detail, and this chapter notes only their political function. The ensemble placed the shah at the center of religious, commercial, and ceremonial life, and it gathered the institutions of the state around a single urban space.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Safavid Monarchy from Tahmasp to Abbas

What changed? The balance between crown and Qizilbash shifted toward the crown. The state acquired a larger paid army of musketeers, artillery, and ghulam units; a larger area of crown land; a new capital; and a cohort of commanders and officials of Caucasian origin who owed their positions to the shah. The shah himself exercised more direct personal oversight of appointments and punishments.

What remained continuous? The dynasty's claim to religious as well as political authority, its Shi'i identity, its reliance on Persian bureaucratic families, the use of Qizilbash cavalry, the custom of princely appointments, the shrine at Ardabil and the shrines of the imams, and the practice of using Georgian and other captives in service, which Tahmasp had already developed.

When did change occur? Gradually and unevenly, from the 1590s through the 1620s, in response to crises and opportunities.

Was it gradual, abrupt, contested, or cyclical? Mostly gradual and contested. Some episodes, such as the removal of Murshid Quli Khan, were abrupt, but the structural changes accumulated over decades.

Who experienced change most directly? Qizilbash governors who lost provinces; ghulams who gained office; the Armenian and Georgian populations who were deported or recruited; and cultivators and townspeople who bore greater tax and labor demands.

Who experienced more continuity? Many rural communities, for whom local landholders, tax collectors, and religious leaders changed less than the court did; and Persian bureaucratic families, who continued to staff the administration.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that Abbas built upon the work of Tahmasp and earlier shahs rather than starting over, and that centralization was a rebalancing within a lasting system, not the creation of a wholly new one.

The court's ceremonial life reinforced this reordering. Royal audiences, processions, receptions of foreign envoys, and religious festivals took place in the new spaces, and the shah's public appearances, including pilgrimage to Mashhad, were part of the representation of monarchy. European visitors described the splendor of the court and the shah's accessibility, including his habit of mingling with ordinary subjects in disguise, a tradition recounted in several sources and difficult to verify. Such accounts must be read with caution, since they reflect both genuine observation and the literary conventions of the genre.

The change in location had practical administrative consequences. Officials handling correspondence, accounts, appointments, and revenue estimates needed reliable access to the royal household. A residence farther from the Ottoman frontier reduced the chance that an unexpected campaign would place the central court itself in immediate danger. Moving the royal center also changed which regional elites could readily approach the shah, seek offices, or present petitions. Those who already possessed estates and retainers near the central plateau benefited from proximity; others had to finance longer journeys or depend on agents. Capital selection was therefore also a redistribution of political access.

Isfahan did not replace every older institution overnight. Provincial chanceries, tribal commanders, and religious establishments continued to operate in their own districts, and the court remained mobile for military campaigns, inspections, pilgrimage, and seasonal residence. Royal orders still moved through couriers and intermediaries, and enforcing those orders remained dependent upon the cooperation of local governors. The new administrative geography strengthened the center, but it did not abolish the immense practical distances between the center and the empire’s frontiers.

The concentration of court consumption had fiscal and commercial implications. A royal household, its guards, workshops, clerks, and visitors required grain, meat, textiles, horses, fuel, construction materials, and credit. State-directed patronage could bring employment to builders and artisans while increasing demands on suppliers and taxpayers elsewhere. Revenue from crown districts, customs, and commercial transactions helped sustain this expenditure. The relationship ran both ways: a successful capital projected royal power, but it depended on the state’s ability to secure roads, collect revenue, and maintain a dependable flow of goods.

The symbolic geography mattered for relations among ruling groups. Public audiences, processional routes, controlled palace gates, and the proximity of religious and commercial monuments made the shah visible as the figure who connected the empire’s otherwise separate institutions. But buildings alone could not guarantee obedience. The capital provided a stage for negotiations over precedence, petitions, gifts, and access; it amplified the monarchy’s prestige while also concentrating the political competition of those who served it. Chapter 62 examines the monuments and urban life in full, whereas this section emphasizes why such a capital mattered to government.

Section 61.18

The Human Costs of State Building: Deportation, Enslavement, Taxation, and Forced Labor

The reordering of the Safavid state under Abbas came at a considerable human cost. The most visible costs were the deportations of Armenians and Georgians and the enslavement of captives, but they were not the only ones. Warfare brought requisitions of grain, fodder, and animals. Tax demands increased as the crown's expenses grew. Peasants and townspeople were levied for service in the army and for labor on roads, bridges, caravanserais, and royal buildings. The burdens varied by region and by social position, and they are better documented for some groups than for others.

Deportation and enslavement deserve particular attention. The movement of populations out of the Caucasus and the northwest was a deliberate policy that served military, economic, and political goals. Captives became soldiers, household servants, laborers, and, for women, members of elite households or the royal harem; others were sold or distributed. Contemporary sources, including Persian chronicles and European travelers, record these movements, but give few details of the individuals involved, and most numbers are uncertain. Modern scholarship generally treats the total as substantial, large enough to alter the demographic composition of some regions of Iran, while declining to endorse any precise figure.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Analytical Composites: How Abbas's State Touched Ordinary Lives

The following are analytical composites constructed by the textbook from patterns in the evidence. They are not individual historical persons, and no words or thoughts are attributed to them.

A cultivator in a district on a campaign route. Households in districts through which armies passed faced requisitions of grain and fodder, demands for labor and transport, and the risk of damage to fields and stores. Such burdens are documented in general terms by chroniclers and travelers, although individual villagers rarely appear in the sources.

A deported Armenian family. Families removed from the Araxes valley experienced the loss of homes and property, hardship on the journey, and resettlement either in the privileged setting of New Julfa or in rural or laboring conditions elsewhere. Their situation varied widely according to wealth, occupation, and the settlement to which they were assigned.

A Georgian boy taken into royal service. A captive child could be separated from family, converted, educated in the royal household, and trained for the army or the administration. The outcome might be modest service or, rarely, high office. Sources record the system's results more than the experiences of those who entered it.

A merchant or artisan in Isfahan. Urban residents benefited from the capital's construction, royal patronage, and trade, but also faced the shah's monopolies, taxation, and the requirements of royal service. Some groups, such as New Julfa's merchants, received special privileges, while others had less protection.

Reflection: The sources for these lives are uneven, and elite and foreign observers dominate. Historians therefore reason from patterns, institutions, and comparison, and acknowledge the large areas in which individual experience cannot be recovered.

Women appear in the sources chiefly in the context of the royal household and of captivity. The royal harem included women of Georgian, Circassian, and Armenian origin, and some wielded influence through family ties. Beyond the court, the sources reveal little of women's work in agriculture, textile production, and households, although such labor was essential. Religious minorities, including Armenian Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, lived under varying conditions of protection and constraint. The same shah who granted privileges to the Julfa merchants also took coercive measures against other communities at different moments, and treatment depended on the shah's calculations as much as on any fixed policy.

Consider the position of households along a contested Armenian frontier route in 1604. They could be ordered to leave fields and orchards precisely when their harvests or winter stores were needed for survival. Transport animals might be requisitioned for an army before a family could load its own belongings. A surviving merchant household that reached Isfahan with part of its property did not represent the experience of a farmer who was sent to a different province without land, patrons, or familiar institutions. These contrasts are drawn from documented categories of displacement, not from a reconstructed diary or invented personal testimony.

Frontier villages also bore costs even when they were not deported. Soldiers needed firewood, fodder, grain, and shelter, and each new army might demand them again. A successful campaign could bring a new governor, new tax registers, and demands to supply a garrison. The threat of attack could interrupt planting and irrigation repairs, with consequences lasting beyond the departure of the troops. Recovery of imperial sovereignty, as recorded in a court chronicle, was therefore not identical to recovery of a village economy. Whether households could rebuild depended upon security, access to land and water, and the ability to negotiate or evade official demands.

Labor inside the reorganized army was differentiated. Qizilbash horsemen served through kinship and patronage structures, musketeers through more regular royal payment, and ghulams through service relationships rooted in captivity, conversion, and the royal household. Some elite ghulams held high office and commanded large revenues. Others remained dependent servants whose choices were sharply constrained. The careers of prominent commanders cannot stand in for the experiences of the many people captured or relocated whose names were not preserved by chroniclers.

Such evidence also makes it important to distinguish gender and age without relying on stereotypes. Women and children were among those uprooted, sometimes separated from relatives or placed in households whose legal and social hierarchies they did not control. Those who entered elite households might acquire influence, yet that possibility did not cancel coercion or guarantee security. Textile production, agricultural work, childcare, and household provisioning continued under changed circumstances, often with little attention in official narratives. Chapter 64 surveys these wider hierarchies; here their relevance lies in showing that imperial expansion altered the conditions under which ordinary labor and family life could continue.

Section 61.19

Royal Suspicion, Princely Confinement, and the Problem of Succession

Abbas's relations with his own family form one of the darkest and most consequential parts of his reign. Having come to the throne amid dynastic conflict, he was acutely aware of the dangers that ambitious princes and their supporters could pose, and he came to treat his sons as potential rivals. His eldest son, Muhammad Baqir Mirza, known as Safi Mirza, a capable and popular prince who had been active in campaigns in the Caucasus, was killed on the shah's orders, as the sources report, after suspicions of disloyalty reached the shah. The execution is usually dated to 1614 or 1615, and the sources differ, with some placing it in the earlier year and some in the later. It is a standard example of the problem of uncertain chronology.

The shah later had other sons blinded or otherwise disqualified from the throne, and the remaining princes were kept confined in the harem, away from military or administrative experience. The practice of confining princes in the harem, rather than sending them as governors to provinces, as Safavid custom had earlier done, was a significant departure. It removed the risk that a prince would become the focus of a rebellion, but it also meant that the princes who might succeed were uneducated in government. At the end of Abbas's reign, the clear successor was not one of his sons but a grandson, the son of the executed Safi Mirza, who took the throne in 1629 as Safi.

The suspicion extended beyond the royal family. Abbas removed or punished commanders and officials whose loyalty he doubted, and the court atmosphere became one of surveillance and fear. The chroniclers and European visitors record the shah's mood swings and his increasingly harsh responses in his later years. As the previous sections show, the pattern began early, with Murshid Quli Khan and Farhad Khan, and continued throughout the reign.

Historical Debate

Did Abbas's Reign Produce a Stronger Dynasty or a Stronger Personal Monarchy?

The question: Abbas's reforms increased royal control, but did they strengthen the Safavid institution or only the power of one ruler who then left a weaker system?

Interpretation A — Institutional strengthening: Historians who emphasize the new army, the expanded crown lands, the ghulam administration, and the capital at Isfahan argue that Abbas created durable institutions that prolonged Safavid rule for a century after his death.
Interpretation B — Personal monarchy and fragility: Others argue that the system depended on the shah's own vigilance, favor, and willingness to punish, that his confinement of princes left the succession dangerously weak, and that the later reliance on harem and court factions reflects this dependence.

Evidence: Persian chronicles, European travelers' reports, administrative documents, and the later course of Safavid politics, which the political history of Abbas’s successors helps to illuminate. The evidence is interpreted differently according to the weight given to continuity of institutions or to the circumstances of succession.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and causation, specifically how much of later difficulty can be traced to Abbas's policies as opposed to other developments, such as economic change, external pressures, and the decisions of his successors. It is not a dispute over the basic facts of the reign.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the later Safavid state. If the dynasty was strengthened, later problems have other causes. If it was personalized, some difficulties were inherited. The chapter avoids the claim that decline was inevitable: later outcomes depended on later choices and circumstances.

Student task: Identify which features of Abbas's system could survive his death and which depended on his own presence, and explain what evidence would support your conclusions.

The older Safavid practice of appointing young princes to provincial posts, each under a guardian or lala, had created one danger while providing an education in government. A prince stationed at Herat or another major center learned how armies, provincial revenues, appointments, and local elites functioned; he might also become the focus of a rebellious faction. Abbas’s own experience under competing guardians helps explain why he distrusted the practice. Confinement inside the royal household reduced immediate opportunities for provincial rebellion, but it made the transition from prince to ruler far more abrupt and dependent on courtiers.

The atmosphere of suspicion should not be described as a simple change in the shah’s temperament. Early experiences of assassination, the use of dynastic relatives as political pawns, and the recurring removal of disloyal commanders gave Abbas practical reasons to fear conspiracies. Yet the decision to eliminate or blind sons was an exercise of extraordinary royal power with consequences that extended beyond the ruler’s lifetime. Removing viable adult successors made political continuity increasingly dependent on the selection of an inexperienced surviving family member and on the cooperation of those who controlled access to him.

The royal household was not a politically empty enclosure. Women of the dynasty, consorts, eunuchs, attendants, tutors, and officials could transmit information, recommend clients, manage resources, and influence relations among princes. Their power varied sharply according to status and circumstances, and the evidence is filtered through chroniclers who often treated household politics as intrigue. The researcher must avoid both denying these actors agency and attributing all political misfortune to the “harem” as an undifferentiated institution.

The succession problem exposes an important distinction. Abbas successfully subordinated powerful commanders to his authority, but the methods that protected him while he lived did not necessarily prepare a capable heir. An administrative apparatus could continue to issue orders and collect taxes after the shah’s death; it could not, by itself, supply the political experience that a confined prince had been denied. The ensuing reign of Safi should be assessed on its own evidence rather than described as the automatic consequence of his grandfather’s decisions.

Section 61.20

Synthesis: The Death of Abbas I, the Accession of Safi, and the Legacy of the Reign

Abbas died on 19 January 1629 (the date is given in the Gregorian calendar here), in Mazandaran, in the northern province where he had spent much time in his later years. He was about fifty-seven. His body was buried at the shrine of Habib ibn Musa in Kashan. Contemporary reports suggest that the placement was initially envisaged as temporary, but the interment remained there. Later tradition has sometimes confused the significance of Abbas’s pilgrimage to Mashhad with the place of his burial. The Kashan burial is well attested, although the identification of particular surviving funerary monuments there requires separate caution. The succession passed to his grandson, who took the throne as Shah Safi (r. 1629–1642), a young man with little preparation for government. The subsequent reign of Shah Safi belongs to later Safavid political history; Chapter 62 instead turns to Isfahan’s buildings, institutions, and urban life.

The reign's achievements were considerable. Abbas ended the crisis of the 1580s, recovered Khurasan and the northwest, and extended Safavid power to Kandahar, Hormuz, and Baghdad. He reordered the army and administration, enlarged the crown lands, and made Isfahan a capital that expressed the dynasty's ambitions. Many of these accomplishments built on the earlier work of Tahmasp, as the chapter has emphasized, and they relied on the labor, skills, and sufferings of many groups beyond the court.

The reign's limits were equally significant. Several of the territorial gains, notably Baghdad, were lost within a decade or so of Abbas's death. The ghulam elite, the royal household, and the Qizilbash formed a balance that depended on the shah's own management. The confinement and elimination of princes left the succession fragile. The financial and military system rested on the continued ability to collect revenue and mobilize labor. None of this made later difficulties inevitable. Historians who read the later seventeenth century backward as a story of decline risk treating contingent developments as predetermined, and the evidence indicates that the dynasty retained considerable strength for generations.

The chapter has argued that Abbas's reign is best understood as a rebalancing of royal power achieved through administrative, military, and coercive means, with the active participation of many groups and at a heavy human price. It linked the internal reconstruction of the state to external wars, diplomacy, and commerce, and it created conditions for the cultural flowering of Isfahan that Chapter 62 will examine, the economic networks discussed in Chapter 63, and the social and religious developments treated in Chapter 64. Students should leave the chapter with a sense of both the scale of the transformation and the uncertainty and cost that accompanied it.

The sequence from 1588 to 1629 reveals a ruler whose opportunities were repeatedly determined by developments outside his control. The death of Abdullah Khan II and disputes among Uzbek successors created an opening in the east; Ottoman commitments in Europe and revolts in Anatolia affected the western frontier; Mughal court divisions helped create an opportunity at Kandahar; and conflict between Portugal and English shipping furnished maritime assistance at Hormuz. Abbas’s achievements consisted partly in identifying these changes and responding with resources that a stronger Safavid administration could mobilize. To celebrate only the victor’s determination would therefore obscure the wider political context of his victories.

The army and fiscal system were mutually sustaining but never independent of society. Crown revenues helped fund troops loyal to the shah, and those troops secured provinces, roads, and collection districts. Yet the same system depended upon cultivators delivering produce, artisans furnishing equipment, transport workers moving supplies, and officials recording and transferring revenue. Military and administrative centralization did not create an economy insulated from harvest failures, resistance to extraction, or the costs of campaigning. Its effectiveness depended on continuous negotiation between the court and provincial institutions.

The experience of displaced communities is indispensable to assessing the reign. Armenian merchants who built a significant commercial center at New Julfa and Caucasian-born officers who entered the highest ranks of government exemplify outcomes that court-centered histories have sometimes celebrated. Other deportees lost property, relatives, autonomy, and familiar institutions, and many captives did not enter the records as individually named people. Economic and military results cannot be used retrospectively to justify coercion or to erase the diversity of experiences among those affected.

There was also a difference between expanding the ruler’s personal authority and guaranteeing dynastic resilience. The removal of rivals and royal princes could prevent a coup in the short term, but it made the succession more dependent on the politics of an enclosed household. After 1629 the state continued to function, and later Safavid rulers oversaw further economic and cultural activity. Its problems did not begin with one death, and the collapse of the dynasty in 1722 should not be projected backward as an unavoidable result of Abbas’s later years.

Abbas’s long-term significance lies in this combination of durable institutional change and unresolved contradiction. The Safavid monarchy became more effective at directing military forces, rewarding royal servants, obtaining revenue, and presenting itself to distant courts. It also became capable of imposing severe costs on the people who supplied its armies and financed its projects. Chapter 62 now turns from the mechanisms of power to Isfahan, where those resources and political ambitions were concentrated in a monumental urban landscape; the commercial networks and social hierarchies that sustained them follow in Chapters 63 and 64.

Chapter Summary

Shah Tahmasp's death in 1576 opened a decade of crisis. Competing Qizilbash factions fought over the throne, the brief reign of Ismail II ended in sudden death, and Muhammad Khodabanda, hampered by poor eyesight and by the hostility his wife Mahd-e Olya aroused, could not command a united army. While the court quarrelled, the Ottomans occupied the Caucasus and Tabriz, and the Uzbeks under Abdullah Khan II took Mashhad and Herat. The crisis did not make the dynasty's fall inevitable, but it created conditions in which a thorough reconstruction of royal power became possible.

After the political and military crises of 1587, Abbas was formally enthroned at Qazvin on 1 October 1588. The Ustajlu commander Murshid Quli Khan had brought the young prince from Khurasan and initially held effective authority; broad regnal labels beginning in 1587 should not be mistaken for a second enthronement. Within about two years Abbas removed the regent and began asserting his own authority, a pattern of removing powerful supporters once they were no longer useful that recurred throughout the reign. Iskandar Beg Munshi's chronicle, our main Persian narrative, presents these events from the standpoint of a court secretary and must be read with that perspective in mind.

The Peace of Istanbul of 1590 ceded Tabriz, Shirvan, and much of the Caucasus to the Ottomans. It was a strategic retreat, not a permanent renunciation, which freed Abbas to deal with the Uzbek threat and to rebuild his forces. After Abdullah Khan II died in 1598, Abbas recovered Herat and Mashhad, restoring the dynasty's custody of the shrine of the eighth imam and clearing the way for an offensive against the Ottomans.

The institutional reforms of the 1590s built on the work of Tahmasp rather than starting afresh. Abbas enlarged the royal household cavalry (the qurchis), expanded the recruitment of Caucasian ghulams, organized salaried musketeers (tofangchis) and artillerymen (tupchis), and shifted provinces into crown administration (khassa) to finance them. The Qizilbash remained important, but the balance shifted toward a shah who commanded forces and revenue that tribal leaders did not control. The Sherley myth, which credits two Englishmen with creating the army, is not supported by the evidence: Safavid forces already used firearms, and the reorganization was carried out chiefly by Iranian and ghulam commanders such as Allahverdi Khan.

The ghulam system rested on capture, purchase, conversion, and displacement. A few ghulams, such as Allahverdi Khan and his son Imam Quli Khan, rose to the highest offices, but most served modestly, and many captives were not elevated at all. The system strengthened the shah's authority and made his administration dependent on men who owed him their position, while imposing heavy costs on the Caucasian populations from which they came.

The war of 1603–1604 reversed the 1590 settlement. Tabriz, Nakhchivan, Julfa, and Yerevan returned to Safavid control, and the victory at Sufiyan in 1605 showed that the position could be defended. The campaign was accompanied by scorched-earth measures and the deportation of many Armenians from the Araxes valley, some of whom were resettled in New Julfa near Isfahan, where merchants received privileges, while others faced hardship, enslavement, or labor in rural districts. Arakel of Tabriz's later account preserves the perspective of the sufferers; Persian chronicles largely omit it.

The treaties of 1612 and 1618 confirmed the Safavid gains without ending the rivalry, and the Georgian campaigns of the 1610s and 1620s showed the limits of conquest through coercion. Georgian princes were divided, deportations removed many people to Iran, and revolts, including the major uprising of 1625, were suppressed without bringing lasting quiet. Safavid control of Georgia depended on garrisons and local cooperation, a recurring distinction between territory gained and control secured.

Diplomacy extended Safavid contacts to Mughal, Russian, European, and Indian Ocean courts. The Sherleys and the Safavid envoy Husayn Ali Beg Bayat represent efforts to find allies against the Ottomans that produced little practical result. In 1622 the Safavids took Kandahar from the Mughals while Mughal succession struggles absorbed Jahangir's court, and in the same year Imam Quli Khan, with English naval support, took Hormuz from the Portuguese. These campaigns extended Safavid reach to the east and south without making Iran a naval power or transforming the regional balance.

In 1623 the Safavids took Baghdad by exploiting Ottoman provincial disorder, and the shrine cities of Iraq came under Safavid rule. The Ottomans failed to retake the city in 1625, but recovered it in 1638, with the Treaty of Zuhab following in 1639. Those events lie beyond Abbas's reign; they confirm that the western gains were less secure than those in Khurasan and the northwest.

The reign's human costs were large: deportation, enslavement, taxation, forced labor, and the elimination of rivals, including the execution of the crown prince Safi Mirza (dated 1614 or 1615) and the confinement or blinding of other princes. Abbas died on 19 January 1629 and was succeeded by his grandson Safi. Historians disagree about whether the reign strengthened the dynasty or produced a personal monarchy dependent on one ruler. This chapter has argued that it was a rebalancing of royal power built on earlier foundations, achieved through administration, force, and the labor and suffering of many groups, and that later difficulties were contingent, not inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

The reign of Abbas I matters because it shows how a state can be reconstructed from crisis, and what that reconstruction costs. The Safavid dynasty of 1587 was weak, divided, and under attack on two fronts. By 1629 it ruled a larger and better organized empire, with a capital that expressed its ambitions. Understanding how that change occurred requires attention to armies and treasuries as well as to courts and cities, and to the people who paid.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in early modern Eurasia. Gunpowder warfare, salaried or slave-based military elites, the growth of crown revenue, and competition among the Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid, and Uzbek states were shared experiences. Abbas's solutions resembled and differed from those of his rivals, and the comparison helps explain both what was distinctive about the Safavid state and what belonged to a wider pattern.

The reign shaped later developments without determining them. The Isfahan that Chapter 62 examines, the commercial networks treated in Chapter 63, and the social and religious history of Chapter 64 all depend on the arrangements Abbas made, and on the Armenians, Georgians, and others who were brought into the Iranian heartland. The weaknesses of the succession and of a system dependent on one ruler also formed part of the legacy, though how they played out depended on later decisions.

Finally, the chapter invites students to hold together achievement and suffering. The same measures that built a capital and strengthened a state also displaced communities and enslaved captives. A serious history neither celebrates the shah as a great ruler alone nor reduces him to cruelty, but asks how historians know what they know, whose experience the sources record, and whose they omit.
