Gulistan, Turkmenchay, and the Loss of the Caucasus

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

A treaty can be signed in a day, but a frontier takes years to become real. The agreements of 1813 and 1828 moved the legal boundary between the Qajar and Russian empires within days of signing; the work of surveying that line, replacing local rulers, collecting an indemnity, moving families, and rebuilding trade took a generation.

On 22 February 1828 in the Gregorian calendar (10 February by the Julian calendar then used in Russia), representatives of the Qajar and Russian empires concluded the Treaty of Turkmenchay in a village near Tabriz. The document required Iran to cede the khanates of Yerevan and Nakhchivan, to pay an indemnity valued at twenty million silver rubles, to accept a boundary along the Aras River and beyond it, to grant Russian subjects legal and commercial privileges, and to receive Russian recognition of Abbas Mirza as heir to the Qajar throne. The people who lived in the ceded territories (Armenian farmers and clergy, Muslim landholders and peasants, Kurdish and Turkic pastoralists, merchants of Yerevan and Nakhchivan) were not parties to the negotiation. Their subsequent decisions, however, determined much of what the treaty came to mean.

Turkmenchay was the second of two settlements. Fifteen years earlier, on 12 October 1813 (Julian), which is 24 October in the Gregorian calendar, the Treaty of Gulistan had ended the first Russo-Persian war. Gulistan obliged Iran to recognize Russian sovereignty over Georgia and a long list of khanates and districts north of the Aras and Kura rivers, yet it left the frontier imprecisely described and the status of several districts disputed. The second treaty cannot be understood apart from the first, because many of the arguments that led to war in 1826 concerned how Gulistan should be read, and because Turkmenchay was drafted with Gulistan’s weaknesses in mind.

Chapter 70 narrated the campaigns and the court politics of the two wars. This chapter asks a different question: what did the peace settlements actually do? Four processes need to be separated. Military conquest had often come first, and Russian forces held many territories before any treaty recognized them. Legal recognition followed, when the Qajar state acknowledged a transfer of sovereignty in a signed text. Institutional transformation came later still, as Russian provincial government replaced or reshaped the khanates and their forms of rule. Finally, human and political consequences unfolded across decades: migration, disputes over land and religion, changed trade routes, and new ways of thinking about what Iran was and what it had lost.

Historians reconstruct these developments from several kinds of evidence. The treaty texts themselves survive in Russian, Persian, and published English and French versions, and where their wording diverges, the differences matter. Diplomatic correspondence, military reports, Russian administrative surveys and tax registers, Armenian church records, Persian court chronicles, and the accounts of travelers and British envoys add further layers. All of these sources have limits. Treaties record what governments agreed, not what happened on the ground; Russian administrative records reflect imperial priorities; Persian chronicles were often written to defend a dynasty or an individual; and the voices of peasants, pastoralists, and women are heard mostly at second hand.

A caution about vocabulary is also needed from the outset. The phrase “loss of the Caucasus,” used in the chapter title and in much later writing, describes the outcome from a Qajar and later Iranian viewpoint. It should not suggest that the Caucasus had been a uniformly governed Iranian province. Many of the territories concerned were khanates whose rulers had shifted allegiance among Qajar, Russian, and Ottoman powers for decades, while Georgia had a long and separate political history. As Chapter 1 stressed (sections 1.9 and 1.13), the modern states of Iran, Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia must not be read back into this period; the identities and borders of 1813 and 1828 belonged to a different political world.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first examines the negotiation and terms of Gulistan. The second follows the implementation and failure of that settlement between 1814 and 1826. The third turns to Turkmenchay, its territorial, financial, and commercial provisions. The fourth, the longest, follows the treaties into the lives of Armenian, Muslim, and other communities along the new frontier. The fifth considers the effect on Iranian political thought and diplomacy and examines how the treaties have been remembered and argued over since. Chapter 72 will place these events in the wider Anglo-Russian rivalry, and Chapter 73 will examine reform efforts shaped by defeat.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish military occupation, legal recognition of sovereignty, institutional transformation, and long-term social consequence as separate processes in the Russian advance into the Caucasus.
  2. Explain the negotiation, signing, and principal provisions of the Treaty of Gulistan (1813), including its territorial, Caspian, and succession clauses.
  3. Identify which territories Iran recognized as Russian in 1813 and which (Yerevan and Nakhchivan) were ceded only in 1828.
  4. Analyze why the frontier clauses of Gulistan produced disputes, and assess competing explanations for the settlement’s failure.
  5. Describe the terms of the Treaty of Turkmenchay (1828) and its separate commercial convention, distinguishing the provisions of each.
  6. Evaluate the financial and legal consequences of the indemnity, capitulatory privileges, and consular jurisdiction for Qajar authority.
  7. Trace how Russian provincial administration replaced or restructured khanate rule after 1813 and 1828.
  8. Compare the experiences of Armenian, Muslim, and other frontier communities, avoiding assumptions that any one group acted uniformly.
  9. Interpret treaty provisions as primary evidence, noting what each text states, what it omits, and what it cannot show about practice.
  10. Contextualize Qajar political thought after 1828 within debates about sovereignty, military reform, and diplomacy.
  11. Assess how Iranian, Russian, Georgian, Armenian, and Azerbaijani traditions have remembered the treaties, and evaluate the risk of projecting modern national categories backward.

Historical Context

The territories at issue lay between the Black and Caspian seas, in the zone where the southern slopes of the Caucasus Mountains descend to the valleys of the Kura and Aras rivers. In the eighteenth century this region was politically fragmented. Eastern Georgia (Kartli-Kakheti) was a Christian kingdom under Iranian suzerainty in the Safavid period that later sought protection from Russia. To its south and east lay Muslim-ruled khanates, among them Ganja, Karabakh, Shaki, Shirvan, Quba, Baku, Derbent, Yerevan, and Nakhchivan, as well as the Talysh khanate along the Caspian. Their rulers governed through local alliances, tribal followings, and tribute, and they shifted allegiance among Iranian, Ottoman, and Russian patrons as circumstances changed. Populations were mixed: Armenians, Georgians, Muslims speaking Turkic languages and Persian, Kurdish and Talysh speakers, Jews, and others lived in towns, villages, and seasonal pastoral circuits that did not respect any later boundary.

After the collapse of Safavid authority and the turbulent century that followed (Chapters 67 and 68), the Qajar founder Agha Mohammad Khan reasserted Iranian claims over the Caucasus, campaigning against Georgia in 1795 and again before his assassination in 1797. His successors inherited the claim but not a settled administrative framework. Russia meanwhile moved south: its annexation of Kartli-Kakheti in 1801 placed Russian forces and officials on the doorstep of the khanates, and a series of military campaigns and negotiated submissions followed in the first decade of the century. Several khans swore allegiance to Russia in these years, sometimes under pressure and sometimes in search of protection against Qajar or neighboring rivals. Iran did not accept these submissions as legitimate, which is part of why the later treaties had to address them explicitly.

The wider diplomatic setting was shaped by the Napoleonic wars. Fath Ali Shah’s court first sought French support, then turned to Britain after the Franco-Russian rapprochement of 1807, and by 1812 Britain and Russia were allied against Napoleon. That alliance explains why a British ambassador, Sir Gore Ouseley, was available as mediator at Gulistan and also why Britain had its own reasons to want the war ended: Russian troops were needed in Europe, and Britain wanted Iran to remain a viable buffer to the approaches to India. These strategic concerns are developed in Chapter 72. What matters here is that the Qajar court negotiated in 1813 and 1828 as a state still seeking external support while facing an opponent whose resources and military organization were growing.

Finally, the legal form of the settlements deserves notice. A written treaty between two states, with articles, ratifications, and appended agreements, was a European diplomatic form that the Qajar court had adopted in its dealings with France and Britain. Its use in dealings with Russia meant that territorial questions once settled by local alliance and negotiation were now framed as questions of sovereign cession and boundary. This shift in the language of political authority is one of the chapter’s recurring themes.

Division I — The Treaty of Gulistan and the First Territorial Settlement, 1813–1814

The negotiations and provisions of Gulistan, and the distinction between military occupation, recognized sovereignty, and unresolved boundary questions.

Section 71.1

From Military Defeat to Peace: The Negotiations at Gulistan, 1813

By the winter of 1812–1813, the Qajar army of Azerbaijan, which Abbas Mirza had been reorganizing along European lines with British and French assistance, had suffered serious defeats. The loss at Aslanduz on the Aras in October 1812 and the Russian storming of the fortress of Lankaran in January 1813 (both narrated in Chapter 70) left the Qajar court without a field army capable of recovering the territories north of the river. Russia, for its part, was engaged in the greatest war in its history in Europe and had limited forces to spare in the Caucasus. Both governments therefore had reasons to accept a settlement, though neither regarded the outcome as final.

The negotiators reflected this imbalance of circumstance. Mirza Abu’l-Hasan Khan Ilchi, an experienced diplomat who had already served as an envoy to Britain, represented the Qajar side. Nikolai Rtishchev, the Russian commander in Georgia, acted for Russia. Sir Gore Ouseley, the British ambassador, served as mediator. His position rested on the Anglo-Persian arrangements concluded in 1809 and 1812 and on Britain’s alliance with Russia against Napoleon. Britain wanted the Russo-Persian conflict ended so that Russian forces could be directed westward, and it also wanted Iran to survive as a functioning state on the approaches to India. Ouseley’s mediation was therefore a product of British strategy and not simply of goodwill toward either party. The negotiations took place at a camp in Karabakh, on territory Russia already held.

The treaty was signed at Gulistan, a place in Karabakh, on 12 October 1813 (Julian), which is 24 October in the Gregorian calendar. Two distinctions help in reading this moment. The first is between signing and ratification. Plenipotentiaries could sign a text, but each sovereign still had to approve it, and the Qajar court’s reluctance, together with the clauses that remained unsettled, meant that the treaty was accepted in form while remaining contested in substance. The second is between the treaty and the circumstances that produced it. Fath Ali Shah’s government accepted terms it regarded as unsatisfactory because the alternative, continued war without a field army, risked greater losses, including loss of Azerbaijan province itself, and because British mediation promised that some of its concerns might be addressed later.

Gulistan was drafted in a European diplomatic idiom (articles, recognitions, and reciprocal undertakings) that the court was still learning to manage. The Persian and Russian texts did not always read identically in the sense that translation choices and drafting habits affected how articles on frontiers and sovereignty could later be argued. Editions of the treaty in English, preserved among British diplomatic papers, became standard references for nineteenth-century European commentators, but a historian should ask in each case which version is being cited and what it was translated from.

The negotiators did not arrive at Gulistan with equal freedom to bargain. The Russian government could appeal to possession of fortresses, town centers, and routes already secured by its army. The Iranian side could appeal to historic claims, local commitments, and the continued importance of the shah to communities south of the frontier, but it could not credibly threaten to recover the principal occupied districts immediately. That imbalance made the mediation of a third power tempting. British diplomats could carry proposals and press for peace, yet the needs of Britain's war against Napoleon did not coincide completely with Iran's desire to recover territory. Peace was consequently a calculation about the risks of continued fighting rather than proof that Iranian officials considered the disputed claims extinguished.

The diplomatic geography of the meeting was itself revealing. The village of Gulistan lay within a contested Caucasian region whose inhabitants had already endured successive occupations and shifts of allegiance. A settlement negotiated there could be implemented through garrisons and instructions to officers, but it could not automatically settle who would collect dues in an upland pasture, how a family would establish ownership before a new court, or which authority a neighboring village regarded as legitimate. Such practical questions would be left to administrators who did not participate in the negotiations. The treaty thus operated simultaneously as an agreement between sovereigns and as a framework imposed upon local political relationships.

Section 71.2

Territorial Cessions under Gulistan: Georgia, Dagestan, and the Caucasian Khanates

The territorial heart of Gulistan lay in its early articles, in which the Qajar government recognized Russia's possession of much of the southern Caucasus. In the widely cited English treaty numbering, Article II describes the frontier by reference to the situation of possession and geographical markers, while Article III specifies the lands acknowledged as Russian: Georgia and adjacent territories, Dagestan, and the khanates or districts of Karabakh, Ganja, Shaki, Shirvan, Quba, Derbent, and Baku, together with the portion of Talysh then held by Russia. The distinction between a boundary description and the list of acknowledged possessions matters because the latter was more definite than the practical line to be applied on the ground. The final frontier required subsequent interpretation and demarcation.

This list conceals significant differences. Georgia, historically a Christian kingdom with its own monarchy and church, had been under Iranian suzerainty in some earlier periods but not in a way comparable to the khanates, and its annexation by Russia in 1801 had followed the sack of Tbilisi by Agha Mohammad Khan in 1795 and the Georgian king’s appeal to Russia. The khanates were Muslim-ruled territories with hereditary dynasties that had long managed relations with Iran, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia. Their khans’ submissions to Russia between about 1805 and 1806 were sometimes made under military pressure, sometimes to protect their own positions against rivals, and the Qajar court had never accepted them. Gulistan therefore did not merely ratify a transfer from an Iranian administration to a Russian one. It converted contested local allegiances, by Iranian recognition, into international legal questions.

One essential distinction concerns what Gulistan did not include. The khanates of Yerevan and Nakhchivan, south of the main concentration of Russian conquests and on the Aras, remained under Qajar authority after 1813. Their cession belongs to 1828. Students should resist the common simplification that Gulistan took “the Caucasus” and Turkmenchay merely adjusted it. Gulistan covered the north and east; Turkmenchay added the south and west of the area, including the principal Armenian religious center at Echmiadzin.

Nor should the list be read as describing equivalent forms of previous Iranian control. In Karabakh, for example, local khans and Qajar forces had contested the region for years, while in Derbent and Baku local rulers had ties with both Russia and Iran at different times. The treaty used the vocabulary of sovereign possession, which compressed varied histories into a single legal category. That compression is an important historical effect in itself, because it was a European-style legal form of territorial sovereignty that was gradually replacing older structures of tribute and personal allegiance.

The different histories of the territories named in Article III are indispensable to interpreting the word cession. Eastern Georgia had an indigenous monarchy, nobility, and church; the lands of Imereti, Mingrelia, Guria, and Abkhazia possessed distinct political histories and varying relations with Russia. Muslim-ruled khanates eastward often maintained their own courts, armed followers, and fiscal arrangements while balancing powerful neighbors. To call all these places simply Iranian provinces before 1813 would efface meaningful differences between direct administration, claimed overlordship, tribute, and an allegiance that could change with the military situation. The treaty's language was politically consequential precisely because it made diverse historical relationships appear equivalent on a diplomatic list.

One can test the difference by following a locality rather than a dynasty. In Ganja, the Russian assault of 1804 ended one local ruling arrangement. In Karabakh, agreements with the ruling family and subsequent conflict gave Russian officers a different set of problems. In coastal Baku, maritime commerce and Russian naval interests shaped policy. Derbent, a fortified Caspian gateway, mattered for access between the eastern Caucasus and the northern shore. The same legal formula of recognized possession did not produce the same administrative transformation in every location. Nor did recognition by Tehran guarantee acceptance among the inhabitants whose governors, institutions, and property rights changed.

This analysis uses the numbered English treaty tradition preserved in the British Library's collection of treaties, engagements and sanads, volume XIII, digitized by the Qatar Digital Library. English diplomatic versions are historical sources in their own right, but not substitutes for comparison with Persian and Russian authentic texts. The account is an attributed paraphrase, not an original-language quotation.

Article-specific paraphrase (not a quotation): Article II describes a frontier based on the positions held by the parties, using geographic reference points and leaving practical specification of disputed localities for later. Article III enumerates Georgia and adjacent lands, Dagestan, the named eastern Caucasian khanates and districts, and Russian-held Talysh as possessions acknowledged by Iran under Russian sovereignty. The line description and the enumeration should be read together, but they perform different functions.

Analysis: The articles reveal how treaty language reshaped political authority. They describe possession and sovereignty in a vocabulary of fixed territorial units, even though many of the units were polities with complicated histories of allegiance. They also show a boundary principle based on the existing situation, a principle that favored the party already in occupation. At the same time, the reference to later specification of the line shows that the drafters knew the geography was not fully mapped.

Limitations: The text cannot tell us how inhabitants understood the change, how consistently Qajar officials had actually governed the listed territories before 1813, or how far the boundary principle was applied on the ground. Differences between versions may also affect interpretation, and the English translation is a diplomatic edition and not a neutral window onto the Persian text.

Perspective: The source represents the interests of two imperial governments and, to a lesser extent, of a third mediating state. The perspectives of khans, Georgian elites, Armenian and Muslim populations, and local religious institutions are absent.

Section 71.3

The Caspian Sea: Naval Exclusivity, Commercial Navigation, and Maritime Power

Article V of Gulistan addressed a different kind of space, the Caspian Sea, which borders both empires. The article granted Russia the exclusive right to maintain warships on the Caspian, a privilege that Iran gave up and that was reaffirmed in 1828. It also provided that merchant vessels of both countries could navigate the sea. Students should read these two provisions separately. The first is a restriction of Iranian naval power; the second describes a reciprocal commercial freedom. Russia’s naval exclusivity is not the same as an exclusive right to all navigation.

Why did naval control matter on a sea where Iran had little naval strength to begin with? Historians generally note that Qajar naval capacity on the Caspian was limited, so the clause formalized an existing asymmetry more than it removed a functioning force. Its significance was legal and strategic. Control over armed vessels affected the security of Caspian ports such as Anzali in Gilan and Astarabad, the protection of commerce, and the ability to project force against coastal provinces. Exclusive naval rights also gave Russia a permanent means of pressure without requiring annexation of any Iranian territory. A state could remain nominally sovereign over a coast while being unable to defend it by sea or to prevent the presence of another state’s warships.

Gulistan's commercial provisions should not be confused with the more elaborate legal protections associated with the later settlement. Article IX of Gulistan already described a five-percent customs duty in the commonly published English treaty text, together with rules concerning reciprocal commercial treatment. The 1828 agreement and its attached commercial convention enlarged and regularized the framework, particularly through consular representation and jurisdiction. Later accounts sometimes attribute the entire commercial regime, including the numerical tariff rate, to Turkmenchay. Accurate comparison instead shows an earlier tariff arrangement followed by an expanded machinery of foreign privilege. Gulistan's significance thus included sovereignty, boundary, naval rights, succession, and trade; the denser consular-legal framework came with the second settlement.

The Caspian provisions were part of a wider transformation in the meaning of security. A merchant sailing between coastal ports needed predictable access, protection against seizure, and assistance after shipwreck. A warship could protect trading vessels, but it could also intimidate local authorities, police waters, or carry troops and guns to a contested shore. By reserving the latter capacity for one empire, Article V marked a distinction between commercial cooperation and a hierarchy of coercive power. Iranian merchants retained the ability to use the sea, while the Iranian monarchy accepted a legal restriction on an instrument of armed defense.

This arrangement had uneven consequences because the southern Caspian littoral did not have one economy. Gilan's connections to silk cultivation and merchant exchange differed from Mazandaran's agricultural networks and Astarabad's eastern frontier trade. Coastal transport could connect local harvests to distant buyers while overland roads across the Alborz remained costly. An imperial navy might offer protection to some traders at the same time as it gave Russian commanders leverage against customs officers or provincial governors. Such mixed outcomes are difficult to capture if naval exclusivity is described solely as an abstract loss of prestige or, conversely, as a technical privilege without political significance.

Trade clauses also require precise chronology. Article IX of Gulistan already specified a five-percent customs duty in the published English treaty tradition, with reciprocal treatment of goods and limits on additional inland charges. The later commercial convention associated with Turkmenchay did not invent that numerical rate. Its importance was to place tariff arrangements within a wider, more explicit system of legal protection, consular involvement, and commercial privilege. This distinction allows an accurate comparison: Gulistan restricted naval power and regulated trade; Turkmenchay reaffirmed important earlier conditions and expanded the machinery through which a foreign government could intervene on behalf of its subjects. A history that attributes every commercial concession to the second treaty obscures the cumulative nature of the agreements.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, cession, navigation rights, and territorial jurisdiction

Definition: Sovereignty here means supreme legal authority over a territory and its people, recognized by other states. Cession is the transfer of that authority by treaty. Navigation rights are permissions to use waters or routes, which may be shared, restricted, or exclusive. Territorial jurisdiction is the authority of a state’s courts and officials over persons and events within its territory.

Historical context: In the early nineteenth-century Caucasus, political authority had often been layered: a khan might pay tribute to one power, have kin ties to another, and exercise local rule himself. Treaty language tended to replace this layering with clearer categories, so that a territory was said either to belong or not to belong to a sovereign.

Why it matters: Distinguishing these terms allows students to see that Gulistan involved cession of territory, restriction of naval navigation rights, and a boundary principle, while Turkmenchay would further reduce territorial jurisdiction over Russian subjects.

Potential misunderstanding: Cession by treaty did not mean immediate or uniform change on the ground; and a naval exclusive right does not mean exclusive use of the sea for trade.

Section 71.4

Dynastic Recognition, Diplomacy, and the Unfinished Political Settlement

Article IV of Gulistan addressed something unusual in a boundary treaty, the question of who would succeed to the Qajar throne. The article provided that the Russian emperor would recognize the prince whom the shah designated as heir and, in the formulation of the commonly cited text, would support him when the occasion arose. The prince in question was Abbas Mirza, who had been named crown prince (vali’ahd) around 1799 and who governed Azerbaijan province from Tabriz.

The article mattered because the Qajar succession was not automatic. Fath Ali Shah had many sons, and several of them governed provinces with their own followings. Elder princes, notably Mohammad Ali Mirza in Kermanshah, were passed over in favor of Abbas Mirza, a decision commonly explained by his mother’s Qajar lineage, which gave his claim a stronger dynastic basis within the tribe. Rivalries among the princes were real, and Abbas Mirza’s position depended on both his father’s support and his control of the army of Azerbaijan. A foreign power’s pledge to recognize him served the crown prince’s interest and gave Russia a continuing stake in Qajar politics.

It also carried a price. A treaty-based recognition of a heir by an external empire implied that dynastic legitimacy had an international dimension, which had implications for sovereignty. The Qajar court gained assurance against rivals, but it accepted that the succession was a topic for diplomacy. The article’s wording about future support was also vague: it did not specify what form support would take, and it created no mechanism for enforcing it. Russia could therefore use the clause as leverage when convenient.

The treaty as a whole left several matters unresolved. The frontier description rested on possession and on later specification, which, as the next division shows, produced disagreement about particular districts. The Qajar court, deeply dissatisfied with the territorial losses and unsure of British support, regarded the settlement as a pause. Russian commanders, for their part, had reasons of their own for seeking additional territory or securing positions they considered strategically necessary. The settlement thus concluded one war and prepared the grounds for later disputes.

The article on succession reveals that the diplomacy of territorial peace reached into the internal reproduction of the Qajar monarchy. Abbas Mirza governed Azerbaijan, the province most directly threatened by Russia, and commanded much of the army engaged on the frontier. His position as designated heir was therefore both a family arrangement and a strategic concern. Other royal princes governed substantial provinces and maintained military and fiscal followings. The shah needed to manage their claims without allowing a contest for succession to turn into civil war. Recognition from a powerful neighboring empire offered reassurance, but it also introduced an outside participant into a question formerly determined through court politics and the capacity to secure obedience.

The diplomatic undertaking should not be imagined as a guarantee capable of settling all future disputes. The Russian monarch's position could change, the named heir could die, and military events might undermine a political promise. Abbas Mirza in fact died before his father, and the Qajar succession later required new decisions. That subsequent history exposes the gap between a clause drafted for an immediate dynastic problem and the uncertainties of actual government. Students should ask what Russia gained by promising recognition: diplomatic influence, a channel to the crown prince's administration in Tabriz, and potential leverage if rival princes appealed for foreign favor.

Division II — The Changing Caucasus and the Failure of the Gulistan Settlement, 1814–1826

The political and social implementation of the first treaty and the reasons its unresolved questions remained important.

Section 71.5

Mapping the New Frontier: Mountains, Rivers, Villages, and Disputed Territory

A treaty line becomes a frontier only when it is identified in the landscape. In the Caucasus, the geography supplied natural reference points, notably the Aras and Kura rivers, the high Caucasus ridge in the north, and the Talysh mountains near the Caspian. But rivers shift their courses and meander, mountain districts were used by pastoralists in seasonal movement, and villages and cultivated lands did not neatly align with the descriptions in treaty articles. Under Gulistan, boundary commissioners were supposed to specify the line, a process that required the cooperation of both governments and accurate local knowledge.

The region’s geography illustrates the problem. The Aras flows eastward along much of the Iranian northwestern frontier, and the Kura runs across the plains of what is now Azerbaijan to the Caspian. The lowland between them, including the Mughan steppe, was used as winter pasture by pastoral groups who moved with their flocks between highland and lowland, regardless of political divisions. Farther west, the highland district around Lake Gokcha (Sevan) lay between the Yerevan khanate and the Russian-held area, and its control was a matter of dispute. In the south-east, the Talysh region combined coastal lowland with forested mountains and a population distinct from its neighbors in dialect and local organization.

Geography Study

Comparing the 1813 and 1828 Frontiers

Using a physical map of the South Caucasus and northwestern Iran, together with the boundary map accompanying the Encyclopaedia Iranica article on the Treaty of Golestan, students should locate and compare:

  • The Aras and Kura rivers, and the Caucasus ridge north of the Kura
  • Karabakh, Ganja, Shaki, Shirvan, Quba, Baku, and Derbent (Russian under Gulistan)
  • The Talysh region and the Caspian coast, including the Astara River
  • The Mughan lowland between the Aras and the Kura
  • Yerevan, Nakhchivan, and Echmiadzin (ceded only in 1828)
  • Lake Gokcha (Sevan), contested between the treaties
  • Tabriz, Khoy, Maku, and the Azerbaijan province that remained Qajar

In general terms, the 1813 settlement left the Yerevan and Nakhchivan khanates in Iranian hands and described the Talysh and Karabakh frontier less precisely. The 1828 settlement added Yerevan and Nakhchivan and described a more detailed line that, west of the Caspian, relied heavily on the Aras but did not follow it throughout; it also crossed upland and lowland districts where other features defined the line. The modern frontier of Iran with Armenia and Azerbaijan broadly reflects the 1828 arrangement, though later adjustments occurred and should not be attributed to the treaty itself.

Geography Analysis Questions:

  1. Why might a boundary that follows a river be easier to describe in a treaty than to maintain in practice?
  2. How would the seasonal movement of pastoralists across the Mughan lowland have been affected by a fixed political boundary?
  3. Which settlements and districts moved from Iranian to Russian authority only in 1828, and what resources or routes did they control?
  4. Why is it inaccurate to say that the whole Russo-Iranian frontier followed the Aras?

Students should learn to distinguish three things that are easily confused: a boundary described in treaty language, a boundary surveyed on the ground by commissioners, and a boundary administered through customs posts, garrisons, and courts. Gulistan achieved the first only in part, and the second and third lagged. The gap between description and implementation was one of the principal sources of frontier disputes in the following decade.

Historical maps can exaggerate the clarity of an unsettled frontier. A solid line printed across a modern atlas suggests a result that everyone recognized, while an 1813 diplomat often worked from descriptions of river junctions, fortified posts, mountain ridges, and territories in the possession of the respective armies. Around Lake Gokcha, for example, rival interpretations of adjacent districts would later become part of the conflict between Iran and Russia. The lake, now called Sevan, lay in a mountainous environment where passes and pasture grounds could be more important to a local household than the name of a political district. Translating such a landscape into an imperial map required choices about whose geographic knowledge counted.

Boundary-making also demanded institutions and money. Surveyors needed interpreters, escorts, measurement equipment, and information from people who knew roads and seasonal routes. Officers might attempt to establish the frontier by placing guards at a crossing, while a village headman could continue to pay dues to an older authority because that was safer or customary. A caravan could pass through several jurisdictions whose tolls did not correspond to the proposed international boundary. As these differences became visible, central governments might complain that the other side had exceeded the treaty even when local officials understood the situation differently. This is one reason border disputes cannot be resolved simply by comparing two modern maps.

Section 71.6

Russian Administration and the Transformation of Caucasian Political Authority

Russian recognition of sovereignty under Gulistan was followed by institutional change, and this change did not proceed evenly. In the first years after 1813, Russian authority in the region rested largely on military commanders, who worked with local khans, beys, and other notables. Over time Russian officials introduced provincial institutions, tax collection, and judicial arrangements of their own, and they asserted greater control over the territories. Several khanates were abolished or reorganized between about 1819 and 1822, including Shaki, Shirvan, and Karabakh, sometimes after a khan fled, rebelled, or was judged unreliable, and sometimes as part of a deliberate policy of reducing hereditary autonomy. Other forms of authority remained for longer.

The pattern is important for two reasons. First, it shows that annexation was a process. A khan might hold his title for years after a treaty recognized Russian sovereignty, and a Russian commander might govern through him before replacing him. Second, it shows that the personnel of the old order were not simply eliminated. Many local notables, beys, and landholders were integrated into the new system. They kept land, received Russian ranks and sometimes pensions, and served as intermediaries between the imperial state and village communities. For those who resisted or fled, the outcome was different, and several khans took refuge in Iran, where they sought support for recovering their positions.

Religious institutions also adapted. Muslim clerics, endowments, and courts continued to function, though under Russian oversight; Armenian and Georgian churches had their own relationship to the imperial state, often more favorable but not free of tension. Merchants found that Russian customs and commercial regulations created new opportunities and costs. For peasants the principal changes were new tax burdens, labor and supply demands connected with military needs, and in some districts disruption caused by campaigns and resettlement.

This administrative transformation was part of what Iranians objected to when they argued that Gulistan was being applied in a one-sided way. The Qajar court saw the abolition of khanates as the consolidation of conquests the shah had never willingly accepted, and as evidence that Russia intended to go beyond the terms of the treaty. From the Russian perspective, it was a rational extension of imperial government. Both views could be defended on their own terms, which is part of why diplomacy had trouble resolving the dispute.

Russian imperial rule first had to decide what to preserve from a local order it had just subordinated. Existing khans knew village heads, irrigation arrangements, tribal obligations, and the location of taxable households. Removing them could demonstrate sovereign authority but also deprive military officials of indispensable intermediaries. Retaining them, on the other hand, risked allowing hereditary commanders to sustain an independent following or negotiate with Tehran. Russian policy therefore varied by place and time. Rather than impose a uniform provincial apparatus immediately in 1813, commanders retained some rulers for years while preparing their replacement through military districts, surveys, and appointed officials.

The sequence of abolition can be stated more precisely. Shaki's khanate was abolished in 1819; Shirvan's ruler lost his position in 1820; and Karabakh's khanate was abolished in 1822. These dates are not interchangeable and they do not describe every Caucasian polity. The Russian survey of Shirvan associated with 1820 and the Karabakh survey ordered after abolition illustrate the administrative follow-through. Officers and officials wanted to know who owned or controlled fields, how many households could be taxed, which villages owed payments to a former khan, and how much revenue an imperial treasury might expect. George Bournoutian's annotated editions of these surveys demonstrate both their evidentiary richness and the limitations of documents drawn up for conquest and fiscal management.

A survey did not merely record reality. By classifying households according to official categories and fixing obligations in writing, it could change which disputes received recognition and which claims disappeared. A landholder who acquired a certificate might become more secure; a pastoral community whose grazing rights were not recorded could become more vulnerable. Religious institutions, village headmen, and merchant families sometimes found new ways to negotiate with Russian commanders. Others lost income or migrated. The removal of a khan should therefore be examined through the competing experiences of a Russian officer, an established landowning family, and a peasant expected to continue cultivating the same plot under a different collection system.

Section 71.7

Negotiation after Gulistan: Iranian Diplomacy and the Search for Revision

The Qajar court did not treat Gulistan as closed. Soon after the treaty, Mirza Abu’l-Hasan Khan led a mission to St. Petersburg in 1814–1815, seeking clarification and revision of the boundary and a better reading of the territorial clauses. The mission was received with courtesy but obtained little. Iran also turned to Britain. The definitive Anglo-Persian treaty concluded at Tehran in November 1814 contained provisions on British assistance, including a conditional subsidy and a role for Britain in mediating boundary questions between Iran and Russia. The Qajar court understood these as commitments it could invoke; Britain’s own interpretation, as events showed, was narrower.

In 1817 the Russian commander General Aleksei Yermolov, newly in charge of the Caucasus, traveled to Tehran as an envoy. His visit combined diplomatic and military aims: he conveyed Russian positions on the frontier while assessing the Qajar court and its army. The talks did not produce an agreed boundary. Over the following years, discussions continued on districts such as Talysh and the territory around Lake Gokcha, with each side presenting claims grounded in different readings of the treaty. Iran argued that certain districts had not been included in what Russia held in 1813 and that the principle of existing possession should be applied to the situation at the time of signing. Russia often emphasized possession as it had developed since, and the strategic value of the positions it had taken.

British diplomatic assurances were a persistent element. Britain had interests on both sides: it valued the alliance with Russia in Europe and was reluctant to be drawn into a Caucasian conflict, yet it also saw Iranian stability as important for India’s security. The result was a policy of limited support and advice. Qajar officials hoped that Britain’s role as mediator would lead to some revision of the line; the repeated failure of that hope contributed to the court’s sense that diplomacy alone would not recover lost ground, and strengthened the case of those, including Abbas Mirza, who argued for military preparation.

Iranian diplomacy in the interwar years operated under a contradiction. The court needed peace while rebuilding fiscal and military capacity, yet it hoped to revise a settlement that the Russian government considered a basis for its legal claims. Missions could offer explanations, appeal to earlier understandings, or request the return of a disputed locality. They could not easily supply the military pressure that makes a reluctant power reconsider a significant gain. British representatives might convey Iranian grievances, but their discretion was constrained by their own government's relations with Russia and its priorities concerning India and European security. Tehran was thus engaged in negotiation without a reliable mechanism for compelling compromise.

The mission of Aleksei Yermolov in 1817 is useful for analyzing that deadlock. Yermolov was no neutral boundary expert: as a Russian commander in the Caucasus he had responsibilities for maintaining imperial control and advancing a preferred understanding of security. His discussions with the Qajar court occurred while Russian officers were expanding their administrative reach. The difference between a diplomatic assurance offered in Tehran and a decision made by a commander at a distant fortification could therefore be considerable. Iranian authorities, likewise, did not necessarily speak with one voice: the shah, the crown prince in Tabriz, provincial governors, and ministers could disagree about what concession was acceptable.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Negotiated Sovereignty before and after Gulistan

What changed

  • Authority over the northern and eastern khanates was now described in treaty language as the sovereignty of the Russian emperor, whereas earlier it had been negotiated through local allegiances.
  • Boundaries became an object of diplomatic argument, with commissioners, maps, and legal readings of articles.
  • Foreign powers, especially Britain, became explicit participants in the interpretation of Qajar territorial rights.
  • Khans and local notables began to be absorbed into, or displaced by, imperial provincial administration.

What remained continuous

  • Villages, pastoral movements, trade routes, and religious ties continued across the frontier for many years.
  • Local elites continued to negotiate among rival powers, now including Russian officials.
  • The Qajar court continued to claim legitimacy over the territories it had lost and to seek their recovery.

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested. Rulers and diplomats experienced it as a sharp legal break, while peasants and pastoralists often experienced continuity until new taxes, border controls, or land arrangements reached them.

Section 71.8

An Unsettled Border: Talysh, Gokcha, and the Road to Renewed War

By the mid-1820s, the unresolved frontier had become a concrete source of conflict. In the south-east, the status of the Talysh region, part of which Russia held, remained disputed. In the north-west, the district around Lake Gokcha, a strategically important highland between the Yerevan khanate and Russian-held lands, was occupied by Russian forces in 1825, which Iran regarded as a violation of the settlement. Local military actions and competing claims accumulated, and a Russian mission to Tehran in 1826 under Prince Aleksandr Menshikov failed to resolve them. War resumed in the summer of 1826. The battles of that war belong to Chapter 70; what matters here is why the settlement could not contain the dispute.

Three factors converge. First, the texts were ambiguous. The principle of existing possession lent itself to competing interpretations, and the reference to later specification of the line did not produce an agreed specification. Second, both states had strategic motives for keeping the question open: Russia regarded positions like Gokcha as valuable, and the Qajar court believed that revision remained possible with British help or after military reorganization. Third, the interests of local actors, including displaced khans seeking restoration and Russian officers seeking advancement, added pressure on both sides. Clerical and popular calls in Iran for a holy war against Russia in 1826 also increased the pressure on the court, though their role is a matter for Chapter 70.

The geography of the dispute multiplied the risks of escalation. A Russian commander seeking to secure approaches to a fort might occupy a position he believed lay within the accepted frontier. A Qajar official could treat the same movement as the first step toward the annexation of another district. The Lake Gokcha and Talysh controversies illustrate this problem because local geography, memories of earlier allegiance, and successive occupations did not correspond to one universally agreed cartographic line. As soon as troops, armed retainers, or tax officers appeared, interpretation ceased to be a purely diplomatic debate. The act of collecting revenue or maintaining a garrison itself became evidence of possession.

The interests of inhabitants could cut across the positions of both capitals. A khan deprived of hereditary rule had reasons to seek restoration; a merchant with customers on both sides could prefer predictable trade to renewed fighting; a village under pressure from several collectors might support whichever authority offered relief. Frontier clergy and military patrons could interpret the losses in moral and religious terms, while households exposed to the costs of mobilization might prioritize survival. These interests did not produce a single Caucasian or Iranian response to the deteriorating peace. Nor should claims that one side alone intended aggression be accepted without considering the strategic calculations, local incidents, and different documentary perspectives.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Settlement of 1813 Fail to Secure a Lasting Peace?

The question: Was Gulistan a failed peace treaty, defective in its drafting, or a settlement whose unresolved terms allowed opposing states to continue a territorial contest they never intended to abandon?

Interpretation A, emphasizing drafting and ambiguity: The treaty’s vague boundary clauses and reliance on possession created disputes that no commission resolved. On this reading, clearer drafting and prompt demarcation might have prevented the breakdown.
Interpretation B, emphasizing Russian expansion: Russian commanders and the imperial government treated Gulistan as a stage in continuing expansion, so no drafting could have secured a stable frontier. The ambiguities were useful to the stronger party.
Interpretation C, emphasizing Qajar non-acceptance: The Qajar court never accepted the loss of the territories and used diplomacy and military reform to prepare for recovery. On this view, the war of 1826 reflects Iranian as well as Russian choices, and British mediation encouraged hopes that proved unrealistic.

Evidence: Treaty texts and their versions; correspondence of Russian, Qajar, and British officials; records of the frontier disputes of the 1820s; accounts of abolished khanates; and later chronicles. Much of the diplomatic evidence is partisan, and accounts by Russian and Iranian participants tend to assign responsibility to the other side.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and responsibility, not the chronology of events. Historians disagree about the weight to give each party’s intentions and about whether the ambiguities were accidents of drafting or deliberate.

Why it matters: How one assigns responsibility shapes the interpretation of Turkmenchay, which can be read as a punitive settlement imposed on a state that provoked war or as the consolidation of a Russian program begun earlier. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that would support each interpretation, and explain which combination of factors you find most persuasive.

Division III — Turkmenchay and the Terms of the Second Settlement, 1827–1828

The negotiation and legal provisions of Turkmenchay: territorial cession, indemnity, commercial privileges, and foreign recognition of the Qajar heir.

Section 71.9

The Negotiations at Turkmenchay: Defeat, Diplomacy, and Unequal Bargaining

The military position in the autumn of 1827 was decisive. Russian forces under General Ivan Paskevich had taken the fortress of Yerevan in October and occupied Tabriz, the seat of Abbas Mirza’s government, soon afterward (Chapter 70). With Azerbaijan province exposed, the Qajar court faced the prospect of further occupation and, potentially, a threat to the dynasty. Negotiations began under these conditions and were concluded in the village of Turkmenchay, near Tabriz.

On the Iranian side, Abbas Mirza and the court minister Allahyar Khan Asaf al-Dawla took principal responsibility. On the Russian side, Paskevich and the diplomat Aleksandr Obreskov led the negotiation, and the writer and diplomat Alexander Griboyedov, serving in the Russian mission, played a significant part in the drafting and in carrying the treaty to St. Petersburg. Historians have noted that the Russian side arrived with relatively detailed demands, while the Iranian side sought to preserve the dynasty, reduce the indemnity and the territorial losses, and avoid further occupation. The negotiation was therefore not a bargain between equals. Russia could threaten to continue the advance; Iran had few counter-pressures, and British support, hoped for in 1826, had not materialized in a form that altered the position.

The treaty was signed on 10 February 1828 (Julian), which is 22 February in the Gregorian calendar. Keeping the diplomatic story separate from the battlefield narrative of Chapter 70 helps clarify what was at stake in the room. The Qajar negotiators did not expect to retain Yerevan, but they could try to limit the claims on the territory south of the Aras, the amount and timing of the indemnity, and the extent of Russian occupation until payment. They could also seek the recognition of Abbas Mirza as heir, which Gulistan had promised and which Russia was willing to reaffirm.

The terms presented at Turkmenchay were shaped by an immediate threat to the Qajar state's economic and administrative center in Azerbaijan. Tabriz was not simply another captured city: it housed Abbas Mirza's court, warehouses, merchants, workshops, and the administration through which much of the frontier war had been supported. Continued occupation could interrupt the flows of revenue and supplies upon which the dynasty depended. For Russian negotiators, such occupation provided a means of enforcing demands; for Iranian negotiators, withdrawal became as important as limiting the territorial claims. The military and financial parts of the proposed settlement were therefore linked before the articles were finally approved.

The people conducting negotiations operated within hierarchical governments, but the terms were not written by abstract states alone. Paskevich had to justify the settlement to the emperor and accommodate his government's strategic priorities. Abbas Mirza had to reconcile the cost of peace with his responsibility to the shah, his soldiers, and a population that had already borne campaigning and occupation. Secretaries and translators worked across Persian and Russian diplomatic conventions; their words determined how payment obligations and border provisions would later be invoked. Diplomatic correspondence and draft articles, including material preserved in British records, reveal that negotiating positions changed under pressure. They should be consulted alongside the final treaty rather than treated as identical to it.

The settlement was accompanied by distinct legal instruments for distinct purposes. The main peace treaty specified territorial transfer, dynastic recognition, navigation, diplomatic privileges, and the basic financial obligation. A special agreement supplied the timetable and security for indemnity payments. A commercial convention elaborated conditions governing merchants and jurisdiction. This documentary architecture matters because later summaries have sometimes treated the entire package as a single uninterrupted text. Identifying each instrument makes it possible to separate rights created by the peace treaty from methods of implementation and the additional privileges that followed in trade and litigation.

Contingency mattered as well, as the later reduction of the indemnity after the 1829 crisis shows.

Section 71.10

Yerevan, Nakhchivan, and the Redrawing of the Russo-Iranian Frontier

The territorial articles of Turkmenchay (Articles III to V in the commonly cited English numbering) accomplished three things. They ceded the khanates of Yerevan and Nakhchivan to Russia, defined a boundary line, and provided for the demarcation of that line. Yerevan, with its fortress and its position on the routes between Tabriz, Tbilisi, and Anatolia, and with Echmiadzin, the seat of the Armenian Catholicos nearby, was of strategic and religious importance. Nakhchivan, on the Aras, linked the Yerevan basin with the lands to the east.

The boundary line took the Aras as a main reference for much of its length, but students should avoid saying that the whole frontier followed the river. West of the ceded khanates, the line was drawn in relation to the Ottoman frontier and the Ararat massif; farther east, after leaving the river, it crossed the Mughan lowland and the Talysh uplands toward the Caspian near the Astara River. These sections used other features and commissioners’ determinations. The treaty also provided for demarcation, reflecting the lesson of Gulistan’s unfinished line, and the commissions that followed worked over several years. Even so, details of the frontier continued to generate local disputes and subsequent adjustments.

The population of the ceded territories was mixed. Muslims, including Turkic-speaking farmers and landholders and Kurdish groups who used highland pastures, lived beside Armenian communities that were concentrated in villages and in the vicinity of Echmiadzin, as well as small numbers of others. Estimates of the proportions in 1828 vary, and they depend on sources (Qajar and Russian tax and survey records, church records, traveler accounts) that counted different units in different ways. Students should treat any single set of figures as an estimate whose basis needs to be identified. What is clear is that the transfer placed a large Muslim population under Christian imperial rule and brought the principal Armenian religious institution into a Christian empire for the first time since the early modern era, with consequences explored in Division IV.

The creation of a more detailed frontier was an answer to one of Gulistan's conspicuous weaknesses. The 1828 treaty identified the ceded khanates and described a boundary with reference to rivers, mountains, and other landmarks. Its detailed wording did not mean that local conditions would be transformed at the moment of signature. The former Yerevan khanate included irrigated agricultural districts, a fortified administrative center, Armenian church institutions, and Muslim landowners and officials; Nakhchivan had its own towns, villages, and connections to neighboring regions. Their transfer posed different problems for Russian administrators, who needed information about local rights and obligations and could not simply import every procedure from distant provinces.

For communities along the Aras, the river's new status had complicated meanings. It marked important stretches of the international boundary, but boats, irrigation practices, kin relationships, and seasonal movements continued to connect its banks. Where land belonging to one owner lay on both sides, a political settlement could produce immediate uncertainty about taxes, inheritance, and legal remedy. The treaty's property provisions can be understood as an attempt—necessarily imperfect—to manage precisely this predicament. Elsewhere, sections of the frontier followed other geographical markers. Readers should reject schematic maps suggesting that every point on the Russo-Iranian border was identical with the river's course.

Section 71.11

The War Indemnity: Financial Obligations and the Costs of Defeat

Article VI of Turkmenchay established an indemnity of ten kurur of tumans—a kurur being half a million tumans—expressed in the treaty as twenty million silver rubles. It referred the schedule, method, and guarantees to a special payment agreement attached to the peace treaty. A specialist reconstruction identifies four original installments: three kurur within eight days of signing; two more fifteen days later; three in April 1828; and a final two before March 1830. Russian occupation and the retention of Khoy were connected with security for payment. These stipulated amounts and deadlines are legal obligations, not a verified ledger of all money eventually transferred. Both the original agreement and later adjustments must be distinguished from actual receipts.

For the Qajar state the indemnity was a severe fiscal burden. The court’s annual revenues, drawn mainly from land taxes, customs, and provincial remittances, were not large by comparison with the sum demanded. Abbas Mirza’s administration in Azerbaijan, itself damaged by war and occupation, had to find money quickly. Measures included extraordinary taxation, requests for contributions from provincial governors and notables, transfers from the royal treasury, and the sale or pledging of valuables, including, according to several accounts, jewelry and court property. Pressure on provincial revenue systems weakened local administrations and increased resentment against tax collectors. The burden fell unevenly, with merchants, landowners, and peasants bearing different shares depending on the province and the means used to collect.

The indemnity also functioned as an instrument of political pressure. Russian military presence in Azerbaijan gave delays an immediate territorial cost, while the prospect of withdrawal encouraged the Qajar government to assemble money rapidly. Later diplomacy altered the obligation without erasing it. After the 1829 attack on the Russian mission in Tehran, Prince Khosrow Mirza went to Saint Petersburg with the court's apology. The Russian government subsequently reduced the final installment from two kurur to one—a remission of one kurur, not cancellation of the entire final installment. A revised liability is still different from a documented history of payments, and the material available for this edition does not support an independently reconciled total of coins and valuables actually collected.

The payment obligation had a documentary structure precise enough to test common generalizations. Article VI of Turkmenchay established ten kurur of tumans, conventionally equated in the agreement with twenty million silver rubles, but expressly referred the timing, method of payment, and guarantees to a special agreement attached to the treaty. A specialist study of Abbas Mirza's will reconstructs four original installments of three kurur within eight days of signing, two kurur fifteen days later, three kurur in April 1828, and two kurur due before March 1830. Khoy served as security for payment during the occupation and staged withdrawal. Neither the original schedule nor a retrospective total should be confused with an audited record of coin and valuables actually delivered.

This timetable placed severe pressure on the royal treasury and on Abbas Mirza's administration. Currency had to be assembled quickly, often from revenue already promised for troops or provincial expenses. Wealth held in jewels or other valuables was not necessarily available as spendable silver in the right place at the right moment. The conversion of agricultural taxes and merchant contributions into indemnity payments required transportation, negotiation, and exchange. A governor obliged to meet an extraordinary demand might recover the cost through subordinate collectors, whose methods affected villages differently. Thus a diplomatic clause directed at the shah could alter relationships between tax collectors and cultivators hundreds of kilometers from the negotiating table.

The final installment was later adjusted, but the correction must be stated exactly. Following Khosrow Mirza's mission to Saint Petersburg after the killing of Griboyedov, Russia reduced the final obligation from two kurur to one—not to zero. On the original timetable this amounted to a one-kurur reduction in the stipulated total. Historians must still distinguish the revised legal liability from evidence of what was actually transferred, and they should not infer that all money originally scheduled was collected on the dates specified. The distinction is essential to explaining both Russian diplomatic leverage and the continuing fiscal uncertainty experienced by the Qajar government.

Section 71.12

Commercial Privileges, Consular Jurisdiction, and Caspian Naval Rights

The provisions that most extended Russian influence beyond the ceded territory appear in the later articles of the main treaty and, especially, in the separate commercial convention concluded with it. Both documents must be read together, and students should be careful to attribute each right to the right instrument. The main treaty (Articles VII to X in the commonly cited numbering) reaffirmed Russia’s recognition of Abbas Mirza as heir, repeated the exclusive Russian right to maintain warships on the Caspian Sea, and provided for diplomatic and consular representation. The commercial convention set out the detailed rules for trade and for the legal position of Russian subjects in Iran.

Under the enlarged arrangements, Russia could appoint consuls and commercial agents in Iranian towns, and Russian subjects obtained defined trading and residential protections. The five-percent customs figure was already present in Gulistan Article IX; the associated 1828 commercial convention reinforced the tariff regime and developed more explicit safeguards for foreign trade. Specified disputes involving Russian subjects were subject to consular protections and procedures, restricting Iranian officials' independent ability to handle such cases. Historians often call this a form of extraterritorial or capitulatory privilege, although the exact scope must be read from the attached convention rather than assumed from the term. Comparable arrangements later became influential in relations with other powers.

The effects were gradual. In the first years, the number of Russian merchants in Iran was limited, and trade routes continued to be dominated by Iranian and Armenian merchants and by commerce with the Ottoman Empire and, increasingly, with Britain via the Persian Gulf and Trebizond. The importance of the provisions lay in the legal framework they created, which Russia and later other powers could use as trade expanded. The framework also limited the ability of Qajar officials to regulate foreign merchants or to protect local producers, issues that become central in Chapter 75.

The commercial framework rested on both continuity and enlargement. Article IX of Gulistan already established the five-percent customs rate in the published English text. Turkmenchay and the associated commercial convention maintained a restrictive tariff environment while specifying more elaborate rights for merchants, agents, and consuls. This matters for economic causation: a low tariff did not suddenly appear in 1828, but the legal machinery that allowed Russian subjects to claim particular treatment became more extensive. A merchant might now invoke not only a general promise of trade but also an official able to raise a complaint before the Qajar government.

Consular jurisdiction had consequences beyond the particular courtroom in which a dispute arose. If Russian subjects could demand consular participation or protection in legal matters, Iranian judges and officials might have to negotiate with an outside representative before enforcing ordinary procedures. Disagreement about debt, inheritance, business partnerships, or criminal accusations could become diplomatic correspondence. Such involvement did not mean that every Russian citizen in every circumstance was beyond all Iranian authority; the precise wording and conditions of the commercial convention matter. But it created an institutional asymmetry: the Russian state could intervene for subjects resident in Iran in ways that Iran could not reciprocate on an equal footing in imperial territory.

The legal arrangement also had a temporal dimension. In the 1820s Russian commercial activity was more significant in some northern routes than in the Iranian interior, and Iranian and Armenian merchants retained important roles as carriers, brokers, and financiers. The implications of legal privilege expanded as merchants and governments learned to use the framework. Tariff ceilings affected the fiscal options of the Qajar treasury, but trade also depended on security, transport, local demand, and competition from Ottoman and British-connected routes. It would be misleading either to portray the convention as immediately transforming the whole economy or to dismiss it because its early practical reach was uneven. Its significance lay in the durable rules it established for later expansion.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulations, extraterritoriality, indemnity, and consular jurisdiction

Definition: Capitulations are agreements by which one state grants foreign subjects specified privileges within its territory; the term was used for older arrangements with the Ottoman Empire. Extraterritoriality is the principle that foreign subjects remain under their own state’s law while abroad. Consular jurisdiction is the exercise of that principle through consuls and consular courts. An indemnity is a payment demanded from a defeated state to compensate the victor for the costs of war.

Historical context: Such arrangements were not unique to Iran. The Ottoman Empire and later other states in Asia granted comparable privileges. In Iran, Turkmenchay and its commercial convention established them with Russia in 1828, and subsequent agreements with other powers followed similar lines.

Why it matters: They restricted Qajar legal authority over foreign residents and fixed customs rates, creating influence without annexation of territory.

Potential misunderstanding: The term “capitulation” in modern usage suggests surrender; in diplomatic history it denotes a specific kind of legal privilege that could arise from agreement and need not be the direct result of military defeat.

Section 71.13

Amnesty, Prisoners, Refugees, and the Treaty Rights of Movement

Treaties between empires usually deal with people as well as territory, and Turkmenchay devoted several articles to prisoners, deserters, refugees, amnesty, and the disposal of property (Articles XII to XV in the commonly cited numbering). Reading them closely shows how the two governments imagined the population of the borderland: as people who might be exchanged, pardoned, relocated, or compelled to choose a side. These provisions apply to different categories, and the differences matter.

The articles have to be distinguished individually. Article XII gave certain owners of immovable property on opposite sides of the Aras three years to sell or exchange it, with specific exceptions. Article XIII regulated exchange of captives and prisoners. Article XIV addressed refugees, political sensitivities, and restrictions on residence near the frontier. Article XV granted an amnesty specifically to the inhabitants and officials of the Iranian province of Azerbaijan for actions during the war and temporary occupation; it provided a one-year period for covered persons to move to Russia with movable property and five years to dispose of immovable property. Article XV should not be summarized as a general amnesty automatically applying to all inhabitants of the annexed Yerevan and Nakhchivan khanates. Formal permissions also did not guarantee transport, buyers, safety, or family consent.

These provisions would prove contentious. Questions about the return of people who had come under the protection of the Russian mission, including women and others of Georgian and Armenian origin held in Iranian households, became a source of friction. The attack on the Russian mission in Tehran in February 1829, in which Griboyedov and many members of the mission were killed, grew out of tensions in which such disputes played a part, along with wider hostility toward the Russian presence in the capital. After the attack, the Qajar court sent a mission to St. Petersburg, and the emperor chose not to press for war, partly out of consideration of Russian strategic priorities elsewhere, including the Ottoman front in 1828–1829. The episode shows how treaty provisions on persons could have political consequences far beyond their drafting.

Articles XII through XV should be read in sequence, because they established distinct rights for distinct categories of people. Article XII allowed owners of immovable property on opposite sides of the Aras three years to sell or exchange it, subject to stated exceptions affecting former rulers. Article XIII dealt with the mutual return of prisoners from the wars and with procedures for delivery. Article XIV addressed refugees and restrictions on the residence of specified politically sensitive persons near the new border. Article XV, by contrast, granted an amnesty specifically to inhabitants and officials of the Iranian province of Azerbaijan for actions during the war and temporary occupation, with one year allowed for certain moves to Russia with movable property and five years to dispose of immovable property. The one-, three-, and five-year periods should never be compressed into one undifferentiated permission.

The political logic behind the distinctions becomes clearer when the people involved are identified. A proprietor who suddenly found inherited land on the opposite side of the river had a problem of title and sale that differed from that of a prisoner waiting to be exchanged. An official accused of cooperating during a temporary occupation faced possible retaliation, while a former khan or a refugee commander could be regarded as a security risk by either government. A family considering departure might fall within more than one category, yet possession of a formal permission did not guarantee safe passage, a willing buyer, or money for resettlement. The documents recognized some problems and ignored others, especially the preferences of dependents whose movement was controlled by household heads.

The dispute that culminated in the attack on the Russian mission in 1829 illustrates the limits of treaty drafting. Claims about the status and return of Georgian and Armenian captives or former captives entered a world of complex household, religious, and legal relationships. The government's responsibility to implement international obligations collided with local understandings of authority, conversion, kinship, and protection. This does not excuse the violence that killed members of the mission, nor does it justify portraying every protester as motivated by one cause. It shows how a clause intended to settle relations between states could become a crisis when implementation involved particular people whose identities and rights were disputed.

Section 71.14

Armenian Communities: Migration, Settlement, and New Imperial Administration

Armenian communities were distributed across Iranian Azerbaijan (Tabriz, Khoy, Salmas, Urmia, and Maku), the Yerevan and Nakhchivan districts, Karabakh, and many other places. After 1828, Armenian migration from Iranian territory to the former Yerevan khanate and neighboring regions became a major movement. The Armenian Church’s leadership played a role: the Armenian prelate Nerses of Ashtarak, who had supported the Russian advance, encouraged resettlement, and Echmiadzin, the seat of the Catholicos, was now within the Russian Empire. Russian officials, seeking to develop the region and strengthen a Christian population in a frontier zone, supported settlement through provisions on land, transport assistance, and tax advantages.

Contemporary and later estimates of migration differ because historians count different populations and periods. The Encyclopaedia Iranica study of Armeno-Iranian relations reports that approximately 45,000 Armenians left Iran in 1828–1829, drawing on Armenian historical studies. This figure should be treated as a source-specific estimate for people leaving Iranian territory during those years—not as an exact census, a total for all Armenians resettled in the Russian Caucasus, or a count that includes migration from Ottoman territory in 1829–1830. The evidence also shows that many Armenian communities remained in Tabriz, Khoy, Maku, Salmas, Urmia, and elsewhere in Iran. Students should compare the scope, dates, and counting units of any alternative estimate before combining figures.

Estimates of the number of Armenians who moved from Iranian territory in these years vary widely in the literature, from several tens of thousands to higher figures, depending on the time span, the districts counted, and whether Armenians from Ottoman territory (some of whom also moved in 1829–1830) are included. Students should always ask what population, origin, period, and source underlie a figure. It is also important to avoid two simplifications. The movement was not entirely voluntary: pressure from war, local insecurity, economic hardship, and the encouragement of clergy and officials shaped decisions. Nor was it entirely compelled: many families made calculated choices about land, safety, religion, and opportunity, and many Armenians chose to remain in Iran, where they continued to live in Tabriz, Urmia, Isfahan’s New Julfa, and other places. Armenian communities were not politically homogeneous; merchants, clergy, peasants, and notables had different interests.

The economic and demographic effects were substantial. In the Yerevan region, the arrival of Armenian settlers altered the balance of the population and the use of land, particularly in districts depopulated by war or by the departure of Muslim households. Newcomers often faced difficult conditions: disease, shortages of housing and seed, and disputes over land. Over the longer term, the Russian administration and the Armenian Church, with Echmiadzin as its center, shaped the development of Armenian communities in the Caucasus, with consequences for later Armenian political and cultural life that go beyond this chapter.

A more disciplined treatment of migration begins by identifying a population, an origin, and a period. The relevant Encyclopaedia Iranica study, drawing on Armenian historical sources, states that about 45,000 Armenians left Iran during 1828–1829. That is an estimate for people leaving Iranian territory, not a count of every Armenian who entered the Russian Caucasus in all the years after 1828. Later or parallel movements from Ottoman territory must be counted separately, and different surveys may use households rather than individuals as their basic unit. The estimate should therefore be treated as evidence of substantial movement, not as an exact census of every displaced or migrating person.

The decision to move was structured by institutions as well as individual preference. Armenian clergy and church networks could help communicate the terms of the settlement and encourage families to resettle under Russian authority. Russian officials had political reasons to attract settlers and to strengthen selected districts. Yet a farmer with irrigation rights, fruit trees, and family graves in Iranian Azerbaijan confronted a different balance of interests from a merchant with trading partners on both sides of the frontier. Some families had relatives in the newly Russian-controlled districts; others feared loss of land or uncertainty about new taxes. The word voluntary cannot fully express choices made amid recent military defeat and significant political pressure, but the word forced cannot adequately describe every departure either.

Iranian policy was not uniformly indifferent to the exodus. Abbas Mirza sought to retain Armenian communities in Azerbaijan, whose merchants and artisans contributed to the province's economy. Specialist historical writing records his offers of concessions to merchant and clerical figures and his support for the monastery of Saint Thaddeus, partly as an alternative center after Echmiadzin passed to Russian jurisdiction. These efforts underline the demographic and fiscal stakes of movement. The Russian administration, meanwhile, had to organize settlement in places where housing, seed, and security were not always adequate. Accounts of migration should therefore follow the people beyond the act of crossing a border: the capacity to settle, cultivate, trade, worship, and remain connected to relatives mattered as much as the treaty's language of permission.

Household Decisions about Migration and Settlement

Did the family own land, and could it be sold within the time the treaty allowed, at a fair price, to a buyer able to pay? Some households moved early with church leaders, some delayed until harvest, some sent part of the family ahead, and others remained. Because most records were produced by officials and clergy, historians see these choices mainly through lists, petitions, and administrative reports and must infer the reasoning behind them.

Section 71.15

Muslim Communities, Azerbaijani-Speaking Populations, and the New Frontier

The majority population of most of the khanates was Muslim, speaking Turkic dialects (ancestral to modern Azerbaijani) or, in some districts, Persian, Talysh, Kurdish, and other languages. Both Shi’i and Sunni Muslims lived in the region, with Shi’ism predominant in much of the east and south, and the two communities had distinct institutions and relations with authorities. For these populations the treaties meant incorporation into a Christian empire whose officials spoke a different language, followed different legal norms, and often regarded local Muslim elites with suspicion, though also with interest in their cooperation.

Responses varied. Some khans and beys resisted or fled to Iran, others accommodated to Russian rule and preserved their local standing; some peasants left districts where war, taxation, and military demands pressed hardest, while most stayed on their land. Emigration of Muslim households from the Yerevan and Nakhchivan districts to Iran or the Ottoman Empire occurred, but its scale and timing are difficult to establish, and estimates depend on contested sources. Disputes over property were common, as land held by departing owners or by families displaced by war was claimed by newcomers, by the state, or by returning relatives. In some districts, the Russian authorities confirmed land rights of loyal beys; in others, they reallocated land, including for Armenian settlers.

Religious and commercial ties with Iran did not end. Pilgrims continued to travel to Shi’i shrines in Iran and Iraq, though now crossing a Russian border with passports and inspection. Family and marriage networks stretched across the frontier for years, and trade in textiles, grain, livestock, and other goods continued, albeit under new customs regulations. Language and culture, by contrast, did not divide neatly at the border: Azerbaijani Turkic was spoken on both sides, and Persian literary culture remained a presence in towns north of the Aras.

Care is needed with identity. In 1813 and 1828, people described themselves and were described by others in terms of religion, locality, tribe, occupation, and allegiance to a khan or a ruler. The modern national identities of Azerbaijanis and Iranians, in their contemporary forms, did not yet exist as they do today. Russian officials often used the label “Tatar” for the Muslim Turkic-speaking population of the Caucasus, a usage that reflects imperial categories. Projecting twentieth-century national categories onto these communities would obscure the actual terms in which they understood themselves.

The new frontier divided political jurisdictions without dividing languages or religious networks neatly. A Muslim landholder in the Yerevan district might continue to speak the same Turkic language as relatives south of the Aras, rely on the same marriage connections, and consult the same religious authorities even after those relatives became subjects of a different ruler. Inhabitants who remained north of the line could attempt to secure certificates and recognition under Russian administration, while others might leave because of wartime losses, political loyalty, insecurity, or uncertainty about property. These trajectories varied by district and by wealth; a former khan had different resources and risks from a sharecropping household.

Russian surveys are especially valuable here when used critically. The surveys associated with Shirvan and Karabakh enumerated villages, revenues, and land relationships from the perspective of an administration trying to know and govern newly acquired territory. They can help recover patterns of settlement and economic production, but they do not provide a transparent register of modern ethnic identity. Officials classified people according to categories that reflected imperial priorities, local informants, and administrative convention. The label Tatar, frequently used in Russian records for Muslim Turkic-speaking inhabitants, cannot simply be substituted for a modern national designation without explanation. Likewise, recording a household as Muslim does not establish its politics or allegiance.

Section 71.16

Land, Trade, Taxation, and Everyday Life across the New Border

The border affected ordinary activities in many ways. Merchants who had moved goods between Tabriz, Yerevan, Tbilisi, and the Caspian ports now crossed a state frontier with customs duties, documentation, and inspection. Caravans had to adapt their routes to official crossing points, and some traders took advantage of price differences between empires. Pastoralists who moved herds between winter pastures in the lowlands and summer pastures in the highlands faced new rules, and their access to grazing lands depended on the treatment of land claims on each side. For many such groups, the frontier at first hardly existed, and conflicts emerged gradually, when states began enforcing it more consistently.

Land and taxation were the most immediate concerns of rural households. Russian administrative surveys sought to record land, assign tax obligations, and identify landholders; these surveys, together with the confirmation or revision of land rights, redefined the relations among peasants, beys, and the state. Tax practices differed from those of the Qajar system, in which payments might be made in kind or through intermediaries, and the shift involved new officials and new forms of record-keeping. Marriage, inheritance, and religious travel were affected by the need for permission to cross the border and by differences in legal regimes. Courts, whether Muslim, Armenian, or Russian, had to decide cases involving property across the frontier, and jurisdictional disputes were common.

In the longer run, commercial networks reoriented. Caucasian towns became linked to Russian imperial markets and to Black Sea and Caspian trade, while the Qajar side of the frontier turned increasingly to Ottoman and British trade routes. These shifts took decades and were not uniform. The point to retain is that territorial transfer was not a single completed event: state institutions, property relations, and economic networks changed over many years, and the people involved adapted as circumstances allowed.

The border's economic effects can be reconstructed through ordinary transactions. A merchant sending cloth from Tabriz toward a Caucasian market needed information about customs rates, the safety of routes, and which documents might satisfy Russian officials. A caravan carrying grain or animals could cross a line whose legal importance had increased even though the road itself was unchanged. Brokers and interpreters acquired value because they understood both commercial networks and administrative procedures. Some merchants lost customers or faced new fees, while others profited from the opening of routes toward Russian cities and Black Sea or Caspian ports. The direction of change cannot be inferred simply from the fact of annexation.

Land rights presented an even more complicated problem. An irrigation channel might serve fields whose owners now lived under separate jurisdictions. A household's agricultural holdings might be mortgaged or inherited across the boundary. The treaty provided certain periods for selling immovable property, but it did not create a neutral market in which every owner could obtain a fair price. If buyers knew a seller had to leave within a deadline, the seller's bargaining position could be weak. If records were lost in war or legal traditions differed, a claim might be difficult to establish. Russian surveys sought to classify such rights, sometimes confirming established claims and sometimes overriding practices that had operated through custom rather than written title.

The effects on pastoralists were particularly sensitive to seasonality. Families that moved herds between uplands and lowlands did not necessarily think of themselves as crossing political territory in the same way as a state surveyor. When the state demanded a permit or restricted grazing, a previously ordinary route could become a legal violation. The degree of enforcement varied with the presence of garrisons, officials, and local bargains. Family and religious relationships likewise persisted despite political separation. The frontier was made through repeated encounters at bridges, markets, pastures, courts, and shrines, not solely by the diplomatic act that drew it.

Historical Vocabulary
Demarcation, borderland, property rights, and customs

Definition: Demarcation is the physical marking of a boundary after its description in a treaty (delimitation). A borderland is a zone whose society and economy are shaped by proximity to a frontier and by movement across it. Property rights are the recognized claims of persons or institutions to land and other goods. Customs are duties and controls on goods crossing a frontier.

Historical context: In the early nineteenth century, many frontiers in the region were zones of overlapping claims and mobile populations. The demarcation of the Russo-Iranian line, in stages after 1828, gradually turned a zone into a line.

Why it matters: These terms help distinguish a treaty line from the experiences of people who lived near it, and explain why consequences unfolded over many years.

Potential misunderstanding: A demarcated line did not immediately end cross-border movement; it changed the conditions under which movement took place.

Division V — Iranian Political Thought, Imperial Relations, and the Historical Legacy, 1828 and After

The consequences for Iranian political authority, diplomacy, historical memory, and interpretations of the Caucasus.

Section 71.17

Territorial Loss and Iranian Political Thought

The defeats and the treaties compelled the Qajar elite to reconsider what sovereignty required. Contemporary responses were varied. Abbas Mirza and those around him drew the lesson that military and administrative improvement was necessary, building on the experiments with European-style infantry and artillery that had begun before the wars and on efforts to send students and envoys to Europe. Court ministers such as Mirza Abu’l-Qasem Qa’im Maqam Farahani, who served Abbas Mirza, combined practical administration with a literary and political sensibility that treated state strength as a matter of governance as well as arms. Diplomats such as Mirza Abu’l-Hasan Khan, who had seen both London and St. Petersburg, brought back direct knowledge of European states. These threads are developed in Chapter 73.

Others emphasized different explanations. Some religious scholars, who had urged jihad in 1826, interpreted the defeat through the lens of the court’s relations with the clergy and the community; some court writers attributed failure to particular commanders or to the unreliability of allies; others stressed the responsibilities of the dynasty and the need for sound diplomacy. Political thought was not uniform, and no single narrative of humiliation, national loss, or reform captured all of it. Because many of the most detailed texts were produced by court circles, the views of merchants, tribal leaders, and ordinary subjects are harder to recover.

Students should distinguish these contemporary responses from later interpretations. In the second half of the nineteenth century and especially in the twentieth, writers increasingly framed the treaties as national humiliations and as proof of the need for reform, constitutionalism, or national revival, as seen in the writing and debates examined in Chapters 77 and 78. These later accounts drew on the experience of the treaties but employed categories (nation, homeland, territorial integrity) that were being elaborated in the intervening decades. The early reactions, by contrast, tended to speak in the language of dynasty, religion, and the duties of rulers. Recognizing this difference prevents the anachronism of treating 1828 as an event experienced by contemporaries as a loss of “national” territory in the modern sense, while also recognizing that the loss deeply affected how the court and its critics thought about sovereignty.

Political vocabulary is itself evidence of historical change. An official writing to defend Abbas Mirza's position might describe lost territories in the language of the shah's sovereignty and the duties owed by a dependent ruler. A religious writer might consider the status of Muslim communities under non-Muslim rule or the responsibilities of a ruler who could not defend them. A diplomat who had worked with British or Russian representatives might focus on the enforcement of written undertakings and the consequences of having no sufficiently powerful ally. Those perspectives could coexist without constituting one shared national program. They also reflected the unequal availability of education, office, and a written voice.

Fiscal and institutional consequences sharpened the debate. The indemnity demonstrated that a foreign power could compel rapid mobilization of resources beyond what the Qajar treasury ordinarily collected. Russian consular and commercial privileges raised questions about the reach of courts, customs officers, and ministers inside Iran itself. These were practical problems of government, not only injuries to prestige. Discussions of military organization, accounting, diplomatic instruction, and technical education therefore acquired greater urgency in the circle around Abbas Mirza, even though reform experiments had already begun before the treaties. Chapter 73 examines those programs in detail; here the point is that the settlements altered the cost of failing to pursue effective institutions.

Section 71.18

Russia, Britain, and the Changing Balance of Diplomatic Power

The treaties changed the terms on which Iran dealt with both Russia and other powers. For Russia, Turkmenchay produced leverage that did not depend on continuous warfare: commercial privileges, consuls in Iranian towns, the indemnity and its schedule, the recognition of the heir, and the demonstrated ability to occupy Azerbaijan province. These instruments gave Russian diplomats routine ways to press the Qajar court on matters of trade, protection of subjects, and frontier management. The crisis of 1829 shows both the leverage and its limits: the Russian response to the attack on its mission was restrained, in part because of its simultaneous war with the Ottoman Empire.

Britain’s position was more ambiguous. It had mediated at Gulistan, concluded the 1814 treaty with conditional obligations, and then declined in 1826 to treat the war as one for which its subsidy commitments applied, a stance that disappointed the Qajar court. After 1828, British diplomats sought to maintain influence in Tehran, to reach new arrangements on the earlier obligations, and to compete with Russian commercial privileges, while balancing their interest in good relations with Russia against concern for the defense of India. The settlement of those matters and the wider rivalry are the subject of Chapter 72, which treats Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, and the broader Anglo-Russian contest.

For the Qajar court, the lesson was that survival required balancing among powers that were more capable and had their own priorities. The treaties had made Iranian foreign policy more constrained, particularly in the north, while also giving the court a continuing interest in British support as a counterweight. This dynamic, in which Iranian rulers sought to preserve autonomy by playing foreign powers against each other without any being able to guarantee outcomes, would continue through the nineteenth century.

Diplomatic leverage could function through an ordinary complaint as well as through an army. Once Russian consuls and agents gained defined privileges, a dispute concerning a merchant's goods or a legal proceeding could be elevated to correspondence between governments. The cumulative effect depended on the capacity of the Russian state to follow up complaints and on the willingness of Qajar officials to resist or accommodate them. The treaties did not eliminate Iranian sovereignty over the rest of the country, but they created selected domains in which Iranian decisions were conditioned by special obligations toward a powerful neighbor. This is a more precise account than saying simply that Iran became a colony or, at the other extreme, that no political change occurred outside the ceded districts.

Britain's position exposed another weakness of reliance on external guarantees. British mediation at Gulistan did not give the Qajar court a permanent power to require Russia to revise contested boundaries. The negotiations and conditional commitments of 1814 had to be interpreted in light of British aims in India and changing relations with European powers. Iranian rulers could seek subsidies, technical assistance, or diplomatic support, but these agreements did not make another state responsible for achieving every Iranian objective in the Caucasus. Disappointment at British conduct around the second war was part of a wider lesson about the difference between friendly diplomatic language and enforceable security obligations.

Section 71.19

Historical Memory, National Narratives, and Competing Interpretations of the Caucasus

The treaties have been remembered in different ways. In Iranian historical writing, Gulistan and Turkmenchay are frequently presented as the moment when Iran lost its Caucasian territories and when European powers began to impose unequal terms; the capitulatory provisions of Turkmenchay in particular became a standard reference in later discussions of foreign privilege. In Russian imperial and Soviet historiography, the treaties were more often presented as part of the consolidation of the empire’s southern frontier and, in some accounts, as bringing Christian populations under a protecting power. Georgian historical memory tends to emphasize the earlier annexation of 1801 and Georgia’s own political history, while Armenian historical memory stresses the transfer of Echmiadzin and the Yerevan region, and the migration of Armenians, as a major event in their national history. Azerbaijani writing, especially in the twentieth century, has emphasized the division of Azerbaijani-speaking populations between two empires.

These traditions are not simply competing accounts of one agreed set of facts. They rest on different questions, different source bases, and different political stakes. Several of them were shaped by later nationalism, which looked to the past for evidence of a national community and its losses. Historians have to take care to distinguish what the treaties and contemporary sources show from what later national narratives have read into them. This chapter presents the relevant interpretations without legitimizing present territorial claims on the basis of ethnic or historical continuity alone; the political boundaries of 1813 and 1828 were products of imperial diplomacy and war, and the identities of the populations were, in many respects, still in formation.

Historical memory reflects the questions later communities bring to the past. In Iran, the treaties became reference points in debates over foreign encroachment and the responsibilities of rulers. Russian imperial chroniclers often described expansion as the establishment of order or strategic security, while later Soviet accounts introduced additional categories of social and national development. Armenian historical writing could emphasize Echmiadzin, population movements, and the changing prospects of religious and cultural institutions. Georgian perspectives paid particular attention to the destruction of the earlier kingdom and Russia's annexation, which preceded Gulistan. None of these frameworks is interchangeable with the diplomatic categories used by the signatories.

The phrase partition of Azerbaijan requires especially careful historical handling. It can describe a later perception that Turkic-speaking populations on opposite sides of an imperial border were separated by the settlements. Yet to treat the phrase as the vocabulary of the treaty negotiators would impose a twentieth-century national framework on an earlier constellation of khanates, Iranian provinces, religious communities, and imperial claims. The treaties did not partition a modern nation-state called Azerbaijan. That observation neither negates the existence of historical linguistic and cultural connections nor determines how later people may understand their identity. It keeps distinct three issues: the legal division between empires, the subsequent development of national movements, and present political claims.

Historical Debate

Contemporary Political Division versus Later National Narratives: The “Partition of Azerbaijan”

The question: Does the phrase “partition of Azerbaijan,” used in some twentieth-century and later writing for the effect of the treaties, explain the events of the early nineteenth century, or does it project later national categories onto a different political world?

Interpretation A, the treaties divided an Azerbaijani homeland: On this view, the Aras frontier split a population sharing language, religion, and culture, creating a division of “northern” and “southern” Azerbaijan whose consequences shaped the later history of both sides.
Interpretation B, the phrase is retrospective: On this view, the name Azerbaijan in the early nineteenth century referred primarily to the Qajar province south of the Aras, while the territories annexed by Russia were known by the names of their khanates and districts. The use of “Azerbaijan” for the northern territory, and the idea of a single divided nation, belong to later developments, including the adoption of the name by a state proclaimed in 1918 in the former Russian territories.

Evidence: The wording of the treaties, which name khanates and districts and not a unified Azerbaijan; Qajar administrative usage of the province name; Russian administrative terminology; and the later development of national movements and state names. Evidence of continued cross-border social ties supports the claim of a divided population, while evidence on contemporary self-identification complicates the claim of a pre-existing national unit.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation rather than events. Both sides can accept that a frontier divided communities with shared language and ties; they differ on whether “partition of a nation” fairly describes what contemporaries understood to be happening.

Why it matters: The terminology can support or undermine later territorial and political claims. Chapters 1 and 85 show how regional names and borders were used in modern politics; this debate illustrates why careful historical terminology matters.

Student task: Identify what evidence would show how people in the 1820s referred to themselves and their region, and consider how historians might look for it.

Section 71.20

Historical Synthesis: From Dynastic Claims to an Imperial Frontier

Comparing the two treaties clarifies their distinct roles. Gulistan recognized Russian sovereignty over Georgia and the northern and eastern khanates, established Russian naval exclusivity on the Caspian, pledged recognition of the heir, and left the boundary imprecise. Turkmenchay added the cession of Yerevan and Nakhchivan, a more detailed frontier with provision for demarcation, a heavy indemnity secured by occupation, and, through its commercial convention, capitulatory privileges and tariff limits. Where Gulistan was a settlement of territorial claims whose ambiguities invited dispute, Turkmenchay created a more comprehensive framework of Russian predominance in the eastern Caucasus and of leverage over Qajar policy.

The consequences followed different rhythms. Legal change was quick, since signatures and ratifications fixed new claims within days or months. Administrative change took years: khanates were abolished at different times, provincial government was introduced unevenly, and surveys and customs systems developed gradually. Social change took longest and was least uniform: Armenian households moved in large numbers over a short time, Muslim populations experienced annexation through changed taxes, law, and religious oversight, and pastoral and commercial life adapted over decades. For the Qajar state, the treaties changed the diplomatic balance and the fiscal position and shaped debates about reform and sovereignty.

The final governing question asked how two diplomatic agreements following military defeat could transform the map of the Caucasus, the authority of states, the lives of frontier communities, and Iranian ideas about sovereignty. The answer developed here is that the treaties were less single acts than beginnings: they set legal terms within which institutions, populations, and ideas then changed. Understanding them requires reading the documents closely, comparing their versions, and attending to the people the documents rarely mention.

Seen together, the two agreements show a movement from limited recognition of existing conquest toward a denser structure of imperial advantage. Gulistan named territories and attempted to stabilize the border, restricted Iranian naval capacity on the Caspian, dealt with succession, and already included commercial tariff provisions. Turkmenchay added Yerevan and Nakhchivan, a more elaborate boundary, an enforceable financial demand, and a commercial convention that strengthened consular and judicial privileges. The change was not simply the number of square kilometers lost. It was the creation of mechanisms through which the stronger empire could demand money, protect its subjects, oversee commerce, and influence the other monarchy's choices beyond the formal border.

A comparison of timelines shows why the treaties must be studied as historical processes. Some districts were administered by Russian officers before their status was formally recognized. Hereditary khanates disappeared on different schedules, and the creation of provincial surveys and offices did not immediately replace every local practice. The new rules on property and migration applied to people facing unequal risks and different opportunities. Armenian migrants, Muslim landowners, pastoralists, merchants, and religious institutions adjusted through decisions that were often constrained by warfare or official policy. To describe the treaties without these consequences would mistake a government's legal claim for the full history of a territory.

The settlements also provide a method for studying other unequal agreements. Students should begin with the operative article, identify the parties and the relevant instrument, and then ask which agencies could implement it. They should check whether later concessions changed an original obligation, as with the indemnity, and whether separate people faced different deadlines, as with the property clauses. They should compare documentary evidence with records of local government and population movement, keeping estimates tied to particular sources. Finally, they should distinguish contemporary political language from the national memories that later developed around the same events. This method gives the chapter's governing question its answer: the treaties redrew authority quickly, but they transformed political life through the much slower operations of law, administration, finance, and everyday adaptation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined what the Russo-Persian peace settlements actually did. It distinguished four processes: military conquest, legal recognition of sovereignty, institutional transformation, and the longer human and political consequences. Each unfolded on its own timeline, and treating them separately is the central analytical move of the chapter.

The Treaty of Gulistan, signed on 12/24 October 1813 after Russian victories and with British mediation, obliged Iran to recognize Russian sovereignty over Georgia and a list of khanates and districts including Karabakh, Ganja, Shaki, Shirvan, Quba, Derbent, Baku, Dagestan, and the Russian-held part of Talysh. Yerevan and Nakhchivan were not included. The treaty also gave Russia exclusive rights to maintain warships on the Caspian, while allowing both countries’ merchant vessels to navigate, and it addressed recognition of the Qajar heir.

Gulistan’s frontier clauses, based on possession and on later specification, were ambiguous, and the treaty did not produce an agreed, surveyed boundary. Between 1814 and 1826 Russian authorities replaced or reorganized several khanates, Iranian envoys sought revision through missions to St. Petersburg and appeals to Britain, and disputes over Talysh and Gokcha deepened. Historians explain the settlement’s failure through some combination of ambiguous drafting, Russian expansion, and Qajar non-acceptance, interpretations that are not mutually exclusive.

After the war of 1826–1828, the Treaty of Turkmenchay of 10/22 February 1828 ceded Yerevan and Nakhchivan, defined a more detailed frontier that relied heavily on the Aras but did not follow it throughout, and provided for demarcation. It imposed an indemnity of ten kurur of tumans, equivalent to twenty million silver rubles, to be paid in installments under security arrangements involving Russian military presence, and with the final scheduled installment reduced from two to one kurur after the 1829 crisis.

A separate commercial convention, to be read together with the main treaty, elaborated consular and jurisdictional privileges, while continuing a five-percent customs rate already stipulated in Gulistan. These provisions limited Qajar legal and fiscal authority without annexing further territory, and they set a model for later arrangements with other powers.

The treaties’ effects on people were diverse. Provisions on prisoners, amnesty, relocation, and property gave inhabitants a formal right to move but said nothing about the means. Armenian migration to the new Armenian Oblast was large but neither wholly voluntary nor wholly coerced, and many Armenians stayed in Iran. Muslim populations, including Turkic-speaking communities, experienced annexation through new administration, land disputes, and continuing ties across the frontier. Identities were not yet those of modern nations.

Trade, taxation, and daily life changed gradually as customs, documentation, and administrative surveys altered the conditions of movement, and as commercial networks reoriented toward Russian and, on the Qajar side, Ottoman and British connections. Territorial transfer was a long process, not a single event.

For the Qajar court, defeat prompted debates about military and administrative improvement, diplomacy, and the duties of rulers, debates taken up in Chapter 73. Contemporary responses spoke largely in terms of dynasty and religion; later nationalist interpretations framed the treaties as national losses. Diplomatic power shifted toward Russia, while Britain’s role remained limited and ambiguous, as Chapter 72 will show.

The treaties have been remembered differently in Iranian, Russian, Georgian, Armenian, and Azerbaijani traditions. The debate over the phrase “partition of Azerbaijan” illustrates how later national categories can be projected onto an earlier political world, and why careful terminology matters. The chapter’s conclusion is that the treaties were beginnings, setting legal terms within which institutions, populations, and ideas then changed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a diplomatic text can mark a turning point without completing the change it records. Gulistan and Turkmenchay did alter the legal map of the Caucasus, but they also began long processes (administrative replacement of khanates, migration, new trade patterns, and new Iranian debates about sovereignty) that no signature could finish.

The treaties also reveal a recurring pattern in nineteenth-century Iranian history: pressure from stronger powers exerted through law, finance, and commerce as much as through force. Indemnities, consular jurisdiction, and tariff limits foreshadow the concessions, loans, and privileges examined in Chapter 75, and the diplomatic balancing described here anticipates the rivalry of Chapter 72.

For the Caucasus, the frontier fixed in 1828 shaped the lives of Armenian, Muslim, Georgian, Kurdish, and other communities in ways that outlasted the empires that drew it. The competing memories of these events, and the dispute over terms like “partition of Azerbaijan,” show how later nationalism reshapes earlier history, and why historians must keep asking who is speaking, in what language, and for what purpose.

Chapter 72 turns to the wider imperial competition between Russia and Britain, and Chapter 73 to the reform efforts that Iranian leaders pursued in the shadow of these defeats.
