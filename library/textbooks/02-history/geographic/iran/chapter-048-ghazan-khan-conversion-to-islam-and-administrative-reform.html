Chapter Opening

In 1295 a Mongol prince who had been raised among Buddhist patrons declared himself a Muslim while contesting the throne of the Ilkhanate. Over the next nine years that prince, Ghazan, changed how the dynasty justified itself, how it taxed, how it paid its soldiers, and how it minted its money, without ever ceasing to be a Chinggisid king.

In June 1295, during a struggle for the throne against a rival Ilkhan named Baydu, Ghazan, the son of the former Ilkhan Arghun and governor of the eastern provinces, publicly became a Muslim and took the name Mahmud. The act was not the first conversion of an Ilkhan. Ahmad Tegüder had embraced Islam and reigned from 1282 to 1284, and his reign had ended in defeat and death. What distinguishes Ghazan is that his conversion became attached to a durable program: a way of presenting Mongol kingship in Islamic terms, and a series of administrative measures intended to make revenue collection, military provisioning, and local government more predictable. This chapter studies that combination.

The chapter therefore addresses two questions at once. The first concerns legitimacy. How did an Ilkhanate whose rulers descended from Chinggis Khan, and whose court had long included Buddhist, Christian, and shamanic specialists alongside Muslim ones, present itself once its king was a Muslim? The second concerns government. Chapter 47 showed how Mongol rule became an Iranian-based dynastic state, with its courts, armies, tax offices, and relations with other Mongol khanates. Ghazan inherited that state in a condition his own officials described as disorderly. The chapter asks what he and his ministers ordered, how those orders were meant to operate, and what the surviving evidence allows historians to conclude about their results.

The evidence for these questions is unusually rich but unusually interested. Much of the detailed account of Ghazan's decrees survives in the writings of Rashid al-Din Fadl Allah, a physician turned statesman who helped carry the reforms out and who later wrote the history of the dynasty. Rashid is indispensable, and he is also a participant defending a program. Persian court historians such as Wassaf wrote in an ornate, laudatory idiom. Mamluk chroniclers and scholars wrote from the other side of a long war. Coins, which Ghazan's mints produced in quantity, are contemporary material evidence of what the dynasty chose to proclaim, though they cannot prove what the proclamations accomplished. Throughout the chapter these kinds of evidence are set against one another.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. One treats Ghazan's conversion as a clean break after which Mongols became Iranian Muslims. In fact, Chinggisid legitimacy, Mongol military organization, and royal prerogative persisted; the king remained a Mongol sovereign who happened to be Muslim, and he fought a Muslim Mamluk sultanate over Syria for years afterward. The other simplification treats the reforms as a triumph because the minister who recorded them said so. A decree announces an intention. Whether households in a given district paid less, paid more regularly, or paid the same as before is a separate question, and often one the sources cannot answer.

The chapter is organized into twenty numbered sections in five divisions. Division I follows Ghazan from his provincial command in Khurasan through the contest of 1295, his conversion, and the elimination of rivals. Division II examines Islam as royal ideology, the treatment of non-Muslim communities, the court's relations with Sunni, Shiʿi, and Sufi figures, and the persistence of Chinggisid tradition. Division III, the institutional center of the chapter, explains the fiscal crisis and the measures taken against it. Division IV covers coinage, land, military provisioning, and communications. Division V treats the Mamluk wars, Ghazan's patronage at Tabriz, and an assessment of how far the reforms worked.

Boundaries with neighboring chapters matter. Chapter 49 takes up Rashid al-Din's career, his historical writing, and the manuscripts that carried it, and this chapter treats him only as an official and as a source. Chapter 50 evaluates urban, agricultural, and commercial recovery under the Ilkhans, and this chapter reserves outcomes for that discussion and concentrates on policy. The chapter closes with Ghazan's death in May 1304 and the succession of his brother Öljeitü, whose reign, and the later fragmentation of the dynasty treated in Chapter 51, lie beyond its scope.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Ghazan's path from governor of Khurasan to Ilkhan, including his conflicts with Nawruz and Baydu and his conversion in 1295.
  2. Distinguish Ghazan's conversion from that of Ahmad Tegüder, and explain why Ghazan's relationship between Islam and kingship proved more durable.
  3. Explain how Islamic legitimacy and Chinggisid political tradition coexisted in Ghazan's court, including the historical meaning of the term yasa.
  4. Assess the evidence for the suppression of Buddhist institutions and for pressure on Christian and Jewish communities, and explain why policy varied across the reign.
  5. Describe the fiscal disorder of the early 1290s and analyze how Ghazan's tax registration, assessment, and collection measures were intended to address it.
  6. Identify the roles of Saʿd al-Din Savaji and Rashid al-Din in the administration, and evaluate the diwan's attempts to discipline officials.
  7. Analyze the monetary reform of 1297–1298 in relation to Abaqa's earlier standardization and the evidence provided by coins.
  8. Compare Ghazan's military revenue assignments with earlier iqtaʿ practice and assess their implications for cultivators.
  9. Interpret the Mamluk wars of 1299–1303 as evidence that conversion did not end Ilkhanid imperial ambition.
  10. Evaluate Rashid al-Din's testimony as a source and distinguish announced policy from demonstrated outcome.
  11. Connect the reforms to the chapters that follow on Rashid al-Din, economic recovery, and the later fragmentation of the Ilkhanate.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Ghazan's conversion and his reforms together reshape Mongol rule in Iran, and which institutions and traditions did they leave largely intact?
  2. Why might a conversion made in the middle of a succession struggle be both politically useful and sincerely held, and what kinds of evidence could distinguish these possibilities?
  3. In what ways did Ghazan's position as a Chinggisid king limit what Islam could mean for his government?
  4. Why did the experience of the religious changes after 1295 differ among Buddhists, Christians, Jews, and Muslims, and why did enforcement change over time?
  5. What problems in taxation, military provisioning, and administration did Ghazan's court identify, and what mechanisms did it propose to solve them?
  6. How far can historians trust the account of the reforms given by the minister who helped implement them?
  7. What can coins establish about a reform, and what can they not establish?
  8. Why did two Muslim-ruled states continue to fight over Syria after the Ilkhan became a Muslim?
  9. How might a decree and the lived experience of a village taxpayer have differed?

Historical Context

Hülegü's conquests in the 1250s and the founding of the Ilkhanate, examined in Chapters 45 through 47, created a Mongol dynasty ruling Iran, Iraq, and much of the Caucasus and eastern Anatolia as a branch of the wider Chinggisid enterprise. The Ilkhans acknowledged, at least in form, the seniority of the Great Khan in East Asia, and they were drawn into recurring conflict with the Mongol rulers of the steppe to the north, the Golden Horde, and of Central Asia, as well as with the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt and Syria. Their own capital region lay in Azerbaijan, with Tabriz as the commercial and political hub, while Khurasan in the northeast was entrusted to princes and commanders who guarded the frontier toward Central Asia.

The religious setting of the court was plural. Early Ilkhans such as Hülegü and Abaqa favored Buddhist and Christian counselors, and Hülegü's principal wife, Doquz Khatun, was a Christian patron. Arghun, Ghazan's father, who ruled from 1284 to 1291, is associated with Buddhist patronage. Muslim scholars and administrators nevertheless staffed much of the civil bureaucracy, as they had under Iranian and Seljuk predecessors, and the great majority of the settled population was Muslim, with substantial Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian minorities. The Ilkhans were thus a non-Muslim, then partly Muslim, military aristocracy ruling a largely Muslim agrarian and urban society through a largely Muslim civil administration.

The fiscal structure was layered. Revenue came from land taxation, from levies on commerce, and from a head-tax-like assessment called qopchur, together with a variety of extraordinary demands for provisions, fodder, and labor. Mongol princes, commanders, and the royal household held assignments and private estates, and relay stations of the yam postal system depended on local supply. After the 1280s, competing court factions, expensive wars, and the use of tax farming and ad hoc requisitions strained this structure. The failed paper-money experiment of 1294 under Gaykhatu, discussed in Section 48.9, was one symptom.

The succession was another source of instability. Mongol practice did not prescribe primogeniture, and from 1282 onward each transition involved rival princes and commanders. Ahmad Tegüder was overthrown by Arghun; Arghun's death was followed by Gaykhatu's accession in 1291, and Gaykhatu's by Baydu's seizure of power in 1295. Ghazan's career must be read within this pattern. His supporters, including powerful commanders in Khurasan, saw an opportunity in each crisis, and he needed their loyalty to win. The reforms he later enacted cannot be separated from the need to keep that coalition together and to fund an army capable of resisting rivals and fighting abroad.

Section 48.1

Ghazan before the Throne: Khurasan, Mongol Kingship, and the Crisis of Succession, 1284–1295

Ghazan was born in 1271 into the Ilkhanid royal house as the son of Arghun and the grandson of Abaqa. He grew up in a court shaped by the religious plurality described above. His father was a patron of Buddhist clergy, and Ghazan's own early environment is generally described in the sources as Buddhist. These descriptions come largely from Muslim authors writing after his conversion, who had reasons to emphasize the contrast between his youth and his later faith. They are plausible, because Buddhist patronage at the Ilkhanid court is documented from other evidence, but the details of his childhood religious instruction cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

When Arghun took the throne in 1284, Ghazan was placed over the eastern provinces, centered on Khurasan and Mazandaran. The assignment was strategic. Khurasan bordered the Chaghadayid and other Mongol domains of Central Asia, and its commanders controlled some of the dynasty's most important military resources. Ghazan was a boy when the assignment began, and in practice authority rested with senior Mongol commanders and their agents. Over the years his role grew. By the early 1290s he was a seasoned provincial ruler dealing with frontier defense, military discipline, provincial finances, and the loyalties of regional commanders.

His chief problem in the region was Nawruz, a son of the powerful commander Arghun Aqa and a prominent figure among the military elite of Khurasan. In 1289 Nawruz rebelled against Arghun's government. The conflict that followed, which drew in Ghazan as the nominal head of the province, lasted for several years and involved shifting alliances, defeats, flight, and negotiation. The sources agree that Nawruz was a formidable commander, that his relationship with the central government and with Ghazan fluctuated, and that his eventual alignment with Ghazan in the mid-1290s proved decisive. Section 48.2 returns to this reconciliation.

Ghazan's provincial experience shaped his later outlook in ways historians can infer but not fully document. Governing Khurasan taught him how a Mongol ruler depended on the military loyalties of commanders and on the productivity of the settled population that supported them. The region had suffered extensively during the Mongol conquest, and its agriculture, towns, and revenues depended on irrigation, security, and the restraint of armed men. Rashid al-Din, writing later, attributed to Ghazan a keen interest in the condition of peasants and a determination to restrain abuses. These claims belong to the literature of royal praise and must be read critically. What is more securely documented is that Ghazan had longstanding experience of provincial government before he ruled the whole dynasty.

The conditions of the central government in these years deserve emphasis. Arghun's reign saw intense factional politics, the rise and fall of ministers, and heavy financial pressure. After Arghun's death in 1291, Gaykhatu's government, discussed in Section 48.9, faced mounting difficulties. Ghazan's province was geographically distant from Tabriz and largely independent in practice, and he did not intervene directly in these struggles until the crisis of 1295. When he did, he came as a prince with a large army, an established base, and experience that his rivals lacked.

A provincial command was not a private kingdom. The eastern administration operated through layered claims to authority: an Ilkhan at the center, a royal prince in the frontier provinces, commanders who could move armed followers, and Iranian fiscal officers who recorded obligations. A commander's influence might therefore rest on his control of troops rather than on a formal administrative title. Ghazan's later efforts to discipline officials are better understood against this political setting than as the ideas of a ruler standing above faction. He needed a workable compact between soldiers and revenue-producing communities because neither component could easily be replaced.

Khurasan also presented a problem of scale. Its towns and irrigated agricultural districts did not always obey the same political timetable as its mobile military camps. Moving a force across the eastern marches required animals, pasture, provisions, and understandings with local intermediaries; keeping a tax office functioning required records, reliable agents, and a measure of security. That contrast helps explain why Ghazan's mature program addressed both army provisioning and the protection of cultivators. It does not establish that he planned his reforms in the 1280s. Rather, it identifies problems he had encountered before holding the throne and distinguishes demonstrable experience from later biographical claims of extraordinary foresight.

Section 48.2

Baydu, Nawruz, and the Struggle for the Ilkhanate in 1295

By 1295 Gaykhatu's government had lost much of its authority. The paper-money experiment of 1294 had caused commercial disruption and widespread resentment, and court factions were divided over responsibility. In the spring of 1295, Baydu, a Mongol prince of the royal house and a grandson of Hülegü, moved against Gaykhatu and seized power. Gaykhatu was killed. Baydu's claim rested on his descent and on the support of commanders who had turned against the previous regime, but his position was weak from the start. He faced a rival with more troops and a more cohesive following, and the sources suggest that many commanders were unwilling to commit to him.

Ghazan's decision to challenge Baydu was shaped by a combination of dynastic claim and military opportunity. As Arghun's son, he had a strong Chinggisid and Ilkhanid pedigree. His army in the east was the largest and best organized force in the Ilkhanate that was not under Baydu's control. But he needed the backing of the commanders of Khurasan, and above all that of Nawruz, whose influence over the eastern military elite was great. The reconciliation between the two men, which the sources place in this period, was therefore a pivot. Nawruz had been Ghazan's adversary for years. By aligning himself with Ghazan in 1294–1295, he brought troops and prestige to the cause and positioned himself as a principal beneficiary of any victory.

The alliance was not purely personal. Nawruz and Ghazan shared practical interests in defeating Baydu and in establishing a government that could pay and discipline its soldiers. Nawruz's role in what followed, including his involvement in Ghazan's conversion, shows that political calculation and religious change were intertwined. Sources written after Ghazan's victory present these events as a coherent narrative of divinely favored success. Historians, aware that the outcome was not predetermined, stress the contingency of the period: Baydu might have attracted more support, Nawruz might have remained hostile, and Ghazan's army might have been defeated.

Baydu himself has a particular place in the sources. Muslim authors describe him as sympathetic to Christians, and they contrast him with Ghazan as a ruler whose inclinations favored the old order. Such descriptions may reflect a real difference, because Baydu is reported to have maintained links with Christian circles, but they also serve a polemical purpose. A victory that could be presented as a triumph of Islam over a pro-Christian rival was valuable to the winners' account. The narrative should be treated as an interpretation constructed after the fact rather than as a neutral description of the combatants' religious commitments.

The succession crisis must be followed at several levels at once. Baydu's claim did not arise merely because he occupied the throne; his supporters included commanders with resources and grievances of their own. Ghazan, meanwhile, could not assume that the contingents attached to his provincial office would remain united through a campaign. Reconciliation with Nawruz made additional military networks available, but it also strengthened a partner whose independence had previously threatened Ghazan. This was a coalition made under pressure, not evidence that earlier disputes had disappeared. Its instability became apparent once the common rival was removed.

Religion mattered within this contest without explaining every choice of allegiance. By the 1290s substantial numbers of Mongol officers had become Muslims, and a claimant who identified with their faith could reach them more effectively. Yet Muslim officers did not necessarily support the same prince, and dynastic calculation, personal loyalty, access to appointments, and opportunities for reward remained decisive. Reading conversion as one instrument of political coordination preserves its importance while resisting a narrative in which a religious declaration mechanically produces victory. The decisive question is how Ghazan's coalition translated a claim into military cooperation during the months before Baydu's defeat.

Section 48.3

Ghazan's Conversion to Islam in June 1295

Ghazan's conversion took place in June 1295, while he was campaigning against Baydu. Encyclopaedia Iranica dates the initial profession of faith to 2 Shaʿban 694 AH (17 June 1295), while a historical tradition giving 4 Shaʿban is frequently converted as 19 June. Reports of conversion and the subsequent public proclamation of a Muslim rulership need not refer to a single ceremony. This chapter therefore treats June 1295, during the struggle for power, as secure and avoids claiming universal agreement on a precise day. The sources describe a ceremony in which Ghazan recited the Muslim profession of faith and adopted the name Mahmud. They associate the Sufi shaykh Sadr al-Din Ibrahim Hamuya with the occasion, and they attribute a significant role to Nawruz, who is said to have encouraged the step.

The act was followed by the conversion of a number of Ghazan's military followers. Rashid al-Din reports that large numbers of commanders and soldiers accepted Islam alongside their ruler, but exact numbers cannot be trusted, and the conversion of the military elite as a whole was probably a longer, uneven process. It is important to distinguish several layers: the personal conversion of the prince; the adoption of Islam by commanders in his immediate entourage; the conversion of broader military and tribal contingents over subsequent years; and the existing religious identities of the settled population, most of whom were already Muslim. The conversion of the royal house did not Islamize the population, because the population was already largely Muslim. It changed the religious character of the ruling class and the dynasty's public claims.

The motivations behind the conversion have long been debated, and the Historical Debate below treats the question directly. Several considerations are generally acknowledged. First, a Muslim claimant to the throne could appeal more effectively to the administrators, scholars, and townspeople who made up the settled population over which the Ilkhans ruled. Second, a conversion would distinguish Ghazan from Baydu and win the support of Muslim commanders. Third, Nawruz, who had Muslim connections, pressed for the step. Fourth, there is no reason to rule out personal conviction: sources describe Ghazan as interested in religious learning, and the weight of the evidence is that a ruler may be both strategically motivated and genuinely devout.

What the evidence shows most clearly is the timing. Ghazan converted before he had won. The act was therefore a part of his bid for the throne, offered at a moment when it could shape the allegiance of commanders and clients still deciding whom to support. It was not a reward for victory or a retrospective change of heart. This timing also explains why the conversion was accompanied by pressure on other communities, discussed in Section 48.6. A new Muslim king whose military supporters included zealous converts, and who had been brought to power in part by a strongly Muslim commander, was in a position where measures against Buddhist temples, churches, and synagogues carried political meaning.

The exact dating of the ceremony illustrates a more general issue in medieval chronology. Different chroniclers worked with Islamic lunar dates, later calculations, and narrative sequences rather than a single modern chronological register. A widely used scholarly reconstruction gives 2 Shaʿban 694 AH, corresponding to 17 June 1295, whereas another historical calculation places a profession of faith on 4 Shaʿban, often rendered as 19 June. Accounts also distinguish, with varying precision, the initial profession of faith from later public ceremonies accompanying accession. The secure historical conclusion is conversion during the June contest, before the November enthronement; claims to a single universally attested day go beyond the surviving agreement.

The setting of the ceremony was politically meaningful. It took place in the company of religious and military figures whose support helped turn a personal profession into a publicly legible act of rulership. That does not allow historians to determine the inward sincerity of the convert, a question inaccessible to direct evidence. It does allow them to ask what contemporaries could observe: the new name Mahmud, the participation of Muslim commanders, the use of Islamic authority in royal communication, and later policies presented as expressions of the new faith. A political explanation and a religious explanation need not be mutually exclusive.

Section 48.4

Victory, Accession, and the Elimination of Political Rivals, 1295–1297

Ghazan's forces defeated Baydu in the autumn of 1295, and Baydu was captured and executed. Ghazan then established his government at Tabriz and was formally enthroned in the closing months of the year. The victory removed the principal rival but did not secure the dynasty. A cluster of problems confronted the new Ilkhan: the loyalty of Mongol princes and commanders who had served earlier rulers, the demands of the soldiers who had brought him to power, a depleted treasury, and the volatile alliance with Nawruz.

Nawruz's position was central. As the leading commander of the east and the architect of much of Ghazan's success, he held enormous influence in the first years of the reign. He is associated with the harshest religious measures discussed in Section 48.6, and he was a dominant figure in appointments and military matters. This concentration of power was both useful and dangerous for the king. Rival commanders resented Nawruz, and Ghazan's own authority was constrained by his dependence on him. The tensions came to a head in 1297, when Nawruz was accused of disloyalty and of contacts with the Mamluks. Ghazan had him arrested and executed, and members of his family and circle were also killed.

The fall of Nawruz illustrates the instability of court politics even after the religious realignment. It would be a mistake to treat conversion as a source of cohesion. A Muslim Ilkhan still faced a Mongol military aristocracy divided by personal rivalries, regional power bases, and competing claims to royal favor. Some princes opposed Ghazan directly, others conspired, and a number of leading figures were purged during these years. The sources, written by the victors, depict the executed as traitors, which demands caution about both the charges and the evidence. What the pattern shows is that Ghazan established his authority through the elimination of rivals, not through persuasion alone.

The consolidation also reshaped the civil administration. Ministers associated with the previous regime, including the officials who had advised Gaykhatu on paper currency, fell from favor, and several were executed. Ghazan's own administration took shape from 1298, with Saʿd al-Din Savaji and Rashid al-Din assuming leading roles, a development discussed in Section 48.11. The reforms of Divisions III and IV were therefore enacted by a government that had been rebuilt in the course of political purges. This helps explain both the energy of the reform program and the tensions within it: the new ministers served a king who had recently demonstrated how he treated rivals, and they operated in an atmosphere in which accusations of corruption or disloyalty could be fatal.

After the campaign, winning an accession was different from securing the realm. The central court had to negotiate relationships among royal relatives, regional governors, and commanders who had played unequal roles in the victory. A ruler could reward one group with office or revenue only by disappointing another. Ghazan's early purges belong to this problem of coalition management, although accusations against particular men cannot be accepted at face value from court accounts written under the victors. The power of Nawruz, initially indispensable, increasingly conflicted with the independence Ghazan sought as ruler. His execution reveals how quickly the political value of an ally could become a threat.

Consolidation also changed who could authoritatively describe the state. Replacing ministers and their networks brought new officials into possession of documents, seals, tax registers, and access to the sovereign. Those changes shaped not only policy but also the records historians use: reform narratives were produced by officials whose advancement followed earlier dismissals. A historian should therefore separate three layers of testimony: the stated charge against a disgraced official, the political fact of his removal, and the later moral lesson drawn from it. Only the second layer is ordinarily secure without corroboration.

Historical Debate

Was Ghazan's Conversion Primarily Religious, Political, or an Inseparable Combination of Both?

The question: Why did Ghazan convert to Islam in 1295? Historians examine whether the act should be understood as an expression of religious conviction, a calculated strategy in a succession struggle, or a process in which belief and political advantage cannot be separated.

Interpretation A — Political calculation: The timing, in the middle of a war for the throne, and the prominent role of Nawruz and Muslim commanders suggest that the conversion served to secure military support and to appeal to the Muslim majority of the Ilkhanate's subjects.
Interpretation B — Religious conviction: Court sources describe Ghazan as interested in religion and as committed to Islam throughout his life, and his subsequent patronage and sustained religious policy suggest more than opportunism.
Interpretation C — Inseparability: Many historians argue that medieval rulers did not regard faith and political advantage as separable categories. A prince could sincerely believe that Islam offered both truth and legitimacy, and his advisers could encourage him for reasons that included both.

Evidence: The principal sources are Persian and Arabic court histories written after the event, coins and inscriptions showing how the dynasty presented itself, and the pattern of Ghazan's later conduct. None records the king's inner thoughts directly.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and the weight to give court narratives. There is little dispute about the date, the role of Nawruz, or the contemporaneous political benefits.

Why it matters: If the conversion was primarily tactical, the later reforms may be seen as expedient responses to a fiscal and military crisis. If it was sincere, the reforms and religious patronage may reflect a coherent ideology of Islamic kingship.

Student task: Identify what additional evidence would help to discriminate among these interpretations, and explain why the inseparability argument does not simply avoid the question.

Division II Islam, Religious Legitimacy, and Mongol Sovereignty Sections 48.5–48.8
Section 48.5

Ghazan as Padishah-i Islam: The New Religious Legitimacy of Ilkhanid Kingship

After 1295 Ghazan was presented publicly as a Muslim ruler, and the court employed Islamic terminology to describe his kingship. Rashid al-Din and other writers refer to him with the title Padishah-i Islam, "king of Islam" or "sovereign of Islam," a formulation that fused the Persian concept of kingship with a religious identity. The language is significant because it shows how the dynasty chose to describe itself to a Muslim audience. A ruler in this idiom was responsible for upholding the faith, defending the community, and ensuring justice among his subjects.

The new legitimacy was expressed in several media. Royal proclamations invoked Islamic themes. Friday sermons were delivered in the Ilkhan's name in the principal mosques, a long-standing marker of sovereignty in Muslim polities. The coinage carried Islamic formulas, as discussed in Section 48.13. Ghazan patronized mosques, colleges, and other religious foundations, and he supported scholars and Sufi figures, which are examined in Section 48.7 and Section 48.18. Together these measures positioned the Ilkhan within the tradition of Muslim kingship that the Seljuks and earlier dynasties had cultivated.

This did not mean ethnic assimilation. Ghazan remained a Mongol king, and the Mongol military and dynastic identity of the court persisted. The new legitimacy was layered upon the old, not substituted for it. Ghazan's decrees continued to invoke the authority of Chinggis Khan, his army retained its Mongol organization, and the dynasty continued to acknowledge, in form, the supremacy of the Chinggisid house. Section 48.8 develops this point. A student should resist the formulation that the Ilkhans "became Persian" or "became Muslim" in a sudden, complete sense. A more accurate formulation is that the dynasty added an Islamic dimension to its legitimacy while maintaining its Chinggisid foundations.

The political advantages were considerable. Muslim scholars and administrators, who had been working for non-Muslim rulers, could now serve a Muslim king without the ambiguity that had marked earlier reigns. The settled population could see the ruler participating in religious observance. And in the struggle with the Mamluks, whose sultans claimed to be protectors of Islam and of the holy places, a Muslim Ilkhan could contest a claim that had previously gone largely unchallenged. The Mamluk response, discussed in Section 48.17, shows that the claim was not simply accepted.

The title 'king of Islam' signaled a reconfiguration of sovereignty rather than conversion of every part of government into a religious institution. Public prayers associated the ruler with the Muslim community, while royal orders still issued through an Ilkhanid court possessing its own hierarchy and military commands. Coins were an especially important medium because they circulated beyond an audience of courtiers. The monarch's name and Islamic formulas proclaimed a claim to lawful authority in a form that merchants, tax collectors, soldiers, and urban households could encounter without attending a royal ceremony. These messages were portable, repeatable, and materially dated.

Legitimation was also contested. Jurists could approve the monarch's profession of faith while disagreeing about the justice of his officials, and Mamluk writers could reject his right to conquer other Muslim lands. Within Iran, Muslim subjects might welcome patronage yet judge the new state by the conduct of a local commander. Thus the language of Muslim kingship created expectations that could expose, rather than conceal, contradictions between public piety and coercive administration. The most useful historical question is not whether Ghazan became fully 'Iranian,' an anachronistic measure, but how Islamic, Persianate, and Chinggisid political vocabularies operated together in concrete institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Padishah-i Islam, Yasa, Sharia, Yarligh

Definition: Padishah-i Islam is a Persian title meaning "king of Islam." Yasa (also yasaq) refers in Mongol usage to the decrees and precedents of Chinggis Khan and, more broadly, to Mongol customary and royal law. Sharia is Islamic law. A yarligh is a Mongol royal edict or decree.

Historical context: After 1295 the Ilkhan's decrees were issued as yarlighs while appealing to Islamic and Chinggisid authority, and the court employed the title Padishah-i Islam in Persian writing. Judges applied sharia in matters of personal status and many civil disputes, while royal and military matters often fell under Mongol custom and decree.

Why it matters: These terms mark the two sources of authority that coexisted at Ghazan's court and show why conversion did not simply replace one legal and political order with another.

Potential misunderstanding: The yasa should not be imagined as a single comprehensive written code that survived intact. Historians generally treat it as a body of decrees, precedents, and customary norms, whose content and application varied. Sharia was likewise not applied uniformly across all spheres of government.

Section 48.6

Buddhists, Christians, Jews, and the Changing Policies of Ghazan's Court

The religious changes after 1295 included coercion. In the period following his accession, measures were taken against the institutions of non-Muslim communities. Buddhist temples were destroyed or converted, Buddhist clergy were forced to convert to Islam or leave, and images were removed. Churches and synagogues in various places were attacked, damaged, or closed, and non-Muslim communities were subject to pressure. Nawruz is identified in several sources as a driving force behind these measures, and they reflect the militant enthusiasm of some of the new Muslim commanders as well as the royal court's decisions.

The effects on Buddhist communities were particularly severe. Buddhist monks and temples had enjoyed patronage under earlier Ilkhans, and some had flourished in Tabriz and elsewhere. After 1295 the institutional presence of Buddhism in Iran was largely extinguished as a public religion, though the sources do not allow a full reconstruction of what became of individual communities. Modern scholarship has examined how Buddhist intellectual networks, which linked Iran to Central and East Asia, were disrupted by the change. Ghazan's own Buddhist upbringing adds an irony that sources comment on: the ruler who had been reared among Buddhists presided over the suppression of their institutions.

Christian and Jewish communities experienced the period differently. Both were protected communities, dhimmis in Islamic legal terms, and both had been favored in varying degrees by earlier Ilkhans. Attacks on churches and synagogues occurred, and some communities suffered harassment and property loss. The sources suggest that Ghazan later restrained the worst excesses and restored a measure of order, though they disagree about the extent and timing. It would be inaccurate to describe the reign as one of uniform persecution, and equally inaccurate to describe it as tolerant. Policy varied by time, place, and the influence of individual officials.

One reason for this variation was the political context. The initial wave of measures coincided with Nawruz's ascendancy. Yet the court showed signs of moderating measures toward some Christian communities before Nawruz's execution in 1297, including renewed privileges recorded for the East Syriac catholicos in 1296. The later elimination of Nawruz changed factional politics, but it should not be represented as the single date at which royal restraint began; the consequences of anti-Buddhist measures remained profound. The relationship between royal decree and local action also mattered. Mobs, local officials, and military units sometimes acted beyond or without royal authorization, and the sources do not always distinguish between the two.

Historians must also consider who wrote the sources. The principal narrators were Muslim court historians, whose accounts of persecution may reflect either the triumph or the discomfort of their community's rise. Non-Muslim voices survive in Armenian, Syriac, and Latin-language materials, but they reflect their own concerns and limitations. A balanced reconstruction combines these perspectives without assuming that any one captured the full experience.

The chronology of moderation deserves particular attention. Syriac accounts of the East Syriac catholicos Mar Yahballaha III describe an audience with Ghazan and an improvement in the reception of Christian leaders; scholarship places a renewal of privileges and customary tax exemptions in July 1296. This preceded Nawruz's execution in 1297. Consequently, the fall of Nawruz cannot be treated as the sole beginning of royal restraint. Some coercive initiatives had strong support among his followers, but the court's response was already subject to political negotiation while he remained powerful. Later reports of protection for tax-paying non-Muslim subjects during the Syrian campaign suggest that Ghazan also used legal arguments for safeguarding communities, although written proclamations do not establish local compliance.

Distinctions among communities are essential. Buddhist religious foundations associated closely with the old court lost institutional protection on a scale that cannot simply be equated with the experiences of long-established Christian and Jewish populations. Christian institutions could appeal through their own ecclesiastical leadership and diplomatic contacts, but these channels offered imperfect protection against extortion. Regional difference also matters: a decree issued in the capital may be remembered quite differently in an Armenian district, an eastern monastery, or an Iraqi town. The evidence invites a history of changing state policy and variable local enforcement, not a single verdict of universal tolerance or persecution.

Section 48.7

Sunni Islam, Shiʿi Communities, Sufism, and Religious Patronage

Ghazan's Islam was not a single, doctrinally defined position. The court included Sunni jurists and judges, Sufi shaykhs, and Shiʿi scholars and notables, and Ghazan's relations with each were shaped by politics, personal contact, and patronage. His conversion was associated with a Sufi shaykh, and he continued to honor Sufi figures, including through the construction of khanqahs, institutions for Sufi communities, discussed in Section 48.18. Judges and scholars of the Sunni legal schools held offices in the administration of justice, and the court supported mosques and colleges.

Shiʿi communities, which were present in Iran and Iraq in significant numbers, also benefited from the new climate. The sources report royal visits to Shiʿi shrines and interest in Shiʿi figures. But it would be a serious error to attribute to Ghazan the official adherence to Twelver Shiʿism that his brother Öljeitü is associated with later in the dynasty's history. Ghazan's approach was inclusive and patronage-oriented, not confessionally exclusive. The relationship between the court and Shiʿism in the following reign belongs to the account that follows and is not anticipated here.

Patronage worked through the institution of the waqf, the pious endowment. A donor could assign the income of land, shops, or other property to support a mosque, college, hospital, or Sufi lodge, with the terms recorded in a deed. Endowments gave Ghazan a means of rewarding the religious elite and of building the institutions that would transmit his legitimacy. They also tied significant properties and revenues to religious purposes and removed them from ordinary taxation, which created a tension with the fiscal aims of the reforms. The balance between royal generosity and fiscal discipline was a recurring problem.

The relationship between patronage and the religious life of ordinary people was indirect. The great majority of Iran's inhabitants were already Muslim, and their religious practice was shaped by local mosques, shrines, Sufi circles, and the guidance of judges and preachers. The court's patronage reached them through the endowments that supported local institutions, not through any direct program of religious instruction. Historians therefore use caution in inferring changes in popular religiosity from the king's conversion. What changed more clearly was the visibility of Islam in royal ceremony and public institutions.

The Islamic institutions with which the Ilkhan negotiated were not controlled by one clerical hierarchy. Jurists derived standing from knowledge of legal traditions and from networks of students and patrons; Sufi figures could draw authority from devotional lineages and local reputations; shrine communities held their own claims to precedence and support. Patronage allowed the crown to engage these actors without making their interests identical. Grants to one foundation could bring prestige while provoking competition over land, taxes, and appointments in another. The court's religious policy therefore had a distributive as well as a devotional dimension.

There is an important distinction between a ruler's visits to sacred places and a durable confessional settlement. Ghazan's support for several kinds of Muslim institution is compatible with his general participation in Sunni political forms, and it cannot be converted into proof of a later official Twelver doctrine. Nor does the record allow the beliefs of artisans, village households, women, or itinerant teachers to be read from royal proclamations. Their religion was mediated through neighborhood prayer, family practice, charitable foundations, and local scholars. The reign changed the political environment in which these practices occurred, but changes in official patronage should not be confused with comprehensive religious transformation.

Section 48.8

Chinggisid Tradition, the Yasa, and Islamic Law: Continuity within Religious Change

Ghazan's conversion did not abolish Chinggisid political ideology. The dynasty's right to rule rested on descent from Chinggis Khan and on the political order that Chinggis had established, and Ghazan continued to invoke this foundation. His decrees refer to the yasa and to the precedents of Chinggis and his successors, and the court retained Mongol ceremonies, titles, and military customs. A king who was a Muslim could therefore also be a Chinggisid sovereign, and the sources show him moving between the two registers according to circumstance.

The term yasa requires care. It refers to the decrees and customary norms associated with Chinggis Khan, which Mongol rulers cited as a source of authority and precedent. Historians have debated its content. The older picture of a single comprehensive written code, a "Great Yasa" that governed the empire, has been largely replaced by a view of the yasa as a body of rules, orders, and traditions, transmitted partly in writing and partly through memory and custom, and invoked flexibly by different rulers. In the Ilkhanate, the yasa's authority was mainly political and military: it supported the legitimacy of the dynasty, the organization of the army, the conduct of court ceremony, and certain royal prerogatives.

Islamic law operated alongside this framework. Judges applied sharia to personal status, family law, property, and many civil disputes among Muslims, and the court maintained a judicial apparatus staffed by Muslim jurists. The boundary between the spheres was not fixed. Questions about taxation, military discipline, and royal prerogative could be treated under either, and the sources show decrees combining appeals to both. This overlap generated friction, and it is one reason that Mamluk critics doubted the sincerity of Mongol Islam, as Section 48.17 shows.

The military institutions retained their Mongol character. The army was organized in the decimal units and under the commanders of the steppe tradition, though its composition included Turkic and other troops, and its leadership was Mongol and Chinggisid. Ghazan's reforms of military provisioning, discussed in Section 48.15, adapted this system to the realities of Iranian agriculture but did not replace it. The persistence of these structures explains why the dynasty's political behavior, including its campaigns in Syria, remained consistent with earlier Ilkhanid policy despite its change of religion.

Legal pluralism was not merely a dispute between two books of law. Administrative decisions could involve royal orders, customary rights of military groups, the advice of Muslim judges, and the fiscal records of provincial officials. Different institutions judged different questions, and their jurisdictions could overlap. A soldier's claim to supplies, a landholder's claim to revenue, and a family's inheritance dispute did not necessarily reach the same forum. The court's problem was to make these competing authorities sufficiently predictable for the army and taxpayers while preserving the sovereign's freedom to issue commands.

The word yasa itself presents an evidentiary trap. Hostile commentators sometimes treated it as if it were a fully articulated alternative law opposed in every respect to Islamic law; some older modern accounts followed this formulation. Specialists instead emphasize the unevenly documented collection of Chinggisid commands, practices, and traditions invoked under that name. Ghazan's Muslim kingship did not erase Mongol command structures or the symbolic authority of Chinggis Khan. The resulting tension was real, but it should be demonstrated through actual administrative practices rather than by assuming the survival of a complete immutable code.

Division III Fiscal Reform and the Reconstruction of Government Sections 48.9–48.12
Section 48.9

The Fiscal Crisis Ghazan Inherited: Taxation, Court Expenditure, and Administrative Disorder

The government that Ghazan took over faced real financial difficulties, and the evidence for this comes from several directions. Court expenditure, including payments to princes, commanders, and the royal household, was high. Warfare against the Mamluks and in the north required provisioning. Revenue collection depended on officials who were often paid indirectly, through shares of what they collected or through the right to farm taxes, which created incentives to extract more than the state recorded. Extraordinary demands for fodder, supplies, and labor were imposed on local populations by passing officials and soldiers, and these were not always accounted for in the formal assessment.

The paper-money experiment of 1294 illustrates the problem. Under Gaykhatu, ministers introduced a system modeled on the paper currency used in Yuan China, issuing notes to be accepted in place of coin. The attempt followed a shortage of funds. Merchants and townspeople refused to use the notes, markets in Tabriz and other cities were disrupted, and the scheme was abandoned within months. Its failure damaged the government's credibility and fed the discontent that supported Baydu's coup. For the history of reform, the episode shows both that the court recognized a monetary and fiscal crisis and that its remedies could be ill-adapted to local conditions.

The sources on this crisis must be read critically. Rashid al-Din describes the conditions before Ghazan in strongly negative terms, with abuses, ruined peasants, and abandoned villages. This description serves his purpose of justifying the reforms, and it may exaggerate. Other evidence supports the broad picture of fiscal strain, but it cannot easily be quantified, and historians do not have reliable data on aggregate revenue or population. The honest statement is that the available evidence indicates serious administrative problems without allowing precise measurement of their scale.

It is also important to place Ghazan's measures within a longer administrative history. Earlier Ilkhanid officials, including the Juwayni family who administered Iraq and much of Iran under Hülegü and Abaqa, had attempted to regulate collection and restrain abuses, and Mongol rulers before Ghazan had issued decrees against extortion. Ghazan did not invent organized fiscal administration. His reforms are distinguished by their scope, by their sustained attention over several years, and by the support of a determined ministerial team, not by novelty.

The burden of taxation was not determined by an assessment written in a register alone. Households might have to deliver grain, animals, transport services, or cash at different points in the year. Extra demands could arise when commanders traveled or armies gathered, and a village might face overlapping obligations claimed by a treasury agent, a landholder, and a military representative. Even where each claimant possessed some recognized entitlement, their combined demands could overwhelm cultivation. The government's interest in preventing flight and abandonment consequently stemmed from the interaction of fiscal and military institutions, not from a simple dislike of corrupt collectors.

Contemporary accounts of crisis are strongest when they describe a mechanism and weakest when they suggest unmeasured totals. For example, uncertainty over repeated levies could make it risky to retain livestock or invest labor in irrigation, but the resulting scale of abandoned land cannot be calculated from general court complaints. Nor can the disruption of the paper-money experiment be converted into a complete index of the economy. A careful reading compares a proposed remedy with the abuse it purports to cure, then asks whether any independent record establishes the remedy's effect. That method is more informative than assuming that a dramatic account of collapse requires an equally dramatic recovery.

Section 48.10

Tax Registration, Fixed Assessments, and the Regulation of Revenue Collection

The core of the fiscal reform was an effort to make taxation regular. According to Rashid al-Din, the government ordered that the tax obligations of districts be recorded in registers, that assessments be fixed for defined periods, and that collection take place on scheduled dates. The aim was to replace a system in which demands could be repeated, varied, or inflated by officials with one in which both the treasury and the taxpayer knew what was owed and when. Written records were central: a village that could point to a register could in principle resist a demand not recorded in it.

Collection was to be regulated by timing as well as amount. Land taxes in agricultural societies depend on harvest cycles, and demands made before the harvest forced peasants to borrow or sell stock. The reforms reportedly instructed officials to collect in installments tied to the agricultural calendar and to avoid demands outside the schedule. Officials were also warned against collecting the same levy more than once, a practice the sources describe as common. These measures aimed to reduce uncertainty, which pressed on cultivators and likely encouraged flight from the land.

The qopchur received specific attention. The word's origin lies in pastoral levies on livestock, and it is sometimes described simply as a tax on herds. In the Ilkhanate, however, its application changed. In conquered Iranian territories, it operated as a capitation or per-household assessment on the settled population, and it became one of the principal burdens. Ghazan's decrees sought to regulate how it was collected, including by fixing assessments and curbing exactions. Students should not read the term as a livestock tax throughout the period.

Whether these measures succeeded is a separate question, and the sources do not give uniform answers. Regularity could benefit both sides: the treasury gained predictable revenue, and taxpayers gained protection against arbitrary demands. But registers require officials to compile and enforce them, and officials had interests of their own. In some regions, strong local magnates or military holders could ignore or reinterpret the rules. The reforms should be read as an attempt to establish a framework, whose operation depended on local conditions and enforcement that the sources describe only in general terms.

A tax register turned a political promise into an administrative test. In principle, the assessment attached to a district could be compared with the sums demanded by a collector and with receipts issued for payment. Without copies available locally, however, officials could still claim that new obligations were authorized elsewhere. The system therefore depended on standard procedures for recording obligations, inspecting officials, and addressing disputes. The existence of registers also presupposed a functioning chain of communication between the capital and provincial offices: changing a number in one place did not automatically alter demands in another.

The agricultural calendar explains why predictable collection could matter as much as nominal rates. Crops, livestock, and irrigation expenses imposed different schedules on households, and some cultivators needed to reserve seed and food before paying taxes. A demand imposed before harvest might force the sale of animals or a loan on unfavorable terms, even if the amount was formally lawful. The proposed reforms thus aimed at the relationship between fiscal extraction and the capacity to produce the next crop. Yet fixed assessments might also penalize a district after drought, flood, or warfare if the treasury failed to adjust them. Regularity could protect or burden a taxpayer depending on how administrators handled changing conditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Diwan, Qopchur, Tamgha, Iqtaʿ, Yam

Definition: The diwan was the central financial and administrative office. Qopchur was a levy that began as a pastoral tax and became a capitation-type assessment in conquered Iranian regions. Tamgha originally meant a brand or seal and came to denote a tax on commerce. Iqtaʿ was an assignment of revenue, usually to a soldier or official. The yam was the Mongol postal and relay system.

Historical context: These institutions, inherited from earlier Mongol and Islamic administrative practice, formed the machinery that Ghazan's reforms sought to regulate.

Why it matters: Understanding each term clarifies what the reforms actually changed: who collected what, from whom, and under what rules.

Potential misunderstanding: Mongol and Islamic terms did not always correspond to fixed categories. Meanings shifted over time and varied by region, so a term cannot be assumed to carry the same sense in 1300 as in 1250.

Section 48.11

The Diwan, Viziers, Provincial Officials, and Administrative Accountability

The reforms depended on people. In 1298 the government was reorganized around two ministers: Saʿd al-Din Savaji and Rashid al-Din Fadl Allah. Savaji, a seasoned administrator, and Rashid, who had entered court life as a physician and risen through Ghazan's favor, shared the office of vizier in some form and divided responsibilities. Their appointment followed the fall of earlier ministers associated with the previous regimes, including the executed Sadr al-Din Zanjani. The two men were rivals as well as partners, and their competition surfaces in later sources, but during Ghazan's reign they operated as an effective team.

The diwan was the instrument of reform. It maintained the records of assessments, supervised the collection of revenue, and heard complaints against officials. Ghazan's decrees expanded its powers to inspect provincial administration and to punish tax collectors found guilty of illegal exactions. Officials who abused their positions were, according to the sources, subject to penalties that included dismissal, fines, and execution. Written registers, sealed orders, and dispatched inspectors extended the diwan's reach into provinces that had previously operated with considerable autonomy.

Accountability was nevertheless limited by the structure of the court. Officials gained appointment through patronage and competed in networks of favor. Mongol commanders and princes held assignments and estates that were outside the ordinary reach of the diwan, and they could resist or ignore officials' demands. Accusations of corruption were also a political weapon. A minister's enemies could use charges of embezzlement to bring him down, and the line between legitimate prosecution of abuse and factional vengeance was blurred. Their later fates show the precariousness of office, but not under a single ruler: Savaji was executed under Öljeitü in 1312, while Rashid was executed under Abu Saʿid in 1318.

For the historian, the key point is that administrative reform was a political process. Decrees required officials willing to implement them and a king willing to back them against powerful opponents. Ghazan's personal engagement, reported in the sources as active and sometimes severe, was part of the mechanism. A reform that relied on the continuing attention of an energetic ruler faced uncertain prospects once that ruler was gone, a theme developed in Section 48.19.

The administrative sequence is worth specifying. Saʿd al-Din Savaji received the vizierate in 1298, with Rashid al-Din associated with his work; scholarly reconstructions place Rashid's elevation to sahib-i diwan, chief of the financial administration, around 1299–1300. These appointments did not create an impersonal civil service. The ministers relied on personal access to the sovereign and on clerks capable of maintaining registers, drafting orders, and comparing provincial reports. Their capacity to discipline a tax collector depended partly on whether a commander or prince protected him. The centralization attempted by Ghazan was therefore a negotiated extension of royal authority, not a straightforward replacement of private influence by bureaucracy.

The later fates of these ministers require precise chronology. Savaji was executed in 1312 under Öljeitü, Ghazan's immediate successor. Rashid al-Din survived Öljeitü, lost his position amid rival accusations, and was executed in 1318 during Abu Saʿid's reign. Their deaths illustrate the continuing vulnerability of ministerial office but must not be presented as a single purge under the same ruler. They also illustrate why historians are cautious about linking a minister's rise or fall to a transparent measure of competence or guilt. Accounts were often composed amid the same rivalries they recount.

Section 48.12

Justice, Royal Decrees, Land Rights, and the Protection of Productive Communities

Ghazan's decrees addressed official behavior directly. According to Rashid al-Din's account, the king ordered that officials, soldiers, and envoys not seize property, livestock, or provisions from villagers without payment or authority, that landholders be protected in their possession, and that illegal exactions be stopped. Provisions concerned the conduct of tax collectors, the treatment of peasants, and the settlement of disputes over land and water. Public security was also addressed: officials were charged with maintaining safe roads and punishing brigandage.

Judicial procedure formed part of the program. Royal orders instructed judges and officials to hear complaints and to proceed according to written rules, and the court emphasized that complaints against officials would receive attention. The sources describe Ghazan as personally receptive to petitions, and a tradition of royal justice, familiar from earlier Iranian and Islamic political thought, supports the rhetoric of these decrees. The ideology of a king who protects the weak from the strong was a standard theme, and Persian writing on kingship, including the works discussed in Chapters 39 and 42, had long articulated it.

The central historical problem is the gap between decree and practice. A decree states an objective. It does not demonstrate that ordinary households obtained protection. Surviving evidence of actual enforcement is limited, and what exists comes largely from the same court sources that report the decrees. Petitions, local judicial records, and complaints from villagers, which would permit direct assessment, are scarce for this period. Historians therefore treat the decrees as evidence of the regime's declared priorities and of the abuses it acknowledged, and they draw cautious inferences about outcomes.

One consistent inference is that the court recognized that the productivity of the countryside depended on restraint. The Mongol state, whatever its origins, now ruled a settled population from which it drew revenue, and its leaders understood that peasants who fled or whose fields went unplanted yielded nothing. The decrees reflect this recognition. They also reflect the political value of presenting the king as a just ruler to the Muslim subjects whose acceptance he sought.

Royal justice had both an ethical vocabulary and a fiscal purpose. In Persianate political literature a just monarch was expected to shield cultivators from predation so that the countryside could sustain the army and court. That expectation was not identical to a modern right against the state. The sovereign's protection might be extended because villagers were productive taxpayers, and the state still claimed the authority to assign their revenues to others. It is therefore possible for a decree to announce protection against unauthorized seizure while preserving substantial legally sanctioned extraction. The distinction between prohibited abuse and legitimate obligation was itself a political question.

Complaints and land disputes also expose unequal access to justice. A wealthy landholder might possess documents, a patron in the capital, or the means to travel to a provincial governor; a tenant or laborer might not. Some communities made claims collectively, while others were divided by differences in status and control of water. The absence of abundant village-level petitions prevents confident reconstruction of who secured relief. The most defensible evaluation is that Ghazan's measures tried to strengthen the capacity of the court to distinguish authorized claims from unauthorized ones, while leaving unresolved how frequently weaker parties could activate that protection.

Cause and Effect

The Causes of Fiscal Disorder and the Intended Effects of Reform

Long-term causes

Conquest-era devastation, a layered system of taxes and requisitions, the dependence of the Mongol military aristocracy on provisioning from settled districts, and administrative practices such as tax farming that encouraged over-collection.

Short-term causes

Rapid turnover of rulers and ministers, heavy court and military spending, the failed paper-money experiment of 1294, and factional competition after 1291.

Immediate context

Ghazan's victory in 1295, the removal of rival ministers, and the installation of Savaji and Rashid al-Din in 1298.

Intended effects

Fixed and recorded assessments, restrained collection, protection of cultivators, more reliable provisioning, and a standardized currency.

Historical complexity: The disorder had multiple roots, and a reform that addressed only some of them could not eliminate it. The reforms also depended on the military elite's willingness to accept restrictions that reduced its own opportunities for extraction.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Taxation, Provisioning, Postal Demands, and Religious Policy Reached Households

For a farming household, the court's policies were experienced through the people who arrived at the village: the tax assessor, the collector, the soldier needing fodder, the courier requiring a horse. A fixed assessment promised that the amount and date of payment would be known in advance. A requisition for supplies, whether authorized or not, could consume a season's surplus. The sources suggest that the difference between regulated and arbitrary demands was real to villagers, though whether it was uniformly improved after the reforms is unknown.

Townspeople and merchants experienced coinage, weights, and road security in their daily trade, and religious minorities experienced the policies of 1295–1297 directly through attacks on their places of worship. Women, children, and dependent laborers rarely appear in the sources, but the burdens of taxation and requisition fell on their households. Historians must supplement court narratives with whatever documentary, archaeological, and material evidence exists, recognizing that the sources overwhelmingly reflect elite concerns.

Section 48.13

The Reform of Ilkhanid Coinage, Weights, and Monetary Authority, 1297–1298

In 1297–1298 Ghazan's government introduced a more uniform silver coinage. Mints across the Ilkhanate had previously produced coins of varying types and standards, and the restoration of a trustworthy currency after the failed paper money of 1294 was a practical necessity. The new issue was designed to be consistent across mints, and it was accompanied by regulation of weights and standards. Merchants and tax collectors alike benefited from a currency whose value could be relied upon, and the state could expect that the coins it collected and paid out would circulate on equal terms.

The reform was not the first. Abaqa had introduced an earlier standardized coinage at Tabriz in 1275, and numismatists identify distinct types across the Ilkhanid period. Ghazan's reform therefore belongs to a tradition of monetary regulation, within which it stands out for its scale and its association with a wider administrative program. The distinction matters because claims that Ghazan "created" a uniform currency overstate the novelty.

The inscriptions on coins carried political meaning. Ilkhanid issues combined elements of Mongol and Islamic authority, and coins from Ghazan's reign include Islamic religious formulas and the ruler's name. Many Ilkhanid issues also carried inscriptions in the Uighur script, the script adopted for Mongol writing, alongside Arabic. The coexistence of these elements on a single object is one of the clearest material expressions of the dual legitimacy discussed in Division II. The currency was a daily, widely circulated statement of how the dynasty wished to be seen.

Weights and measures were also regulated. According to the sources, the court sought to establish uniform standards so that trade and taxation would operate on common units. The mechanics are less well documented than the coinage itself, and the extent to which local custom yielded to the new standards is unclear. Standardization is a persistent challenge for premodern states, and the evidence is better for the court's intentions than for market practice.

The numismatic evidence permits unusual precision about the policy's intended technical standard. Specialist study identifies the reform of 696–697 AH (1297–1298 CE) as establishing a widely used silver dirham of approximately half a mithqal, conventionally twelve nokods, or about 2.16 grams. There were other denominations, but not all circulated equally widely. Silver was more closely standardized by weight than gold, which remained variable and was commonly weighed in exchange. This difference cautions against describing 'uniform currency' as if every metallic issue followed a single fixed standard. Surviving coins can be classified by inscriptions, weight, and mint attribution, though wear and local irregularities complicate individual comparisons.

A second point is chronological continuity. Abaqa's 1275 issues at Tabriz already linked Uighur-script political statements with Arabic inscriptions, so bilingual visual authority was not invented at Ghazan's conversion. His monetary program enlarged and modified an existing practice while incorporating Islamic claims appropriate to his reign. The actual gain for traders would have depended on exchange practices, local acceptance, the availability of silver, and the willingness of mints to follow the standard. A coin gives direct evidence of a design decision; a pattern of many coins from different mints allows a stronger inference about monetary coordination. Neither, on its own, gives the price of grain or the purchasing power of village households.

Section 48.14

Land Registration, Abandoned Fields, and the Restoration of Cultivation

A central concern of Ghazan's court was the condition of agricultural land. Revenue depended on cultivation, and the sources describe widespread abandonment of fields and villages after the turmoil of the preceding decades. The reforms included measures to identify land that was abandoned or underused, to encourage its resettlement, and to protect those who worked it. Reported provisions included allowing cultivators to take up uncultivated land on favorable terms and ensuring that fields were not taxed at full rates before they returned to production.

The people involved were varied. Peasants worked the land and bore the heaviest burdens. Landlords, including old Iranian families, endowed religious institutions, and members of the Mongol elite, held rights to rents and revenues. Administrators compiled the registers, and regional officials were responsible for implementation. Competing claims were common: a landlord might claim land that cultivators had abandoned, and the state might seek to reassign it. How such disputes were resolved depended on local power and on whether the diwan's officials were willing to enforce the court's priorities.

Specialist research emphasizes that the implementation and actual consequences of these policies cannot always be established from surviving records. The decrees show intent, but few local records survive to document how many fields were returned to cultivation or how many households benefited. Rashid al-Din's account is optimistic, but it is the account of a minister responsible for the program. This section therefore concentrates on the policy and its logic, and the evaluation of agricultural recovery is reserved for Chapter 50, which draws on a broader range of evidence.

Environmental conditions limited what a decree could achieve. Much of Iran depends on irrigation, whether from rivers, springs, or underground channels, and these systems require maintenance and labor. A field could not be restored to cultivation by proclamation alone if its water supply had failed. The court is reported to have supported the repair of irrigation works, but the scale and success of such efforts are uncertain. Restoration of cultivation was thus a matter of water, labor, security, and incentive, and the state could address only some of these.

One reconstruction of the reform identifies a special diwan-i khalisa responsible for the registration and classification of uncultivated or abandoned lands. A decree around 1298–1299 reportedly transferred extensive estates into this administrative category, with the intention of resettling cultivators and making seed, draft animals, or equipment available. This was more concrete than a general exhortation to cultivate: it linked classification, assignment, and input provision. But the terms offered to peasants were not necessarily generous, and no comprehensive records survive demonstrating how much land was actually restored. A proposal to supply plough animals cannot itself establish that they were delivered in a particular district.

Different environments made implementation uneven. In canal-irrigated or qanat-dependent villages, fields might lie unused because a water channel had failed or because people able to maintain it had fled. On rain-fed margins, insecurity or uncertain harvests could discourage settlement even without major infrastructure damage. On estates controlled by military households, an administrator might face claims that conflicted with the reassignment program. Measures conceived at the center therefore met distinct ecological and political obstacles. These distinctions help explain why the following chapter must evaluate actual regional outcomes rather than extrapolating recovery from royal plans.

Section 48.15

Military Land Grants and the Reform of Army Provisioning, c. 1303

Among the most consequential measures was the reorganization of how soldiers were paid. Mongol and allied troops had been supported through a mixture of salaries, plunder, requisition from the countryside, and grants. The system was irregular and strained: soldiers who were not paid regularly took what they needed from the population. Around 1303, according to the sources, Ghazan's government assigned revenues from specified lands to soldiers, in arrangements described with the term iqtaʿ. The assignment gave the holder the right to collect certain revenue from designated districts in lieu of regular pay.

The reform had a logic tied to the larger program. If soldiers were supported by assigned revenues, they would have less reason to requisition supplies arbitrarily, and the treasury would be relieved of the burden of cash payments in a period of fiscal stringency. The soldier gained a stable income, and the government gained a means of keeping its army provisioned. The sources also indicate that the assignments were regulated, with rules about what holders could collect and how land could be transferred, though the details are debated.

These arrangements differed from the iqtaʿ system of earlier Seljuk military landholders, discussed in Chapter 38. The Seljuk iqtaʿ had been associated with a system of provincial military governors who collected revenue and supported troops. Ghazan's assignments were adapted to Mongol military organization and to the circumstances of the Ilkhanate, and historians stress that the same word covers different arrangements at different times. Students should avoid assuming continuity of institution from continuity of terminology.

The consequences for cultivators were ambiguous. The assignments placed holders in direct relationship with the rural population, and the potential for conflict between soldiers and cultivators was substantial. A holder who collected more than allowed, or who interfered with farming, could undermine the very productivity that the reform sought to protect. The measure also shifted powers and obligations from the central administration to individual holders, which made central oversight more difficult. It would be a mistake to suppose that the reform automatically relieved every farming household. It may have reduced some forms of arbitrary requisition while creating new forms of dependence.

The available specialist description of the scheme is more detailed than the general term iqtaʿ suggests. In 703 AH (1303–1304 CE), assignments were to be distributed through the Mongol hierarchy of commanders of thousands and hundreds, with the individual holder recorded and shares of both cultivated and waste land specified. A government revenue official was supposed to inspect cultivation, while land earmarked for military support was linked to the routes and seasonal quarters of the army. The system attempted to transform a diffuse burden of irregular military demands into a mapped and registered relationship between armed service and revenue. Its design combined Mongol command organization with Iranian documentary practice.

The scheme also revealed coercive possibilities that cannot be softened by the language of agricultural improvement. Specialist accounts mention the proposed use of captive and enslaved labor to bring waste land under cultivation. The interest of soldiers in increasing the value of their assignments need not coincide with the welfare or freedom of those doing the work. If the assignment system entrenched the military holder's local power, it could weaken the position of villagers even while reducing unauthorized requisition by other soldiers. Because evidence of practice is thin, historians should distinguish the decree's institutional ambition from its distributional effects and refrain from describing every assignment as a secure, hereditary landholding.

Section 48.16

The Yam, Communications, Commercial Regulation, and Public Security

The postal relay system, the yam, was a Mongol institution that supported rapid communication across the empire. Stations stocked with horses and supplies allowed couriers, officials, and envoys to travel quickly, and the system depended on the local population to provide animals, fodder, and labor. Authorized travelers carried tablets known as paizas as proof of their right to use the facilities. The system was essential to government, but it was also a major burden, since unauthorized travelers and abusive officials could demand supplies far beyond what was allowed.

Ghazan's reforms addressed these abuses. According to the sources, the court restricted the use of postal facilities to authorized persons, regulated the supplies that officials and envoys could demand, and sought to prevent exactions beyond the stated allowances. The aim was to make the cost of administration predictable and to limit the extent to which the machinery of government exploited the population it served. As with other reforms, the impact depended on enforcement.

Road security and commercial regulation belonged to the same project. Merchants and caravans needed safe passage, and the government depended on trade for revenue through levies such as the tamgha. Measures concerning weights, measures, and the conduct of officials toward merchants were intended to create confidence in commercial transactions. Public security, including the punishment of brigands, affected both trade and the movement of government personnel. The state's interest in commerce is well documented in this period, and Tabriz in particular became an important commercial center, as Chapter 50 discusses.

It is worth stressing why these seemingly technical measures mattered to economic recovery. The costs of administration were borne largely by the productive population, and when those costs were unpredictable and excessive they discouraged cultivation, trade, and settlement. By seeking to regularize them, the reforms aimed to reduce a persistent drag on economic activity. Whether they succeeded is a question for the broader assessment in Division V and in Chapter 50.

Communication reform illuminates how authority was exercised across long distances. A court decree might be drafted in the capital but required relay horses, verified orders, and cooperating provincial personnel before it could shape conduct elsewhere. The yam made that communication possible, but its privileged travelers could become a source of the very arbitrary exactions the fiscal reforms sought to restrain. Limiting credentials and allowances was therefore more than a measure of economy; it was an effort to determine which claims on local communities counted as commands of the sovereign. Control of the relay system and control of official identity were closely connected.

The practical impact varied along different routes. A settlement near a major relay station might bear heavier demand for fodder and pack animals than one distant from official traffic. Merchants depended on road security but did not necessarily enjoy the same access to royal transport. Local authorities might welcome regulations that restrained opportunistic travelers while resisting inspections that reduced their freedom to supply preferred clients. The distinction between commercial taxes, compulsory labor, and provisioning services matters because the state could restrict one while preserving another. Successful coordination would require separate kinds of enforcement, not simply a universal order to protect trade.

Section 48.17

Ghazan, the Mamluks, and the Continuation of Ilkhanid Imperial Ambition, 1299–1303

Ghazan's conversion did not end Ilkhanid rivalry with the Mamluk sultanate. The struggle over Syria had begun under Hülegü, and it reflected strategic interests, including control of trade routes, Syrian cities, and the eastern Mediterranean coast, that were independent of religious affiliation. Ghazan's court continued to pursue these aims. At the same time, both sides now claimed an Islamic mandate, which transformed the way each justified the conflict.

In the winter of 1299, Ghazan led a large army into Syria. In December 1299 the Ilkhanid forces defeated the Mamluk army at Wadi al-Khaznadar near Homs, and the road to Damascus lay open. The Mongols occupied Damascus in the following weeks, though the citadel reportedly held out, and for a short period Ilkhanid authority extended over a large part of Syria. But the occupation was temporary. Ghazan withdrew early in 1300, leaving a portion of his forces, and the Mamluks reasserted control. Later expeditions in 1300–1301 achieved little.

The Ilkhanid effort ended in April 1303, when an expedition was defeated at Marj al-Saffar, south of Damascus. Ghazan did not lead this campaign in person, and its failure ended serious Ilkhanid attempts to conquer Syria under his rule. The Mamluks, under their regime of military commanders, had proven capable of mobilizing resources and of resisting repeated incursions, and the logistic difficulties of campaigning far from the Ilkhanid heartland were considerable.

The conflict shaped the intellectual climate. Mamluk scholars questioned whether Mongol Islam was genuine, and the legal and theological arguments they developed have a prominent place in the historiography. The question of how to treat Muslims who ruled by a non-Islamic code, and whether it was permissible to fight them, was a live one. The most famous contributor to this debate is Ibn Taymiyya, whose writings are the subject of the source window below. The Ilkhanid side, for its part, used its Muslim identity to argue that the Mamluks should submit to a legitimate Muslim ruler, and the exchange of letters, embassies, and propaganda formed part of the war.

The Syrian campaigns demonstrate the limits of treating conversion as a diplomatic settlement. Both Ilkhanid and Mamluk rulers could profess Islam while maintaining irreconcilable strategic aims. Possession of Syrian cities offered military position, prestige, and leverage over routes connecting Iraq, Anatolia, and the Mediterranean. The Mamluk state, meanwhile, had its own elite cohesion and political standing to defend. Campaigns depended on seasonally available pasture and grain, the passage of large numbers of horses, and the ability to retain garrisons after the main field army departed. Victory in a battle was therefore not equivalent to permanent conquest. The withdrawal after the success of 1299 illustrates this distinction.

For residents of Syria the experience cannot be reduced to an argument about which Muslim ruler possessed the better legal title. Military occupations generated demands for provisions and exposed towns and villages to uncertainty, while local notables negotiated with whichever authorities controlled the roads. Mamluk and Persianate accounts present those encounters through incompatible political lenses. Ibn Taymiyya's writings are indispensable for a history of Islamic political argument but must be read as partisan interventions within a wider war. They should not be treated as a direct inventory of every law applied by Ghazan's officers or as a single securely dated document.

Section 48.18

Tabriz, Shanb-i Ghazan, Religious Endowments, and Royal Patronage

Ghazan's building program expressed his legitimacy in stone. At Tabriz, he developed a large complex known as Shanb-i Ghazan, a suburb that became the site of his funerary monument and an array of associated institutions. The sources describe a tomb, a mosque, colleges, a Sufi lodge, and other foundations, supported by endowments. Construction began during his reign, and the complex served as a statement of the dynasty's religious commitment and of its permanent attachment to Tabriz as a capital.

The complex also illustrates how patronage and administration were linked. Endowments assigned the revenues of lands and properties to the upkeep of the institutions, and the deeds specified their management. The endowments provided stable income for scholars, students, and Sufi residents, and they tied significant resources to royal memory. Rashid al-Din's own pious foundations in Tabriz, discussed in Chapter 49, followed a similar model. Students should be careful not to confuse Ghazan's complex at Tabriz with the later mausoleum of his successor Öljeitü at Soltaniyeh, which belongs to a different reign and a different capital.

Royal patronage extended to learning. Ghazan supported scholars and, according to the sources, ordered the compilation of a history of the Mongols, which led to the great historical project of Rashid al-Din. This chapter mentions the commission only briefly, since the intellectual achievements of that project and the career of its author are reserved for Chapter 49. It is nonetheless significant that the king's program of legitimation included the writing of history. The narratives that shaped later memory of his reign were products of the same court that carried out the reforms.

Architecture and patronage had political audiences. Mosques and colleges addressed the Muslim population and scholars, Sufi lodges addressed devotional communities, and the monumental scale of the complex addressed rival rulers and foreign visitors. The visual language of Ilkhanid building drew on Iranian and Islamic traditions, and the monuments of Tabriz made visible the claim that the dynasty had become a patron of Islam. The extent to which the buildings survive varies, and historians reconstruct some of them from textual descriptions.

A royal funerary and charitable foundation materialized several claims simultaneously. A mausoleum linked the Chinggisid ruler to an enduring place of commemoration in an Iranian city; a mosque and teaching institutions presented the king as a Muslim patron; endowments created obligations that could continue after his death. The complex's revenues depended on property and administrators outside the monument itself, so its survival required more than initial construction. The very mechanisms that supplied a pious foundation also redirected income that might otherwise have supported ordinary treasury expenditure. Patronage and fiscal reform consequently operated in a relationship of mutual support and potential competition.

The complex is also a problem in material historical evidence. Much of Shanb-i Ghazan is known through written description rather than through a fully preserved ensemble. Plans, sizes, and individual institutional functions in later narratives should therefore not be treated as measurements of surviving structures. Students should distinguish the establishment of the foundation from claims about the precise original appearance of every building. In the history of royal memory, the location of burial and the circulation of stories about the complex are as important as architectural reconstruction. The site expressed an Ilkhan who intended his Muslim kingship to remain visible beyond his own lifetime.

Section 48.19

Did Ghazan's Reforms Work? Evidence, Administrative Resistance, and Uneven Outcomes

Assessing the reforms requires distinguishing three questions: what was ordered, what was implemented, and what changed as a result. The first is well documented. The second is only partly documented. The third is the hardest, because it requires evidence of revenues, production, prices, and household conditions that the surviving sources rarely supply in measurable form. Rashid al-Din's account is a substantial record of the first and a claim about the third. It is not sufficient to prove either the second or the third.

Some evidence supports the view that the reforms had effects. The coinage was standardized, and numismatic evidence confirms the issue of regulated types. The government endured for the rest of Ghazan's reign and into the next, which suggests at least basic fiscal functioning. Contemporary writers, including those not directly responsible for the reforms, describe the reign as a period of greater order than the years before. These sources, however, wrote in a laudatory mode, and their testimony is not independent confirmation of economic recovery.

Other evidence points to limits. Officials continued to be accused of corruption, and the careers and executions of leading ministers show that factional struggle persisted. Military holders retained the power to resist regulation. Fixed assessments could become burdensome if production fell, and the reforms could not alter underlying constraints such as irrigation, climate, and security. The dynasty's later history, including the fragmentation examined in Chapter 51, indicates that whatever stability the reforms achieved did not secure the state in the longer term. This does not prove that the reforms failed, since many other factors shaped later events, but it cautions against attributing lasting transformation to them.

The proper conclusion is therefore measured. Ghazan's reforms were a serious and sustained attempt to regularize fiscal administration, and they probably improved the predictability of some parts of government, particularly currency and the framework of taxation. Their actual effects on rural and urban households varied by region and are difficult to measure. The difference between royal decrees, practical enforcement, and structural change remains the central analytical problem, and the further evidence of economic outcomes is the subject of Chapter 50.

Different reforms invite different standards of proof. For coinage, surviving specimens from multiple dated mints can be compared against the proposed weight and inscriptional program. For land restoration, one would ideally need local assignments, irrigation records, settlement evidence, and indicators of actual harvests. For taxation, registers and receipts from more than one administrative level would show whether prescribed limits were obeyed. Such materials survive very unevenly. It follows that a historian may confidently describe the scale of monetary innovation while remaining uncertain about the net benefit to cultivators. A single judgment of 'success' or 'failure' collapses these distinct evidentiary situations.

The problem of causation is equally important. Even if an individual city or district became more prosperous, improved security, changing trade patterns, local investment, favorable harvests, or recovery after war might account for part of the change. Conversely, the persistence of disorder in one province would not prove that every regulation failed elsewhere. Rashid al-Din's proximity to government makes his descriptions unusually informative about administrative intention, but the same proximity makes his evaluation interested. Source criticism should not require discarding him; it requires asking which propositions his testimony alone can establish, which need corroboration, and which must remain uncertain.

Historical Debate

How Effective Were Ghazan's Fiscal and Administrative Reforms?

The question: To what extent did Ghazan's reforms produce regular taxation, restrained officials, and economic recovery, as the main court source reports?

More positive assessments: Emphasize the volume and specificity of the decrees, the monetary standardization, the durable ministerial team, and contemporary descriptions of greater order, and treat the reforms as a real turning point in Ilkhanid government.
More skeptical assessments: Emphasize that the principal source was a participant, that implementation is poorly documented, that corruption and factionalism persisted, and that economic outcomes cannot be attributed confidently to the reforms.
Intermediate assessments: Treat the reforms as meaningful in intent and partly effective in practice, with outcomes that varied by region, sector, and the strength of local enforcement.

Evidence: The evidence includes Rashid al-Din's account, other Persian court histories, coins, endowment documents, and later narratives, alongside the limited surviving documentary and archaeological material.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns the reliability of sources, the weight given to decrees versus outcomes, and causation: whether recovery, where it occurred, resulted from the reforms or from other conditions such as security and a period of relative peace.

Why it matters: The assessment affects how historians understand Ilkhanid state capacity and the relationship between Mongol conquest and the economic history of Iran.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence not currently emphasized that could help decide between the positive and skeptical views, and explain what each could show.

Section 48.20

Chapter Synthesis and Review: Islam, Mongol Sovereignty, and the Transformation of the Ilkhanid State

Ghazan's reign combined three processes: the conversion of royal authority to an Islamic idiom, the continuation of Mongol dynastic institutions, and attempts to make taxation, military finance, and public administration more regular. None of them can be understood alone. The conversion gave the dynasty a language with which to address its Muslim subjects and to compete with the Mamluks, but it did not remove Chinggisid legitimacy, Mongol military organization, or royal prerogative. The reforms were enabled by political consolidation and carried out by ministers whose own positions depended on the king, and they operated within a structure of military power that limited what regulation could achieve.

The period was also marked by violence and coercion. The suppression of Buddhist institutions, attacks on churches and synagogues, the elimination of rivals, and the executions of ministers belong to the same history as the decrees protecting cultivators. A balanced account holds both together. The reforms and the purges were parts of a single political process in which a Muslim Mongol king reshaped the elite and institutions of his state.

Ghazan died on 17 May 1304, still a young man, and was succeeded by his brother Öljeitü. The succession passed without the civil war that had marked earlier transitions, which suggests that the consolidation of the dynasty had achieved at least a measure of stability. His tomb at Shanb-i Ghazan became a center of memory. The history written in his name, and the program of administration associated with his ministers, shaped how his reign was understood by later generations.

For the chapters that follow, this account establishes a foundation. Chapter 49 takes up Rashid al-Din, the historian-minister whose writing preserves much of what is known of these reforms, and examines his work as a project of world history. Chapter 50 turns to the evidence for urban, agricultural, and commercial recovery, testing the claims explored here against a broader range of material. Chapter 51 treats the political dissolution of the Ilkhanate after the mid-fourteenth century, which sets the limits of what Ghazan's reforms could secure.

The reign therefore marks an important reconfiguration rather than a moment when one coherent system replaced another. Islamic formulas were integrated into royal authority; Mongol military command remained central; Persianate administrators attempted to make fiscal extraction legible through documents and defined obligations. These changes affected constituencies differently. Muslim scholars acquired new opportunities for patronage, Buddhist institutions experienced profound loss, commanders received renewed forms of support, and ordinary taxpayers faced both promises of protection and continuing demands. The very coexistence of reform and coercion is part of the explanation, not a contradiction to be edited out.

Finally, the evidence supports a bounded historical assessment. Ghazan's state demonstrated enough organizational capacity to design and publicize ambitious regulations and to issue a recognizable monetary program. It does not follow that all districts became orderly or that recovery proceeded uniformly. His death transferred authority to another Chinggisid ruler and left administrative practices open to later alteration. What endured most securely was an altered model of Ilkhanid Muslim kingship and a large body of court writing about the reforms. That writing is both a legacy of state formation and the subject of further critical inquiry in the chapters ahead.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Ghazan Khan's conversion to Islam and his administrative reforms reshaped the Ilkhanate between 1295 and 1304. Its central argument is that Ghazan's importance lies less in being a Muslim Ilkhan, since Ahmad Tegüder had converted earlier, than in establishing a durable relationship between Ilkhanid kingship, Muslim political legitimacy, and administrative reform, while retaining Chinggisid foundations.

Ghazan came to the throne through a succession struggle. After a decade as governor of Khurasan under Arghun, and after years of conflict with the commander Nawruz, he allied with Nawruz in 1294–1295 and challenged Baydu, who had overthrown Gaykhatu. In June 1295, during that struggle, he converted to Islam and took the name Mahmud. The conversion preceded his victory and formed part of his bid for power. Baydu was defeated in the autumn of 1295, and Ghazan was enthroned at Tabriz.

Consolidation was not peaceful. Ghazan eliminated rivals, and in 1297 he executed Nawruz, who had been central to his rise. Conversion did not end factional conflict or make the political order stable. The administration was rebuilt in the following years, with Saʿd al-Din Savaji and Rashid al-Din assuming leading roles from 1298.

The religious transformation included both patronage and coercion. Ghazan was presented as Padishah-i Islam, and Islamic formulas appeared in sermons and on coins. Buddhist institutions were suppressed, and Christian and Jewish communities faced attacks and pressure, though policy varied over time and place. His relations with Sunni, Shiʿi, and Sufi figures were broadly inclusive and patronage-oriented, and the later official Twelver Shiʿism of Öljeitü should not be attributed to him.

Chinggisid tradition continued. Ghazan's decrees invoked the yasa, understood as a body of decrees, precedents, and customs and not a single written code. The army kept its Mongol organization, and royal prerogative persisted alongside the application of sharia by judges. This coexistence explains why Mamluk critics such as Ibn Taymiyya questioned the sincerity of Mongol Islam, and why the Syrian war continued.

The fiscal reforms aimed to regularize taxation. They sought to record assessments in registers, fix them for defined periods, schedule collection to the agricultural calendar, prevent repeated levies, and regulate the qopchur, which in conquered Iranian regions functioned as a capitation assessment. The diwan, under Savaji and Rashid al-Din, was given greater powers of oversight. These measures built on earlier attempts at regulation and did not invent organized administration.

Monetary and economic policy included a more uniform silver coinage in 1297–1298, following Abaqa's earlier standardization at Tabriz in 1275, and the regulation of weights. Efforts to restore cultivation, the assignment of revenues to soldiers around 1303, and restrictions on abuses of the yam and on exactions all aimed at making administration predictable. Each depended on enforcement, and the evidence for outcomes is limited.

Ghazan's campaigns against the Mamluks, including the victory at Wadi al-Khaznadar in December 1299, the temporary occupation of Damascus, and the defeat at Marj al-Saffar in April 1303, demonstrate that conversion did not end imperial ambition. His patronage at Tabriz, centered on Shanb-i Ghazan, expressed the dynasty's new religious identity in architecture, and should not be confused with Öljeitü's later mausoleum at Soltaniyeh.

The assessment of the reforms requires careful source criticism. Rashid al-Din's account is indispensable and interested. It establishes what was ordered and how sponsors justified it, but it cannot alone prove that every regulation was implemented or that Iran's recovery was primarily Ghazan's achievement. Coins show proclamation, not prosperity. The proper judgment is that the reforms were a serious and partly effective attempt at regularization whose results varied.

Ghazan died on 17 May 1304, and Öljeitü succeeded him. The chapter prepares students for Chapter 49, on the historian-minister and his world history, and Chapter 50, on the evidence for recovery in cities, agriculture, and trade.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a conquest dynasty adapted to the society it ruled without giving up its origins. Ghazan's Ilkhanate neither remained a purely Mongol enterprise nor became simply an Iranian Muslim monarchy. It became a hybrid in which Chinggisid legitimacy, Muslim kingship, Persian administrative practice, and Mongol military organization coexisted, sometimes uneasily. That pattern recurs in other periods of Iranian history, where ruling groups of different origins fashioned new political forms from available traditions.

It also illustrates the difficulty of reading administrative reform from the records of reformers. The decrees and narratives that survive show what a court wished to be seen doing, and they must be weighed against whatever other evidence can be found. Students who learn to distinguish announced policy from demonstrated outcome acquire a skill that applies far beyond this reign. The same caution applies to coins, buildings, and other expressions of royal ideology, which communicate claims without proving results.

The chapter's themes connect forward. Chapter 49 will examine the historian whose account shaped later memory of the reign, and Chapter 50 will test the claims of recovery against wider evidence. Chapter 51 will show the political fragmentation that followed the dynasty's decline. Ghazan's reforms did not prevent that outcome, and they did not cause it; they were one attempt among many to stabilize a state whose long-term fortunes depended on forces that no decree could control.

Finally, the chapter reminds students that religious change in medieval Iran was neither simple nor uniform. A king's conversion altered the religious character of the ruling elite and the public language of the state, and it brought hardship to some communities and opportunity to others. The population as a whole experienced the transformation through local institutions, officials, and economic pressures, in ways that the sources record only partially. Understanding that complexity is part of what the study of this period is for.
