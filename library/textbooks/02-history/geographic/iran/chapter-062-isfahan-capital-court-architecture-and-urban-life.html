Chapter Opening

Safavid Isfahan was a royal stage and a working city at once. Its square, mosques, bridges, and gardens made the authority of the shah visible, but the city ran on water channels, kilns, looms, rents, endowments, caravans, and the labor and obligations of people who appear in the sources far less often than the shah does.

Imagine the great rectangular square of Isfahan on a day of public celebration in the 1620s, after the principal royal mosque and the mosque of Shaykh Lotfollah had taken shape but before later rulers completed every palace feature. The scene that follows is a composite reconstruction drawn from the kinds of events that travelers and chroniclers describe, not an account of any single day, and its elements are drawn from documented practices and building phases rather than a purported eyewitness description of one event. On the western side rises the multi-storied palace gateway later known as Ali Qapu: its early form had upper rooms and a roof used for viewing, but not the projecting columned talar added under Abbas II around 1643–1644. To the south rises the new royal mosque, whose tiled portal is turned toward the square while the prayer hall behind it is angled toward Mecca. To the east is the smaller domed mosque associated with the scholar Shaykh Lotfollah. To the north, an arched entrance opens into the covered passages of the Qaysariya, the market quarter that leads toward the older heart of the city. Two levels of shopfronts run around the whole perimeter, so that every side of the square is both monument and marketplace.

A visitor standing at the center would see the square used in several ways at once. Soldiers could be reviewed there, horsemen could play polo between the goalposts, processions could cross it, and the court could stage celebrations that drew crowds from the city and its suburbs. The same space held stalls and shopkeepers, and on other occasions it was a place where punishments were carried out in public. The square was therefore not merely an open ornamental space. It was an institution through which the dynasty organized, displayed, and sometimes enforced its relationship with the urban population.

But the festival would not have happened without people whose names the chroniclers rarely record. Tile makers fired and glazed the revetments on the new mosques; masons, carpenters, plasterers, and hauling crews built them. Merchants opened stalls beneath the arcades; water carriers and street sweepers kept the surrounding quarters usable; servants carried provisions into the palace compound; and families in the neighborhoods behind the square navigated crowded lanes, shared water from canals, prayed in local mosques, and paid rents and dues that supported the buildings. The central contrast of this chapter lies between the imperial capital as a representation of royal power and the imperial capital as a lived social environment. Both were real, and neither can be understood without the other.

The central question of the chapter is therefore how the Safavid rulers transformed Isfahan into an imperial capital, and how the city's architecture, markets, institutions, neighborhoods, and everyday practices expressed the relationship between royal authority and urban society. The chapter argues that Safavid Isfahan was not simply a collection of royal monuments. It was an integrated but socially unequal urban environment, in which ceremony, religious patronage, commerce, household life, and public activity intersected. Its creation depended on an older city that already existed, on substantial financial investment, on architects and specialized craftworkers, on migrant and displaced communities, and at times on coercive state policies.

Historians reconstruct this city from several kinds of evidence, each with its own limits. The buildings themselves survive in large numbers, but many have been restored, altered, or reinterpreted, and the present appearance of the royal square, with its modern landscaping, paving, and central pond, should not be taken as the seventeenth-century setting. Persian court chronicles, especially the history of Abbas I by Iskandar Beg Munshi, describe royal building projects from the viewpoint of the court. Armenian chronicles and community records describe New Julfa from the perspective of a displaced and then privileged minority. European travelers such as Pietro della Valle and Jean Chardin left detailed but partial descriptions, shaped by their own expectations and often by their connections to elite circles. Endowment deeds, inscriptions, coins, and archaeological and architectural surveys add further layers. The chapter weighs these sources against one another rather than treating any one observer as an impartial witness.

This chapter occupies a particular position in the textbook. Chapter 61 narrated the political, military, and fiscal achievements of Shah Abbas I; the present chapter makes the city itself the principal subject and refers back to Chapter 61 for the wars, reforms, and deportations that formed its background. Chapter 63 will turn from the city to the international commerce of silk and the Persian Gulf, in which Isfahan's Armenian merchants played a major part, and Chapter 64 will examine Safavid society across the empire rather than in its capital alone. Chapter 65 takes up the wider history of clerical and shrine authority, so the religious institutions of Isfahan appear here only as features of the urban landscape. The collapse of the capital in 1722 belongs to Chapter 67. Chapter 1 argued that the historical regions of Iran must not be read through modern borders; the same discipline applies here, because the city's builders, workers, merchants, and visitors came from the Caucasus, Anatolia, Arab lands, India, and Europe as well as from the Iranian plateau.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Isfahan was a significant city before the Safavids and how its earlier institutions shaped later development.
  2. Analyze the political, strategic, and symbolic reasons for the relocation of the principal royal center to Isfahan in 1597–1598.
  3. Describe the spatial arrangement of the old city, the royal square, the Qaysariya, the palace precinct, Chahar Bagh, the river, and New Julfa.
  4. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Safavid Isfahan was built from a coherent master plan or through cumulative, multi-patron development.
  5. Interpret the four sides of Naqsh-e Jahan Square as parts of a single urban and political system, attributing each building to its correct period and patron.
  6. Trace the connections among royal demand, architectural construction, commerce, endowments, and urban craft production.
  7. Assess the roles of gardens, bridges, and water infrastructure in both royal display and everyday urban life.
  8. Compare the social geography of the old city, royal districts, New Julfa, and poorer neighborhoods, and evaluate the cosmopolitan-versus-segmented debate.
  9. Explain how municipal officials, religious institutions, and waqf endowments operated in practice and where state authority was delegated or negotiated.
  10. Reconstruct aspects of everyday life in Isfahan for different social groups while acknowledging the limits of surviving evidence.
  11. Critique Persian, Armenian, and European sources on the city, identifying author, date, perspective, and what each omits.
  12. Distinguish the contributions of Abbas I from those of later shahs, officials, patrons, merchants, workers, and communities.

Historical Context

By the 1580s the Safavid state, whose formation Chapters 56 and 57 narrated, had survived nearly a century of pressure. Chapter 59 showed that defeat at Chaldiran in 1514 did not destroy the dynasty, and Chapter 60 traced the long reign of Shah Tahmasp I, during which the court moved from Tabriz to Qazvin as Ottoman armies pressed on the northwest. After Tahmasp's death in 1576 the realm suffered factional struggles among Qizilbash commanders, Ottoman and Uzbek attacks, and a period of weak kingship. When Abbas I was formally enthroned at Qazvin on 1 October 1588, after the accession struggle that began in 1587, he inherited a capital that lay relatively close to the Ottoman frontier and to the Qizilbash tribal territories whose power he intended to restrain. The reforms, wars, and fiscal arrangements through which he did so belong to Chapter 61 and are only summarized here.

The central Iranian plateau offered a different setting. Isfahan lay on the Zayandeh Rud, a river rising in the Zagros that watered an oasis in an otherwise arid interior, as Chapter 1's discussion of plateau water and settlement would lead us to expect. The city sat among roads linking the northwest, the Persian Gulf and Fars, Khurasan, and the Zagros passes, and it drew food, fuel, and labor from a densely cultivated hinterland. It was also a city with a long history as a regional and, in the Seljuk period, an imperial center, as Chapters 38 and 39 discussed in connection with the Seljuk state and Nizam al-Mulk. The older city possessed a great congregational mosque, bazaars, neighborhoods, gardens, and a scholarly and artisan population long before any Safavid building began.

Architecture in this period drew on several traditions. Builders and decorators in Iran had inherited Seljuk brickwork, Ilkhanid and Timurid domes and tilework, and the artistic and scholarly legacy of Herat, which Chapter 54 examined. Safavid artisans developed these traditions further, notably in the extensive use of painted glazed tile. At the same time, the Safavids ruled over a society that included Persian-speaking townspeople, Turkic tribal military households, Georgian and Circassian court servants of slave origin, Armenians, Jews, and others, and these populations contributed to the city in different and unequal ways. The relationship between ruler and subjects described in Chapters 61 and 64 shaped who built, who paid, who lived where, and who watched from which side of the square.

Finally, the economy mattered. The Safavid crown drew revenue from land, customs, and trade, and it participated directly in commerce, especially in raw silk, a subject Chapter 63 develops. Royal and elite investment in construction produced demand for materials and skilled labor, while the markets and endowments attached to the new buildings generated rents that maintained them. This chapter studies those links at the scale of one city. It asks how a dynasty with large ambitions and limited administrative reach translated them into streets, buildings, and institutions, and what that process meant for those who lived within them.

Division I — From Historic City to Imperial Capital Section 62.1

Isfahan before the Safavids: Geography, the Zayandeh Rud, and the Older City

Isfahan occupies a plain on the Iranian plateau at an elevation that gives it a continental climate of cold winters and hot, dry summers, as the discussion of plateau geography in Chapter 1 would predict. What made it a city rather than a desert halt was the Zayandeh Rud, which descends from the Zagros mountains to the west and flows eastward across the plain before disappearing into the interior basin. The river's snowmelt supported irrigated agriculture in a belt of villages and fields around the city, and its water, distributed through canals, fed orchards, gardens, and neighborhoods. This was not abundance in a modern sense: water was a limited resource, and its allocation between fields, gardens, and urban users was a recurring matter of dispute and regulation, a theme Section 62.12 develops.

The surrounding region supplied the city with grain, fruit, fuel, livestock, and raw materials for crafts, and Isfahan stood on roads connecting several parts of the plateau. Caravans moved between the northwest and Fars, between the central plateau and the Persian Gulf, and toward Khurasan and beyond. This position did not determine the city's fortunes, since many regional centers shared similar advantages, but it created conditions in which a ruler with the means and motive could build up a major capital. The settled population of the plain, its tradition of irrigation management, and its networks of artisans and merchants were assets that Safavid builders did not have to create from nothing.

Isfahan's earlier history was substantial. In the Seljuk period of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, discussed in Chapters 38 and 39, the city functioned as a principal Seljuk center, and the Great Friday Mosque, the Masjed-e Jameh, took on much of the monumental form for which it is known. That building grew over many centuries by accretion, with brick domes, courtyard arrangements, and later additions, and it served as the congregational center of the old city. Around it lay the old bazaars, residential neighborhoods, and an older open space known as the Maydan-e Kohna, the "old square." The old city was a dense, irregular urban fabric of lanes, courtyards, mosques, shrines, and markets, shaped over centuries by the movements of people and goods rather than by a single design.

Between the Seljuk period and the late sixteenth century, the city passed under Mongol, Timurid, and Turkmen rule, as the chapters of Volume IV described. Some of these periods brought destruction and others brought building, and the evidence for the city's condition varies. What can be said with confidence is that by the early Safavid period Isfahan was an established regional city with a large congregational mosque, bazaars, gardens, and a population of artisans and merchants. Early Safavid patrons also built there before the move of the capital; a shrine and tomb complex dated to the early sixteenth century, for example, belongs to the first generation of Safavid rule in the city, though its precise dating and attribution should be checked against specialist studies.

The point of this section is therefore a corrective one. Popular accounts sometimes describe Abbas I as the founder of Isfahan or as building a capital on empty ground. The evidence points the other way. He inherited a functioning and historically significant city, with institutions, property claims, and social groups that constrained and enabled what he could do. The old center did not disappear when the royal focus moved southwestward. It remained a living part of the city, with its mosque, bazaars, and neighborhoods continuing to serve residents, and the new Safavid structures were built in relation to it. Understanding Safavid Isfahan means understanding how new and old were joined, a question the next sections examine through the choice of the city as capital and the planning of its royal district.

Section 62.2

Shah Abbas I and the Selection of Isfahan as the Safavid Capital, 1597–1598

Safavid capitals had moved before. Shah Ismail I took Tabriz in 1501, as Chapter 57 described, and the Safavid court remained there for decades, in an area exposed to Ottoman attack, as Chapters 59 and 60 showed. Under Tahmasp, around the middle of the sixteenth century, the principal royal seat shifted to Qazvin, which lay farther from the Ottoman frontier but remained in the northwest and close to the Qizilbash heartlands. Tahmasp's court also spent time at other places, so that "the capital" was not always a single fixed point. By the time Abbas I took the throne, the habit of relocation was well established, and the question was where the next center should be.

The court chronicler Iskandar Beg Munshi dates Abbas's decision to make Isfahan his seat to the year 1006 of the Islamic calendar, which corresponds to 1597–1598. This is the date generally used, but it should be understood as a formal marker. Royal attention to Isfahan, and building there, began somewhat earlier, and construction of the royal district continued for decades afterward. Historians therefore distinguish the formal designation of the capital from the long process of making it. They also note that the court did not abandon travel: Abbas spent time at other residences, at Qazvin and elsewhere, and the new capital became the usual seat rather than the only place the shah resided.

Several considerations are generally offered to explain the choice. The first is strategic. Isfahan lay farther from the Ottoman frontier than Tabriz or Qazvin, and the Ottoman occupation of Tabriz in the 1580s, which Chapter 61 discusses, had shown the vulnerability of the northwestern capitals. A central plateau location gave more room to respond to attacks and allowed the court to regard the northwestern frontier as a zone to be defended rather than as the seat of government itself. A central position also made it easier to maintain communication with Fars, the Persian Gulf, and, through roads to the northeast, with Khurasan, where Uzbek pressure remained a concern until Abbas's campaigns of the late 1590s.

The second consideration is political. The Qizilbash tribal leaders, whose power Abbas sought to limit, were concentrated in the northwest and in certain provinces. A capital away from their principal bases helped distance the court from their influence, and the shift of many provinces into crown lands, a policy treated in Chapter 61, gave the crown direct revenue from the central plateau. The region around Isfahan was among those administered as crown territory rather than assigned to tribal governors. In this sense, the relocation reinforced a wider restructuring of the Safavid state, though the capital cannot be reduced to a mere instrument of policy.

The third consideration is symbolic and practical. Isfahan was an old and respected city with Seljuk associations, a wealthy hinterland, and an existing population that could supply labor and goods. It also had room to expand to the southwest, where the Zayandeh Rud and open land allowed the construction of a royal district without demolishing the entire old center. The decision should not, however, be read as the outcome of a single foresighted plan. The chroniclers present it as the shah's choice, but we know the reasons largely through the explanations of court writers, who had an interest in portraying a coherent royal intention. Section 62.3

Planning the Safavid Capital: The Old City, New Royal District, and Urban Axes

The key spatial fact about Safavid Isfahan is that the new city was added to the old one rather than replacing it. The old core, centered on the Friday Mosque and the Maydan-e Kohna, lay to the northeast. The new royal square, the Maydan-e Naqsh-e Jahan, lay to the southwest of it, and the two were joined by commercial passages that ran from the north side of the new square toward the older markets. This arrangement meant that the royal center was physically linked to the existing economy of the city. A person walking from the old Friday Mosque to the new square passed through covered and arcaded market streets, so that the monarch's square sat at the end of an established commercial artery rather than in isolation.

The royal square was a large rectangle oriented roughly northeast to southwest, with its long sides lined by two-story arcades containing shops and chambers. At its western side lay the gateway building leading into the palace precinct, which was itself a large area of pavilions, courtyards, and gardens rather than a single building. To its south and east were two mosques, and to its north the entrance to the bazaar. A second major axis, the Chahar Bagh avenue, ran southward from the palace precinct, lined with trees and watercourses, and crossed the Zayandeh Rud by bridge to reach the southern bank, where gardens, residences, and later the Armenian quarter of New Julfa lay. The river thus formed a southern boundary of the main royal city and a corridor of leisure and movement.

These arrangements connected different kinds of power. The palace gateway linked the royal household to the square, the mosques linked the dynasty to religious legitimacy and public worship, the market linked it to commerce and revenue, and the avenue and garden pavilions linked it to royal leisure and to wealthy residents. Gateways, arcades, and thresholds regulated movement: some spaces were open to crowds, others were reserved for officials and the court. Historians who study the plan emphasize that these elements formed a legible sequence, so that moving from the market through the square to the palace, or from the palace down the avenue to the river, meant passing through distinct zones of access and meaning.

At the same time, the geometry should not be exaggerated. The older city had its own irregular fabric, the new axes had to be fitted into existing property and water arrangements, and several important buildings, such as the Shah Mosque, had to resolve conflicts between the square's alignment and the direction of prayer, as Section 62.8 explains. The "plan" of Safavid Isfahan was therefore a set of coordinated decisions made within constraints, and the extent to which it was a single design is a matter of debate, taken up in Section 62.4. Section 62.4

Financing and Building an Imperial City: Land, Labor, Materials, and Patronage

Building on this scale required money, land, materials, and a large and organized work force. The sources, particularly the court chronicle, credit Abbas I as the principal patron, and royal expenditure from crown revenues was certainly central. But the financing of Safavid Isfahan came from several streams. Royal funds paid for the palace and many public works. Endowments, discussed further in Section 62.15, directed the rents of shops and other properties toward the upkeep of mosques and religious institutions, so that buildings could be maintained after their initial construction. Commercial rents from the arcades and markets around the royal square generated continuing income. And wealthy officials, provincial governors, military commanders, royal women, religious patrons, and merchants built mosques, bridges, bathhouses, caravanserais, and gardens, either by royal command or on their own initiative.

Land had to be acquired and cleared. Since the royal district was laid out southwest of the old city, part of it occupied gardens and open land, and the process of acquiring and assigning property involved the crown and existing owners. Evidence for how this was done in individual cases is limited, and historians should be wary of assuming either that all land was simply seized or that all was purchased on fair terms. The crown's capacity to direct property use was considerable, but it operated within a legal and customary framework of ownership and endowment.

The work force was large and diverse. Architects and master builders designed and supervised projects, and only a handful are named in inscriptions and chronicles. Masons, brick makers, carpenters, plasterers, and stone cutters executed the structures. Tile makers produced the glazed ceramic revetments that cover the mosques; the technique known as haft rangi, or "seven-color" tilework, in which several colors could be applied and fired together, allowed faster and cheaper production of large decorative surfaces than the mosaic tilework of earlier periods. Calligraphers designed inscriptions, painters and designers prepared decorative schemes, and a very large unskilled labor force hauled materials, dug foundations, and carried water. Some of this labor was paid, some was conscripted or required as an obligation, and the proportions are poorly documented. Transport networks, including animal caravans and river and canal channels, brought bricks, lime, timber, and other materials to the sites.

The distinction between patronage and production matters. To say that Abbas "built" the Shah Mosque is shorthand for saying that he commissioned and financed it, as the architects, artisans, and laborers actually made it. Court chronicles naturally emphasize the patron. A historian who wants to understand the city must therefore look behind the royal credit to the organization of building work, to the guilds and workshops that supplied it, and to the later patrons who completed or altered earlier projects. Construction also continued long after 1629, so that decoration and structural work were finished under Abbas's successors. Historical Debate

Was Safavid Isfahan the Realization of a Master Plan or the Product of Cumulative Development?

The question: The coordinated geometry of the royal square and Chahar Bagh invites the idea that the capital was designed as a whole. Did a coherent royal plan govern the city's creation, or did it develop through several campaigns, existing constraints, and the initiatives of numerous patrons?

Interpretation A — A coherent royal design: Some accounts emphasize the order of the square and avenue and the alignment of elements, and a later tradition credits particular scholars and architects, including Shaykh Baha'i and the master builder Ali Akbar Isfahani, with devising the plan. On this view, the shah and his advisers conceived a program in which palace, mosque, market, and garden were deliberately integrated.
Interpretation B — Cumulative, multi-patron development: Other historians stress that building spanned decades and several reigns, that existing streets, property, water, and the older city limited what could be done, that the Shah Mosque required an awkward change of axis, and that many major buildings were commissioned by different patrons. The attribution of the plan to specific individuals appears in later tradition and is not firmly documented. On this view, coherence emerged from successive adjustments within shared aesthetic and political aims.

Evidence: Both views draw on the surviving buildings, the court chronicle, inscriptions, endowment records, and later traditions. Neither can rely on a surviving master drawing for the whole city, and the attribution of overall authorship is difficult to verify.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation: how much weight to give central intention relative to circumstance, and how far the later tradition naming individual planners reflects genuine evidence. The two views are not mutually exclusive, since a general royal program could have been realized through many contingent decisions.

Why it matters: If the city was a master plan, it appears as the direct expression of royal will. If it was cumulative, it appears as a product of negotiation among the crown, existing institutions, patrons, and builders, a view that better matches the chapter's emphasis on inherited foundations and multiple agents.

Student task: Identify two features of the square or avenue that support a planned design and two that suggest adaptation to constraints. Then state which interpretation, or which combination, you find more persuasive and what additional evidence would help decide.

Division II — Naqsh-e Jahan and the Architecture of Royal Power Section 62.5

Maydan-e Naqsh-e Jahan: The Royal Square as an Imperial Institution

The Maydan-e Naqsh-e Jahan, whose name is often translated "Image of the World," is the best-known part of Safavid Isfahan and the clearest case of architecture as political organization. It is an elongated rectangle roughly half a kilometer long and about 160 meters wide, enclosed on all four sides by a continuous two-story arcade and entered through monumental portals. Behind the arcades lay rows of shops, workshops, and chambers, and the four great structures set into the perimeter, the palace gateway, two mosques, and the market entrance, break the otherwise regular line of the arcade. The result is a coordinated ensemble in which a viewer standing anywhere in the open space can see all four principal buildings and the commercial frontage that links them. UNESCO, which inscribed the square on the World Heritage List in 1979, describes it as a place where governmental, religious, and commercial functions met.

The square's activities were varied. It was used for military reviews and for polo, played with goalposts at the two short ends, and the surviving stone goalposts recall that use. It served as a setting for processions, public celebrations, court ceremonies, performances, and religious commemorations, as Section 62.18 examines. The arcades housed shops and dealers throughout the year, so the square was also a daily marketplace, and on some occasions it was a site of public punishment and of political demonstration. A space that could hold a royal polo match, a bazaar, and an execution in the same week made visible a close relationship between sovereignty, commerce, and the control of crowds.

For the dynasty, the square offered a way to display authority to a mixed audience. The palace gateway on the west side placed the shah above the open ground and, when he appeared, in view of the people assembled below. The two mosques gave the monarch a religious frame, one oriented to court devotion and the other to large public worship. The shops and the market entrance tied royal presence to everyday exchange. The square thus connected ruler and ruled without making them equals: the elevated balcony, the arcade, and the thresholds of the palace marked clear differences of rank.

Historians must avoid reading the present appearance of the square back into the seventeenth century. Later centuries changed its paving and landscaping, and the central pond and gardens visible today are not reliable evidence for the Safavid setting. UNESCO's own documentation acknowledges later alterations. For the seventeenth century, travelers describe an open space used for markets and displays, with trees, water channels, and temporary stalls in different periods. The Safavid square should therefore be imagined less as a manicured park than as a hard-working civic space.

Historical Vocabulary
Maydan (Maidan)

Definition: A Persian term for an open public square or field, used in Iranian cities for spaces devoted to markets, gatherings, military exercises, games, and ceremonies. The word is associated with several earlier open spaces in Isfahan, including the old square beside the Friday Mosque.

Historical context: The royal square of Abbas I was not the first maydan in Isfahan, and it reflects a longer tradition of open urban spaces. Its distinctive feature was the way it combined an enclosing commercial arcade with royal and religious buildings in a single coordinated form.

Why it matters: The term helps students see the square as a civic and ceremonial institution rather than as an empty space. Control of the maydan was control of a stage on which authority could be displayed and tested.

Potential misunderstanding: A maydan should not be equated with a modern park or a European plaza designed primarily for leisure. In the seventeenth century it was a working space, with stalls, crowds, animals, military and sporting activity, and sometimes punishment.

Section 62.6

Ali Qapu: Royal Audience, Palace Access, and the Staging of Sovereignty

On the western side of the square stands the monumental gateway building known as Ali Qapu. The name is usually interpreted as a "lofty" or "sublime" gate, a title that echoes the language of royal thresholds in the wider Islamic world, though the precise origin of the name is not securely established. The building served as the principal entrance from the square into the royal palace precinct, a large and loosely arranged complex of pavilions, courtyards, gardens, and offices that extended westward behind it. Ali Qapu is therefore best understood as a threshold: it stood between the public space of the square and the more restricted world of the court.

Its multi-storied structure combined several functions. The lower levels controlled movement into the palace precinct. Upper floors contained reception and audience rooms. During Abbas I’s reign the building was already a multi-storied palace entrance, and a roof level gave views of the square. The conspicuous projecting, columned talar familiar today was added later, probably in 1643–1644 under Abbas II; only then did it provide its distinctive shaded viewing platform for the shah and his guests. From the early upper rooms and later terrace, rulers and courtiers could watch polo, military reviews, and festivities, while access remained carefully restricted. This sequence matters because architectural power took different visible forms in different decades: modern photographs of the terrace cannot be used as direct evidence for court ceremony in the 1610s.

Ali Qapu was not built all at once. Part of the structure goes back to the period of Abbas I's building program, but later additions and modifications, notably the projecting columned talar and some upper-level features, belong to the reign of Abbas II. Architectural research associates the terrace with 1053 AH/1643–1644 CE, using the absence of such a projection in earlier travelers’ descriptions as corroborating evidence; the dates of some other phases remain debated. (See Priscilla P. Soucek, “ʿĀlī Qāpū,” Encyclopaedia Iranica.) Students should avoid describing the building in its present form as a single creation of Abbas I. The cumulative character of the building supports the interpretation offered in Section 62.4 that Safavid Isfahan was built in stages.

The palace precinct behind the gateway was a place of audience, administration, and residence. Diplomats, including European envoys and the representatives of neighboring Muslim states, were received by the court in Isfahan, and the arrangement of the building, with its stairs, thresholds, and reception rooms, structured how visitors were admitted and ranked. Etiquette governed who stood where and when, and access to the shah was itself a political resource. The precinct also housed the royal household, including women and servants, whose lives were separated from public view by walls and thresholds and are poorly documented in the surviving sources. What the architecture accomplished was a graduated sequence of spaces from the crowded square to the inner court, with each step marking a stage in access to royal power.

Ali Qapu thus illustrates the interplay between visibility and restriction in Safavid kingship. The shah was publicly visible on occasions of ceremony, and his presence over the square connected him to the city's life, but most of the court's work occurred behind controlled thresholds. The building made monarchical authority simultaneously present and distant, a combination that suited a dynasty attempting to project both accessibility and sacral dignity.

Section 62.7

The Mosque of Shaykh Lotfollah: Court Piety, Geometry, and Architectural Meaning

Facing Ali Qapu across the square, on the eastern side, is the mosque associated with Shaykh Lotfollah. It was built over about a decade and a half in the early seventeenth century, with construction commonly dated to roughly 1603–1618. It is smaller than the congregational mosque on the south side and differs from it in several important ways. It has no large courtyard and no tall public minarets, and it is entered from the square through a portal and a bent passage, so that a visitor turns from the square's axis before entering a domed chamber oriented toward Mecca. This arrangement allowed the building to face the square while its prayer space aligned correctly with the qibla.

The mosque is celebrated for its dome and decoration. The interior is covered in glazed tile in blues and other colors, with inscriptions and patterns arranged around the base of the dome and rising into the vault, while the exterior dome is covered in tile on a light ground. The building is not large, but its decoration is concentrated and refined, and its geometric and calligraphic design has been widely studied. Students should resist the temptation to treat its beauty as sufficient explanation. The key historical questions are who commissioned it, who used it, and what its form says about access.

The mosque is named for Shaykh Lotfollah, a Shi'i scholar who came to Iran from a Shi'i scholarly milieu in the Arab lands, a pattern Chapter 58 described when it examined how the Safavids recruited religious scholars from beyond the plateau. According to later tradition, the building served the court and, in some accounts, particularly the women of the royal household, and a connection to the palace is often asserted. The evidence for such specific claims is limited, and historians treat them cautiously. What is secure is that the mosque lacks the large courtyard and the open public capacity of a congregational mosque, that it stands directly across from the palace gateway, and that it was associated with a scholar patronized by the court. These features point to a space linked to court piety and scholarly teaching rather than to mass public prayer.

The mosque therefore shows the relationship between Shi'i religious scholarship and royal patronage in a small and elegant form. The dynasty supported scholars and housed them within the symbolic geometry of the capital, and in return the scholars lent religious weight to the dynasty. How widely such relationships operated, how scholarly authority developed, and how it related to the shrines and endowments of the empire are subjects for Chapter 65. For present purposes, students should investigate how the building's design differs from that of a large congregational mosque, and ask what the differences reveal about who was expected to pray, teach, or look within.

Section 62.8

The Shah Mosque: Monumental Architecture, Religious Authority, and Public Worship

The south side of the square is dominated by the great congregational mosque begun under Abbas I in 1611. In the Safavid period it was known as the Shah Mosque, and since the twentieth century it has commonly been called the Imam Mosque. The name change reflects later political history, not the seventeenth-century designation, and the older name fits the dynastic context in which the building was created. It is the largest and most prominent of the square's religious structures and the one most clearly meant to serve large public worship.

Its plan follows the four-iwan arrangement familiar from Iranian congregational mosques: a large open courtyard with a vaulted hall, or iwan, opening on each side, and a domed sanctuary on the side facing Mecca. Tall minarets flank the entrance portal, whose deep recess is decorated with stalactite-like vaulting (muqarnas) and covered in glazed tile. Inscriptions in calligraphy, the domes, and the surfaces of the courtyard are covered in tilework, much of it in the haft rangi technique that made large-scale decoration practical. The mosque is therefore a major demonstration of the Safavid tilework industry as well as of architectural skill.

A distinctive problem shaped the building's form. The square is aligned on one axis, while the direction of prayer toward Mecca lies at a different angle. The builders solved the problem by placing the monumental portal on the axis of the square and then turning the passage behind it so that the courtyard and sanctuary are aligned with the qibla. The turn is visible in plan and gives the building a skewed relationship to the square. This is an instructive case of architecture negotiating between urban design and religious requirement, and it shows that the coherence of the square involved compromises.

The project was not completed in Abbas I's lifetime. The shah initiated it and it was built over many years, with decoration and finishing continuing into the 1630s under his successor. An architect named in the sources, Ali Akbar Isfahani, is associated with the work, though the attribution should be treated with the caution applied to named planners generally, and many unnamed craftworkers made it. Religious officials oversaw its use, and endowments, discussed in Section 62.15, supported its maintenance and the activities connected with it. For the city's Muslim population, it provided a very large space for communal prayer and for major religious occasions, and it linked the dynasty's religious legitimacy to the everyday ritual of worship.

Taken together, the four sides of the square form a system. The palace gateway expressed monarchical authority, the Lotfollah mosque expressed court-centered piety and learning, the Shah Mosque offered monumental public worship, and the Qaysariya, examined next, tied the ensemble to commerce. Each element supported the others: the mosques lent religious meaning to the shops and the palace, and the market provided rents and activity that sustained the mosques and the city. The square's integration of these functions is its most characteristic historical feature, and it is the reason the space is better described as an urban and political system than as a set of separate monuments.

Division III — Markets, Gardens, Bridges, and the Working Urban Landscape Section 62.9

The Qaysariya Gateway and the Transformation of Isfahan's Bazaar System

The north side of the royal square is occupied by a tall portal, the Qaysariya, which gives onto a long covered market and links the new square to the commercial heart of the old city. The word qaysariya, associated with imperial markets in the wider Islamic world, indicates a planned and enclosed commercial complex. The building belongs to the period of Abbas I's construction, with work generally dated to the first decades of the seventeenth century, and the portal carries decoration associated with royal themes. Its importance lies less in its facade than in what it connected.

Through the Qaysariya a person could pass from the royal square into a system of covered streets, vaulted passages, and open courtyards that formed the city's bazaar. These passages were lined with shops grouped by trade, and they led to caravanserais, workshops, mosques, bathhouses, and the older market areas near the Friday Mosque. The covered bazaar protected merchants and buyers from heat, cold, and rain, kept goods in a controlled environment, and facilitated supervision of trade. It also integrated royal building with existing neighborhoods: the new square did not replace the old market but extended and reoriented it, and the movement of people and goods between old and new parts of the city became part of the royal landscape.

The commercial arrangements were tied to the crown and to religious foundations. The shops in the arcades and passages produced rents, and tradition, along with the logic of endowment, associates some of the commercial property of the square with the support of the great mosques and other institutions. Customs and market dues, collected by officials, added to the revenue available to the crown. Royal regulation of prices, weights, and measures, and the supervision of market conduct by officials such as the market inspector, helped to organize retail activity, although the degree of effective control is difficult to measure. The bazaar was thus a physical environment and a fiscal and legal institution.

Students should distinguish the bazaar of Isfahan from the broader international commerce of the Safavid empire. The bazaar was the place where raw materials were bought, goods were made and sold, and imported items reached consumers. The long-distance trade in silk, the Armenian merchant networks, and Persian Gulf commerce, which Chapter 63 treats, depended on the bazaar but extended beyond it. Here the focus remains on the city as a working market, where the arrangement of passages, rents, and regulation shaped everyday economic life.

Cause and Effect

Urban Rebuilding, Commercial Activity, and Fiscal Revenues

Long-term causes

Isfahan's existing role as a commercial and agricultural center; the older bazaar system and its artisan and merchant population; the Safavid crown's growing reliance on revenue from crown lands, customs, and trade; and a tradition of endowing commercial property to support religious and charitable foundations.

Short-term causes

The decision to make Isfahan the capital; the fiscal and political aims of Abbas I's reign; royal and elite willingness to invest in construction; and the need to provide upkeep for new mosques and public buildings.

Immediate trigger or context

The relocation of the court around 1597–1598 and the laying out of the royal square and its arcades, which created large amounts of new commercial space attached to the old market system.

Event or transformation

The construction of the square, the Qaysariya, and related commercial structures over several decades.

Immediate effects

New shops, workshops, and passages; increased demand for labor, materials, and goods; rents and dues flowing to the crown and to endowed institutions; and the concentration of court demand in Isfahan.

Long-term consequences

A capital with a dense and diversified urban economy tied to the court; continuing dependence of religious and public institutions on commercial rents; and exposure of the city's prosperity to political and fiscal change, as later chapters show.

Historical complexity: It would be a mistake to say that rebuilding automatically produced prosperity. Gains were unevenly distributed among the crown, endowed institutions, merchants, artisans, and laborers, and the evidence does not allow reliable estimates of revenue or growth. Building stimulated trade, but it also depended on pre-existing trade and on forms of coerced and conscripted labor.

Section 62.10

Caravanserais, Workshops, Guilds, and Urban Craft Production

The commercial economy of Isfahan rested on a layered physical infrastructure. Caravanserais provided lodging for merchants and animals, storage for goods, and space for wholesale dealing. Smaller shops lined the bazaar passages, and workshops, often behind or above the shops or in separate courtyards, produced goods for sale. Warehouses held raw materials and finished goods, and baths, mosques, and tea and coffee establishments served the people who moved through the market. These elements were not separate: a merchant might lodge and store goods in a caravanserai, sell through shops, and commission work from nearby workshops.

Historical Vocabulary
Caravanserai

Definition: A building, usually arranged around a courtyard with rooms on one or more levels, providing lodging for travelers and merchants, stabling for animals, and storage for goods. In towns, caravanserais often functioned as wholesale commercial centers and offices as well as hostels.

Historical context: Rural and road caravanserais supported long-distance travel across Iran, a network that Safavid rulers, as discussed in Chapter 61, maintained and extended. Urban caravanserais, such as those within Isfahan's bazaar and the later Safavid foundation associated with the Chahar Bagh madrasa, combined hospitality with trade and were often supported by endowments.

Why it matters: The caravanserai shows how travel, commerce, and accommodation were linked, and how merchants of different origins, including Armenians, Indians, and others, found places within the city.

Potential misunderstanding: A caravanserai was not simply a roadside inn. In cities it was a base for business, with storage, negotiation, and sometimes manufacture, and its ownership and revenues often belonged to endowed institutions.

Craft production was organized by occupation. Weavers, dyers, metalworkers, potters, tile makers, bookbinders, carpenters, tanners, and many other trades worked in Isfahan, and travelers record a large number of specialist crafts. Historians of Safavid Iran debate how formal and autonomous the occupational associations, often called guilds, were in this period. The evidence suggests that artisans of a trade tended to be grouped in particular parts of the bazaar and often had recognized leaders who dealt with officials and settled disputes, but the sources do not support a simple picture of rigid, uniform guilds. Some artisans worked in their own small workshops, some in larger establishments, and some in royal workshops that produced for the court, an arrangement that tied part of the craft economy directly to the crown.

Royal and elite demand shaped production. The court and wealthy patrons purchased silks, brocades, carpets, metalwork, ceramics, and manuscripts, and the decoration of the new buildings required tile in vast quantity. Silk weaving, drawing on the raw silk that the Safavid state treated as a principal commodity, was among the most important urban industries, and the decorative arts of the period, from carpets to book painting and bindings, flourished in the capital. These activities were connected: the same calligraphers and designers who prepared inscriptions for a building might contribute designs for manuscripts or metalwork, and artisans moved between types of work according to commissions.

A finished luxury object or architectural component depended on many specialized workers, and tracing one example makes the point. Consider a panel of painted tile for a mosque. A designer or calligrapher prepared the pattern or inscription on paper, and a cartoon was transferred to the surface. Potters prepared and fired the clay body in kilns that required fuel and skilled attention. Specialists prepared glazes and pigments, whose ingredients had to be sourced and traded. Painters applied colors, the tiles were fired again, and carriers delivered them to the site, where masons set them in place under the direction of a master builder. Each stage involved a different group of workers, with its own training, tools, wages or payment, and place in the market. The splendid result was therefore the product of a network of occupations rather than the act of a single artist or patron, and much of that labor is anonymous in the surviving record.

The economic links among royal demand, building, and ordinary production ran in both directions. Royal orders sustained workshops and attracted skilled migrants, and in turn the density of skill in Isfahan made large projects feasible. At the same time, artisans depended on the vagaries of patronage and on the state's willingness to pay, and some were drawn into royal service by obligation as much as by choice. Historians must therefore treat the capital's craft brilliance as both an achievement of skilled urban communities and an outcome of relations of power that structured who produced, for whom, and on what terms.

Section 62.11

Chahar Bagh Avenue, Royal Gardens, and the Architecture of Leisure

South of the palace precinct, the broad Chahar Bagh avenue formed the principal north-south axis of the new city. It was lined with trees and watercourses, flanked by garden enclosures and pavilions, and arranged so that water flowed along its length. Its name, associated with the traditional four-part garden layout, suggests the idea of the Persian garden, a bounded and ordered landscape organized by water channels, shade, and symmetry. In Iranian culture such gardens carried associations of paradise, order, and refinement, and they were also places of leisure, entertainment, and sometimes government.

The avenue connected the palace to the river and, across the Allahverdi Khan Bridge, to the southern suburbs, where large gardens and residences were established. It functioned as a promenade for the court and for wealthy residents, a setting for processions and receptions, and a route for the movement of people. The riverside and southern gardens, including large royal garden estates, extended the royal landscape beyond the square. Wealthy officials and nobles built their own garden residences near the avenue, so that the area became a zone of elite housing and leisure.

Palace and garden architecture developed over the century and should be dated carefully. Abbas I laid out the avenue and began the royal garden landscape, but several of the best-known palaces belong to later reigns. The Chehel Sotun, a garden pavilion with an open columned porch facing a pool and interior rooms decorated with wall paintings and reception scenes, was completed principally under Abbas II in the 1640s, and it was altered and repaired in later periods. The Hasht Behesht, an octagonal garden pavilion, belongs to the later seventeenth century, with a date around 1669 often given and associated with the reign of Suleiman. Neither should be credited wholesale to Abbas I. Another major foundation, the Chahar Bagh madrasa associated with the shah's mother, was built much later, in the early eighteenth century under Sultan Hosayn, and its associated caravanserai belongs to the same later phase.

Gardens were not only leisure spaces. They required water, labor, and skilled gardeners, and their upkeep competed with other uses for scarce water, as Section 62.12 shows. They were also places where court business, diplomacy, and display occurred, and where the elite entertained. For most inhabitants, entry to royal gardens was restricted, so the avenue and its pleasures marked a social boundary as well as an aesthetic ideal. The architecture of leisure belonged to a society in which access to shade, water, and open space varied sharply by rank.

Section 62.12

The Zayandeh Rud, Si-o-se-pol, Khaju Bridge, and the City's Water Infrastructure

The river was the lifeline of the city and its agricultural hinterland. The Zayandeh Rud supplied water to fields, orchards, and gardens, and a system of canals, known as madis, diverted it to different districts, where it was distributed according to customary and legal arrangements. Wells, underground channels, and cisterns supplemented surface water in places. The distribution of water was central to the allocation of land value, to agricultural production, and to the maintenance of gardens and urban neighborhoods, and it was a recurrent source of dispute. Tradition credits certain figures, including Shaykh Baha'i, with devising schemes of water division, but such attributions should be treated cautiously, and the system probably developed over time. Accounts also report attempts to increase the river's flow by diverting water from rivers on the other side of the Zagros, an ambitious project that did not succeed, though the details of these efforts are uncertain.

Two great bridges mark the river's role in the Safavid city. The Allahverdi Khan Bridge, commonly called Si-o-se-pol, the "bridge of thirty-three," takes its name from its many arches. It is generally dated to the first years of the seventeenth century, around 1602–1603, though some sources suggest a slightly longer period of construction, and it was built under the patronage of the Georgian-born commander Allahverdi Khan, whose career in Abbas I's service Chapter 61 discussed. It carried the Chahar Bagh across the river and linked the royal avenue to the southern bank, and the bridge also provided a lower gallery and walkways where people could gather. The Khaju Bridge, built under Abbas II around the middle of the seventeenth century, farther downstream, combined a crossing with a weir and a pavilion, and its sluice arrangements helped to regulate the water level. Both bridges served as crossings, as water-control structures, and as places of public sociability.

Bridges and canals joined districts and shaped movement. They allowed residents to cross between the northern city and the southern suburbs, supported the movement of goods and animals, and created spaces for leisure, gatherings, and public festivals. The river's banks and its islands of garden were part of the capital's recreational landscape. For ordinary city residents, however, the river and canals were first of all a source of drinking and washing water, irrigation for gardens, and a power for workshops. Access to clean and plentiful water was unequal: wealthy households had garden channels and private supplies, while poorer districts depended on shared canals, public fountains, and water carriers. Standing water, fouled channels, and seasonal low flows created health and nuisance problems.

Students should therefore understand the monumental waterways as parts of everyday infrastructure. Building and maintaining bridges, weirs, and canals required labor, funding, and administrative attention, and it relied on officials responsible for water and on local arrangements. The river made the city possible and set limits to its growth, and the water system was one of the clearest expressions of the relation between engineering, social hierarchy, and the environment in Safavid Isfahan.

Division IV — Neighborhoods, Communities, and Urban Institutions Section 62.13

New Julfa: Armenian Urban Space, Churches, and Community Organization

South of the Zayandeh Rud, across from the royal avenue, lay one of the most distinctive neighborhoods of the Safavid capital. New Julfa was established beginning in 1605 for Armenians brought from the frontier region around Julfa on the Aras river. The origins of the community were coercive. During the wars with the Ottomans, discussed in Chapter 61, Abbas I ordered the removal of large numbers of Armenians from the border region, partly to deny resources to the enemy and partly to settle skilled and commercially experienced people within the Safavid heartland. Many deportees suffered severe hardship on the journey and in resettlement, and the numbers and mortality are uncertain and debated. The military and displacement narrative belongs to Chapter 61; this section concerns what the resettled people made of their new home.

New Julfa was laid out as a separate quarter with its own streets, residential wards, churches, merchant houses, and communal institutions. The Armenians were allowed to keep their Christian religion and to organize much of their communal life under their own leaders, and the community had a headman, often called a kalantar, who represented it to the Safavid government and managed its internal affairs. Churches were built, including small early foundations, and from the middle of the seventeenth century the community developed larger institutions. The present All Savior’s (Vank) Cathedral was built between approximately 1653 and 1664, with support from the Armenian patron Khwaja Avetik, as an enlargement of the community’s earlier religious precinct; its scriptural murals and painted decoration attest to artistic contacts across several traditions. An earlier church and monastery should not be conflated with the mid-seventeenth-century cathedral building. Schools, manuscript production, and religious institutions maintained Armenian language, worship, and learning. At All Savior’s Monastery the archbishop Khachatur Kesaratsi established an Armenian letterpress in 1636; its Psalter was printed in 1638, followed by other Armenian religious books. The distinction between founding a press and printing a surviving dated book is important. The workshop also illustrates the skills of printers, type founders, scribes, and papermakers, as well as tensions between printing and manuscript copying. These details are documented in the Safavid Julfa and architectural entries of Encyclopaedia Iranica.

The community's relationship with the crown was complex. Some Julfan merchants rose to great wealth and acquired privileges as commercial agents, particularly in the silk trade that Chapter 63 examines, and they built houses and churches that testify to their prosperity. The shah valued their commercial skills and their networks reaching India, the Mediterranean, and Europe. But privilege was uneven. Many of the displaced Armenians were not wealthy merchants but farmers, artisans, and laborers, who were resettled in conditions of dependence, and they experienced the move very differently from the elite. The community also lived under restrictions and periodic pressures, whose severity varied across reigns, and its autonomy depended on royal favor rather than on secure legal rights.

New Julfa therefore illustrates both the coercive foundations and the creative adaptations of urban life in the Safavid capital. It was a neighborhood made by force and remade by its inhabitants, a place where a minority maintained its institutions and conducted international commerce under the protection and supervision of a Shi'i monarchy. The Armenian sources are essential for understanding it, because Persian court sources say little about its internal life.

The evidence for New Julfa differs in kind from the evidence for the royal center. Persian court chronicles mention the Armenians chiefly as subjects of royal policy, whereas Armenian chronicles, colophons in manuscripts, inscriptions on churches and gravestones, and the surviving papers of merchant families describe the community from within. These sources preserve memories of the deportation, of losses on the road, and of the work of building a new settlement, and they often interpret events through Christian religious categories. Historians therefore read Persian and Armenian accounts together and compare them with European observations, recognizing that each source group reflects its own purposes and that none gives a complete picture of the neighborhood.

Scholars have debated why the Julfan merchants prospered. One emphasis falls on royal favor: the crown granted commercial privileges, entrusted some merchants with trade in the state's silk, and protected the community's institutions. Another emphasis falls on the community's own organization, especially family-based commercial partnerships, relations of trust among kin and co-religionists, and agents stationed in distant markets who could handle credit and goods across great distances. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the commercial details belong to Chapter 63. For the history of the city, the point is that New Julfa was not only a charitable settlement for displaced people. It was a commercial and religious center whose wealth flowed back into churches, houses, and communal institutions on the southern bank of the Zayandeh Rud.

The neighborhood's relation to the rest of Isfahan was also one of daily contact. Armenians worked as merchants, craftsmen, and brokers in the markets north of the river, and Muslims, Europeans, and members of other communities visited New Julfa for trade and for services. Armenian communal leaders negotiated with royal officials over taxes, over the protection of church property, and over disputes involving members of the community. The quarter was both separate and connected, which is a useful reminder that the social geography of the capital was not one of sealed enclaves.

Section 62.14

Urban Neighborhoods and the Social Geography of a Cosmopolitan Capital

The city of Isfahan was composed of districts with distinct characters. The old city around the Friday Mosque remained dense and irregular, with long-established neighborhoods, mosques, madrasas, and workshops. The newly planned royal district around the square and avenue was more orderly, with palaces, offices, and elite residences. The southern bank included garden estates and New Julfa. Commercial districts clustered along the bazaar passages and around caravanserais, and poorer neighborhoods lay at the margins, where housing was cheaper and water and services were less reliable. Wealth, occupation, religion, language, political patronage, and legal status all influenced where people lived and what opportunities they had.

The population was diverse. Persian-speaking townspeople formed the core of the urban society, and the court and military brought Turkic-speaking households. Georgians and Circassians, often of slave origin and trained for state service, held important positions at court and in the army, as Chapter 61 described. Armenians formed a large and visible community. Jews lived in their own quarter and worked in various trades. Indian merchants, including Hindu traders, operated in the markets and caravanserais, and a Zoroastrian community is reported in a suburb in some sources. European diplomats, merchants, and missionaries, including religious orders and representatives of trading companies, resided in or visited the capital. Each group had its own language and customs, and each stood in a different relationship to the state.

Interaction was constant, since people of different communities met in markets, workshops, caravanserais, and public spaces, did business, shared services, and sometimes joined in festivals. Merchants and artisans of different origins depended on one another, and the court drew on talent from many backgrounds. At the same time, institutional boundaries limited interaction. Legal and fiscal status differed by religious community, minorities lived under rules that restricted certain activities and that could be tightened or relaxed by particular rulers, and some groups, including the Jews of Isfahan, experienced severe state pressure. In approximately 1656–1662, during Abbas II’s reign and amid policies associated with his grand vizier Mohammad Beg, Jews in Isfahan and other Iranian towns faced expulsion from homes, violent persecution, and coerced conversion to Islam. The Judeo-Persian verse chronicle Ketab-e Anusi by Baba’i ben Lotf of Kashan is a crucial contemporary communal testimony; Persian and Armenian records independently corroborate elements of the campaign. Earlier persecutions under Abbas I must not be conflated with this later wave, and later permission for many converts to return to Judaism did not undo the coercion and suffering. The available testimony does not establish a secure total number of victims. (See Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Baba’i ben Lotf”.) Residential patterns tended to reflect community identity, and neighborhood institutions reinforced it.

Reconstructing the social geography of the capital is difficult because no single source describes it. Historians combine the observations of European visitors, such as Engelbert Kaempfer, who stayed in Isfahan in the mid-1680s and produced plans and descriptions, with Persian chronicles, endowment deeds that locate shops, houses, and gardens, surviving buildings, and the results of modern architectural and archaeological study. Each type of evidence favors particular places. Travelers concentrated on the square, the avenue, the palaces, and the quarters where foreigners lived. Endowment deeds describe property that was endowed, which was not all property. The densely built houses of poorer residents are the least well documented, and later rebuilding has altered much of the old city.

Even so, the available evidence points to a city that was layered rather than divided into a single royal and a single popular zone. The new royal precinct was built beside, not in place of, the old city, and the two interacted constantly through the bazaar passages that joined them. Wealthy officials and merchants built houses and gardens in a range of districts, so that elite and ordinary residents could be neighbors in some areas and live far apart in others. Water access added a further dimension. Districts closer to the main canals and gardens enjoyed better supplies, while others depended on wells and on distribution arrangements regulated by custom and officials. Differences in access to water, security, and services were among the most concrete expressions of social inequality in the capital.

Historical Debate

Was Safavid Isfahan a Cosmopolitan City of Interaction or a Socially Segmented City?

The question: Travelers and later admirers have described Isfahan as an unusually cosmopolitan capital in which peoples of many origins and religions met. Others stress that its communities lived under unequal conditions and often separately. How should historians characterize the city's social life?

Interpretation A — Cosmopolitan interaction: The city drew migrants, merchants, and artisans from many regions; courtly culture absorbed Georgian, Armenian, Indian, and European elements; and economic and cultural exchange crossed community boundaries. On this view, the capital's openness and its use of diverse talent were defining features.
Interpretation B — Social segmentation and inequality: The city's communities were organized by religion, origin, and legal status, often in separate neighborhoods and under different obligations. Some groups were displaced by force, and minorities were vulnerable to restrictions and pressure. On this view, interaction occurred within a framework of hierarchy that limited equality and security.

Evidence: Both views rely on travel accounts, chronicles, community records, legal and fiscal evidence, and the layout of neighborhoods. Each type of source gives a partial picture, and the evidence for ordinary people's relations is limited.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis rather than whether interaction and inequality existed. Historians differ in how to weigh them, and in whether the term "cosmopolitan," a modern concept, is a useful description of a city so structured by status and privilege.

Why it matters: The answer affects how we understand Safavid society: as a plural order in which difference was managed through hierarchy, rather than as either a tolerant ideal or a simple system of exclusion. The evidence supports the view that interaction and institutional exclusion operated simultaneously.

Student task: Find one piece of evidence from this chapter for interaction across communities and one for separation or inequality, and explain how both can be true at once. Then say whether you think "cosmopolitan" is a helpful term, and why.

Section 62.15

Religious Institutions, Madrasas, Endowments, and the Sacred Urban Landscape

Beyond the great royal mosques, Isfahan's religious landscape consisted of many layers. The older Friday Mosque remained the principal congregational mosque of the old city and continued to receive attention and patronage. Neighborhood mosques served local communities, madrasas trained students in law and religious sciences, and shrines and tombs attracted devotion. Charitable establishments, bathhouses, and public fountains were often attached to religious foundations. Christian churches stood in New Julfa, and the Jewish community maintained its own religious institutions. Each part of the landscape tied religion to neighborhood identity, since a mosque, a church, or a madrasa was often the center of a community's social life.

Historical Vocabulary
Waqf (Endowment)

Definition: A charitable or religious endowment in Islamic law, in which property, such as shops, land, or a bathhouse, is dedicated in perpetuity and its income used for a stated purpose, such as maintaining a mosque, supporting a madrasa, or feeding the poor.

Historical context: Safavid rulers, officials, royal women, and merchants established waqfs, and the revenues of commercial property in places like the royal square and bazaar supported religious and public institutions. The deed of endowment set out the property, the purposes, and the administrators.

Why it matters: Waqf explains how buildings were maintained after construction, how religious education and public services were funded, and how patronage tied religion to commerce. It also gave founders a way to preserve property for family or charitable purposes.

Potential misunderstanding: Waqf was not simply state charity. It was a legal instrument used by many kinds of founders, and its administration could involve family managers, officials, and disputes over management and income.

Endowments were the economic basis of this landscape. A founder dedicated shops, farmland, or other property, and the income paid for building maintenance, teachers' stipends, student support, lighting, and charity. In Isfahan, the connection between commercial space and religious institutions was close: the arcades and bazaar generated rents that supported mosques and madrasas, and a religious foundation could thus be a major urban landlord. Administrators, often members of a founder's family or appointed officials, managed the property, and the work of supervision, repair, and rent collection formed part of urban administration. Because endowed property was legally protected, it provided stability, but it could also be misused or neglected, and disputes over management appear in the sources.

Religious diversity was real but unequal. The Safavid state promoted Twelver Shi'ism, whose institutionalization Chapter 58 traced, and Muslim religious institutions enjoyed royal patronage and legal advantage. Christians, Jews, and other minorities could practice their religions under conditions that varied by community and by reign: Armenians had churches and relative autonomy, while Jewish and other communities experienced restrictions and, at times, pressure. The degree of toleration should not be generalized, and the sources show both accommodation and coercion. The sacred landscape of Isfahan therefore reflected both the religious plurality of the population and the hierarchy in which its communities were placed. The wider question of clerical authority and shrine economies across the empire belongs to Chapter 65.

The records of endowment are among the most valuable sources for the religious and social history of the city. An endowment deed, called a waqfnama in Persian usage, identified the founder, listed the property dedicated, specified the purposes of the foundation and the salaries of its staff, and named the person responsible for management. Such documents reveal who financed religious buildings, what kinds of property supported them, and what the founder hoped to accomplish, whether piety, family security, public reputation, or a combination. They also show the roles of women, officials, merchants, and members of the royal family as founders. Their limitations are equally clear. Deeds record intentions and legal formulas, and they do not show how well the property was managed, or what students, worshippers, and beneficiaries actually experienced.

Religious life in the city was not limited to formal institutions. Residents attended neighborhood mosques, took part in commemorations, visited tombs of holy figures, and joined the rituals of the Muharram season, which gave the Shi'i community a shared calendar of mourning and devotion. Sufi groups and devotional circles existed in the city, although the dynasty's own relationship to Sufism had changed since the period described in Chapter 56, and royal and clerical attitudes toward particular groups varied. Sunni Muslims also lived in the Safavid realm, and their treatment depended on place and circumstance. These points caution against reading the royal religious program as a complete description of the beliefs and practices of the city's population.

The relationship between the shah and religious institutions in Isfahan was one of mutual advantage. Royal patronage gave scholars and institutions resources and prestige, and religious figures in turn provided legitimacy for the dynasty and trained the judges, teachers, and preachers that the state and society needed. At the same time, patronage created dependence, and religious officials who relied on royal appointments and endowments had reasons to be careful in their relationship with the court. The balance between the crown and the religious class changed over the seventeenth century, a development that belongs mainly to Chapters 65 and 66.

Section 62.16

Municipal Government, Public Order, Bathhouses, and Urban Services

A capital with a very large population needed administration, but the Safavid state had no municipal government in the modern sense. Authority over the city was divided among several officials and institutions. The region of Isfahan was administered largely as crown land, so royal officials were responsible for taxation and for the management of the court's holdings. A senior civil official, often called the kalantar, acted as a link between the crown and the urban population, helping to organize taxation and to represent the townspeople. A police and magisterial official, commonly called the darugha, was responsible for public order, and a market inspector supervised weights, measures, and conduct in the bazaar. Judges administered Islamic law, and customary and royal authorities handled other disputes. At the neighborhood level, headmen, often called kadkhodas, represented residents and handled local matters, and occupational leaders and religious communities handled their own affairs.

These arrangements mattered because the state relied on intermediaries. Taxation, rent collection, dispute settlement, street maintenance, and security were carried out partly by officials of the court and partly by local notables and community leaders. The royal government could exert strong pressure when it chose, but day-to-day governance depended on negotiation with the people and institutions that knew the city's neighborhoods. Armenian New Julfa had its own leadership, and Jewish and other communities also managed internal affairs, within limits set by the state.

Public services were uneven. Public baths, which were important for hygiene, social life, and religious purity, were found throughout the city, often built as endowments. Fountains and canals supplied water, and bridges and streets required repair. Risks of fire and disease were significant in dense districts, and the evidence for municipal response is limited. Policing relied on watchmen, officials, and neighborhood networks, and security varied by district and time. Commercial disputes could be heard by market officials or judges, and rent and tax conflicts moved through the crown's administrative channels.

One of the central questions about the Safavid capital is how far the royal government could effectively supervise so large and complex a population. The evidence suggests a mixed answer. In the royal precinct and the square, state control was strong and visible. In many neighborhoods, authority was delegated to local leaders, religious institutions, and occupational groups, and the state depended on their cooperation. The city's order therefore rested on a combination of royal power, legal institutions, and negotiated local arrangements, rather than on direct state management of every street.

Water supply was the service on which the city's life most depended, and it was also the most contested. Isfahan lay on the Zayandeh Rud, whose flow varied with the seasons and with the winter snowfall in the mountains to the west, and the river's water was divided among gardens, fields, and urban districts through a system of canals. Allocation followed customary and administrative rules, and disputes over water rights, damaged channels, and the unauthorized diversion of water appear in the evidence as recurring problems. An earlier, unsuccessful diversion scheme had been considered under Tahmasp I. Under Abbas I, officials investigated bringing water from the upper Karun basin in the Kuhrang region across the Zagros watershed to the Zayandeh Rud. The court ordered a survey in 1618 and further work around 1620–1621, but this Safavid scheme did not bring the planned additional flow to Isfahan. It should not be confused with later, much more successful twentieth-century Kuhrang diversion projects. The attempted engineering reveals how seriously the crown took the problem of feeding and supplying a growing capital, and why reliable river flow could not be assumed. (See the Encyclopaedia Iranica study of the Zayandeh Rud and Robert D. McChesney’s 1988 analysis of Safavid building documents.) Chapter 1's discussion of plateau water and settlement is directly relevant here: the monumental city was built in a place where water was a limiting resource, and its gardens, pools, and fountains were possible only because of careful management.

The provisioning of food required comparable organization. Grain, meat, fruit, and other staples were brought from the surrounding countryside and from more distant regions, and officials supervised markets, weights, and prices. In ordinary times the system functioned through merchants, millers, bakers, and butchers, and the state intervened mainly to maintain order and revenue. In years of poor harvest or disruption, shortages and price rises affected the poorest residents most severely, and the capacity of the government to respond depended on its resources and on the attention of particular officials. The evidence for specific episodes is uneven, and historians should avoid generalizing from single reports.

Agriculture around the capital was closely tied to the city. Gardens and orchards supplied fruit and vegetables, and the countryside supplied grain and animals. Travelers noted the many pigeon houses around Isfahan, where droppings were collected for use as fertilizer, particularly for melon and vegetable cultivation. Such details are a reminder that the city was embedded in a rural economy, and that the work of the surrounding villages and the labor of those who farmed crown and private land underpinned the prosperity of the capital.

Division V — Everyday Life, Court Culture, and the Legacy of Safavid Isfahan Section 62.17

Living in Safavid Isfahan: Households, Food, Work, and Public Sociability

Most of the people of Isfahan lived in houses built around courtyards, a form common across Iranian cities. A courtyard house turned its rooms inward, offering privacy, shade, and a space for domestic work, and many wealthier houses included gardens, pools, and separate reception rooms. Poorer households lived in smaller dwellings, sometimes sharing courtyards or renting rooms. The household was the basic unit of economic and social life, including family members, servants, apprentices, and sometimes enslaved or dependent persons. Work, childrearing, cooking, and religious observance were organized within it.

Food markets supplied bread, grain, meat, fruit, dairy products, and other staples from the surrounding region, and cooks and vendors prepared meals for sale. Clothing and household goods came from local workshops and from imports. Wages and prices are poorly documented, and historians should be cautious in reconstructing the cost of living. Daily life followed religious and commercial rhythms: the call to prayer, market hours, Friday congregational worship, festivals, and fasts structured the day and week. Public sociability centered on markets, bathhouses, mosques, and the coffeehouses that appear in travelers' accounts, where men gathered to talk, hear poetry and storytelling, and pass time.

Women's lives are harder to recover. Women were members of households, and in Islamic law they could own property and, within limits, manage it and establish endowments. Royal and elite women were patrons of buildings and religious foundations, and women worked in textile production and domestic industries and took part in religious and neighborhood life. But most surviving sources were written by men, and many describe the segregated life of elite households from outside. European travelers wrote about women with limited access and strong assumptions, so their observations must be used with caution. The experiences of poorer women, enslaved women, and minority women are especially thinly documented.

The experiences of people differed by position. A courtier or official lived in a large house with servants and access to the court. A merchant family might combine a town house, warehouse space, and garden. An artisan lived near the workshop, often above or behind it, and relied on apprentices and family labor. A service worker, such as a porter, water carrier, or servant, lived on irregular earnings and in cramped housing. The following features set these perspectives side by side and then reconstruct a working day.

Childhood and education offer another view of everyday life, although the evidence is thin. Boys from many urban families attended elementary schools, often attached to mosques or run by teachers in neighborhood premises, where they learned to read the Qur'an and, in many cases, Persian texts, writing, and arithmetic. Boys who were trained as artisans or merchants often entered apprenticeships at an early age, learning within the workshop or the family business. Some elite girls were taught at home, though the extent of women's literacy is difficult to measure, and most women learned domestic and craft skills within the household. Advanced study of law and religious sciences took place in madrasas and was open to men who could find a teacher and support.

Public life included entertainment and leisure that cut across, though did not erase, social differences. Coffeehouses, which European travelers described, were gathering places where men heard poetry, stories, and news. Gardens, public squares, and the long avenue were used for promenades, picnics, and games. Festivals, religious commemorations, and markets brought crowds together at fixed points in the year. The court's attitude toward some forms of entertainment, including drinking, music, and smoking, varied with the ruler and with religious pressure, and the regulation of public morality fluctuated across the seventeenth century. Such variations show that the lived city was shaped by changing policy as well as by habit.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Capital: Households, Artisans, Merchants, Minorities, and Service Workers

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical person, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

A court household: For the royal and elite households, the city was a setting of audience, service, and display, organized by rank and protocol. Its women lived largely out of public sight, though some acted as patrons and managers of property. Court life depended on the work of servants and of a market that supplied its needs.
A merchant family: For merchants, Isfahan was a node in trade networks. The bazaar, the caravanserai, and the court's demand offered opportunity, while customs, rents, and royal favor shaped risk. Success could bring wealth and the ability to build and endow, but depended on the goodwill of officials.
An artisan workshop: For artisans, the great building and luxury production meant work, but also dependence on patrons and on the state's willingness to pay. A master, journeymen, and apprentices worked within the conventions of their trade, and some artisans were attached to royal workshops by obligation.
A service worker: For porters, water carriers, servants, and laborers, the city meant irregular wages, crowded housing, and exposure to the demands of construction and ceremony. Their experience is the least documented, and it is usually visible only through the observations of others.
An Armenian neighborhood: For the Armenians of New Julfa, the capital was both the place of a coerced resettlement and a base for commerce and communal life. Wealthy merchants and poorer residents experienced it differently, and the community's autonomy depended on royal favor.
Everyday Life and Social History

A Working Day in Seventeenth-Century Isfahan

This is a composite reconstruction from patterns documented in the sources, not an account of any named person or particular day. Before dawn, the call to prayer woke the quarters, and households began their work. Bakers fired ovens, water carriers drew from canals and fountains, and shopkeepers in the bazaar opened shutters and swept their frontage. In a tile workshop near the market, a master and his apprentices prepared glazes and loaded a kiln for a commission destined for a mosque under construction. In a weaving shop, workers set up looms for silk cloth.

Through the morning, porters carried goods from caravanserais to shops, merchants negotiated prices and credit, and officials of the market inspected weights and measures. In the royal square, stalls opened beneath the arcades, and in the palace precinct servants carried provisions through the gateway. In a courtyard house, women of a merchant family managed the household, cooked, and supervised servants. At midday, people prayed and rested, and in the afternoon the baths and coffeehouses filled with men who had finished their work. In the evening, the markets closed and the neighborhoods quieted, while kilns cooled and ledgers were balanced.

The evidence for such a day comes from travelers, chronicles, and the surviving fabric of the city, and historians must read it against its biases, especially its focus on elites and men. Many details, such as wages, hours, and the experience of laborers, can only be inferred.

Section 62.18

Royal Ceremony, Festivals, Diplomacy, and Urban Performance

The Safavid court used Isfahan as a stage for political display. Royal processions moved through the city, diplomatic receptions were held in the palace and its gardens, and festivals such as Nowruz, the Persian New Year, were celebrated with public events. Shi'i commemorations, including the mourning ceremonies of Muharram, were major occasions in which court and city participated, and processions and gatherings filled streets and open spaces. Polo, military reviews, music, and entertainment in the royal square gave the court opportunities to display wealth, strength, and legitimacy before large audiences. Foreign envoys and visitors were received with ceremony and often invited to events, and their descriptions provide much of what we know.

Ceremony depended on many people. Artisans produced costumes, decorations, and equipment; musicians, entertainers, and servants performed and worked; soldiers marched and guarded; merchants supplied goods; and crowds attended. The architecture was the stage, but the performances were the work of people who are rarely named. Historians should distinguish the built setting from the events that took place within it, and should ask how audiences understood what they saw. A spectator might have seen a festival as royal generosity, as religious devotion, as a chance for trade and amusement, or as a demonstration of power to be endured. The sources, which mostly describe the court's view or the view of foreign guests, do not let us recover these reactions fully.

Ceremony also involved restriction and coercion. Access to the palace and to some events was limited by rank. The royal square was also a place of punishment, and public displays of royal justice, including executions and the exposure of offenders, were part of the repertoire of authority. Political demonstrations occasionally occurred there, and the state could respond forcefully. Spectacle therefore communicated both the dynasty's splendor and its capacity to punish. Understanding Safavid ceremonial life requires keeping both aspects in view, and recognizing that the same space that hosted festivals could host fear.

Diplomacy gave the city an international audience. Under Abbas I and his successors, Isfahan received envoys from neighboring and distant states, including the Mughal Empire and Russia, as well as representatives of European states, trading companies, and religious orders. Envoys often waited for audience, were entertained at banquets, exchanged gifts, and were housed in the city for extended periods. The rituals of reception communicated the shah's rank and the court's wealth, while visitors' own accounts record what they were allowed to see. Because diplomats and travelers also sought commercial and political advantage, their descriptions reflect their purposes as much as the city itself.

The royal square demonstrates the point that urban architecture and ritual were designed to work together. The gateway of the palace faced the square; elevated rooms and rooftop viewing existed in Abbas I’s period, while the prominent columned viewing terrace was a later addition of Abbas II around 1643–1644. Descriptions of polo matches and receptions must therefore be matched to the phase of the building in which they occurred. The stone goal posts used in polo are associated with the square's early use as a playing field, although the modern setting of the square should not be read back into the seventeenth century. Merchants' stalls and ceremonies could coexist in the same space, and the daily commercial activity of the square provided an audience for royal display. That combination of market and stage was one of the distinguishing features of Safavid Isfahan.

The principal court narratives, such as the history of the reign of Abbas I by Iskandar Beg Munshi, and the accounts by foreign visitors do not record the perspective of most participants. Artisans, soldiers, servants, and townspeople appear mainly as crowds or as providers of services. This silence does not mean that ordinary people lacked their own interpretations of royal ceremony. It reflects the limits of the surviving record, and it is a reason to read descriptions of ritual critically, asking whose account is being given and for whom.

Section 62.19

Isfahan through Travelers' Eyes and under Later Safavid Rulers

Much of what is known about Safavid Isfahan comes from European visitors. The Italian Pietro della Valle was in Persia in the late 1610s and wrote detailed letters and a travel narrative. The French jeweler Jean Chardin, who lived in and traveled through Persia in the 1660s and 1670s, produced one of the most extensive descriptions of the city and of Safavid society. Others, including diplomats, merchants, and missionaries, added their own observations. These writers were interested in monuments, markets, customs, and politics, and their accounts are rich, but they were shaped by their own expectations, by the elites with whom they dealt, and by language barriers, and they sometimes repeated stereotypes.

Travelers disagree about the size of the city. Some estimates of Isfahan's population, given by particular observers, range from well under a hundred thousand to several hundred thousand, and they were based on impressions, hearsay, and counts of houses or other indicators. These figures cannot be verified, and the exact population is unknown. Historians treat them as claims made by particular observers rather than as demographic totals, and many prefer cautious ranges.

The city continued to change after Abbas I's death in 1629. His successors completed the royal mosque, added palaces and garden pavilions, built bridges, and expanded residential districts. Abbas II's reign saw the Chehel Sotun and the Khaju Bridge, and the later seventeenth century saw additional pavilions and gardens. Under Sultan Hosayn, in the early eighteenth century, further major foundations were built, as Chapter 66 will discuss in the context of the late Safavid state. Over time the cultural character of the capital shifted, from the court-driven construction of its first decades to a more mature city with settled institutions, elite gardens, and commercial networks. Observers writing in different decades therefore saw different cities, and a description from the 1670s should not be read back into the 1620s.

The sequence of rulers after Abbas I affected the city in different ways. Safi, who succeeded in 1629, reigned until 1642. Abbas II followed from 1642 to 1666, a period with substantial building in and around the capital and one in which several European visitors recorded their impressions. Suleiman reigned from 1666 to 1694, and Sultan Hosayn from 1694 until the fall of the dynasty's capital in 1722. Historians differ on how far these reigns represent continuity or decline, a question for Chapter 66. For the study of the city, the main point is that Isfahan continued to function as the capital, and that its buildings, markets, and communities were maintained and developed within changing political and economic circumstances.

Travelers' accounts also carry assumptions that influenced how they described Persian society. European writers often compared Safavid practices with those of their own countries, and some framed Persian government through ideas about oriental despotism that were current in Europe. Chardin, for instance, combined detailed, often reliable observation with judgments about Persian rulers and institutions that reflected European debates. Modern historians value these accounts as evidence of what visitors saw and heard, but they test them against Persian chronicles, documents, and the physical remains of the city. When travelers agree with local sources, confidence grows. When they stand alone, caution is needed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Three Isfahans: The Old City, Abbas's New Capital, and Later Safavid Additions

What Changed

  • The royal center shifted southwestward, and a coordinated square, avenue, and palace precinct were added
  • Large new religious, commercial, and public buildings were built, and new communities such as New Julfa were established
  • Later rulers added palaces, bridges, and gardens, and residential districts expanded
  • The city's role changed from a regional center to the dynasty's principal capital

What Stayed the Same

  • The river, the oasis setting, and the dependence on water allocation
  • The old Friday Mosque and bazaar as living institutions
  • Commerce and craft production organized by occupation
  • Endowment as the basis for religious and public institutions

When and how: Change was gradual and cumulative, occurring over decades and reigns, rather than abrupt. Who experienced it: The court, officials, and builders experienced the change most directly; residents of the old city, many artisans, and ordinary households experienced greater continuity in daily routines, though they were affected by new rents, demands, and opportunities. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Safavid Isfahan cannot be dated to a single moment or credited to a single ruler, and that old and new coexisted.

Section 62.20

Historical Synthesis: Isfahan as the Spatial Expression of the Safavid State

Safavid Isfahan expressed the political order of its builders in stone, tile, water, and commerce. The royal square organized authority, religion, and exchange in one space. The palace gateway displayed and controlled access to the shah. The mosques tied the dynasty to religious legitimacy, with a court-centered chamber on one side and a monumental congregational mosque on another. The Qaysariya and the bazaar linked royal building to the commercial economy. The avenue, gardens, and bridges extended the royal landscape and created spaces of leisure. Neighborhoods, endowments, and municipal institutions anchored the capital in the life of its inhabitants. Together these features made the city a spatial expression of the state, but one that depended on inherited foundations and on the daily activities of its people.

The city's brilliance coexisted with costs. Its monuments were financed by taxes, rents, and royal revenues, and built by labor that was sometimes paid and sometimes obligatory. A community of Armenians arrived by forced deportation. Social inequality structured access to water, space, and security, and minorities lived under unequal conditions. The same square that hosted festivals hosted punishment. A fair assessment of Isfahan must keep these elements together: artistic achievement, commercial vitality, and religious patronage on one hand, and coercion, hierarchy, and inequality on the other.

The achievements of Abbas I should be distinguished from those of others. He chose the capital, initiated the royal square, the avenue, and several major buildings, and supported the new districts and communities. But the old city, the artisans, the guilds, the merchants, the Armenian community, the religious scholars, the officials, the royal women, and later shahs all shaped the capital. Abbas II and his successors completed or added palaces, bridges, and gardens, and later patrons built major institutions. The city that travelers described in the 1670s was not the city of 1600, and the credit for its making is shared.

Isfahan's later vulnerability should be noted without anticipating the narrative of Chapter 67. A capital whose prosperity relied on royal favor, fiscal revenue, commerce, and political stability was exposed to political and military crisis, and Chapters 66 and 67 will examine how the late Safavid state faced such pressures. For present purposes, the lesson is that the monumental landscape was sustained by a fragile combination of resources and relationships. Chapter 63 now turns from the city to the commercial networks, particularly the silk trade and the Armenian merchants, that connected Isfahan and the Safavid state to the wider world.

The question of how to describe the relationship between the city's unity and its inequality can now be answered more precisely. Isfahan was integrated in the sense that its parts were linked: the royal precinct with the old city by the bazaar, the northern and southern banks by bridges and canals, the court with markets and religious institutions through patronage and endowment, and the different communities through trade and daily contact. It was unequal in the sense that these links did not give residents equal access to wealth, water, safety, legal protection, or political voice. A merchant of New Julfa, a courtier, a bazaar apprentice, a water carrier, and a woman in an elite household inhabited the same capital but experienced it differently. A history of the city that includes both integration and inequality is more accurate than one that stresses only monuments or only exploitation.

Isfahan's historical reputation has been shaped by later memory. The Safavid city has become a symbol in modern Iranian and international discussions of architecture, art, and national heritage, and the principal square was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1979 as Meidan Emam, Esfahan, using the name it received after the revolution of that year. Such recognition has encouraged conservation and public interest, but it can also encourage a view of the city as a finished masterpiece rather than a place that was repeatedly built, altered, and restored. Students should therefore ask how modern appearances relate to seventeenth-century realities and treat the surviving monuments as evidence that has itself been preserved, repaired, and reinterpreted over time.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has made the city of Isfahan the principal subject of Safavid history. Its central argument is that Safavid Isfahan was not merely a collection of royal monuments but an integrated, socially unequal urban environment in which ceremony, religious patronage, commerce, household life, and public activity intersected, and that its creation depended on inherited urban foundations, substantial investment, specialized labor, migrant and displaced communities, and sometimes coercive state policies.

The chapter began with Isfahan before the Safavids: a city on the Zayandeh Rud with irrigated agriculture, caravan connections, a Seljuk-era Great Friday Mosque, an old square, bazaars, and neighborhoods. Abbas I inherited a functioning city, and the old center remained vital after the royal focus shifted southwestward.

The formal relocation of the principal royal seat to Isfahan in 1597–1598 followed earlier Safavid movements of the court from Tabriz to Qazvin. Strategic security, central location, political aims in relation to the Qizilbash, and the city's resources and symbolism all contributed, though the reasons are known largely through court explanations. Construction began before and continued long after the formal designation.

The new royal district was added to the old city and linked to it by commercial passages. The royal square, the palace precinct, the Chahar Bagh avenue, the river, and later New Julfa formed a spatial system. Financing and construction depended on royal funds, endowments, commercial rents, officials, merchants, and a large workforce of skilled and unskilled laborers. Whether the result reflected a master plan or cumulative development is debated, and the evidence favors multi-patron, incremental development within a shared royal program.

The four sides of the royal square formed a political and architectural system. Ali Qapu staged royal visibility and controlled access to the palace; the mosque associated with Shaykh Lotfollah expressed court-centered piety and learning; the Shah Mosque provided monumental public worship and solved the problem of aligning the square with the qibla; and the Qaysariya connected the ensemble to the bazaar. These buildings were completed over time and must be attributed to the correct reigns and patrons.

The working city rested on markets, caravanserais, workshops, and occupational organization. Luxury objects and architectural decoration depended on networks of specialized workers, and royal demand tied construction to craft production. The Chahar Bagh, the gardens, and the great bridges expressed royal display, but they also depended on the river and on a water system whose distribution was unequal. Chehel Sotun, Khaju Bridge, and Hasht Behesht belong to later reigns.

Neighborhoods and institutions shaped everyday life. New Julfa began as a product of forced deportation and became a community with churches, leadership, schools, and merchant wealth, though experiences within it varied. The city's population was diverse, and its social life involved both interaction and unequal status, so that the cosmopolitan and segmented interpretations each capture part of the truth. Waqf endowments, madrasas, mosques, churches, and other institutions supported religious and public life, while municipal governance relied on officials, headmen, and community leaders.

Households, work, food, bathhouses, and coffeehouses made up the daily experience of residents, whose lives varied by wealth, gender, religion, and occupation. Evidence about women, workers, and the poor is thin and filtered through elite and foreign observers. Royal ceremony, festivals, diplomacy, and public punishment used the city as a stage, and the participation of artisans, musicians, soldiers, and crowds made them possible.

Travelers such as Pietro della Valle and Jean Chardin provide detailed but partial evidence, with population estimates that cannot be verified. The city changed after Abbas I's death, with later rulers adding palaces, bridges, and districts. Comparing European, Persian, and Armenian sources shows how perspective shapes evidence.

The chapter concluded that Isfahan was the spatial expression of the Safavid state, one of the dynasty's most consequential achievements, sustained by religious patronage, commerce, skilled labor, and diverse communities, and also by taxes, coercion, and inequality. The achievements of Abbas I are distinct from those of later shahs, patrons, merchants, artisans, and residents. Its vulnerability to later crises is addressed in later chapters, and the next chapter turns to the commercial networks connecting Isfahan to the wider world.

Chapter Reflection

Isfahan matters because it shows how political authority can be turned into a landscape. The Safavid state, which had survived defeat, factionalism, and frontier war, expressed its confidence and its aims in a square, a gateway, mosques, an avenue, gardens, and bridges. The study of that landscape reveals what lay behind it: water, rents, workshops, endowments, and the labor of many people who rarely appear in royal chronicles.

The chapter also shows that cities are made by accretion. The old Seljuk and later city did not vanish when Abbas I moved the court, and later shahs added their own palaces and bridges. Recognizing this protects students from crediting a single ruler with a collective achievement, and from reading today's appearance of heritage sites back into the seventeenth century.

The history of New Julfa, of artisans and service workers, and of the city's minorities reminds us that magnificence and coercion could coexist. Armenian and Persian sources, and the cautious use of European travelers, allow a fuller picture than any one voice, though many people remain barely visible. That limitation is itself part of the historian's task.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter links the imperial consolidation of Chapter 61 to the commercial and social histories that follow. Chapter 63 examines the silk trade, the Persian Gulf, and Armenian commerce that connected Isfahan to the world, and Chapters 64 and 65 widen the lens to Safavid society and religious authority across the empire. The fate of the capital in the early eighteenth century, treated in Chapter 67, can only be understood against the achievements and vulnerabilities described here.
