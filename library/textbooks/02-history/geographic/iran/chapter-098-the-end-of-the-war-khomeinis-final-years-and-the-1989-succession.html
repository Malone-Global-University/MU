Chapter Opening

On 4 June 1989 the Assembly of Experts met in Tehran to choose a successor to a leader who had founded the state it served, while the constitution that defined the office was still being rewritten. Within a year the Islamic Republic had ended an eight-year war, executed thousands of political prisoners, lost its designated heir, revised its constitution, and replaced its founder. It survived all of this by reorganizing itself.

The Assembly of Experts, the body of clerics charged with selecting and supervising the Leader, assembled within hours of the announcement that Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini had died. The occasion was both a funeral gathering and a constitutional emergency. The 1979 Constitution gave the Assembly responsibility for naming a successor, yet the person widely expected to be that successor, Grand Ayatollah Hossein Ali Montazeri, had been removed from the position in March. The constitutional qualifications for the office, which in the 1979 text were tied to the rank of marjaʿ-e taqlid, a senior religious authority followed by lay believers, were under revision by a council that Khomeini himself had appointed in April and that had not finished its work. The Assembly therefore had to act under one legal framework while the framework was being replaced by another.

That problem cannot be understood without the preceding year. In July 1988 Khomeini had accepted United Nations Security Council Resolution 598 and, in a statement remembered for its imagery of poison, acknowledged that he was reversing a policy he had defended for six years. The decision followed Iraqi recoveries on the battlefield, financial strain, the mounting cost of the Gulf confrontation with the United States, and the judgment of senior commanders and officials that victory was out of reach. Within weeks of the decision the state carried out one of the gravest episodes of political violence in its history: the secret execution of imprisoned opponents, mainly members and supporters of the Mojahedin-e Khalq (MEK) and of leftist organizations.

This chapter treats these events as connected but not identical. War termination, repression, succession, and constitutional change had different immediate causes, involved different actors, and left different legacies. They intersected, however, in a shared institutional problem. The Islamic Republic had been built around the extraordinary personal authority of its founder, and that authority had been used in the final years to resolve disputes the formal institutions could not resolve. Khomeini’s letter of January 1988 asserting the primacy of the state’s interest, the creation of the Expediency Council in February 1988, and the 1989 amendments belong to a single process by which personal arbitration was turned into institutional procedure.

The chapter’s evidence is uneven. For the diplomatic history there are public United Nations records. For constitutional change there are the two texts of the constitution, published decrees, and referendum records. For the leadership transition there are the published letters of Khomeini and Montazeri, Montazeri’s memoirs, and the recollections of participants such as Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, all of which are later accounts or partisan documents to be weighed as such. The Assembly of Experts’ deliberations were not fully published, so reconstructions of what was said on 4 June depend on participants’ testimony. For the 1988 executions, the Iranian government has never released a full account, and historians rely on survivor testimony, family testimony, leaked or published official correspondence, and investigations by human rights organizations. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes documented facts from later claims.

The chapter has five divisions. The first examines the end of the war: the military and financial crisis of 1988, Resolution 598, Khomeini’s acceptance, and the incomplete peace. The second examines the postwar inheritance of an exhausted economy, thousands of veterans and displaced families, the prison killings, and factional disputes over postwar priorities. The third follows Khomeini’s last decisions, including his statement on absolute guardianship, the rise and fall of Montazeri, and the Rushdie fatwa. The fourth, the constitutional centerpiece, examines the 1989 amendments. The fifth narrates Khomeini’s death, the selection of Ali Khamenei, the referendum and presidential election of 28 July, and the reorganized state.

The boundaries with neighboring chapters are deliberate. Chapter 93 explained the original constitution, Chapter 95 the revolutionary consolidation, and Chapters 96 and 97 the war’s origins and conduct. Chapter 99 will examine Rafsanjani’s reconstruction program, and later chapters the Revolutionary Guards’ economic expansion and Khamenei’s long-term consolidation of authority. Here those subjects appear only as background or as foreshadowing.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the military, economic, and international pressures that led Iran to accept Resolution 598 in 1988.
  2. Distinguish the adoption, acceptance, and implementation of Resolution 598, and the ceasefire from a comprehensive peace settlement.
  3. Analyze Khomeini’s explanation of the ceasefire as evidence of the relationship between revolutionary principle and state interest.
  4. Describe the economic and humanitarian inheritance of the war without attributing every difficulty to warfare alone.
  5. Assess the evidence for the 1988 executions of political prisoners, distinguishing documented names, attributed estimates, and unresolved totals.
  6. Trace the development of Khomeini’s doctrine of absolute juristic guardianship from the 1979 constitution to January 1988 and 1989.
  7. Evaluate why Montazeri was designated, why he lost the designation, and why the rupture cannot be reduced to one event.
  8. Compare the 1979 and 1989 constitutional provisions on the Leader, the executive, the judiciary, and amendment.
  9. Explain the difference between marjaʿiyat and juristic qualification and why the change mattered for Khamenei’s eligibility.
  10. Distinguish the initial selection of 4 June, the referendum of 28 July, and the confirmation of 6 August 1989.
  11. Interpret competing arguments about the legality and legitimacy of the succession.
  12. Evaluate whether 1989 completed the revolution’s institutionalization or began a new political era.

Historical Context

The revolution of 1977–1979 (Chapter 91) replaced the monarchy with a republic whose constitution, approved by referendum in December 1979 (Chapter 93), combined elected institutions, including a president and a parliament (the Majles), with clerical oversight. Its most distinctive feature was velayat-e faqih, the guardianship of the Islamic jurist, which made a senior jurist the supreme authority over the state. The constitution named Khomeini as Leader for life and provided that a successor would be chosen by an elected Assembly of Experts. It also reflected the compromises of its drafting: a prime minister alongside the president, a Supreme Judicial Council, and a Guardian Council to vet legislation against Islamic law and the constitution.

The years from 1979 to 1987 tested these arrangements. Presidents and prime ministers quarreled over authority, as Bani-Sadr’s fall in 1981 showed. Rival political groups were eliminated or driven from legal politics (Chapter 95). The Iraqi invasion of September 1980 (Chapter 96) turned a revolutionary government into a wartime one, and the war’s mobilization, losses, and economic distortions are the subject of Chapter 97. After the recovery of Khorramshahr in 1982, Iranian leaders chose to continue the war into Iraqi territory, with the stated aim of removing Saddam Hussein’s government, until 1988.

By 1987–1988 the international environment had shifted. The “tanker war” in the Persian Gulf drew in the navies of the United States and other states, and the Security Council, whose earlier resolutions had had little effect, adopted Resolution 598 unanimously in July 1987. Iraq, supported by many Arab states and by Western and Soviet arms sales, was better supplied than Iran, and its use of chemical weapons continued. Inside Iran, institutional disputes over how far the state could regulate the economy and property in the name of Islamic law hampered government. The Islamic Republican Party had been dissolved in 1987, removing the main party vehicle of the clerical elite, and the designated successor’s criticisms of the security apparatus were becoming public.

The chapter’s main actors were therefore working in an unusually fluid setting. The institutions were formally in place but contested; the founder’s authority was decisive yet visibly limited by age and illness; and the state faced simultaneous demands to end the war, rebuild, deal with its victims, and settle the question of who would lead it next.

Division IEnding the Iran–Iraq War
Section 98.1

Military Reversals, Economic Exhaustion, and the Crisis of 1988

In May 1982, when Iranian forces recovered Khorramshahr, the Islamic Republic had been in a position of strength. It had expelled most Iraqi forces from its territory, its mobilization of Revolutionary Guards and Basij volunteers (Chapter 97) had proved effective, and its leaders believed that continuing the war would topple Saddam Hussein. By early 1988 almost none of those conditions held. The war had settled into a series of costly offensives that produced limited gains, and Iraq had improved its air power, artillery, logistics, and defensive works with large external supplies of money and weapons.

The year 1988 brought a run of Iraqi successes. In April Iraqi forces recaptured the Faw peninsula, which Iran had held since 1986, in an offensive of unexpected speed. Further Iraqi attacks in the following weeks and months retook positions near Basra and, in June, the Majnoon islands. Iraq also used chemical weapons in these operations, as it had earlier in the war. The Iraqi attack on the Kurdish town of Halabja in March, after Iranian forces had entered the area, drew international attention, though historians treat its causes and context separately from the main fronts. The immediate military effect was that Iran’s forces, which had been the attacking side for most of six years, were now responding to Iraqi initiative.

Iran’s manpower problem was not only a matter of casualties but of motivation and organization. Volunteer mobilization, which had provided large numbers of young recruits for earlier offensives, yielded fewer volunteers by 1987–1988, and the government turned more to conscription. Commanders reported shortages of heavy weapons, spare parts, and aircraft. Sanctions and arms embargoes, along with the costs of black-market purchases, made replacement expensive. The government’s revenues were constrained by low oil prices after the 1986 collapse and by attacks on oil facilities and shipping, and the budget deficit and inflation were widely felt.

The Gulf naval confrontation added a different risk. After Kuwaiti tankers were reflagged under the American flag in 1987, the United States Navy escorted convoys, and incidents multiplied. In April 1988, after the USS Samuel B. Roberts struck an Iranian-laid mine, American forces attacked Iranian oil platforms and destroyed or damaged several Iranian warships. On 3 July 1988 the USS Vincennes shot down Iran Air Flight 655, killing all 290 people aboard. The incident outraged Iranian opinion. Leaders also drew from it the lesson that the United States could escalate at will, and some later accounts treat it as a factor in the decision to end the war, though the extent of its influence is debated.

Finally, the gap between aims and outcomes had widened. Iranian leaders had declared that the war would end only when Iraq’s government was removed and Iraq accepted responsibility, which was a position that Resolution 598 only partly addressed. By mid-1988 the conditions of 1982 had reversed. Then, Iran had held the initiative and its enemy had been disorganized; now the opposite was true, and the political cost of declaring victory had risen along with the cost of continuing to fight.

The relationship between military capacity and political purpose had changed since the liberation of Khorramshahr. A government defending internationally recognized territory could appeal to a wide constituency that did not necessarily agree with the revolutionary leadership on other questions. Continuing the conflict across the frontier demanded a different justification: the government had to persuade soldiers and their families that the political results expected from another campaign justified the additional sacrifice. As the prospect of imposing a settlement on Baghdad diminished, the relationship between the achievable military objective and the declared political objective became increasingly unfavorable. This distinction helps explain why the same leaders could support continuation in 1982 and acceptance of a negotiated ceasefire six years later without accepting the Iraqi account of the war's origins.

The imbalance was also a matter of replenishment. A military organization may retain substantial personnel and still lose the ability to sustain a major campaign if repairs, transport, medical facilities and supplies cannot keep pace with losses. The conventional army and the Revolutionary Guards had different recruitment systems and institutional cultures, but neither could escape dependence on industrial capacity and public revenue. The decline in oil income following the mid-decade price collapse made this dependence especially important. Resources assigned to restoring equipment were unavailable for housing, imports and civilian investment. The ability of Iraq to purchase supplies abroad and maintain access to credit made the strategic calculation more difficult for Iran.

Cause and Effect — Feature 1

Why Continuing the War Became Unsustainable

Long-term causes

Years of attritional warfare, dependence on mass mobilization, arms embargoes, the 1986 oil-price collapse, and growing regional and international hostility to Iran.

Short-term causes

Iraqi offensives in spring and summer 1988, continued chemical attacks, shortages of equipment, and the weakening of volunteer mobilization.

Immediate context

Confrontations with the United States Navy in the Gulf, including the destruction of Iran Air 655 on 3 July 1988.

Effect

Senior leaders concluded that victory was unattainable at acceptable cost and recommended accepting Resolution 598.

Historical complexity: No single factor decided the matter. Military assessments, finance, domestic morale, and the risk of confrontation with the United States interacted, and participants later gave different weights to each.

Section 98.2

United Nations Resolution 598 and International Peace Diplomacy

The United Nations had been involved in the war from its first weeks. Security Council Resolution 479 of September 1980 called for an end to the fighting, and Resolution 514 of July 1982 called for a ceasefire, withdrawal to internationally recognized boundaries, and observers. Neither was implemented. Iraq, which had initiated the war, was willing to accept ceasefires when it was losing ground, while Iran, which saw itself as the victim of aggression, would not accept one that left Saddam Hussein in power and did not identify Iraq as the aggressor.

Resolution 598, adopted unanimously on 20 July 1987, was a more comprehensive and more carefully negotiated document. It demanded an immediate ceasefire and the withdrawal of forces to internationally recognized boundaries; it called for the release and repatriation of prisoners of war; it requested the Secretary-General to send observers to verify and supervise the ceasefire and withdrawal; it asked him to explore the entrusting of an impartial body with inquiring into responsibility for the conflict; it recognized the need for reconstruction efforts, with international assistance, once the war ended; and it called for negotiations on a comprehensive settlement and for regional security arrangements. The resolution was adopted under Chapter VII of the Charter, which makes it binding in form, though the Council did not itself coerce compliance.

Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar was the central mediator. His task was to move the two governments to accept the resolution as a package in a sequence they could present at home as consistent with their own positions. Iraq publicly welcomed the resolution but had reservations about its sequence; Iran’s leaders criticized it for not naming Iraq as the aggressor first and refused an unconditional ceasefire. Iran’s response in 1987 was to say that it could accept parts of the resolution, in particular an inquiry into responsibility for the war, before any ceasefire. This reflected a position that held for a year, and the delay can be understood partly as a negotiating stance and partly as a reflection of internal disagreement.

The interpretation of the resolution is important to understanding what followed. Resolution 598 provided a framework for terminating the conflict; it did not itself end it, and it left two difficult issues unresolved: the order in which the ceasefire, withdrawal, prisoner exchange, and inquiry would occur, and who would determine responsibility. Over the following years, each side would accuse the other of obstructing implementation. The text matters, finally, as the thing Iran’s leaders eventually accepted, and in accepting it they gave up the requirement that Iraq’s guilt be established before any ceasefire.

The diplomatic importance of the resolution lay in its combination of immediate obligations and questions requiring additional negotiation. Its demand to stop hostilities did not itself adjudicate the parties' competing claims about responsibility. The proposed inquiry into responsibility, the possible study of reconstruction needs and the consideration of regional security offered Iran reasons to participate without guaranteeing it the verdict or compensation it wanted. The resolution also appealed to outside states to avoid enlarging the conflict. Students reading the operative paragraphs should distinguish the Council's decisions from requests directed to the Secretary-General and from matters left for consultation. These distinctions indicate who was expected to take action and which results depended on cooperation. See the text of Resolution 598 in the documentation section.

The Secretary-General faced a problem of sequencing rather than simply persuading both governments to endorse a declaration. Iran wanted its claims about aggression addressed; Iraq favored bringing the fighting to an end on terms that did not immediately require it to accept responsibility. A ceasefire before final agreement could appear to one party to surrender bargaining leverage, whereas insisting on a final settlement first could permit months or years of further fighting. The intermediary's contribution was to establish a common procedure that did not require agreement on all those issues at once. The August arrangements paired an effective ceasefire with direct talks under United Nations auspices, leaving the remaining obligations to be worked out through further diplomacy.

Primary Source Window — Feature 2

United Nations Security Council Resolution 598 (20 July 1987)

Creator: UN Security Council · Date: 20 July 1987 · Type: Binding-form diplomatic resolution adopted under Chapter VII, unanimous vote

Context: The resolution followed seven years of war and the escalation of the Gulf tanker conflict. It was negotiated among the permanent members of the Council, which is itself a significant fact.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The Council demands that the two states observe an immediate ceasefire, cease all military actions, and withdraw to internationally recognized boundaries; it asks for the release and repatriation of prisoners and for UN observers; it calls for an impartial inquiry into responsibility for the conflict; and it urges negotiations toward a comprehensive, just, and honorable settlement.

Analysis: The document sets out the main elements of a settlement without sequencing them. Its inclusion of an inquiry into responsibility acknowledged a core Iranian demand, while its call for an immediate ceasefire reflected the preference of Iraq and most of the Council.

Limitations: The resolution shows what the Council agreed to say, not what each government intended to do. It cannot explain why Iran took a year to accept it, or how the parties interpreted its sequence. Students should consult the full text in the UN Digital Library.

Perspective: The text represents the compromise among Council members. The experiences of civilians, prisoners, and displaced persons are absent from it.

Section 98.3

Khomeini’s Acceptance of the Ceasefire

The decision to accept Resolution 598 was taken by Khomeini after consultation with senior officials in June and July 1988. Accounts of the process come mainly from participants’ later statements and memoirs, and should be read as such. Rafsanjani, who as Speaker of the Majles had been given wide responsibility for the war effort, and who in June 1988 was appointed acting commander-in-chief of the armed forces, was a central figure. He and others had argued for some time that the war should be ended on acceptable terms. The government under Prime Minister Mir-Hossein Mousavi reported that the economy could not sustain the war, and commanders, including Mohsen Rezaei of the Revolutionary Guards, presented assessments of what would be needed for victory. In widely repeated reports, Rezaei’s assessment listed requirements for armor, artillery, aircraft, and manpower far beyond what Iran could obtain within a reasonable time. The exact figures are cited from later accounts and should be treated with caution.

Khomeini’s own position had been that the war should continue until Iraq’s government fell. The change was accordingly more than an administrative decision; it was a reversal of a policy tied to his authority. He is reported to have discussed the matter through his son Ahmad and a small circle of advisers. On 17 July 1988, President Khamenei sent a letter to the Secretary-General notifying him that Iran accepted Resolution 598, and on 20 July Khomeini issued a public statement explaining the decision.

The statement said that the decision had been very difficult, using the image of drinking poison, and presented it as a sacrifice made out of the interest of the revolution and Islam, based on the recommendations of experts. The comparison to poison is often cited, but its significance is in how it frames the decision: as one made against the leader’s own preference, on the basis of expert advice, and as a reflection of the principle, which Khomeini was elaborating at the same time (Section 98.9), that the state’s interest could take priority over other considerations. A leader whose authority rested on revolutionary commitment could change course without conceding that the commitment was wrong, by presenting the change as service to a higher requirement.

The change also had political consequences. It affected those who had spent years in mobilization and who had lost relatives; many accepted the decision, while some regarded it as a betrayal of the war’s martyrs. The government and the Guards, for their part, interpreted it differently: as proof that the war’s defensive purpose had been achieved, and as evidence that the leadership’s sense of what was possible had changed. Historians should separate the documented assessments, which are few and mostly retrospective, from claims about individual responsibility, such as later arguments about who had delayed or accelerated the decision, which were often shaped by postwar rivalries.

An important aspect of the decision was the distribution of information among institutions. Commanders were in a position to estimate military requirements, the cabinet knew the condition of foreign exchange and supply, and officials involved in diplomacy assessed the likelihood of further foreign intervention. No single assessment necessarily determined the result. Khomeini had to make a political decision in which those different kinds of information were weighed against the public commitments made over previous years. Rafsanjani's role was important partly because he linked the legislature, the military command and the founder's circle. His later account should therefore be examined as testimony from a participant with substantial knowledge and a stake in the retrospective interpretation of the decision.

The distinction between accepting Resolution 598 and accepting an enemy's political demands helped the government present its decision domestically. The United Nations framework permitted leaders to retain their account of Iraqi aggression while agreeing that further combat could not serve Iranian interests. Khomeini's public language in July emphasized the difficulty of the decision and the responsibility of leadership, rather than presenting the ceasefire as a previously desired outcome. For citizens who had heard years of exhortations to sacrifice, the change in policy required an explanation that acknowledged those commitments. Religious rhetoric provided a means of reinterpreting the protection of the Islamic Republic as requiring a different policy under changed conditions.

Section 98.4

The August 1988 Ceasefire and the Unfinished Peace

Iran’s acceptance did not stop the fighting immediately. Iraq did not at first agree to a ceasefire date, and continued some offensive operations in the days after 17 July, which Iran protested. In late July the MEK, which had its base in Iraq and fought on the Iraqi side, launched an armed incursion into western Iran, which Iranian forces defeated in a counteroffensive within days. The episode has two separate historical significances: it was a military event with the particular context of the end of the war, and it was cited by the Iranian authorities in justifying the executions discussed in Section 98.7, a justification that, as that section explains, does not withstand analysis.

After diplomatic exchanges, the Secretary-General announced that both governments had agreed to a ceasefire to take effect on 20 August 1988. On 9 August the Security Council adopted Resolution 619, which established the United Nations Iran–Iraq Military Observer Group (UNIIMOG), with several hundred military observers drawn from a number of countries, to verify and supervise the ceasefire and the withdrawal of forces. The ceasefire took effect in the early hours of 20 August. UNIIMOG remained in place until early 1991.

The ceasefire was thus a real achievement, but it was not a peace treaty. Direct talks began in Geneva on 25 August 1988 under the Secretary-General’s auspices, but made little progress for some time. Iraqi forces continued to hold some Iranian territory, and Iran held some Iraqi territory. The sequence of withdrawal remained disputed, as did the status of the Shatt al-Arab waterway, over which Iraq had abrogated the 1975 Algiers Agreement in 1980. Tens of thousands of prisoners of war remained in captivity on both sides, and their return took years. Iran continued to demand an inquiry into responsibility.

A brief epilogue shows the settlement’s partial resolution. In August 1990, shortly after invading Kuwait and in need of peace on its eastern front, Saddam Hussein announced that Iraq accepted the 1975 border arrangements and agreed to withdraw from Iranian territory, and a large exchange of prisoners followed. In December 1991, the Secretary-General’s report concluded that Iraq’s initial attack had not been justified under the UN Charter, which met a long-standing Iranian demand, though it brought little practical redress. These later developments belong to the following period; the point here is that the ceasefire of 1988 ended large-scale fighting but not the war’s diplomatic consequences.

The observer mission was designed to work within the limits of a ceasefire rather than administer the frontier. UNIIMOG maintained contact with the military authorities of both countries, investigated reported violations and monitored compliance with the cessation of hostilities and withdrawal requirements. A complaint made by one party was not automatically accepted as an established violation; the observers sought to examine conditions and communicate their findings. This institutional process was important because a local disagreement need not immediately become a dispute between the two governments about whether the ceasefire itself had collapsed. The mission's official history records separate arrangements concerning its status with Iraq in November 1988 and Iran in March 1989, illustrating how legal and administrative details remained to be negotiated after the fighting stopped.

The POW question exposed the distance between military disengagement and social recovery. The Third Geneva Convention established obligations concerning release and repatriation following the cessation of active hostilities. Families nevertheless remained dependent on governments and humanitarian intermediaries for reliable information about relatives. Verification of identities, registration and transport required cooperation, and disagreement between the states prolonged uncertainty. For people whose relatives were missing, an official proclamation of peace could not resolve whether a family member was alive. The subsequent exchanges of prisoners, especially in 1990, therefore belong to the consequences of the 1988 decision rather than to the ceasefire date itself.

Section 98.5

The Economic Inheritance of Eight Years of Warfare

The end of the war left the government with an economy that was functioning but strained. The damage was concentrated in the west and southwest: the frontier provinces of Khuzestan, Ilam, Kermanshah, and Kurdistan had suffered from fighting, bombardment, and the displacement of civilians, and cities such as Abadan and Khorramshahr were heavily damaged. Industrial and energy facilities, including parts of the oil and petrochemical sector, had been hit by Iraqi air and missile attacks, and attacks on shipping and on Gulf installations had reduced exports at times. Reconstruction of the frontier cities was a major requirement, and the estimates of its cost put forward in 1988–1989 varied widely, as they came from different bodies using different methods; students should not treat any single figure as authoritative without examining its source.

The national accounts showed other pressures. Oil revenue, which supplied most of the state’s foreign exchange, had fallen sharply after the price collapse of 1986, and the budget was in deficit, financed in part by borrowing from the central bank. Inflation, which affected household purchasing power, was high by the standard of Iran’s earlier history, and consumer goods were rationed and subsidized through coupons and state shops, which also encouraged parallel markets. Foreign exchange was scarce and tightly allocated, and imports of machinery and spare parts were restricted, which held back industry. Investment in new capacity had been deferred during the war, and agriculture and industry had lost labor to the front. Unemployment and underemployment, particularly among young men and in the cities swollen by migration and displacement, were substantial, and housing was in short supply.

Analysts need to separate causes. The destruction directly caused by warfare was one; the effects of the 1986 oil-price collapse, which was an international market event, another; the consequences of the revolution’s own economic policies, including nationalizations, price controls, and the exodus of capital and skilled personnel after 1979, a third; and the effects of sanctions and embargoes, a fourth. Postwar Iranian officials emphasized war damage, partly because it served political purposes, and later critics emphasized mismanagement. Economists have generally argued that all of these contributed and that disentangling them precisely is difficult with the data available. Chapter 97 presented the documented economic series for the war years; this section builds on them without repeating them.

The result was a set of constraints that the next administration would inherit. A government wishing to rebuild needed money, which meant oil exports, foreign credit, or domestic revenue; it needed imports, which meant foreign exchange; and it needed to decide how far to rely on the state and how far on private enterprise, a question that divided its own supporters (Section 98.8). The reconstruction program that Rafsanjani adopted after August 1989 is the subject of Chapter 99, and the point here is that its priorities followed from the conditions described.

The constraints on recovery were more than an aggregate shortage of money. When a public enterprise needed replacement machinery imported from abroad, the state had to allocate scarce foreign currency as well as domestic credit. An official exchange rate favorable to the importer did not create additional export earnings. Priority access to foreign exchange could keep essential plants operating, but it also gave considerable discretion to the administrators who determined which imports were necessary. A reconstruction strategy consequently had to address the allocation mechanisms inherited from the war as well as the physical damage. Debates about private investment, state enterprise and foreign borrowing were in part debates about which institutions should exercise this discretion.

Section 98.6

Demobilization, Veterans, Displaced Families, and Humanitarian Obligations

When the fighting ended, a large part of the population was affected. Men who had served in the army, the Revolutionary Guards, and the Basij returned to civilian life in an economy that offered limited work, and for those who had spent years in military service or volunteering since adolescence the transition was difficult. Demobilization was not a single event: the armed forces were not dissolved, since the frontier remained tense and UN observers were deployed only along the line, but many conscripts and volunteers were released over time, and the Revolutionary Guards, which had grown into a large military organization, had to define its postwar role (a subject for Chapter 105).

The war had left many people disabled and chronically ill. Veterans with amputations, head injuries, and psychological trauma needed long-term care, as did those injured by Iraqi chemical weapons, many of whose conditions were lifelong, particularly respiratory and skin diseases. The state expanded its welfare apparatus, including the Foundation of the Oppressed and Disabled, the Martyrs Foundation, and the Foundation of Veterans Affairs, to provide pensions, housing, education, and medical care to veterans and to the families of those killed. These institutions were revolutionary foundations, part of a system in which welfare was delivered through religious and political networks as well as ministries, and their growth had consequences for the later political economy.

Families of the dead faced loss and, in some cases, uncertainty. Official commemoration emphasized martyrdom, which gave meaning and status to bereaved families, but that language did not address all experiences, and families of missing soldiers waited for years for news. Thousands of Iranian prisoners were held in Iraq, and the International Committee of the Red Cross visited camps and facilitated correspondence, though access was limited and the repatriation of prisoners did not take place until after the 1990 agreements. The distinction between the ceasefire and the release of prisoners is therefore important: ending hostilities did not return prisoners.

Civilians displaced from frontier cities had also to be housed and resettled, and many remained in other cities for years. Rebuilding Abadan, Khorramshahr, and smaller towns required decisions about where to invest, which the central government made in competition with demands from other regions. The war had also contributed to migration to the cities, and housing shortages affected both displaced families and others. These obligations explain why the state’s welfare commitments grew rather than shrank after 1988, and why the ending of the war did not end the wartime economy’s distortions.

For displaced households, the decision to return involved practical questions about shelter, documentation, employment and schooling. The destruction of a dwelling was not necessarily compensated by the existence of an empty property elsewhere, and a household might have developed new connections in its place of refuge. A merchant returning to Khorramshahr depended on the recovery of customers and suppliers as well as the repair of a shop. Agricultural families needed access to fields, water and livestock, not merely permission to cross an administrative boundary. Reconstruction thus involved the restoration of networks of economic and social relationships that could not be measured solely in kilometers of road or numbers of completed buildings.

Care for the wounded similarly connected state institutions with family life. The cost of a disability included continuing medical treatment, reduced earnings and the labor of relatives who provided assistance. Women in many households had acquired greater responsibility for the allocation of food and income during the conflict, and those obligations did not automatically end with demobilization. State recognition of veterans and bereaved households provided a channel for assistance, but eligibility rules could create differences between people with comparable material needs. The families of civilian victims and people injured outside officially recognized service categories could face different administrative problems from those whose military service was documented.

Recovery was also regionally uneven: Arabic-speaking and Persian-speaking communities in Khuzestan and Kurdish, Lur and other communities in western Iran faced different local conditions. Language alone cannot establish political loyalty or the degree of reconstruction need.

Section 98.7

The Mass Executions of Political Prisoners in 1988

The most serious episode of repression during the transition from war to ceasefire was the killing of imprisoned political opponents in the summer of 1988. It is a topic on which the historical record is substantial but incomplete, and on which the Iranian authorities have given no full public account. The account below relies on survivor and family testimony, documents that have come into the public record, including Montazeri’s own correspondence and later statements, and investigations by human rights organizations, notably Amnesty International and the Iran Human Rights Documentation Center.

The context was the prison system that had grown since 1981 (Chapter 95). Thousands of members and supporters of the MEK, and of leftist groups including the Tudeh Party and various factions of the Fedaian, had been arrested during the early 1980s and held in prisons such as Evin and Gohardasht. Many had been sentenced to terms by revolutionary courts, and by 1988 a considerable number had completed or were nearing the end of their sentences. Others were held without a sentence, or after proceedings that did not meet standards of fair trial. These prisoners were, by definition, in the custody of the Iranian state.

The immediate trigger, as officials later presented it, was the MEK’s armed incursion of late July 1988. But the prisoners had no connection to that incursion in any operational sense; they were in prison in Iran and had not taken part in it. The distinction is essential: an armed organization’s military activity abroad did not remove the lawful protections of individuals already in custody. In the weeks that followed, Khomeini issued a directive, which has been published in later years, that prisoners who remained loyal to the MEK were to be put to death, and committees of officials, usually described as including a religious judge, a prosecutor, and a representative of the intelligence ministry, were established to question prisoners in several prisons. Survivors reported that prisoners were asked about their loyalty and their willingness to repudiate their organizations, and that answers decided who lived. Prisoners were then removed in groups; many families were told nothing, and visits and mail were stopped.

After the MEK prisoners, a second phase concerned leftist prisoners. Survivors and researchers describe questioning about religious belief and, in some cases, about whether prisoners considered themselves Muslims, and the executions extended to many leftist detainees. The killings continued into the autumn of 1988. The bodies were buried in unmarked graves, often in mass graves, and families were not told where. In the following years families who sought information were refused or intimidated, and some places of burial were later built over or altered. The practice of concealing the fate of victims also meets the definition of enforced disappearance used in international law.

Estimates of the number killed differ and must be attributed. Amnesty International, in its investigations, has stated that at least about 5,000 people were killed and that the real number may be higher; other organizations and opposition groups have given higher figures, and Montazeri’s later statements referred to numbers in the low thousands for the period. Iranian officials have acknowledged executions of prisoners but have denied or minimized them, and have defended them as punishment of collaborators with an enemy in wartime. Documented lists of named victims are smaller than the estimates, because many families could not identify their relatives. Students should treat named lists, attributed estimates, and comprehensive totals as three different kinds of evidence. Calls for accountability have continued, including from United Nations human rights experts and families’ associations, and the subject remains politically contested, as later chapters show.

Montazeri's contemporaneous objections are valuable because they challenge the claim that every senior revolutionary official accepted the executions as necessary. His letters addressed the leadership from within the system and raised questions about the treatment of people already sentenced and the authority under which they were being put to death. A surviving record of his August 1988 meeting with officials further establishes that objections were made at the time rather than invented only after his political break with Khomeini. Such evidence is especially significant when official archival access is restricted. It can be compared with accounts from survivors and with contemporary human rights reporting, rather than treated as a complete register of what happened in every prison.

Investigations identify participation by judicial, prosecutorial and intelligence officials. Accounts of the Tehran commission identify Hossein Ali Nayyeri, Morteza Eshraqi and Mostafa Pourmohammadi among the officials involved; other officials are identified in the documentary and testimonial record. These names refer to reported institutional responsibilities and should not be used as a substitute for a legal adjudication of individual criminal liability. The process operated outside ordinary guarantees of a fair trial. Prior membership in an opposition organization, or a prisoner's political or religious convictions, did not relieve the state of its obligations toward a person in custody. Amnesty International's later investigation describes the killings and related concealment as crimes against humanity; this is an attributed legal assessment supported by its evidence, rather than a claim that all relevant facts have been adjudicated by a court.

Section 98.8

Political Factionalism and the Struggle Over Postwar Priorities

The suppression of independent opposition did not remove competition within the system. By 1988 the Islamic Republic’s governing elite was divided over the same questions that the end of the war made urgent: how far the state should control the economy, how to treat property and land, how open the country should be to foreign trade and investment, and how to define its relations with the world.

One group, often called the Islamic left, supported state intervention: land reform, price controls, nationalization of trade, and redistribution in favor of the poor. It was strong in the Majles and had supporters in the prime minister’s office. Opposed to it were traditional conservatives, many linked to the bazaar and to senior clerics, who defended private property and the market and were wary of state economic control. They were supported by the Guardian Council’s reading of Islamic law, which had struck down parts of the left’s legislation. A third current, associated with Rafsanjani, was pragmatic and favored economic recovery, a greater role for private enterprise and foreign capital, and an end to international isolation.

The party structure had been weakened. The Islamic Republican Party, the main vehicle of the clerical elite since 1979, was dissolved in 1987 at Khomeini’s urging, after it had become a source of disputes. In 1988 clerics of the left, led by figures such as Mehdi Karrubi, formed the Association of Combatant Clerics, breaking from the older Society of Combatant Clergy, which was conservative. The elections to the third Majles in 1988 were held under these new alignments, and the Majles contained blocs that often opposed one another on economic bills. Khomeini’s role was to arbitrate and, increasingly, to override.

The categories of 1988 should not be mechanically identified with those of the 1990s and later. The Islamic left of the 1980s, for instance, was more statist in economics and more committed to revolutionary export than the reformists of the late 1990s, and many of its figures later changed their positions. The conservatives of 1988 were also not the same as later conservative factions. Students should treat these labels as snapshots of a shifting field, and attend to the specific issues and institutions of each moment. The point to retain is that factional competition coexisted with repression of outsiders, and that the question of postwar priorities, together with the question of succession, would be settled partly through constitutional change.

The parliamentary elections of 1988 supplied an institutional setting for these competing priorities. Elections within the Islamic Republic were not contests between all the political organizations that had existed in 1979. By the late 1980s many of those organizations had been excluded or suppressed, while competition persisted among groups that accepted the constitutional order. The split between the Society of Militant Clergy and the Association of Combatant Clerics was one manifestation of this competition. Their constituencies and leaders overlapped with wider networks of merchants, seminaries, officials and veterans. The labels left and right are consequently useful descriptions of certain disputes over economic and religious policy, but do not correspond exactly to parties of those names elsewhere.

A specific problem arose when the preferences of a parliamentary majority could not be translated into enforceable law. Labor regulation, for example, concerned both the state's ambition to protect employees and the jurists' understanding of contractual obligations. A bill might be supported as a means of achieving social justice and opposed on the ground that it exceeded the permissible authority of government. Both arguments could be presented within an Islamic framework. This explains why the creation of the Expediency Council was politically significant: it created a forum in which the interests of the system could be invoked in deciding disputes that ordinary legislative review had not resolved.

Section 98.9

Khomeini and the Expansion of Absolute Juristic Guardianship

The 1979 Constitution (Chapter 93) gave the Leader broad powers, but it also contained elected institutions and a legal framework that limited government by religious law as interpreted by the Guardian Council. Khomeini’s own theory, set out in lectures of 1970, held that the jurist’s guardianship over the community extended to the government of the state. Between 1979 and 1988 conflicts between the Majles, which passed laws on land, labor, foreign trade, and other matters, and the Guardian Council, which vetoed many of them as contrary to Islamic law, produced repeated deadlock. Khomeini intervened at times, allowing some legislation under the heading of necessity, and this practice raised the question of what the basis of such authority was.

The idea of maslahat, meaning public interest or expediency, had a long history in Islamic legal thought. In the 1980s it became the justification for allowing the state to take measures that strict application of the law would prevent. A statement by President Khamenei in a Friday sermon in December 1987 that the government’s powers were bounded by Islamic law drew a response from Khomeini in a letter of 6 January 1988. In that letter Khomeini said that the government was an absolute guardianship delegated by the Prophet, that it was among the primary religious obligations and took precedence over secondary ones such as prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage, and that it could unilaterally cancel legitimate agreements when they conflicted with the interest of Islam. The doctrine is called velayat-e motlaqeh-ye faqih, absolute guardianship of the jurist.

The doctrine was not new in 1988; it had been implicit in his lectures and in his practice since 1979, and the January letter restated it in the context of a dispute. What was new was the explicit and public affirmation of its implications for ordinary law. It meant that the state’s interest, as judged by the Leader, could override the findings of the Guardian Council and the provisions of ordinary legislation. The establishment of the Expediency Council on 6 February 1988, by Khomeini’s decree, was a practical consequence: the Council was to decide cases in which the Majles and the Guardian Council disagreed. The Council’s members were appointed by the Leader and included leading officials and clerics.

Students should note two points. First, the doctrine did not claim that the Leader was free of all religious constraint, but that his judgment of the interest of the Islamic order was authoritative, and this was criticized by some clerics, including Montazeri in some respects, as exceeding what the tradition allowed. Second, the doctrine matters for the 1989 amendments (Division IV), because the constitution’s revised text recognized absolute guardianship explicitly, and because the successor would inherit an office whose powers had been defined in practice by Khomeini’s exercise of them.

The January correspondence arose out of a practical legal disagreement. The president had discussed the limits of state authority in a public religious address, and Khomeini responded by explaining his conception of Islamic government. In the translation maintained by the Iran Data Portal, examples include acquiring property for a public road and restrictions affecting religiously permissible activities. The argument was that government must be able to undertake functions required for public administration even when the ordinary application of a legal rule would obstruct them. This was not simply a proposal to enlarge the president's personal powers. It concerned the source and scope of the Islamic government's authority and, within that framework, the decisive position of juristic leadership.

A useful comparison is between an exception authorized because a particular emergency exists and a general power to determine policy in the interests of the system. In the former arrangement, the ordinary rule remains the starting point and an exceptional circumstance must be established. In the latter, the governing institution has a broader basis for assessing whether implementation of an ordinary rule is compatible with public purposes. Khomeini's argument moved the debate toward the second understanding. This created room for government intervention in economic and administrative affairs, but also raised a question about the mechanisms by which such intervention could be reviewed, limited or contested. The theoretical breadth of the claim should not be confused with evidence that every official could exercise it.

Section 98.10

Hossein Ali Montazeri and the Problem of Revolutionary Succession

Hossein Ali Montazeri, born in 1922 near Najafabad, was a senior cleric who taught in Qom and was one of the best-known students of Khomeini. He was arrested several times under the Pahlavi monarchy and was active in the movement against it. After the revolution he became a leading figure in the new order: a member of the Assembly of Experts for the constitution, a Friday prayer leader in Tehran, and an advocate of velayat-e faqih. He had religious standing as a teacher and, by the 1980s, was recognized by many as a marjaʿ. His role in building the Islamic Republic’s institutions was considerable, and this should be remembered when considering his later opposition.

In November 1985 the Assembly of Experts named him as Khomeini’s anticipated successor. The decision reflected both his religious rank and his closeness to Khomeini, and it was meant to settle the question of succession. Under the constitution the Assembly could choose the Leader only when the office fell vacant, so the designation had no formal legal status; it was a political arrangement. Montazeri was thus a successor-designate, not an incumbent.

Difficulties developed during the later 1980s. Montazeri’s office, with its own staff and network, became a rival center of influence, and its relations with the security services were poor. The Mehdi Hashemi affair was an early rupture. Mehdi Hashemi, a Revolutionary Guards official in charge of an office supporting Islamic movements abroad and a relative of Montazeri’s son-in-law, was arrested in 1986 after his group was linked to the disclosure of secret American arms dealings with Iran, the affair known as Irangate, and he was executed in 1987 after trial on other charges. Montazeri’s supporters regarded it as an attack on his circle; his critics saw it as the removal of an unruly faction. The affair damaged his standing.

Montazeri also became a critic of the government’s conduct. He objected to abuses by the security services and to the treatment of prisoners, argued for a more tolerant political order, and questioned the revolution’s foreign policy. His objections to the 1988 executions, which he communicated to Khomeini in letters and in a meeting with officials, are among the best-documented sources for those events (Section 98.7). They also placed him in conflict with the people who were exercising judicial and intelligence authority in the state.

The case of Montazeri shows tensions the Islamic Republic had not resolved: a successor chosen for his religious authority and loyalty who developed independent judgments; the distinction between religious authority and governmental power; and the question of whether those holding power could be held accountable. His later reputation as a critic, which came after his dismissal and house arrest in the late 1990s, should not obscure that in the 1980s he was an architect of the system he later criticized.

The selection of an anticipated successor served an important purpose for a system that could not rely on hereditary succession. It communicated to officials that there was an identifiable person around whom a future transition might be organized. Montazeri possessed a combination of revolutionary credentials, recognized religious expertise and experience in institutional debates that made the arrangement plausible. Nevertheless, the 1979 constitution did not create a permanent office of deputy Leader whose occupant automatically acquired all the powers of the Leader upon his death. The political expectation established by the Experts in 1985 and the actual constitutional selection of a future Leader were related but different events. This distinction explains why his withdrawal did not legally abolish the Assembly's responsibility to choose a successor.

The political authority acquired through association with Khomeini also had a potential disadvantage for an anticipated successor. Independent judgments could be understood by officials as threats to the unity of leadership rather than as ordinary disagreements among jurists. Montazeri's criticisms of abuses in the prison system and the treatment of opponents raised precisely this difficulty. A senior cleric's public stature could protect his capacity to make objections, but that same stature gave his statements wider significance. Officials concerned with unity had incentives to control the circulation of criticism, while a jurist claiming responsibility for the ethical character of the Islamic system had reasons to object to government conduct.

Section 98.11

Montazeri’s Removal and the Breakdown of the Succession Arrangement

The break came in the spring of 1989, but it had been prepared over several years. In 1988 Montazeri wrote to Khomeini, and later spoke publicly, about the executions, which he described in strong terms and said history would judge. Khomeini’s replies insisted on the necessity of the measures. In the months that followed, Montazeri continued to express criticisms in lectures and in his correspondence, which circulated beyond his circle, and in early 1989 he spoke on the treatment of prisoners and on the legitimacy of the government’s actions, in remarks that were reported abroad.

In March 1989 Khomeini wrote a series of letters. In one dated in late March he rebuked Montazeri sharply, accused him of lacking judgment and of being manipulated by others, and said that he could no longer be Leader after him. Montazeri replied and, within days, withdrew from the succession, and Khomeini accepted. The letters were publicized; the date and wording of Khomeini’s letters, Montazeri’s replies, and the public announcement should be kept distinct, since later accounts sometimes blur them. The Assembly of Experts did not formally remove him, because he had no constitutional office; the designation was ended by the withdrawal.

The consequence was that the Islamic Republic had no designated successor, an elderly Leader, and a constitution that required the Leader to be a marjaʿ, a requirement few clerics both met and could be trusted by the political establishment to fulfill. The senior marjaʿ were mostly quietist or distant from the state, and the officials who controlled the institutions did not regard them as suitable. The problem was real and was understood by the participants; it explains why, within weeks, Khomeini ordered a constitutional revision.

It would be wrong to attribute the rupture to one event. The conflict combined disagreements about repression, institutional rivalry, the Hashemi affair, and Montazeri’s growing independence, and it was probably encouraged by those who preferred a different successor or none. Participants’ accounts differ in the weight they give to each factor, and some were written to justify later positions. After the removal, Montazeri withdrew from public life in Qom and continued to teach; his criticism of the system increased in the 1990s, but that is outside this chapter.

The documentary chronology separates the loss of political confidence from the formal abandonment of the succession arrangement. Khomeini's denunciatory letter of March 26 was followed by Montazeri's withdrawal on March 27 and Khomeini's acceptance of it on March 28. The circulation and later publication of the correspondence have themselves been subjects of debate. The March letters should therefore be read in conjunction with earlier evidence, including the dispute over the prosecution and execution of Mehdi Hashemi, a figure linked to Montazeri's circle, and the objections to the prison killings. It is insufficient to explain the break as solely the consequence of one conversation or one document. The issue had accumulated political and institutional significance over several years.

The disagreement exposed a structural problem: the people who were considered to possess the highest religious qualifications were not necessarily those whom the revolutionary leadership trusted to direct the state. Montazeri's departure made the problem immediately practical. The Assembly of Experts could no longer rely on the expectation that a single, politically acceptable and widely recognized jurist would be available at Khomeini's death. The option of collective leadership remained in the original text, but a permanent council would have introduced questions about internal decision-making and the resolution of disagreements among its members. A single office was more compatible with the desire of many officials for a clear line of political authority.

Section 98.12

Khomeini’s Final Foreign Policy Decisions and Revolutionary Legacy

The end of the war, together with Iran’s isolation, raised the question of what foreign policy the Republic would pursue. Pragmatic officials favored restoring relations with Europe and Asia and attracting investment, while revolutionary doctrine emphasized independence from both superpowers and support for Islamic movements. Khomeini’s final decisions showed both tendencies.

In early January 1989 he sent a message to the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, delivered by a delegation of Iranian figures. The message urged the Soviet leadership to study Islam seriously and suggested that communism had reached the end of its course; it was received as an unusual combination of ideological advocacy and diplomatic outreach. It occurred when the Soviet Union was withdrawing from Afghanistan and when relations with Moscow, which had supplied Iraq, were improving. Whatever its religious tone, it contributed to the normalization that followed.

The second event was the controversy over Salman Rushdie’s novel The Satanic Verses, published in Britain in September 1988. The book offended many Muslims, who regarded its portrayal of the Prophet and early Islam as blasphemous, and led to protests in Britain, India, Pakistan, and elsewhere. On 14 February 1989 Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for the author’s death, and the Iranian authorities and affiliated foundations later offered rewards. Britain and other European governments condemned the ruling, the European Community countries recalled their ambassadors for a time, and Iran’s relations with Britain were broken in March.

The episode can be examined as a historical and diplomatic event without endorsing either view of the book. It raised questions about freedom of expression, about the authority of a religious ruling issued in one country concerning an individual in another, and about the limits of state jurisdiction. Within Iran, some argued that it rallied the Muslim world around revolutionary leadership at a time of domestic difficulty and diverted attention from the war’s outcome; others saw it as the product of Khomeini’s convictions. Evidence for his motives is limited, and later accounts attribute different purposes to him. In practical terms, the fatwa complicated the effort to restore ties with Europe, and its consequences continued for years.

The two decisions illustrate the problem that faced the state after Khomeini: his personal authority allowed him to pursue contradictory lines, and a successor would need to decide among them through institutions. The relationship between personal authority and institutional continuity thus formed the background of the constitutional revision.

The final months also illustrate the difference between a state's bilateral diplomacy and a religious claim directed beyond its boundaries. Khomeini's February 1989 pronouncement concerning Salman Rushdie was addressed to a transnational audience rather than negotiated with the government of the country in which the author lived. Britain and other European governments treated the resulting threats as a matter of sovereignty, security and freedom of expression. For the Iranian government, the dispute arose at a time when increased commercial and diplomatic engagement could have helped reconstruction. These competing consequences demonstrate why the end of the war cannot be treated as a uniform shift from ideological confrontation to diplomatic accommodation.

The succession raised the question of which of these commitments could be modified and by whom. A political leader might adjust trade and diplomatic arrangements, but a pronouncement defended as a binding religious ruling presented a different kind of problem. Officials had to consider not only anticipated gains from changing policy but also the political significance of publicly repudiating an action associated with Khomeini. The new leadership inherited these constraints along with the constitutional office. The details of subsequent diplomatic pragmatism belong to Chapter 99. Here the important conclusion is that the foreign policy inheritance was internally varied: it included the negotiated termination of a major conflict, continuing claims of revolutionary responsibility and a growing requirement for access to international economic resources.

Section 98.13

The Constitutional Revision Council and the Reform Process

The constitution approved in December 1979 was a compromise among competing visions, and a decade of experience had revealed its weaknesses. Executive authority was divided between a president, elected by the people, and a prime minister, who chaired the cabinet and was approved by the Majles. In practice this produced conflict, first between President Bani-Sadr and his rivals, then between President Khamenei and Prime Minister Mousavi, who disagreed over appointments and economic policy and whose division of responsibilities complicated the wartime administration. Disputes between the Majles and the Guardian Council, already described, had been handled by the ad hoc Expediency Council. The judiciary was headed by a five-member Supreme Judicial Council, whose collective form made administration slow. The text also did not say how the constitution could be changed.

Montazeri’s removal made the leadership qualifications under the 1979 text an urgent issue: eligibility was linked to the qualifications for religious emulation, with collective leadership available as an alternative. On 24 April 1989 Khomeini issued a directive appointing a revision council to address leadership, executive and judicial centralization, state broadcasting, legislative arrangements, institutional arbitration and future amendment procedures. Twenty members were designated by Khomeini and five elected by the Majles. President Khamenei participated, while Ayatollah Ali Meshkini, president of the Assembly of Experts, chaired the revision council. Deliberations began in late April; its thirty-eighth and final session took place on 8 July.

The original constitution contained no explicit amending procedure. Khomeini’s appointment of the council was therefore an exercise of the Leader’s authority and not the use of a constitutional mechanism. The revised text introduced such a mechanism in a new Article 177, which would govern future revisions. The distinction is significant: the revision of 1989 was carried out under extraordinary authority, whereas later revisions were to follow a defined process.

The council’s deliberations were not conducted in public, although a record of the debates was later published, and the speed of its work reflected the situation. Khomeini’s death on 3 June interrupted it, but the council resumed within days, completing the proposed amendments in July. They were submitted to a national referendum on 28 July 1989. Thus the council deliberated, as the Assembly of Experts chose a successor, under the old text, and the new text was approved only afterwards. Section 98.18 returns to the legal consequences.

The directive of April 24 responded to requests from governmental institutions, including members of the Majles, for reform. Its scope was not an unrestricted commission to design a different political system. It identified defects that the leadership wanted addressed and established the body that would prepare the changes. The distinction between appointment of the council and final approval of the amendments is important. The council did not hold a separate popular mandate to enact its decisions. Its work depended initially on the authority of Khomeini and subsequently on the acceptance of the proposed package by voters in the referendum.

The reformers were also dealing with several distinct forms of collective government. A permanent leadership council under the original provisions was one possible form of religious-political leadership; the Supreme Judicial Council was a collective administrative head for an entirely different branch. The Expediency Council, by contrast, had been created to resolve disputes among institutions. A decision to replace a collective executive or judicial arrangement with a single head did not necessarily imply opposition to councils altogether. Indeed the revised constitution gave other councils explicit standing. The institutional logic was to assign clear responsibility for direction while preserving consultation and arbitration where conflict across branches was expected.

Section 98.14

Redefining the Qualifications and Powers of the Supreme Leader

The central change concerned the office of Leader. In the 1979 Constitution, Article 5 provided that during the Occultation of the Twelfth Imam the guardianship of the community belonged to a just and pious jurist, aware of the age, courageous, and capable of administration, whom the majority recognized as Leader. If no single jurist was so recognized, a council of jurists with these qualifications would exercise leadership. Article 107 named Khomeini as Leader because he was recognized as a marjaʿ and as leader of the revolution, and provided for the Assembly of Experts to select a successor from among the marjaʿ, or to choose a council. Article 109 set out the qualifications, including the qualifications needed for issuing legal opinions and for religious emulation.

To understand the amendment, students must distinguish three things. A mujtahid is a jurist qualified to derive legal rulings independently. A marjaʿ-e taqlid is a mujtahid whom lay believers choose to follow, a position conferred by recognition within the religious community, not by any state. A political leader is someone who holds governing authority. The 1979 text connected juristic competence and the qualifications associated with emulation to the third. The 1989 text kept the requirement of juristic scholarship but removed the requirement of marjaʿiyat, and replaced the alternative of a leadership council with a single Leader. The amended Article 5 refers to a just, pious jurist, aware of the age, courageous, and capable of administration; Article 109 lists scholarship sufficient for legal opinion in the various fields of jurisprudence, justice and piety, and political and social insight, courage, and administrative ability. Article 107 was amended to provide for the Assembly to select the Leader on these qualifications and named Khomeini as the first.

The consequence was that the eligibility pool changed: a cleric with sufficient juristic scholarship and political ability could be Leader even if he was not a senior source of emulation. This mattered directly to Khamenei, who was a mid-ranking cleric and had not been regarded as a marjaʿ. It also had longer-term effects on the relation between the religious establishment and the state, since it reduced the dependence of the Leader on the independent recognition of the clergy. Students should not conclude, however, that the amendment removed all religious requirements: the Leader remained required to be a jurist of recognized scholarship, and the Assembly of Experts, composed of clerics, remained responsible for judging his qualifications.

The amendments also enlarged the Leader’s powers and defined their exercise. Article 57 now referred to the authority of the Leader as absolute guardianship, giving constitutional form to the doctrine Khomeini had asserted. Article 110 expanded the Leader’s responsibilities to include determining the general policies of the system after consultation with the Expediency Council, supervising their implementation, and resolving problems that ordinary means could not. His appointments included the head of the judiciary, the heads of the state broadcasting organization, the commanders of the armed forces, and jurist members of the Guardian Council. Article 111 provided for the Assembly to investigate and decide on his incapacity, with a temporary council exercising his functions in the interim. Thus the amendments clarified and extended the existing removal procedure and specified interim leadership, while the powers of the office were stated more fully than before.

The original and amended constitutions also differed in the relationship between recognition by believers and institutional selection. Under the original Article 107, the recognition already enjoyed by Khomeini supplied a special basis for his position. For future selection the Experts were to identify a qualified religious authority or, failing an appropriate individual, establish collective leadership. Article 109 referred to the qualifications necessary for giving legal opinions and for religious emulation, as well as political and administrative competence. The wording must be read alongside Articles 5 and 107 rather than isolated from them. One point of debate was whether possessing the necessary qualifications and already enjoying recognition as a source of emulation were identical conditions. Khamenei's status made the distinction especially consequential.

A comparative reading of Article 110 reveals both continuity and expansion. The original constitution already assigned the Leader command of the armed forces, important appointment powers, formalization of the presidential election and a conditional power to dismiss the president. The revised provisions added express responsibility for setting and supervising general policies, resolving differences among the branches, ordering referenda and referring otherwise insoluble problems to the Expediency Council. The expanded list also reflected changes elsewhere in the constitution, such as the appointment of a single judicial head. The constitutional office became more explicitly the source of system-wide direction. These provisions did not make the president or parliament redundant, but they established the overarching framework within which those bodies exercised their own responsibilities.

The revised Article 111 must be read with equal care. The Experts can determine that a Leader can no longer perform his duties, has lost the required qualifications or did not possess them initially. The last of these grounds is especially important in a system where religious competence and political capacity are assessed by a body of experts. In the event of a vacancy, temporary functions pass to a council specified in the article until a new Leader is chosen. This interim arrangement is distinct from the permanent collective leadership option removed from Articles 5 and 107. The existence of a constitutional procedure for removal also does not establish the frequency or effectiveness of its use. Formal accountability and practical political accountability are separate historical questions.

Section 98.15

Abolishing the Premiership and Reorganizing Executive Government

The second major reform concerned the executive. The 1979 Constitution had created a dual executive. The president, elected by the people, was the highest official after the Leader and was responsible for implementing the constitution, but the prime minister, nominated by the president and approved by the Majles, headed the cabinet and ran the government. This arrangement reflected the compromises of 1979, including the wish of the drafters to limit presidential power after the experience of the monarchy and, in the early period, the particular political situation.

In practice the division proved unworkable. Bani-Sadr’s conflicts with Prime Minister Rajai and others were part of the political crisis of 1981 (Chapter 95). From 1981 to 1989 Khamenei as president and Mousavi as prime minister differed over cabinet choices and over the direction of economic policy, and Khomeini had at times to settle disputes between them. Under wartime conditions, with the need for rapid decision on supply and finance, the overlapping responsibilities were an obstacle, and many of the political elite concluded that one clear head of administration was better.

The 1989 amendments therefore abolished the post of prime minister. The president became the head of the executive branch, with the responsibility of heading the Council of Ministers and appointing ministers, subject to the Majles’s vote of confidence. Article 113 defined the president as the highest official after the Leader and responsible for the implementation of the constitution and for the head of the executive, except in matters directly concerning the Leader. Article 124 provided for vice-presidents, one of whom might be designated the first vice-president, with the president able to assign functions to them; Article 126 gave the president direct responsibility for planning, budget, and administrative and employment matters; and Articles 133 and 134 provided that ministers be appointed by the president, subject to the Majles’s approval, and that the president supervise the cabinet’s work.

The reform strengthened the presidency within the elected branch, but it did not make the president the highest authority. The Leader continued to determine general policy, to command the armed forces, to appoint the head of the judiciary and other officials, and to resolve major disputes, and the Majles retained its powers to question and impeach ministers and to give confidence. The abolition of the premiership was not the abolition of executive government but its concentration. The new arrangement was in effect when Rafsanjani took office as president in August 1989, and it shaped the presidencies that followed.

An example helps clarify the reorganization. Suppose a government needs a coordinated program of rebuilding industrial facilities, allocating foreign exchange and expanding transport. Under the original dual executive, decisions involving ministries and cabinet coordination fell primarily to the prime minister, while the president had a distinct popular mandate and constitutional responsibilities. Disagreement could occur not only about which projects were desirable but about who had authority to coordinate them. Under the amended system, the president directed the Council of Ministers and was responsible for overall executive coordination, subject to other institutions' constitutional powers. This hypothetical example describes the allocation of functions, not a specific project documented in 1989.

Article 124 authorized presidential deputies and assigned the first vice-president a coordinating role with the president's approval. The post was not a separately elected American-style vice presidency. Article 126 placed the planning and budget and administrative affairs within presidential responsibility while permitting delegation. These arrangements allowed the president to distribute work within the executive without recreating an independent premier. The Majles, meanwhile, retained the vote of confidence in individual ministers and the power to question them. Articles 89 and 134 helped define accountability for the president and cabinet. A more unified executive therefore did not imply that the cabinet could govern without parliamentary support.

Section 98.16

The Expediency Council, Judiciary, National Security, and Constitutional Amendment

Four further reforms completed the reorganization. The first was the constitutional recognition of the Expediency Discernment Council in a new Article 112. Khomeini had created the Council by decree in February 1988 to arbitrate between the Majles and the Guardian Council; the amendments made it a constitutional body, with members appointed by the Leader, and extended its functions to advising him on matters he referred to it, including general policy. Its relation to the other bodies is that when the Guardian Council rejects a law passed by the Majles and the Majles does not accept the objection, the Expediency Council decides, in the interest of the system. Students must keep the dates apart: it was established in 1988 by order, and incorporated into the constitution in 1989.

The second reform replaced the Supreme Judicial Council of five members with a single head of the judiciary, a jurist appointed by the Leader for a five-year term under Article 157, responsible for judicial administration. Mohammad Yazdi was the first to hold the post. The reform made the judiciary easier to direct but also concentrated authority in an official appointed by the Leader. The third reform created the Supreme National Security Council under Article 176, chaired by the president, with the task of coordinating national security policy and including the heads of the branches, military commanders, and relevant ministers, with its decisions subject to the Leader’s approval; it replaced the earlier Supreme Defense Council provided for under Article 110 of the 1979 text.

The fourth reform, Article 177, set out a procedure for future revision: the Leader, after consulting the Expediency Council, would order a revision council, whose proposals would be put to referendum. It protected certain features of the system from amendment, including the Islamic character of the system and its laws, the basis of faith, the republican character of government, the guardianship of the jurist, the dependence of administration on popular vote, and the official religion. The limits are of lasting importance, since they place the foundations beyond revision by this route.

There were also other changes. Article 175, as amended, placed state radio and television under the supervision of a council representing the three branches, with the head of the organization appointed by the Leader; since broadcasting was the main medium of mass communication, this had political significance. Taken together, the amendments gave constitutional form to practices that had grown up since 1979, transferred some arbitration from the founder’s personal intervention to named bodies, and made the Leader the appointing and supervisory authority for several of them.

The Expediency Council illustrates how a temporary innovation can become part of permanent government. Before February 1988, legislation disputed by the Guardian Council could require intervention by Khomeini or another accommodation. After the decree creating the council, disputes could be addressed in a dedicated forum; after the constitutional revision, the institution's existence and principal functions were expressly provided for. Article 112 specifies the circumstances in which it deals with a dispute and provides for matters referred by the Leader. It is not simply a second elected legislative chamber or an appellate court reviewing every Guardian Council decision. The Leader's role in appointing its members also connected arbitration to the constitutional leadership.

The changes in the judiciary responded to administrative concerns, but their significance extended beyond efficiency. A single head appointed for five years could establish more unified direction over judicial organization and administrative responsibilities than the former council. Articles 157 and 158 should be read together to identify the position and its functions. The change did not convert every judicial decision into a decision personally made by the Leader; judges and courts continued to have roles specified elsewhere. The relevant distinction is between appointment and supervision of the judicial administration, on the one hand, and adjudication of particular cases on the other. Whether judicial institutions could act independently in sensitive political cases remains a question of practice as well as legal design.

The Supreme National Security Council likewise had a broader coordinating remit than the old Supreme Defense Council. Under Article 176 the president chairs it; membership links civilian branches, military bodies, ministers and representatives of the Leader. Its responsibilities include coordinating political, intelligence, social, cultural and economic activities connected with security and defense policy, within general policies determined by the Leader. Its decisions require the Leader's confirmation. This structure allows the executive to organize policy across ministries while keeping final approval in the leadership. It reflects the lesson of wartime administration that military affairs cannot be treated as isolated from economic and diplomatic decisions.

Article 177 established a revision procedure unlike the extraordinary process of 1989. After consultation with the Expediency Council, the Leader issues a directive to the president identifying the matters to be considered. The revision council includes Guardian Council members, heads of the three branches, permanent Expediency Council members, representatives of the Experts and other designated groups and appointees. Its conclusions require the Leader's confirmation and signature before being submitted to a popular referendum, where an absolute majority of participants must approve. The unamendable provisions encompass both Islamic and republican elements of the constitutional system, including the official religion and school. These restrictions limit the legal scope of future reform under this article, without eliminating political disagreement over how the protected principles should be interpreted.

Section 98.17

Khomeini’s Death and the Immediate Succession Crisis

Khomeini was in his late eighties, and his health had been failing. In the spring of 1989 he underwent surgery for internal bleeding, and the leadership was aware that his death was probably near. Preparations were made at the same time as the constitutional revision: the council was working, the question of who would succeed him was being discussed among senior officials, and Khomeini had prepared a political-religious testament addressed to the nation. He died on the night of 3 June 1989, and his death was announced on state media in the early hours of 4 June.

The announcement was followed by official mourning and by public displays of grief in Tehran and across the country. The funeral, held on 6 June, drew very large crowds to the capital, and the proceedings were disorderly: mourners surged around the body, and the shrouded corpse was nearly seized by the crowd, so that the ceremony was suspended and the burial took place later. Estimates of the crowd vary widely and are not reliable. It would also be misleading to treat the mourning as evidence of unanimous political agreement. Many Iranians grieved for the leader of the revolution; many others, including opponents, families of the executed, and exiles, responded differently, and the evidence for these responses is more limited because state media dominated coverage.

While the public mourned, the institutional question had to be answered at once. The 1979 text required the Assembly of Experts to choose a successor when the office became vacant, and the political leadership wished to do so without delay, to avoid a vacuum and to prevent rivalry among factions. The revision of the constitution was not complete, and the qualifications in force were those of the old text. The Assembly met on the day of the announcement. Khomeini’s testament, which was read publicly, asserted continuity with his own principles and warned against departures; it was also a document that different factions would interpret in different ways.

The death of the founder tested whether the institutions that had been formally set up in 1979, and reorganized in the preceding months, would carry authority without him. The answer in the following days was yes, in part because the political elite shared an interest in survival and in part because the procedure for succession, however flawed, had been followed.

Khomeini's death presented a problem unlike the resignation or replacement of an ordinary government official. Much of the Islamic Republic's public identity had been expressed through his personal standing as a revolutionary and religious authority. His constitutional responsibilities were extensive, but his influence also operated through personal relationships with senior clerics, political officials and representatives in public institutions. Some of these relationships had not been created by the constitution. The new Leader would inherit the office and its formal responsibilities, but could not simply inherit the founder's biography or the same forms of recognition. The success of a succession therefore had to be evaluated in terms of institutional operation rather than resemblance between personalities.

The speed of the Assembly's meeting mattered to other institutions. Ministries had to continue functioning, military command had to remain defined and negotiations with Iraq had to proceed. The constitutional revision was not yet complete and the presidency itself would soon be reorganized. A prolonged struggle over leadership might have made those processes more difficult. The Assembly's decision therefore served an immediate continuity function, even though questions about its conformity with the old text remained. This is why the history of the transition must connect the funeral and public mourning with the constitutional proceedings without interpreting either as sufficient evidence of the legitimacy of the other.

Section 98.18

The Assembly of Experts and the Selection of Ali Khamenei

Ali Khamenei was born in Mashhad in 1939 and studied in Mashhad and Qom, where he was a student of Khomeini. He was active in the movement against the monarchy, was imprisoned and exiled several times, and after the revolution was a founder of the Islamic Republican Party and a leading figure of the new state. In June 1981 he was injured in a bomb attack that left his right arm impaired. After the assassination of President Rajai he was elected president in October 1981 and was re-elected in 1985. He had served as Friday prayer leader in Tehran, and as a representative of Khomeini in the Supreme Defense Council. At the time of Khomeini’s death he held the rank of hojjatoleslam, a middle clerical rank, and was not recognized as a marjaʿ.

The Assembly of Experts met on 4 June to select a leader. The deliberations were not published in full, and what is known comes largely from participants’ accounts, including Rafsanjani’s, which have the limits of memory and self-interest. According to these accounts, the Assembly considered a leadership council as well as a single leader, and various individuals were mentioned. Rafsanjani reported statements by Khomeini that Khamenei was suited to leadership and that the leader did not need to be a marjaʿ, and Ahmad Khomeini was said to have supported this. Khamenei is reported to have objected, saying that he lacked the religious qualifications and that he was not suited to the post. Accounts also report that others suggested that the Assembly appoint a council of leaders, which was not adopted.

The Assembly chose Khamenei provisionally; Encyclopaedia Iranica and published scholarship report 60 votes in favor among 74 members present. Within hours his selection was announced, and he was called Ayatollah in the media, an elevation of title that itself drew comment among clerics.

The initial selection’s constitutional basis was contested. On 4 June 1989, the 1979 text still linked leadership eligibility to qualifications for religious emulation and provided a collective alternative. Khamenei was not a recognized marjaʿ, and the Assembly chose an individual rather than a council. On 28 July the referendum approved revised qualifications without the emulation requirement and removed the permanent leadership council option. On 6 August the Experts selected him again under the amended framework. These decisions must not be collapsed into a single appointment.

Interpretations of the first selection diverge. Defenders argue that the emergency justified it, that the revision, ordered by Khomeini himself, was already in progress and intended to permit just such a choice, and that the confirmation cured any defect. Critics argue that the selection violated the text then in force, that it was arranged by a small group, and that the qualifications were altered to suit the person. Historians can establish the sequence of events and the content of the two texts; whether the process was legitimate depends on the criteria of legitimacy used, which the evidence does not settle.

The Assembly's deliberations are a particularly useful case for examining evidence of political decisions. A participant's memoir may report a private statement that cannot be independently reconstructed word for word. A recording can establish what was said at the meeting but may not establish all the negotiations that preceded it. A constitutional text establishes the formal qualifications but does not necessarily explain how the assembly interpreted them. Where these forms of evidence overlap, the historian can distinguish an established event from a reported justification. The reported intervention of Rafsanjani concerning Khomeini's assessment of Khamenei is significant in precisely this sense: it was presented to the Assembly as evidence of the founder's preference, rather than itself constituting an amendment to the constitution.

The consideration of collective leadership was not merely an incidental detail. Under the original framework a council of suitably qualified jurists was contemplated as an alternative to an individual. Its potential advantage was that religious qualifications and governing experience might be distributed across several people; its potential disadvantage was that it could make the attribution of authority and responsibility less clear. The choice to elect a single individual before the referendum therefore had consequences for the organization of the state, independent of the identity of that individual. The subsequent removal of the permanent leadership council option from the constitutional text made that organizational preference a durable feature of the revised system.

Section 98.19

The Constitutional Referendum and Rafsanjani’s Presidential Election

On 28 July 1989 two separate votes took place. In the first, electors were asked to approve or reject the amendments prepared by the revision council. In the second, they elected a president, as Khamenei’s presidential term approached its end following his selection as Leader. The two coincided in date, and polling arrangements were shared, but they concerned different questions, and the results must be recorded separately.

The referendum was approved by a large majority among those voting. The Iran Data Portal records 16,428,976 participants from 30,139,598 eligible voters (54.51 percent turnout); Encyclopaedia Iranica reports approximately 97.4 percent approval. It is important to distinguish approval among participants from the proportion of all eligible voters who approved. A high approval rate among a turnout of roughly half means that those who voted yes were a smaller share of the electorate, and the turnout figure is part of the evidence that must be weighed. The referendum offered a single package, so voters could not accept some amendments and reject others, and the restrictions on organized opposition limited the political choices available.

The presidential election was an uncompetitive contest. The Guardian Council screened candidates, and only two were approved: Rafsanjani, then Speaker of the Majles, and a minor candidate, Abbas Sheibani. Rafsanjani received 15,550,528 votes and Sheibani 635,165, out of 16,452,677 ballots recorded by the Iran Data Portal. Turnout was 54.59 percent. His campaign emphasized reconstruction and the restoration of the economy. Mousavi, the prime minister, left office, because the amendments abolished the post, and many of his functions passed to the president.

Rafsanjani’s election brought to the presidency a politician who had been central to the wartime government, the succession, and the constitutional revision. He took office in August 1989 and nominated a cabinet that included technocrats alongside figures from other factions, and the Majles approved it. Expectations were that he would rebuild the economy and loosen some restrictions, while keeping the Republic’s institutions. The implementation of his program, however, is the subject of Chapter 99, and the point here is that the new executive arrangements, the new Leader, and the new president took shape together.

The official electoral statistics make it possible to compare participation across the country. The Iran Data Portal's compilation lists presidential turnout of about 85.39 percent in Ilam, 59.02 percent in Khuzestan, 49.5 percent in Tehran and 38.68 percent in Sistan and Baluchestan. These figures demonstrate variation in participation, not the motives of voters or abstainers. Wartime experience, the accessibility of polling places, differences in local mobilization and the choices offered to voters are possible subjects of investigation. A comparison of provincial figures should consider the relevant population and electoral geography in 1989, rather than automatically apply the boundaries of later provinces.

The national candidate totals also make clear why denominators matter. Dividing Rafsanjani's reported vote count by the total number of ballots gives approximately 94.52 percent; dividing it by the eligible electorate gives about 51.60 percent. These are answers to two different questions. The first indicates the size of the winner's share of participation; the second indicates the proportion of eligible people recorded as having voted for him. The remaining ballots not attributed to either candidate should not be assigned to a particular category without additional documentation. Likewise, abstention cannot be treated as if it were a recorded vote against the winner. The electoral result established the presidency under the institutional rules, but cannot by itself establish the views of every citizen.

The screening of candidates is relevant independently of the size of the majority. The portal reports that only two of seventy-nine registrants were allowed to stand after the vetting process involving the Ministry of Interior and Guardian Council. Participation occurred within that restricted choice. The referendum presented an additional analytical problem because voters were asked to accept or reject a package containing changes to multiple branches of government. A voter who supported abolishing the premiership but opposed the revised qualifications of the Leader could not register those preferences separately on the ballot. The results consequently provide evidence of approval of the package, not separate approval percentages for each provision. These distinctions will remain important when studying political competition in the following chapters.

Section 98.20

The Reorganized Islamic Republic and the End of the Founding Era

By late 1989 the Islamic Republic had changed in important respects. The war had ended after eight years, though without a peace settlement. The founding Leader was dead, and his successor held an office whose qualifications and powers had been rewritten. The executive was concentrated in a president with no prime minister; arbitration among the branches was carried out by a constitutional Expediency Council; the judiciary was headed by one person appointed by the Leader; and a Supreme National Security Council coordinated security. The Assembly of Experts had shown that it could select a Leader, and the constitution had a procedure for its own amendment.

The continuities were equally important. The state’s legitimacy continued to rest on the revolution and on guardianship of the jurist, now stated more explicitly. The Revolutionary Guards and the revolutionary foundations, which had grown during the war, remained powerful and would have a large role in the postwar economy (Chapter 105). The repression of 1988 left a legacy of unanswered questions and a population of bereaved families, and the state’s refusal to account for it became a lasting grievance. Factional competition within the governing order continued, over the economy, foreign policy, and the meaning of the revolution. The economic task, rebuilding with scarce resources, was ahead of the new government.

A new relationship was emerging between revolutionary authority and administrative government. In the founder’s lifetime, the authority of the revolution was personal and was used to resolve disputes the institutions could not. After 1989 more of that authority was embodied in offices and procedures, with a Leader who held broad powers but lacked the founder’s religious and revolutionary stature, and a president with a stronger administrative role. How these two offices would interact, and how far the Leader would build his own base, was open, and later chapters show the answers.

The Islamic Republic in late 1989 was neither identical to the revolutionary government of 1979 nor a different regime. The amendments made institutional what had been improvised, extended the Leader’s powers, ended the divided executive, and removed the barrier of religious rank to the succession. They did so inside the original framework of the guardianship of the jurist and without a break in leadership. Continuity of the Republic and transformation of its governing structure thus went together.

One way of assessing the transformation is to compare the problems faced by the Islamic Republic in 1979 with those confronted a decade later. The original constitutional settlement had to accommodate institutions emerging from the overthrow of the monarchy, a powerful revolutionary Leader and elected republican offices. By 1989 the question was no longer whether these institutions could be established but how they could operate without their founder. The replacement of the premiership, clarification of leadership qualifications and constitutional recognition of arbitration bodies addressed recurrent disputes about governing authority. The reforms did not abolish the tension between different claims to legitimacy; they created new institutional arrangements for managing it.

The preservation of political authority had a cost for those excluded from the governing system. The repression discussed in this chapter cannot be treated as an accidental footnote to otherwise technical administrative reform. Political choices concerning who was permitted to organize, participate and criticize shaped the circumstances in which the revised institutions came into operation. Conversely, the fact of repression does not remove the need to analyze the legal differences between the 1979 and 1989 constitutions. Historians need both kinds of evidence to explain how the state acquired continuity. The experience of a family waiting for information about a prisoner and the experience of an official trying to resolve a legislative dispute belong to the same history, but they illuminate different aspects of it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the events of 1988–1989 were a reorganization of the Islamic Republic and not an abandonment of the revolution. Four overlapping processes, the end of the war, postwar repression and economic strain, the loss of a designated successor, and constitutional revision, converged in a single year and shaped the transfer of authority from the founder.

The war ended because Iranian leaders concluded that victory was unattainable at acceptable cost. Iraqi offensives in 1988, shortages, financial strain, the continuing use of chemical weapons, and the confrontation with the United States in the Gulf narrowed Iran’s options. Resolution 598, adopted in July 1987, provided the framework, and Iran’s acceptance in July 1988, explained by Khomeini through the image of poison, reversed a policy tied to his authority.

The ceasefire took effect on 20 August 1988 under UNIIMOG supervision, but it was not a peace treaty. Territorial, prisoner, and responsibility questions remained, and they were only partly settled in 1990 and 1991.

The postwar state inherited a strained economy, thousands of veterans and bereaved families, displaced civilians, and obligations that the end of the fighting did not end. The foundations that provided care became part of the political economy.

In the summer of 1988 the state executed large numbers of imprisoned political opponents, first MEK supporters and then leftists, in secret proceedings, and concealed the burial places. Estimates must be attributed to the investigations that made them, and named lists, estimates, and totals are different kinds of evidence. The episode remains a central unresolved question of accountability.

Khomeini’s January 1988 letter and the creation of the Expediency Council show the expansion of the doctrine of absolute guardianship and the use of expediency to resolve institutional conflict. Montazeri, designated in 1985, lost his place in March 1989 after cumulative disagreements, leaving no successor.

The constitutional revision of 1989 removed the requirement of marjaʿiyat, replaced the option of a leadership council with a single Leader, expanded his powers, abolished the premiership, constitutionalized the Expediency Council, created a single head of the judiciary and the Supreme National Security Council, and established an amendment procedure.

Khomeini died on 3 June 1989. The Assembly of Experts selected Khamenei on 4 June under the old text, the voters approved the amendments on 28 July, and the Assembly confirmed him under the new text on 6 August. The same day as the referendum, Rafsanjani was elected president.

The resulting state retained the revolution’s legitimacy and the guardianship of the jurist, and changed the structure through which authority was exercised. The era of the founder ended, and the problems of reconstruction, political contest, and the relations among Leader, president, and security institutions defined what came next, as the following chapters show.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a revolutionary state survives the loss of its founder. The Islamic Republic did not do so by returning to its starting point, but by turning improvised authority into procedure, at a cost that included the prisoners killed in 1988 and the rivals set aside in 1989.

It also shows that the end of a war is a political process and not only a military one. The ceasefire changed the state’s priorities, left obligations to the wounded and bereaved, and altered the balance among factions.

The settlement of 1989 shaped what followed: a Leader who needed to build his own authority, a president with a stronger administrative role, an Expediency Council and a Security Council that would become central, and a Republic whose legitimacy still rested on the revolution. Chapter 99 turns to Rafsanjani’s reconstruction, and later chapters to the long consequences of the arrangements made in 1989.

For historians, the period also demonstrates the need to separate documents from memory, and sequence from legitimacy. The dates of 4 June, 28 July, and 6 August cannot be merged, and the evidence for the 1988 killings must be weighed with care.
