World War I, Foreign Occupation, Oil, Reza Khan, and the Fall of the Qajars

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between a coronation in July 1914 and an oath of office in December 1925, Iran passed through foreign occupation, a humanitarian catastrophe whose scale remains disputed, an oil economy of rising strategic value, a postwar sovereignty crisis, a military coup, and the parliamentary termination of the dynasty that had ruled since the 1790s.

On 21 July 1914, Ahmad Shah, then sixteen years old, was crowned in Tehran, ending the regency that had governed in his name since 1909. Eleven and a half years later, in December 1925, a constituent assembly amended the Supplementary Fundamental Law so that hereditary monarchy passed to the family of Reza Khan, the military commander and former prime minister who took the oath of office that month. Between these two ceremonies lay one of the most turbulent periods in modern Iranian history. The ruler crowned in 1914 never returned to Iran after leaving for Europe in 1923, and he was deposed in absentia. The ruler who took the oath in 1925 had begun his career as a soldier of modest origins in the Persian Cossack Brigade. The distance between the two is the subject of this chapter.

The chapter's central problem is how a constitutional monarchy with weak institutions was transformed into a new dynastic order. The answer developed here is that no single cause suffices. Foreign armies crossed Iranian territory throughout the First World War although the government had proclaimed neutrality. The treasury could not pay for an effective army, a reliable bureaucracy, or relief. Harvest failure, requisitioning, transport collapse, and epidemic disease combined in a subsistence crisis whose toll historians still debate. Regional movements in Gilan, Azerbaijan, Khurasan, and elsewhere proposed competing answers to the question of who should govern Iran. Successive cabinets could not establish authority across these regions. Within that environment a commander with an armed force at his disposal acquired influence that no civilian ministry could match.

The chapter also tells a story that is easily lost in political narrative. Oil, discovered in commercial quantities in the southwest in 1908, became during the war a strategic resource for the British Royal Navy, and the oilfields, pipeline, and refinery created an enclave of industrial activity with its own labor force, settlements, and diplomacy. The wartime crisis of food and health fell on villagers, townspeople, refugees, and laborers who rarely appear in diplomatic dispatches. Armenian, Assyrian, Kurdish, and Muslim communities in the northwest experienced the war as displacement, violence, and loss. A history of the fall of the Qajars that considered only ministers, generals, and ambassadors would omit much of what the collapse meant for the populations it affected.

Reza Khan's ascent was neither inevitable nor universally welcomed. Contemporaries held very different views about what Iran needed after 1918. Some wanted a strong centralized government that could restore security and resist foreign domination. Others feared that military rule would extinguish the constitutional gains of 1906. Still others sought regional autonomy or social revolution, or hoped to preserve a Qajar monarchy in which the Shah reigned under the constitution. The historian must explain why one of these projects prevailed without treating the outcome as the only possible one. The chapter therefore attends to the 1924 republican movement, which failed, and to the parliamentary maneuvering of 1925, in which formal legality and coercion are difficult to separate.

The evidence for this period is richer than for most earlier chapters, but it is uneven and partisan. It includes treaty texts, parliamentary proceedings, cabinet proclamations, newspapers, British, Russian, Ottoman, German, and American diplomatic and military records, memoirs of politicians and officers, company records of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, missionary and relief reports, and local accounts of hunger and disease. Iran had no reliable nationwide census or system of death registration, so demographic claims must be read with caution. Foreign archives describe Iran largely from the viewpoint of imperial officials. Iranian memoirs were often written decades later, in a political climate shaped by the Pahlavi state and then by the Revolution of 1979. Part of the chapter's task is to teach students to weigh these sources against each other.

Finally, this is the concluding chapter of Volume VI. It takes over from Chapter 79, which narrated the constitutional struggle through 1911, and hands on to Chapter 81, which examines the consolidation of Pahlavi rule from the coronation of 1926 onward. Section 80.20 therefore offers a synthesis of the whole Qajar period, from the dynastic reconstruction under Agha Mohammad Khan in Chapter 69 to the events of 1925. It asks why a dynasty that had rebuilt Iranian political authority at the end of the eighteenth century ultimately lost the capacity to preserve its own rule.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Iran declared neutrality in November 1914 and why that declaration could not prevent its territory from becoming a theater of war.
  2. Distinguish neutrality, military occupation, and belligerency, and apply these distinctions to Russian, Ottoman, British, and German activity in Iran.
  3. Trace the movement of Russian, Ottoman, and British forces across northern, western, and southern Iran and assess the consequences for civilian populations.
  4. Analyze why some Iranian nationalists cooperated with Germany and the Ottoman Empire, distinguishing tactical alliance from ideological agreement.
  5. Explain how Iranian petroleum became a strategic resource during the war and describe the Anglo-Persian Oil Company's relations with the British government, the Iranian state, and regional leaders.
  6. Evaluate the causes of the famine and mortality crisis of about 1917–1919 and the limits of the evidence on which mortality estimates rest.
  7. Assess the Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919, including the difference between its signature and its legal ratification, and the reasons for its rejection.
  8. Compare the Jangali movement, the Khiabani movement, the Khurasan gendarmerie rising, and the Shikak revolt as distinct political projects.
  9. Interpret the coup of 21 February 1921 and distinguish documented British encouragement from claims of a centrally directed British plan.
  10. Examine Reza Khan's military and political consolidation between 1921 and 1925, weighing gains in state capacity against costs to constitutional government and regional autonomy.
  11. Reconstruct the parliamentary and constituent assembly decisions of 1925, separating legislative procedure from the political pressure under which they were taken.
  12. Synthesize the main causes of the end of Qajar rule across the dynasty's whole history.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What can a government do to protect its territory when it lacks the means to enforce its declared neutrality?
  2. Why did oil, a commodity with a modest place in the Iranian economy before 1914, become central to the foreign policy of a great power?
  3. How should historians weigh deficiencies in evidence when a catastrophe such as the wartime famine left no reliable statistical record?
  4. Did the movements of Gilan, Azerbaijan, and Khurasan represent variations of one national project or distinct political programs?
  5. How much responsibility for the coup of 1921 belongs to British officials, and how much to Iranian actors?
  6. In what sense does a stronger state serve a society, and in what sense might it diminish that society's capacity for self-government?
  7. Why did the republican project of 1924 fail while the dynastic change of 1925 succeeded?
  8. Can a decision be both procedurally lawful and politically coerced? What evidence would help a historian decide?
  9. What are the main reasons why the Qajar dynasty fell, and which were structural rather than contingent?

Historical Context

By 1914 the Qajar state had spent more than a century in a difficult relationship with its two principal imperial neighbors. Chapters 70 and 71 traced the Russo-Persian wars and the treaties of Gulistan and Turkmenchay, which cost Iran its Caucasian territories and imposed commercial privileges. Chapter 72 examined the competition between Russia and Britain for influence. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, concluded while Iran's constitutional revolution was in progress, divided the country into a Russian sphere in the north, a British sphere in the southeast, and a neutral zone between them, without consulting the Iranian government. That arrangement shaped the strategic geography of the First World War in Iran.

The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1906, examined in Chapters 78 and 79, created the Majles, a written constitution, and a political culture of newspapers, associations, and public debate. Its achievements were fragile. In December 1911, after a Russian ultimatum over the American treasurer-general Morgan Shuster, the Second Majles was dissolved. Russian troops remained in Tabriz, Rasht, and other northern cities, and the shrine of Imam Reza at Mashhad was bombarded by Russian artillery in 1912. The constitution remained the legal framework of the state, but its main institution did not sit for roughly three years.

The state's capacity was limited by its finances. Chapter 75 described how customs revenue, loans, and concessions had left the treasury dependent on foreign creditors. The army was small and poorly paid. Order outside the cities depended largely on tribal leaders, provincial magnates, and a few foreign-trained forces, among them the Persian Cossack Brigade, created in the late nineteenth century under Russian officers, and the Gendarmerie, organized from 1911 with Swedish officers. Those forces mattered later because two of them, the Cossacks and the Gendarmerie, became the principal armed instruments in the postwar political struggle.

The economy was also changing. Oil exploration under the D'Arcy concession of 1901 yielded a commercial discovery at Masjed Soleyman in 1908, and the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC) was formed in 1909. Chapter 75 owns the broader story of concessions and the political economy of foreign capital. This chapter takes up oil at the point when it became a strategic wartime commodity. The reader should keep in mind that these developments occurred in a country with a population overwhelmingly dependent on agriculture and pastoralism, with poor roads, no major railway network, and very limited public health provision. These conditions explain why the war's disruption of transport and commerce could so quickly become a crisis of survival.

Section 80.1 · Division I

Iran after the Constitutional Crisis of 1911

The dissolution of the Second Majles in December 1911, narrated in Chapter 79, left the constitutional system in an ambiguous condition. The Fundamental Law of 1906 and the Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907 had not been repealed, and the Shah, cabinet, and courts continued to operate within their language. But the central representative institution had been closed under foreign pressure, and the cabinets that governed in its absence were accountable to the regent and to the realities of foreign power rather than to elected deputies. For most of the next three years Iran was governed by ministers without a parliament.

Ahmad Shah had been a child when his father, Mohammad Ali Shah, was deposed in July 1909. Regents governed on his behalf, first Azud al-Molk, who died in 1910, and then Nasir al-Molk, who held the office until the young monarch reached his majority. Historians differ in how they assess Ahmad Shah. Some emphasize his caution, his dislike of political controversy, and his preoccupation with his private finances. Others point out that he reigned throughout his adult life under circumstances in which neither the throne nor the cabinet commanded reliable armed force, and that any monarch might have struggled. The evidence for his personal character comes largely from memoirs and diplomatic reports written by people with their own interests, and it should be used with that in mind.

Ministerial instability compounded the weakness of the executive. Cabinets formed and fell in rapid succession, often in response to pressure from the Russian and British legations, to factional disputes among notables, or to the state's inability to pay its officials and troops. The treasury relied on customs revenue administered in part under foreign supervision, on loans whose terms increased British and Russian leverage, and on irregular remittances from provinces whose governors retained much of what they collected. The Swedish-officered Gendarmerie, created in 1911 to police the roads outside the cities, was small relative to its task. The Persian Cossack Brigade, trained by Russian officers and paid with Russian funds, remained the best-disciplined force in Tehran, which gave Russia a degree of influence over the capital's security.

Russian forces were not confined to the Cossack Brigade. After the ultimatum of 1911 Russian troops stayed in Azerbaijan, Gilan, and elsewhere in the north. In Tabriz, where constitutionalists had resisted the royalist siege in 1908–1909, Russian-backed authorities executed prominent constitutionalists at the end of 1911. In March 1912 Russian artillery fired on the shrine of Imam Reza in Mashhad during a dispute connected to unrest in the city. These events were widely reported in Iranian newspapers and informed a political culture in which foreign military presence was understood as a direct threat to sovereignty. When war broke out in Europe, Iranians already had a decade of experience of foreign intervention.

Ahmad Shah's coronation took place on 21 July 1914 and was a statement of normal rule at a moment of gathering danger. Elections in Tehran began on 28 January 1914, before the coronation; provincial elections followed unevenly. The Third Majles opened in early December 1914: Mansoureh Ettehadieh’s accounts give 4 December for its opening and 7 December for the commencement of official session. The distinction is retained rather than treating either date as the sole date of parliamentary restoration. Its convening mattered for two reasons. It restored an arena in which the nationalists who had fought for the constitution, notably the Democrat Party, could organize in public. It also placed deputies in Tehran at the moment when the war in Europe was beginning to reach Iran, and it was the Majles, along with the cabinet, that was expected to decide the country's response. As Section 80.5 shows, the Third Majles was to be short-lived.

Students should note the limits of what can be said about this period from the evidence. Records survive in the form of Majles debates, cabinet decrees, newspapers, and the correspondence of foreign legations, but the Iranian state archives of the period were incomplete, and much of the day-to-day working of provincial administration is known only through memoirs and consular reports. The overall picture is of a state that retained the form of constitutional government but had lost, in practice, much of the capacity that constitutionalists had hoped to build after 1906. It was this state, with its depleted treasury and divided armed forces, that confronted the outbreak of the First World War.

The prolonged electoral process shows how fragile parliamentary revival was. Article 39 of the Supplement required the monarch to swear the constitutional oath before the assembly, giving the regent a reason to permit elections as Ahmad Shah approached majority. Yet the constitutional texts did not provide a robust enforcement mechanism for convening a new legislature. In the intervening years the executive borrowed from Britain and Russia even though the constitution required parliamentary approval of loans. Russian authorities obstructed elections in Azerbaijan; the national chamber consequently opened without representatives from a province central to the earlier revolution. In the Third Majles the Democrats, Moderates, clerical grouping, and independents continued to disagree over land taxation, conscription, and education. Some reforms to justice and finance were enacted notwithstanding the deteriorating international situation. The record is a reminder that the inactivity of the legislature in 1911–1914 and again after 1915 was not evidence that constitutional politics had ceased to matter.

Section 80.2 · Division I

Iranian Neutrality and the Outbreak of World War I

When the European war began in the summer of 1914, Iran was not a party to any of the alliances that drove it, and the Iranian government's first impulse was to stay out. Britain and Russia were allies against Germany and Austria-Hungary, but both had pursued their own interests on Iranian soil for years, and Germany, which had no territorial presence in Iran, enjoyed a certain reputation among Iranian nationalists as a power without a history of direct encroachment. The Ottoman Empire, Iran's western neighbor, entered the war on the side of the Central Powers at the end of October 1914. On 1 November 1914 the Iranian government declared neutrality.

The declaration reflected rational calculation. A small state lacking a capable army could hope to avoid the worst consequences of war only by avoiding alignment, and a declaration of neutrality placed on record the principle that Iranian territory was not a legitimate field of operations. Some deputies and officials favored leaning toward one side, partly in the hope of extracting concessions. The cabinet, however, concluded that any alignment would invite retaliation from the other side. The government also feared that open support for the Central Powers would give Britain and Russia a pretext to tighten their control further, since both already maintained troops and influence on Iranian soil.

Yet neutrality is a legal status that has practical meaning only when the neutral state can defend it. The Iranian government could not. Russian troops were already in the north, and the Ottoman Empire had longstanding border disputes with Iran, which had been under international arbitration before the war and which Ottoman forces had pressed in the years leading up to it. Britain had major commercial and strategic interests in the southwest, and the Anglo-Persian Oil Company's installations were exposed. Germany, for its part, saw in Iran a way to threaten British India and the Russian rear by encouraging tribal and nationalist uprisings, and sent agents and officers to exploit the opportunity. Each belligerent power therefore had its own reasons for treating Iranian territory as a theater, and none was willing to respect neutrality when it conflicted with military advantage.

The result was an unusual wartime situation. Iran never formally entered the war, and the Iranian government never fought as a belligerent. But its territory became the scene of military operations by four foreign powers, several of which were also supporting or opposing Iranian groups with arms and funds. Battles were fought around Tabriz, Urmia, Hamadan, and Kermanshah, and British forces landed in the south. The population experienced the war as a series of occupations, requisitions, and displacements. This chapter therefore treats the war not as a distant conflict that Iran escaped but as a catastrophe that happened to it, while insisting on the legal and political distinction between being occupied and being a belligerent.

The legal claim of neutrality did not disappear because the country could not enforce it. Diplomatic protests, demands for withdrawal and compensation, and appeals to other governments continued to affirm Iranian jurisdiction. The problem was one of unequal enforcement rather than voluntary surrender. An official compelled to permit troop movements could maintain that the permission was given under duress. For a local population, however, the distinction between formal sovereignty and coercive power acquired immediate material significance whenever a commander controlled access to a road or demanded provisions.

Historical Vocabulary
Neutrality, Military Occupation, Territorial Sovereignty, and Belligerency

Definition: Neutrality is a state's declared position of non-participation in a war between other states. Belligerency is the status of a state that is a party to the war. Military occupation is the control of a territory by foreign armed forces without a transfer of sovereignty. Territorial sovereignty is a state's recognized right to exercise exclusive authority within its borders.

Historical context: Iran's neutrality, proclaimed on 1 November 1914, was recognized in principle by the belligerents but not observed in practice. Russian, Ottoman, British, and German forces and agents operated in Iranian territory. Occupation did not alter Iran's sovereignty in law, and occupying powers generally professed respect for Iranian independence, but it changed what the government could actually control.

Why it matters: The distinction explains why Iran's postwar claims at Paris and in international bodies rested on its status as a neutral that suffered foreign military operations. It also explains why political debates about the war were debates about sovereignty.

Potential misunderstanding: Neutrality did not mean that Iran experienced the war lightly, nor that Iranians were uniformly neutral in sentiment. Some Iranians favored Britain and Russia, some favored the Central Powers, and many sought only to survive.

Section 80.3 · Division I

Russian and Ottoman Military Operations in Northern and Western Iran

The northwest was the first part of Iran to feel the effects of the Ottoman entry into the war. The region, historically a corridor between the plateau and the Caucasus and Anatolia (see Chapter 1), lay along the front between the Russian Caucasus army and Ottoman forces. Russian troops were already in Tabriz and elsewhere in Azerbaijan. In the winter of 1914–1915, as fighting developed along the frontier, Ottoman forces and irregular units advanced into Iranian territory, and Russian forces withdrew from some towns and returned to others. Tabriz changed hands briefly in January 1915 before Russian troops reoccupied it. The movement of forces back and forth over the same districts imposed requisitions, quartering, and violence on villages and towns repeatedly.

Urmia, on the western side of the lake, was home to Muslim, Armenian, and Assyrian Christian communities. American and other missionary institutions had operated there for decades, and many Assyrian and Armenian villagers sought protection in missions and consulates when the Russian withdrawal exposed them to Ottoman and local irregular forces. Accounts of what followed come largely from missionary reports, consular dispatches, and later community memory, and they should be read with attention to the interests of their authors. They describe massacres, refugee flight, disease in crowded compounds, and retaliations in several directions. Muslim communities also suffered, from the fighting itself, from requisitions, and from reprisals. Scholars generally agree that civilian suffering was extensive, but precise numbers of dead and displaced are not securely established.

The war changed the relations among these communities. Assyrian and Armenian communities had in some places aligned with Russia, and Ottoman and local Muslim forces regarded them with suspicion as a result, but alignment was not universal and was often a matter of survival. Kurdish tribal leaders likewise pursued their own aims, sometimes cooperating with Ottoman forces, sometimes with Russians, and often with neither. The violence of 1914–1918 deepened communal distrust and left bitter memories in the northwest. In March 1918 the Assyrian patriarch Mar Shimun was killed in a meeting with Simko Shikak, a Kurdish leader discussed in Section 80.12, and in the summer of 1918 a large Assyrian population left Urmia in a flight toward British-held territory around Hamadan and then Mesopotamia. Many died on the way, and many more suffered in refugee camps.

Farther south, the cities of Hamadan and Kermanshah acquired strategic importance because they lay on the roads between the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia, where British forces were fighting the Ottomans. Over 1915 and 1916 Russian forces, with limited British coordination, advanced toward Hamadan and Kermanshah, while Ottoman and German-supported forces, together with Iranian Gendarmerie units and tribal allies, contested them. Control of both cities shifted more than once. Each change of control brought new demands on local populations for food, fodder, transport animals, and labor, and new accusations of collaboration against those who had dealt with the previous occupier.

These operations also had political effects. Because the front crossed Iranian territory, Tehran could not stand aside, and both sides tried to enlist Iranian officials and politicians. The Russian advance on the capital in late 1915, discussed in Section 80.5, was a direct consequence of the war in the northwest and the west. Throughout, the Iranian government protested violations of its territory, but its protests had little practical effect. The experience of the northwest and west taught many Iranians that the sovereignty promised by neutrality depended on the actual presence of force.

The geographical pattern also helps explain why occupation was not identical across the country. The Russian Caucasus army operated from supply centers north of the border, whereas Ottoman forces depended on connections across eastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia. The movement of refugees carried political violence beyond the battlefield. The murder of Mar Shimun Benyamin in 1918 and subsequent attacks and reprisals belong to a history that includes persecution of Christian communities and violence against Muslim civilians, not to an account in which an entire ethnic or religious category is assigned collective guilt. Mission records, survivor accounts, and military documentation should be compared against the particular place and date to which each refers.

Section 80.4 · Division I

British Military Operations and German Influence in Southern Iran

Britain's principal concern in southern Iran was the security of the Persian Gulf and the oil installations in Khuzestan. The Persian Gulf was the sea link between India and the Mediterranean world, and the oilfields, pipeline, and refinery at Abadan were of growing importance for the fuel supply of the Royal Navy (Section 80.6). When war with the Ottoman Empire began, the British Indian government landed forces at the head of the Gulf in November 1914, and British forces took up positions to protect Abadan and the surrounding region. The consent of the Iranian government was not sought, and Sheikh Khazal of Mohammareh, who had long cultivated British support, cooperated with the landing.

Farther east along the Gulf coast, the port of Bushehr and the hinterland of Tangestan and Dashti became a focus of Anglo-German rivalry. Germany sent agents to stir up resistance to Britain and to threaten British interests, and one of the most active was the consul-turned-agent Wilhelm Wassmuss. Wassmuss lived among tribal leaders in the Bushehr hinterland and encouraged resistance to British demands. In 1915 British forces occupied Bushehr, and fighting between British troops and local forces followed. The most prominent local leader was Rais Ali Delvari of Tangestan, who fought British incursions and who died in the fighting later that year.

Students should be careful about how the relationship between German agents and Iranian actors is characterized. Wassmuss and other German representatives brought money, arms, and promises, and these were useful to some Iranian leaders. But local leaders had their own grievances, among them resentment of British commercial and naval dominance of the Gulf, concern about their own autonomy, and in some cases a longer history of opposition to foreign intrusion. To treat Rais Ali and his followers solely as instruments of German policy would be to deny them historical agency and to adopt the perspective of British officials who found it convenient to explain resistance as the result of German intrigue. The better view is that German support amplified existing resistance but did not create it.

The conflict spread into Shiraz and the south. In late 1915 members of the Iranian Gendarmerie in Shiraz, many of them sympathetic to the nationalist cause and led in part by Swedish officers, seized British officials and aligned themselves with the anti-British opposition. Britain responded by pressing for the dismissal of the Swedish officers and by raising its own force, the South Persia Rifles, in 1916 under British command, to secure the roads and maintain order in the British sphere. The South Persia Rifles were organized under Sir Percy Sykes in 1916 with the consent of the cabinet of Sepah-Salar, but British officers exercised effective command; governmental consent did not amount to Iranian operational control, and it exemplifies the way in which the war led to the creation of parallel armed forces in Iran.

Not all armed activity in the south was directed against Britain. Tribal leaders in Fars, Khuzestan, and Luristan pursued their own interests, and the Bakhtiari khans, who held shares in the oil enterprise, had commercial and political reasons to remain on good terms with Britain. German officers and adventurers crossed Iranian territory on missions to Afghanistan and elsewhere, some of them aided by Iranian sympathizers and some by tribal leaders. The result was a landscape of multiple, overlapping, and shifting alignments, in which the line between collaboration and resistance depended on the interest of the actor and the moment.

The southern forces also illustrate divergent wartime institutions. The gendarmerie had been created as an Iranian service with Swedish assistance; individual officers could turn its detachments toward nationalist resistance, without thereby establishing a consistent nationwide policy. The South Persia Rifles, although staffed largely by locally recruited men, depended on British officers, funds, and supply. A single uniformed soldier's nationality therefore does not identify the political authority that directed his unit. The debates over the existence and financing of these formations anticipated the arguments about a unified army after 1921.

Multiple Perspectives

Iranians and Foreign Powers in Wartime Iran

Iranian nationalists, especially Democrats and deputies: Many saw the war as an opportunity to end Anglo-Russian domination. For them, cooperation with Germany or the Ottoman Empire was a tactical means to restore independence, not an endorsement of those powers.
Provincial and tribal leaders such as those of Tangestan: Their calculations were local. They weighed British naval and commercial power against their own autonomy, accepted weapons and funds from whoever offered them, and sought to protect their communities and prerogatives.
British officials: They viewed the south chiefly in terms of imperial communications and the oilfields, interpreted resistance as German-inspired subversion, and justified the occupation of Bushehr and the creation of the South Persia Rifles as necessary protection.
Russian commanders: They sought to secure the northern frontier and the roads from the Caucasus, and treated Iranian territory as an extension of their own theater of operations, requisitioning supplies and supporting friendly local authorities.
Ottoman and German representatives: They tried to use Iranian territory and Iranian discontent to threaten British India and the Russian rear. Their promises to respect Iranian independence were limited by their own war aims.

Note on power: These groups did not have equal power. The foreign armies had troops and money; most Iranian actors had at best limited capacity to choose their circumstances.

The Nationalist Migration and the Provisional Government at Kermanshah

By the autumn of 1915 the political position of the capital had become precarious. Russian forces, in the north, advanced toward Qazvin, within a short distance of Tehran. Britain and Russia pressed the government to end the pro-German activities of some officials, and members of the cabinet and Majles were divided over whether the Shah should remain in the capital or leave for the south. Some nationalists believed that if the Shah left Tehran, Britain and Russia would treat the government as having lost legitimacy and that Iranian independence would be lost. Others, including nationalists in the Democrat Party, believed that the only way to preserve independence was to move the center of resistance out of the reach of Russian troops.

The Shah did not leave. Britain and Russia, wishing to avoid a collapse of the central government, pressed him to stay, and he remained. But a number of deputies, Democrat activists, and Gendarmerie officers departed Tehran in November 1915 in what is often called the nationalist migration (mohajerat). They traveled to Qom, where a Committee of National Defense was formed, and then continued westward toward Kermanshah, a town near the Ottoman frontier where the Ottoman and German forces were present and where Gendarmerie units sympathetic to the movement were stationed. The progression through Qom and Isfahan into western Iran occupied late 1915 and early 1916; the cabinet associated with Nezam al-Saltaneh took shape in Kermanshah in February 1916.

At Kermanshah the emigrants set up what is generally called a provisional government, associated with Nezam al-Saltaneh Mafi, a notable with a record of political activity. Other prominent emigrants included the cleric and deputy Sayyed Hasan Modarres, whom we meet again in Sections 80.18 and 80.20. The provisional government claimed to speak for the nation in the absence of an independent government in Tehran. It organized forces, sought Ottoman and German aid, and tried to raise taxes in the territory it held. Its resources were small and its authority depended on the presence of Ottoman troops. A renewed Russian advance in May 1916 drove the cabinet toward Qasr-e Shirin and Baghdad; after Ottoman forces retook Kermanshah in June, emigrants returned and reconstituted their government. Renewed Russian pressure in 1917 and subsequent reversals ended this phase of their enterprise; some emigrants continued to Ottoman territory or Europe.

Why did nationalists who had struggled for the constitution ally with the Ottoman Empire, a monarchy, and with Germany, an imperial power? The answer lies in the structure of the problem they faced. For many Iranian politicians the principal immediate threat to independence came from Russia and Britain, which had divided the country in 1907, bombarded Mashhad, and held troops in the north and south. In that situation Germany, which had no territory in Iran, could appear as an ally of convenience, and the Ottoman Empire could appear as a fellow Muslim state. Most nationalists did not admire German imperial policy or adopt German ideas. They regarded the alliance as a way to relieve the pressure of the nearer imperial powers. It is therefore misleading to call them pro-German in the sense of ideological agreement. A better term is that they pursued a tactical alliance, and in doing so exposed themselves to the risk that the Central Powers would in turn treat Iran as an instrument.

That risk was real. The Central Powers' promises of Iranian independence were limited by their own war aims, and the Ottoman Empire had its own territorial claims on the frontier. As the war went on, the military position of the Central Powers in Iran weakened, and with it the position of the nationalists who had relied on them. The migration also had costs for the constitutional movement, because it removed many of the most active deputies and left Tehran under cabinets more susceptible to British and Russian influence. The Third Majles ceased to function, and no parliament met again until the Fourth Majles convened in 1921.

Historians evaluate the episode differently. Some see in the migration the first sustained attempt to carry out a national resistance outside the framework of the Qajar court and a precedent for later nationalist politics. Others stress its military weakness and its dependence on foreign support, and regard it as evidence of the divisions among Iranian nationalists. Both views contain truth. What can be said with confidence is that the migration revealed the dilemma of Iranian politics in wartime: the political forces most committed to independence had no independent means of enforcing it.

The movement did not abandon constitutional forms altogether. The emigrants developed a council of representatives alongside their executive, and the renewed government at Kermanshah included an assembly associated with Modarres. Its claim to national authority was challenged by the existence of the Shah and his ministers in Tehran, who continued to be recognized internationally. The nationalists therefore faced two separate problems: meeting the military threat and establishing an accepted basis for political representation. Neither was solved by relocating the government. The Ottoman reconquest of Kermanshah in June 1916 made renewed activity possible but also displayed the movement’s dependence on a belligerent power. A government that required another army to recover its seat had limited capacity to determine its own policies. Nevertheless, the careers and networks of the emigrants did not end with the wartime organization; figures such as Modarres continued their constitutional politics in the 1920s.

Section 80.6 · Division II

The Anglo-Persian Oil Company and the Strategic Importance of Iranian Petroleum

The background of Iranian oil is told in Chapter 75 and requires only a brief summary here. In 1901 the Iranian government granted William Knox D'Arcy, a British investor, a concession for petroleum exploration across most of the country. After years of costly and unsuccessful drilling, oil was found in commercial quantity at Masjed Soleyman in 1908. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company was formed in 1909 to exploit the discovery, a pipeline was laid to the island of Abadan on the Shatt al-Arab, and the refinery at Abadan began operation in 1912.

The transformation of oil from a commercial venture into a strategic resource came with the naval policy of the British government. In the years before the war the Royal Navy was converting from coal to oil for its warships, a change that offered advantages of speed and range but required secure supplies of fuel. Britain had no significant oil production of its own, and the Admiralty looked for sources under British control. In 1914, with the company in financial need, the British government agreed to purchase a controlling share of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company and to enter a long-term fuel contract with it. Parliament debated the investment before the outbreak of war, and the capital-acquisition act received royal assent on 10 August 1914. From that point the company was in effect a partly state-owned enterprise whose oilfields lay in a country that was not a British possession.

The consequences were considerable. British protection of the oilfields became a strategic priority, which helps explain the landings of November 1914 (Section 80.4). The pipeline and installations were targets of attack, and in the early months of the war tribal raiders damaged parts of the pipeline, forcing repairs and the use of guards. Production and refining continued through the war, and the company's output supplied fuel to British naval and military forces in the Gulf and elsewhere. The wartime importance of this supply shaped British policy toward Iran in the postwar settlement.

The oil industry also created a new kind of place. At Abadan and the oilfields the company built refineries, housing, and administrative buildings and recruited a labor force from several sources, including Iranian tribal and rural migrants, Arab laborers from the region, and workers from India, who served in skilled and unskilled posts. The company established settlements with sharply different standards of housing and amenity for European staff and for laborers, an inequality that would later become a major political grievance. Relations with local leaders were also important. The company dealt with the Bakhtiari khans, who controlled the territory around the oilfields and held an interest in the enterprise, and with Sheikh Khazal of Mohammareh, who had leased the land for the refinery and received payments for protection. In these negotiations the company often dealt directly with regional leaders rather than through Tehran, which reinforced the autonomy of those leaders and the perception that the central government was bypassed.

The Iranian government's share of benefits was limited. Under the concession it received a stated percentage of the company's profits and some initial payments of shares, and during the war the amounts were small, and the method by which profits were calculated became a source of dispute in later years. The government, which had no ability to audit the company, was not in a position to challenge the figures. The question of how much revenue the Iranian state obtained from its oil, and on what terms, became a recurring theme in Iranian politics, examined in later chapters including those on the Pahlavi state and on the oil nationalization crisis of the early 1950s (Chapter 86).

Students should avoid two simplifications. It would be wrong to treat the oil industry as simply the cause of British policy in Iran, since Britain's concerns in the Gulf, India, and the Russian frontier long predated oil. It would equally be wrong to treat oil as marginal. Between 1914 and 1925 petroleum made the southwest a region of particular concern to the British government, and this concern became one factor in the political calculations of both Iranian and British actors, including those who dealt with Sheikh Khazal in 1924 and 1925 (Section 80.19).

The financial transaction in London had a distinctive structure. The capital-acquisition legislation authorized an expenditure of up to £2.2 million. The 1914 proposal, as reconstructed in the specialist history of Iranian oil agreements, envisaged a 51 percent shareholding and two Treasury-appointed directors with veto rights in major strategic matters. The fuel supply arrangement with the Admiralty was negotiated separately for twenty years. Accounts of the company's eventual share structure and additional contractual arrangements sometimes give different percentages or durations; the important distinction is between the original terms and later arrangements, not a claim that all reported figures describe the same contract. The investment placed British strategic objectives inside the governance of a commercial corporation while the concession itself remained an agreement with Iran.

Relations with the Bakhtiari chiefs and Sheikh Khazal were therefore part of the company's political infrastructure, not peripheral to the industrial operations. Negotiations for land, security, and access provided regional intermediaries with income and influence. The Iranian treasury, meanwhile, was to receive an entitlement tied to the profits defined under the D'Arcy concession. The ability to calculate that entitlement depended on access to the corporation's accounts and on the interpretation of which operations fell within the concession. A company could enlarge its overseas network without proportionately enlarging the profits recognized as subject to the Iranian royalty. During the war disputes arose about unpaid dues, damages, and the company's counterclaims. These disputes continued after the armistice.

The 1920 negotiations led by Sydney Armitage-Smith are a bridge between the wartime and postwar history. APOC offered £1 million to settle arrears through March 1919 and withdrew counterclaims. Iran accepted the money and waiver but objected to an interpretation that restricted its claim to the sixteen-percent entitlement, particularly in relation to overseas operations. The interpretation was not submitted to the Majles for approval, while the company proceeded as though its position were enforceable. The political collapse of the 1919 agreement accordingly did not dissolve the oil concession. This explains why even a stronger Iranian executive after 1921 inherited an unresolved dispute about resource accounting, rather than obtaining immediate control over the industry.

Section 80.7 · Division II

Famine, Food Shortages, Inflation, and Economic Dislocation

The most severe human crisis of the wartime period was the subsistence crisis of about 1917–1919. Its roots were in the whole experience of the war. Iran's agriculture was based on rain-fed grain farming in many areas and on irrigated agriculture in others, and its transport relied on animals and a few poor roads. Armies that moved across the country needed grain, fodder, animals, and labor, and they obtained them through purchase, requisition, and sometimes seizure. The movement of troops disrupted planting and harvesting in the northwest, west, and parts of the south. Commercial routes were interrupted, particularly after the Russian Revolution and the closure of trade with the Caucasus and Russia, which had been an important outlet for Iranian products and a source of imports.

Food prices rose steeply. Grain was in short supply in many towns, and its price increased far faster than wages. The causes of price increases included reduced supply, difficulty of transport, currency pressures, and speculation. Contemporary observers and later historians have debated the role of hoarding by landlords and merchants who held grain in expectation of higher prices. The state, with its weak finances and limited administrative reach, could do little to control prices or distribution. In some places local authorities and notables organized relief, and some missions and charities distributed food, but these efforts were small compared with the scale of need.

The experience of the crisis differed between regions. Areas that lay directly on the paths of armies, such as parts of Azerbaijan, Hamadan, Kermanshah, and districts along the route to Baghdad, suffered heavy requisitioning and disruption. Cities with large populations and dependence on grain brought in from outside, including Tehran, faced shortages and high prices. Some regions with better harvests or less exposure to troop movements were less affected, although they, too, could be hit by price increases and by the arrival of refugees. Because Iran lacked a reliable system of statistics, it is difficult to compare regions with precision, and much of what is known comes from consular reports, missionary accounts, newspapers, and later reminiscences.

For rural cultivators the crisis could mean the loss of seed grain, animals, and tenancy rights, the sale of belongings, and the abandonment of villages for towns. For urban households it meant reduced consumption, the sale of household goods, borrowing at high interest, and dependence on charity. Merchants had varying experiences. Those with access to grain and credit could profit, while those whose trade depended on routes that were closed were ruined. Laborers faced falling real wages. Displaced people, including refugees from the northwest and the Armenian and Assyrian refugees in the west, were among the most vulnerable, since they often arrived in places that were themselves short of food and shelter.

Food insecurity also depended on access rather than solely on the national volume of grain. A household with land or stored supplies might survive a price rise that devastated a wage earner whose income did not adjust. A merchant with credit could purchase stocks in advance, whereas a displaced family might have neither cash nor accepted guarantees. In a region bypassed by secure routes, even a nearby surplus was difficult to obtain. Requisitions might take animals as well as grain, diminishing the next season's cultivation and the capacity to move food to another community. The experience of a famine therefore extended beyond the period of peak prices: households that sold livestock, tools, or land to eat could remain impoverished after supplies recovered. These mechanisms are particularly important when direct evidence of household conditions is fragmentary; they explain why national averages, even if they existed, would not describe every population's experience.

Everyday Life and Social History

Food Markets, Household Survival, and Displacement during the Wartime Crisis

For ordinary families the crisis was a daily problem of obtaining bread. Wheat and barley were the staples of most households, and when their price rose faster than wages the household had to choose what to give up. Sources describe the sale of clothing, tools, carpets, and animals; the substitution of cheaper grains and, in the worst cases, of food not normally eaten; and the departure of family members to seek work or relief elsewhere. Women and children are visible in the sources as petitioners for charity, as laborers in households, and as the most numerous among those displaced, although the evidence on their experience is thinner than the evidence on the actions of men and officials.

Markets themselves changed. In towns, bakers and grain dealers were subject to official attempts to control prices, which sometimes led to disputes and to the hiding of stocks. Neighborhood notables, mosques, and associations sometimes organized distributions of bread or money. Roads filled with people moving toward cities in search of food, where overcrowding increased the risk of disease (Section 80.8). Historians reconstruct these experiences from consular reports, relief organization records, newspapers, memoirs, and oral accounts recorded later, and each of these has limits: officials reported what they could see, relief workers described those who reached them, and memories recorded decades afterward may be shaped by later political narratives.

Section 80.8 · Division II

Epidemic Disease, Mortality, and the Historiography of the Wartime Crisis

Famine and disease reinforced each other. Malnourished populations are more vulnerable to infection, and the movement of refugees, soldiers, and traders carried disease along routes of travel. Several diseases circulated in Iran during these years, among them typhus and relapsing fever, associated with crowded and unsanitary conditions, as well as malaria and cholera, which had longer histories in the region. The public health apparatus was limited: the Sanitary Council existed, but its reach and resources were insufficient for the nationwide emergency. There were few trained physicians outside the largest cities, limited quarantine and sanitation capacity, and almost no state hospitals. Foreign missionary and consular doctors, and local practitioners using both traditional and modern methods, provided most of the care available.

The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 reached Iran in the autumn of 1918 and spread along trade and military routes. The pandemic was global, with high mortality in many parts of the world, and in Iran it struck a population already weakened by hunger. The ports of the Gulf, the cities on the main roads, and the large cities were affected, and the disease spread to the countryside. Authorities had no effective treatment and little ability to enforce isolation. Contemporary descriptions of the epidemic speak of rapid spread and heavy loss in towns and villages, but no national count was made.

No reliable national total of wartime deaths survives. The sources include consular and missionary observations, burial records, local population counts, and uncertain reconstructions of prewar and postwar populations. Amir A. Afkhami’s 2003 study of influenza estimated approximately 902,400 to 2,431,000 influenza-associated deaths, drawing on evidence with major regional and demographic limitations. Mohammad Gholi Majd subsequently argued for roughly eight to ten million deaths from the famine; this much higher estimate and his argument about British responsibility remain disputed. Other general histories, including those of Ervand Abrahamian and Homa Katouzian, offer substantially lower assessments or approach the crisis through different demographic and political evidence. These are estimates of different outcomes, not interchangeable observations. Influenza deaths, deaths attributed to hunger, and total excess mortality cannot be added together as if their categories were mutually exclusive.

Disagreement concerns not only the number of deaths but also their causes. Some scholars emphasize the role of foreign armies, whose requisitions, purchases, and movements disrupted food supply, and who in some accounts diverted grain for their own use. Others stress the weakness of Iranian administration, the effects of drought and poor harvests in certain years, the speculation of landlords and merchants, and the pandemic. Many historians now argue that these causes interacted, and that a single-cause explanation is unpersuasive. The question of how far British policy was responsible has also been entangled in later political argument, including in nationalist narratives that stress the culpability of foreign powers and in other accounts that stress internal social conditions. The historian should separate the documented facts, such as local reports of requisitioning and local losses, from larger claims that go beyond the evidence.

Afkhami's research adds an important qualification to a common description of the influenza pandemic as a primarily urban disaster. He found evidence of disproportionately severe rural mortality in Iran and considered the debilitating effects of malaria, anemia, hunger, and other pre-existing conditions. The rural distribution matters because medical and consular reports often concentrate on towns. A village population lacking physicians or transport could suffer extensive deaths without leaving the records that would permit an accurate regional count. An observer's statement that a particular village had lost a large fraction of its residents cannot be projected uniformly across provinces with different disease exposure, harvests, migration, and access to relief.

The methods of demographic reconstruction matter to the historical debate about responsibility. If a population estimate before the war is too high, a comparison with a lower estimate afterward may create the illusion of deaths that did not occur; if survivors migrated or enumeration missed them, the difference is also misleading. Conversely, relying only on recorded burials risks underestimating deaths among displaced and unregistered populations. To attribute deliberate mass destruction to a state requires evidence of intention and implementation as well as evidence of harmful military and economic conduct. The documented role of occupying forces in aggravating shortages does not depend upon accepting a particular national death total. The moral gravity of the suffering is not diminished by statistical uncertainty.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Explain the Iranian Famine and Mortality Crisis of World War I?

The question: What caused the wartime crisis, how many people died, and how far should responsibility be assigned to foreign powers, to Iranian elites, to climate, or to disease?

Interpretation A — Foreign military activity as the principal cause: Some historians emphasize the effects of occupation: requisitioning, disruption of trade, diversion of food and transport to military purposes, and the destruction of the economic networks on which supply depended. In the strongest versions of this view, the crisis was largely a consequence of imperial war policy.
Interpretation B — Multiple interacting causes: Others argue that the crisis resulted from the combination of military disruption, weak state capacity, poor harvests, speculation, transport failure, and epidemic disease, and that attributing it chiefly to one cause oversimplifies the evidence. Some who take this view add that regional variation was great and that the evidence does not support a single national picture.
Interpretation C — Very high mortality estimates: A minority position, argued most prominently in one widely discussed study, holds that mortality reached several million and was comparable in proportion to some of the worst famines in modern history. Critics reply that the underlying data are too thin to support such precise and large claims.

Evidence: Consular dispatches, missionary and relief reports, newspapers, Majles records, memoirs, scattered local records, and comparisons of population estimates. None provides a nationwide count.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale (how many died), causation (what weight to give each factor), and interpretation (how to read foreign reports written from imperial perspectives). Terminology is also contested, since some scholars use the word genocide for the crisis while others reject it as inconsistent with the evidence of intent.

Why it matters: How the crisis is explained affects how the period is remembered, whether it is treated as a central event in modern Iranian history or as a poorly documented episode, and how the responsibilities of foreign powers and Iranian elites are assessed.

Student task: Identify one kind of evidence that could narrow the range of mortality estimates and explain what it would show and what it could not. Then explain why a historian might be cautious about both the lowest and the highest estimates.

Section 80.9 · Division III

The Russian Revolution, Military Withdrawal, and the Changing Imperial Balance

The Russian Revolutions of 1917 changed the balance of power in Iran more abruptly than any event of the war. After the February Revolution, the Provisional Government continued the war, but discipline in the Russian forces in Iran deteriorated. After the October Revolution, soldiers' committees and desertions reduced the army in the north to a disorganized remnant, and Russian units withdrew toward the Caucasus and Russia. The empire that had occupied northern Iran for years, bombarded Mashhad, and dominated Tehran's security through the Cossack Brigade ceased in practice to exist.

The new Soviet government made a public break with tsarist policy. In the winter of 1917–1918 it announced that it renounced the tsarist treaties and secret agreements concerning Iran, including the 1907 Convention and the wartime understandings that had assigned Iranian territory to Russian and British influence, and that it would withdraw its troops and cancel Iranian debts and privileges. The Iranian government welcomed these declarations, although it could not be sure how far they would be carried out. Students should distinguish the policy of revolutionary Russia in 1917–1918, which was concerned with undoing the legacy of the old regime and with surviving a civil war, from the later interests of the Soviet state, which in 1920–1921 intervened in Gilan and signed a treaty with Iran that contained its own security clauses (Sections 80.11 and 80.15).

Britain moved to fill the vacuum. British forces, already present in the south, extended their presence northward in 1918. A small expedition under General Dunsterville, known as Dunsterforce, traveled through western Iran toward the Caucasus and Baku in 1918, passing through Hamadan and Gilan, where it met resistance from the Jangali movement (Section 80.11). A British military mission was sent to Mashhad to observe and influence events in Transcaspia and Khurasan. After the end of the war a British force, the North Persia Force (Norperforce), remained in northwestern Iran, and British officials considered how to preserve British interests without the cost of a permanent occupation. The Ottoman collapse in late 1918 removed the other wartime occupier, so that after the armistice Britain was the only great power with substantial forces in Iran.

The changed situation affected Iranian internal politics. The Russian Cossack Brigade, whose officers had been Russian, now lacked its patron. Its commander and officers faced uncertainty, and British funding and supervision assumed importance (Section 80.13). Political activity revived in northern cities, where Russian domination had been replaced by a vacuum, and where news of the Russian Revolution, with its ideas of land reform, workers' councils, and national self-determination, circulated among workers, merchants, and intellectuals, in some places through Iranian migrant workers who had returned from the Caucasus.

Iran also sought to assert itself internationally. Though neutral, it had suffered heavily, and the government sent a delegation to the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 to present claims, including the abolition of the capitulations, revision of treaties, and compensation for wartime damage. The delegation was not admitted to the formal proceedings, a disappointment that deepened nationalist skepticism about the great powers' professions of self-determination. Iran was, however, among the states that joined the League of Nations. The contrast between Iran's international standing and its actual situation under a British military presence helps explain the intensity of the debate that followed the Anglo-Persian Agreement.

The end of the tsarist presence also changed practical choices for Iranian nationalists. Earlier, constitutionalists had been able to condemn the two imperial partners simultaneously; after the Russian revolutions, their possible allies and adversaries changed. British forces used northern Iranian routes in operations connected with the Caucasus and the struggle for Baku, while Russian revolutionary forces, anti-Bolshevik formations, and later Soviet naval forces pursued their own goals around the Caspian. The retreat of one foreign army therefore did not mean that the northern frontier became secure. The Iranian delegates who sought a hearing at the Paris Peace Conference hoped to convert the country's neutral status and wartime losses into international recognition of its grievances. Their limited success made the question of how to obtain effective diplomatic support still more urgent.

Section 80.10 · Division III

The Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919

In 1919 the British government, under the direction of Foreign Secretary Lord Curzon, set out to establish a stable relationship with Iran that would secure British interests without maintaining a large occupying force. The instrument was an agreement negotiated with the cabinet of Vosuq al-Dowleh, in which Foreign Minister Firuz Mirza Nosrat al-Dowleh was a leading figure, and signed on 9 August 1919. Britain's representative in Tehran, Sir Percy Cox, played an important part in the negotiations.

The agreement provided for British advisers to the Iranian government in finance and for British officers and equipment to assist in reorganizing the Iranian army. It envisaged a British loan to the Iranian government, to be secured against customs revenue, and cooperation in the construction of railways and other communications and in the revision of the customs tariff. In its preamble and opening article Britain affirmed respect for the independence and integrity of Iran. For Curzon and his supporters the agreement was a means of helping Iran to build an effective administration and army that could resist Bolshevik pressure and maintain order. For its British critics inside the government, it was an expensive commitment of doubtful value.

Within Iran the reaction was largely hostile. Critics held that the agreement would place Iran under British tutelage, with British advisers controlling finances and the army, and that it would convert the country into a protectorate in all but name. They pointed out that it had been negotiated in secret, without consulting the Majles or the public, and that its financial terms would add to the national debt. Nationalist newspapers, religious leaders, and politicians who had been active in the Constitutional Revolution denounced it. France, the United States, and Soviet Russia also expressed concern or opposition. British payments totaling £131,000 associated with Prime Minister Vosuq al-Dowleh, Finance Minister Sarem al-Dowleh, and Foreign Minister Nosrat al-Dowleh were publicly confirmed in the House of Commons in November 1920. The documented payments should be distinguished from later, less securely evidenced accounts of what happened to the money.

A central point is the distinction between signature and ratification. The Fundamental Law required that international agreements of this kind be approved by the Majles. There was no sitting Majles in 1919, since the Third Majles had effectively ended in 1915 and elections for a new one were delayed. The government argued that the agreement could be implemented provisionally and submitted to the Majles when one met. Parts of it were put into effect: British advisers began to arrive, and Sydney Armitage-Smith headed the financial mission, and a British military mission was established. But the agreement was never ratified. When the Fourth Majles finally met in 1921, it did not approve it, and the new circumstances created by the coup of February 1921 and the Soviet-Iranian treaty led to its abandonment (Section 80.15). The agreement therefore remained a signed but legally incomplete instrument.

The affair mattered for two reasons. First, it became a lasting symbol in Iranian political memory of the danger of foreign control, and later nationalists, including those in the 1950s oil nationalization movement, would invoke it. Second, it illustrated the dilemma of the Iranian state. The need for financial and military reorganization was widely acknowledged, but the only available sources of assistance were foreign powers whose terms impinged on sovereignty. The question of how Iran could build a strong state without surrendering control to outsiders was one of the problems to which Reza Khan's ascendancy would appear, to many, to offer an answer.

The published instruments demonstrate the scope of the proposal. The six clauses of the main agreement coupled a declaration of respect for sovereignty with Iranian-financed advisers, military expertise and equipment, a financing facility, transport development, and tariff revision. The separate loan instrument specified £2 million at seven percent annual interest; the repayment structure extended over twenty years, and existing customs revenues were assigned as security subject to earlier obligations. Advisers were thus not simply technicians offered without cost: the government would be borrowing to finance institutions whose direction was closely linked to the creditor. The accompanying letters addressed possible treaty revision, war damages and frontier questions. A reciprocal waiver excluded government claims for British troop-maintenance costs and Iranian claims against Britain for damage caused by those troops, while leaving individual and private institutional claims to separate treatment. Here the distinction between state and private claims mattered to civilians seeking restitution.

The transitional implementation exposed a contradiction. Financial and military experts could begin preparatory work, but without a legislature the executive could not conclusively establish the agreement’s constitutional authority. After Vosuq al-Dowleh fell in June 1920, Moshir al-Dowleh suspended it. The February 1921 government repudiated it, and in the new parliamentary setting it was not ratified. Opposition brought together people who disagreed about the best means of reform but considered the proposed concentration of foreign influence unacceptable. The failure of Curzon's policy was consequently not a rejection of administrative expertise in general; Iranian politicians later hired American financial advisers under a different arrangement.

Section 80.11 · Division III

Mirza Kuchak Khan and the Jangali Movement

The forested province of Gilan on the Caspian coast was the home of one of the most distinctive political movements of the period. Its leader, Mirza Kuchak Khan, was a religious student and activist from Rasht who had taken part in the Constitutional Revolution. In 1915 he began to organize an armed movement in the forests (jangal) of Gilan, from which the movement took the name Jangali. It was associated with an organization often called the Union of Islam (Ettehad-e Eslam), and its stated aims were to resist foreign intervention, to defend Iranian independence, and to reform government. The forests gave it security, and the Caspian region's distinct geography (see Chapter 1) helped shape its history.

The movement had a base among small landowners, merchants, clerics, and peasants of the region. It cooperated, at different times, with different foreign actors. During the war it had contacts with German and Ottoman agents, as many resistance groups did, but its aims were primarily local and nationalist. In 1918 it clashed with and negotiated with the British Dunsterforce that passed through Gilan, and relations with British forces remained hostile. Kuchak Khan's own ideas combined Islamic reformism, constitutionalism, and anti-imperialism, and he was wary of the revolutionary Marxism that arrived from Russia.

The next stage came in 1920. In May, Soviet naval forces landed at Anzali, ostensibly to pursue White Russian ships that had taken refuge in the port. The small British garrison withdrew, and Soviet forces and Iranian communists, many of them from the Caucasus, entered the political scene. In June 1920 a revolutionary republic was proclaimed in Gilan, with Kuchak Khan at its head, in cooperation with Iranian communists and Bolshevik representatives. The republic is sometimes called the Soviet Republic of Gilan or the Persian Socialist Soviet Republic.

The alliance was uneasy from the start. Kuchak Khan and the Jangalis were Muslim reformers, rooted in local society, and concerned with Iranian independence, while many of the communists wanted a radical social revolution, including measures against landowners and religion, and looked to Moscow for guidance. Differences over land confiscation, the role of Islam, and the leadership of the movement led to open conflict in the summer and autumn of 1920, and Kuchak Khan withdrew to the forests. Bolshevik policy itself was divided between the aim of fostering revolution in Iran and the aim of normalizing relations with the Iranian government and with Britain, an aim that prevailed in early 1921 and led to the Soviet-Iranian treaty (Section 80.15).

The movement's end came with the withdrawal of Soviet support and the advance of Iranian government forces under Reza Khan in 1921 (Section 80.16). Kuchak Khan died in the mountains in December 1921. The Jangali movement therefore cannot be described simply as a communist revolution, which it was not, nor simply as a nationalist campaign, since it entered into an alliance with Bolshevism and took part in a republic proclaimed under Soviet protection. It is best understood as a regional movement that took advantage of the political space created by war and revolution and whose leader combined nationalism, reform, and religion in a way that did not fit comfortably with either the communists or the central state.

The alliance in Gilan contained unresolved questions of property, religion, and political method. Kuchak Khan sought to preserve his appeal among religiously observant and nationalist supporters while cooperating with revolutionary forces whose programs could entail confiscation and aggressive social transformation. A temporary common enemy did not settle who should control the republican administration. Conflict between Jangali leaders and more radical communists fractured the coalition in 1920. Subsequent attempts at reconciliation were affected by Moscow's pursuit of an interstate settlement with Tehran. The disappearance of Soviet military backing and the central army's offensive were therefore interacting developments: a shift in international policy deprived the provincial revolutionary project of resources at precisely the moment government forces became more effective. Kuchak Khan died in the mountains in late 1921 after the collapse of his movement.

Section 80.12 · Division III

Provincial Insurgencies and Competing Visions of National Government

In the years after the war, several regional movements arose in different parts of Iran. They had different leaders, social bases, and goals, and one of the main tasks of the historian is to avoid merging them into a single story of regionalism or separatism.

In Azerbaijan, Sheikh Mohammad Khiabani, a cleric and leader of the Democrat Party in Tabriz, led a movement in 1920. Khiabani had been a prominent constitutionalist, and his movement sought reform, local control of provincial administration, and resistance to the central government in Tehran, which he saw as dominated by the Anglo-Persian Agreement and as unwilling to reform. In April 1920 his movement renamed the province Azadistan, meaning the land of freedom. Khiabani did not call for separation from Iran; he and his supporters spoke of reform for the whole country and of the province's role as a base for it. Forces supporting the newly appointed governor Mokhber al-Saltaneh Hedayat suppressed the movement in September 1920; Khiabani died on 14 September after Cossacks located his hiding place. Accounts differ over the precise circumstances of his death.

In Khurasan, Colonel Mohammad Taqi Khan Pessian, a Gendarmerie officer with nationalist convictions and a record of service in the war, played a role in the politics of the province. After the February coup, Pessian initially acted on Sayyed Zia’s instructions, arresting the governor-general Qavam al-Saltaneh in April. Qavam’s subsequent appointment as prime minister transformed the relationship into open conflict. Pessian controlled Mashhad and administered much of the province until he was killed fighting Kurdish tribal forces near Quchan on 3 October 1921. Pessian's movement was military in character, based on the Gendarmerie rather than on a party or popular movement, and it was concerned with national politics, in particular with opposition to the coup and its leaders.

In the northwest, Simko Shikak, a Kurdish tribal leader, rose to prominence during the war and its aftermath. Simko's activity was bound up with the local power struggles of the Kurdish tribes, with relations with the Ottoman Empire, Russia, and Britain, and with the vacuum left by the withdrawal of the Russian presence. He was responsible for the killing of the Assyrian patriarch Mar Shimun in 1918 (Section 80.3), and he established control over a large area west of Lake Urmia in the following years. His revolt was suppressed by government forces under Reza Khan in 1922, though he returned in later years and was killed in 1930. Simko's revolt reflected tribal ambition and local politics more than a developed program of national government, though modern interpretations of him vary and reflect later political concerns, including Kurdish nationalist memory.

These movements show that demands for autonomy, constitutional reform, local authority, security, and national independence were not the same thing. Khiabani sought reform and provincial initiative within Iran. Kuchak Khan sought independence and reform and allied for a time with communism. Pessian initially served the post-coup government but subsequently resisted Qavam’s authority, pursuing a reforming provincial administration with national ambitions. Simko pursued local power. Their opponents could and did group them together as threats to central authority, and the central authority of the following years took up the language of national unity against all of them. For historians the lesson is that the disintegration of central control in 1918–1921 opened a range of possible political futures, and that the form that prevailed was not the only one that had been proposed.

Khiabani's movement developed out of the experience of the Constitutional Revolution and the provincial Democratic organization, not simply the retreat of Russian troops. Its public politics centered on Tabriz and its press, and it challenged the national government in the name of reform. The killing of Khiabani after the Cossack advance into the city ended that administration, but not the broader argument about how provincial institutions might exercise authority within Iran. Pessian's career presents a different trajectory. His service under Sayyed Zia began within national executive instructions; once Qavam returned to power, the former provincial governor could draw on tribal leaders opposed to the gendarmerie. The encounter near Quchan reflected local resentment of revenue collection and military operations as well as rivalry at the center. Neither case should be treated as a direct contest between Reza Khan and a unified separatist movement. These defeats created opportunities for the army but involved other political and local actors.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Provincial Political Authority from the Constitutional Revolution to the Postwar Crisis

What Changed

  • After 1906, provincial associations and constitutionalist committees had claimed a share of authority; by 1920 armed movements, rather than elected councils, were the main vehicles of regional politics.
  • Foreign powers' roles shifted from Russian and British occupation to British predominance and Soviet intervention.
  • The Gendarmerie and Cossack forces became central political actors.

What Stayed the Same

  • Provincial notables, tribal leaders, and clerics continued to hold local power and bargain with the center.
  • Geography (forests in Gilan, mountains in the northwest, distance in Khurasan) continued to shape the capacity of the center to control the provinces.
  • Tehran's fiscal weakness limited its ability to impose authority.

Why the pattern matters: The postwar movements were not new departures but variations on a long pattern of negotiation between center and region (see Chapter 1). What was new was the availability of an armed centralizing force that did not need to negotiate.

Section 80.13 · Division IV

Reza Khan, the Cossack Brigade, and the Crisis of Central Authority

Reza Khan was born in 1878 (the exact date is variously reported) in the district of Savadkuh in Mazandaran, into a family of modest means with military connections. He entered the Persian Cossack Brigade as a young man and rose through its ranks. The Brigade had been created in 1879 under Russian officers, and its Iranian soldiers and noncommissioned officers served under the authority of the Russian command. Reza Khan acquired a reputation as a capable and forceful soldier, and by the time of the First World War he held officer rank. His background was neither aristocratic nor clerical, and his career was a product of the military institutions that the late Qajar state and its foreign patrons had created.

The Cossack Brigade was an unusual institution. It was Iranian in its rank and file, but its command, funding, and discipline were Russian. After 1917 the Russian officers' position became uncertain, and by 1920 changes were taking place. British officers, particularly under General Edmund Ironside, who took command of the British force in northern Iran in 1920, became involved in arranging the Brigade's future. Ironside and other British officers favored Iranian command of the Brigade, supported by British funds and supervision, and the Russian officers were removed. In this reorganization, Reza Khan emerged as the principal Iranian commander in the Qazvin area.

The strategic context was Britain's decision to reduce its commitments. After the failure of the Anglo-Persian Agreement to gain acceptance, and with the cost of maintaining forces in Iran under criticism at home, the British government decided at the end of 1920 to withdraw its troops from northern Iran in the following spring. British officers on the spot were concerned about what would happen when they left. A weak central government might be unable to resist the Bolshevik presence in Gilan, and the Cossack Brigade, the best available Iranian force, might fall into the hands of political rivals or disintegrate. British officers therefore had reasons to want an Iranian military commander who could preserve order, and Reza Khan was one candidate.

How far did the British make Reza Khan? This is one of the most debated questions of the period. The evidence establishes that British officers and diplomats were in contact with Iranian politicians and officers, that the British supplied funds and equipment to the Brigade, that Ironside favored a strong Iranian military leader, and that British officials knew in advance, at least in general terms, of the planned move on Tehran. It does not establish that the coup was devised and ordered by the British government in London. Historians such as those who have worked on the British and Iranian archives emphasize that British policy was divided and that local officers acted with considerable independence. The Iranian actors had their own reasons: Sayyed Zia al-Din, Reza Khan, and others were convinced that the existing government could not govern, and their planning drew on Iranian political currents. A reasonable summary is that Britain encouraged and facilitated the coup, but that the claim that Reza Khan was simply installed in a centrally directed British plan goes beyond the evidence. The question remains a source of controversy in Iranian political memory.

The deeper condition for Reza Khan's emergence was the crisis of central authority described in earlier sections. The government in Tehran could not pay its officials or maintain order. The Majles was not sitting. The Anglo-Persian Agreement had discredited the cabinet that signed it. Regional movements challenged the center, and Soviet forces were in Gilan. In these circumstances the one institution that retained organization and arms was the military, and the commander of its best unit was in a position to determine events.

The former Russian command had maintained cohesion in the Cossack force through an officer hierarchy that was unusual among Iranian military institutions. After the collapse of imperial Russia and the unsuccessful campaigns against Gilan, the dismissal of Russian commanders created a problem of leadership. The nominal Iranian commander, Sardar-e Homayun Qasem Khan Vali, and the experienced officers at Qazvin were competing for authority within an institution undergoing reorganization. Lieutenant Colonel Henry Smyth helped retrain and reorganize the Qazvin contingent; Ironside selected Reza Khan for operational responsibility. The choice depended in part on his demonstrated military capacity. It did not mean that Iranian officers lacked initiative or that all the British officers agreed about the political purpose of the reorganization.

The archival distinction is particularly significant. Niloofar Shambayati's account of the coup identifies an approved British-legation proposal to exchange the Tehran Cossack contingent for a smaller replacement from Qazvin. The force that actually marched was far larger than that projected replacement. Walter Smart, the Oriental secretary of the legation, and Smyth were connected to the preparations, while Herman Norman's information about the ultimate objective appears to have been incomplete until shortly before the event. Stephanie Cronin emphasizes the importance of direct support by British officials in Iran and regards their initiative as likely. That interpretation is compatible with the absence of evidence of a Foreign Office directive authorizing the specific seizure of power. Students should not confuse a policy objective, a logistical action, foreknowledge, and command responsibility; the archival evidence for each is different.

Section 80.14 · Division IV

The Coup of 21 February 1921

On 18 February 1921, Cossack forces and a small gendarmerie contingent under Reza Khan began the march from Qazvin toward Tehran, which they occupied overnight on 20–21 February. Their movement was planned in coordination with Sayyed Zia al-Din Tabatabai, a journalist and politician who had been prominent in the previous years, held views critical of the existing political class, and had contacts with British officials. The forces entered the capital with little resistance. They occupied government buildings and strategic points, and the cabinet, already in a weak position, collapsed.

Reza Khan issued proclamations in which he announced that he had taken control in order to restore order and security and in which he addressed the population of Tehran, calling for calm and for observance of orders. Public order was to be maintained by martial law, and certain newspapers were closed. Arrests were made on a considerable scale, and a number of prominent politicians and members of the Qajar aristocracy and the political elite were detained. Among the justifications offered were the weakness and corruption of the previous government and the need to confront the threats facing the country. The proclamations were short and directive, and they asserted the authority of the new force rather than explaining a program in detail.

The coup did not abolish the monarchy. The Shah, Ahmad Shah, was kept on the throne, and in the following days he appointed Sayyed Zia as prime minister, a step he took under pressure and with, according to some accounts, reluctance. Reza Khan received a title that gave him command of the army, Sardar Sepah. The arrangement can be described as a division of roles in which Sayyed Zia was the civilian head of government and Reza Khan was the military head, and in which the Qajar monarch lent a formal legitimacy that neither of the others possessed. It is important to emphasize the point: the coup initially displaced a government, not a dynasty. The end of Qajar rule came only more than four years later, by a different process.

The role of the British in the coup requires careful treatment, as discussed in Section 80.13. Certain British military and diplomatic personnel assisted the preparations, although officials differed in their knowledge of the intended operation and its scope. Ironside's own writings and the diplomatic correspondence of the time indicate that he and others regarded a coup by an Iranian commander as a way to avoid the collapse of order after the British withdrawal. Whether London had authorized the specific move is not demonstrated by the documents available, and the surviving records show disagreement and ambiguity among the British officials involved. Iranian participants and later memoirists differ in how they describe the relationship. Historians must therefore work with an incomplete record in which the sources, British and Iranian, are partial.

The mobilization was an organized operation rather than a spontaneous mutiny. Accounts cited in the specialist literature identify approximately 2,200 Cossacks and one hundred gendarmes departing Qazvin; the numbers are estimates of the marching contingent, not a census of its supporters. Senior Cossack officer Ahmad Aqa Amir-Ahmadi and gendarmerie officers Masud Khan Keyhan and Kazem Khan Sayyah participated in the planning or movement alongside the better-known leaders. Orders from the crown to halt or return were not obeyed. On approaching Tehran the force presented its action as necessary to establish a government able to resist expected Bolshevik incursions. The entry on 21 February was achieved with limited combat. There is disagreement over whether the government's refusal to resist reflected British advice or the unwillingness of troops to fight; what is clear is that the existing government's command was inadequate.

The coup announced itself through coercive changes to the daily life of the capital. Meetings and press publication were restricted under martial law, government offices were temporarily shut for reorganization, and military authorities gained responsibility for public order. The arrests served more than one purpose: preventing opponents from organizing, intimidating the established elite, and in some cases obtaining money. The reform rhetoric of the civilian leadership did not erase the implications of a seizure carried out by armed men. Yet the retention of Ahmad Shah was politically useful. The Shah provided the appointments through which the leaders sought to transform an occupation of the capital into a recognized government. The prior constitutional framework was thus both constrained and used; the coup could be defended in the language of national necessity even as it made parliamentary oversight harder to exercise.

A contemporary communication from British minister Herman Norman to Foreign Secretary Curzon on 21 February records Reza Khan's explanation that a stronger Iranian government was required to meet the anticipated threat after British withdrawal. It also records Norman’s advice to the Shah to negotiate with the victors. This is evidence of British diplomatic accommodation at the moment of the seizure, not conclusive proof of who originated every element of the plan. The distinction is essential in interpreting the later careers of the coup partners: their joint enterprise in February was followed almost immediately by competition over who would control the army, cabinet, and finances.

Section 80.15 · Division IV

Sayyed Zia's Government, the Soviet-Iranian Treaty, and the New International Environment

The government of Sayyed Zia lasted about three months. It announced a program of reform that included measures against corruption, a reorganization of finances, and proposals concerning land, among them a plan to distribute Crown lands. These proposals were not carried out in that period. The government also arrested a large number of notables and politicians, a step that made enemies among the established elite, and it quickly came into conflict with Reza Khan and with the Shah. Sayyed Zia was dismissed in late May 1921 and left Iran. Reza Khan had already entered Sayyed Zia’s cabinet as minister of war in April; he retained the portfolio in the Qavam al-Saltaneh cabinet formed on 4 June 1921. The fall of Sayyed Zia showed that the real center of power was the military commander and not the civilian partner of the coup.

Meanwhile Iran's foreign relations were being reorganized. On 26 February 1921, days after the coup, a treaty of friendship between Iran and Soviet Russia was signed in Moscow. On 15 December 1921 the Fourth Majles authorized exchange of the treaty following a Soviet clarification of Articles 5 and 6 on 12 December. Parliamentary approval, subsequent exchange of ratifications, and international registration on 7 June 1922 were distinct stages. Under the treaty the Soviet government renounced the privileges and concessions held by tsarist Russia, canceled Iranian debts to Russia, and recognized Iranian sovereignty. These provisions answered a long-standing Iranian grievance and gave the treaty considerable popularity. But it also contained clauses that allowed Soviet forces to enter Iranian territory if a third power used it as a base of attack against Soviet Russia and if Iran could not remove the threat. Critics pointed out that this gave Moscow a basis for intervention, and the interpretation of these provisions remained controversial. In later decades they became the subject of diplomatic dispute.

The treaty also altered the British position. Britain had planned to withdraw from northern Iran, and with the treaty and the change of government in Tehran, the Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919 lost any realistic prospect of acceptance. British forces completed their withdrawal in 1921, and the agreement was abandoned. The result was a new triangular relationship among Iran, Britain, and Soviet Russia. Britain retained its oil interests and its influence in the south. Soviet Russia, subsequently part of the Soviet Union formed in December 1922, had a treaty with Iran and an interest in preventing a British return to the north. Iranian governments could try to use the rivalry between them, though the room for maneuver was limited.

Sayyed Zia's political weakness lay in his inability to organize a constituency comparable to Reza Khan's military base. Detaining established notables reduced their immediate freedom of action but did not create an alternative system of revenue collection, administration, or durable political support. The dismissal of the prime minister while Reza Khan retained his ministerial position illustrated the asymmetry. The same Shah who was compelled to accept the coup's arrangements could participate in removing its civilian protagonist. Qavam, previously detained on instructions from Sayyed Zia, reentered politics at the head of the government. The outcome was not a straightforward military dictatorship from February onward, but a sequence of negotiations and contests within which the military commander increasingly held the decisive power.

The Soviet treaty had been negotiated before the coup and was not simply a result of Sayyed Zia's accession. Its provisions restored rights and property and renounced a range of tsarist privileges. In return, Iran was obliged to prevent hostile activity against Soviet Russia. Article 6 provided a conditional right of Soviet military entry in the event of a threat connected with a third power and Iranian inability to remove it; troops were to leave once the danger had passed. In December the Soviet representative clarified that Articles 5 and 6 concerned preparations for serious armed attack, including by enemies of the revolutionary regime supported by foreign powers. That assurance was attached to the process of Iranian parliamentary consideration, but a diplomatic assurance did not remove all potential disagreement over when the conditions were satisfied. The Fourth Majles's authorization on 15 December 1921, after the clarification, underlines the continuing relevance of constitutional approval even when military power was expanding.

The Fourth Majles, which convened in June 1921, also became the venue for financial and military arguments that the absence of parliament had postponed. Its existence made the contrast with 1919 clearer: a representative institution had returned, and foreign arrangements could again be addressed through parliamentary procedure. Yet the new balance of coercive power made that procedure vulnerable to the executive.

Historical Vocabulary
Coup d'État, Military Government, Centralization, and State Capacity

Definition: A coup d'état is a sudden seizure of governmental power by a small group, usually involving armed force and often without a change in the formal constitutional order. Military government is government in which armed forces or their commanders hold decisive power. Centralization is the concentration of authority in a central government at the expense of regional and local authorities. State capacity is the ability of a government to collect revenue, enforce its decisions, maintain order, and deliver services across its territory.

Historical context: The coup of 1921 displaced a cabinet but retained the Shah and the constitution. Over the next years the army expanded and extended its authority across the country, which increased state capacity in the sense of enforcement and revenue, while limiting the independence of regional and parliamentary bodies.

Why it matters: The terms allow historians to distinguish the restoration of central control from the achievement of accountable government. A state may become more capable without becoming more representative.

Potential misunderstanding: Increased state capacity is not necessarily positive or negative. Its value depends on how it is used and on what is lost in acquiring it. Nor does a coup necessarily end the previous constitutional order, as the Iranian case shows.

Section 80.16 · Division IV

Military Consolidation and the Defeat of Regional Challengers

From his position as minister of war, Reza Khan set about building a unified and better-funded army. In 1921 and 1922 the Cossack units, the Gendarmerie, and other forces were brought under a single command, and the dual structure of rival armed forces was ended. Officers were appointed, pay was regularized, and a portion of government revenue was devoted to the military. He made himself indispensable to a succession of cabinets, which came and went while he remained at the Ministry of War, and he built a political network among officers, politicians, and officials.

The new army was used against the regional movements described in Section 80.12. In 1921 Kurdish tribal forces acting on Qavam’s orders defeated Pessian’s gendarmes near Quchan. Reza Khan’s forces separately moved against the Jangali movement in Gilan, ending its revolutionary republic in the autumn of 1921. In 1922 forces suppressed Simko's revolt in the northwest, though Simko himself survived and returned. The suppression of these movements established the army as the main instrument of central authority. Provincial autonomy, which had been a feature of Iranian politics since the Constitutional Revolution, was progressively curtailed.

For many Iranians the new security was welcome. Roads were safer, banditry and tribal raiding declined in some areas, and the central government's authority was visible in places where it had been absent. Merchants, landowners, and urban residents who had suffered from disorder had reason to support a force that promised stability. Nationalists who had been frustrated by foreign domination saw in the army a means of building an independent state. The appeal of order after years of war, famine, and foreign occupation should not be underestimated.

But the same process had costs. The suppression of regional movements involved violence and the death of leaders, and the army's growth strengthened the position of its commander relative to civilian institutions. The Majles that met after 1921 was increasingly subject to the influence of the military and of Reza Khan's allies. Critics such as Mohammad Mossadegh and Sayyed Hasan Modarres warned that military centralization was undermining the constitution. It is therefore necessary to distinguish between the restoration of central authority, which was achieved, and constitutional accountability, which was not. The army's consolidation reduced the likelihood that Iran would fragment, but it also removed the checks that might have limited the ruler.

This chapter stops short of the extensive tribal, administrative, and social policies of the later Pahlavi state, which are examined in Chapters 81 and 82. What is important here is the logic by which military consolidation before 1925 created the conditions for dynastic change. By the time the Majles voted in 1925, Reza Khan controlled the principal armed force in the country, had defeated most of the movements that had challenged the center, and was the principal figure in a government whose civilian members depended on his support.

Unification of the armed forces was administrative as well as operational. The Cossacks and gendarmes had possessed separate chains of command, recruitment arrangements, and institutional loyalties. Bringing their units into a national framework required appointments, expenditures, and the allocation of responsibilities over a large territory. Consolidation did not automatically eliminate personal loyalties; commanders with careers linked to Reza Khan acquired positions within the new structure. The creation of a coordinated command thus enhanced the state's ability to move forces between provinces while also magnifying its minister's personal political leverage.

The Gilan campaign highlights the conjunction of diplomacy and military power. Soviet policy moved toward normal relations with the central government, while internal disputes reduced the revolutionary republic's cohesion. Tehran's forces could consequently enter territory where the opponents' previous external support and political alliances had weakened. The 1922 campaign against Simko's forces in the northwest proceeded in another environment, where the army sought to restore control over routes, settlements, and the borderlands. It should not be confused with the suppression of Khiabani's Democrat movement in 1920 or Pessian's defeat in 1921. In each case the central government faced a different local political structure. The new army obtained prestige from ending visible challenges, but its success does not establish that an enduring settlement with every community had been negotiated. That unresolved issue carries into the next volume.

Cause and Effect

How the Crisis of State Capacity Enabled Reza Khan's Ascendancy

Long-term causes

Fiscal weakness, dependence on foreign loans and concessions, the absence of a strong standing army, and a long record of foreign intervention that discredited civilian governments.

Short-term causes

Wartime occupation, the famine and epidemic crisis, the collapse of the Russian presence, the failure of the Anglo-Persian Agreement, regional movements, and the impending British withdrawal.

Immediate trigger and event

The coup of 21 February 1921, followed by Reza Khan's appointment as minister of war and the amalgamation of rival armed forces under his command.

Effects

Restoration of central military authority, defeat of regional challengers, and a concentration of power in the army, with reduced accountability of government to the Majles.

Historical complexity: Reza Khan's ascent did not follow automatically from state weakness. Other outcomes, including a civilian reform government, regional autonomy, or a republic, were proposed. His success also depended on the army he commanded, on the support or acquiescence of important social groups, on the attitudes of Britain and the Soviet Union, and on the decisions of rivals.

Section 80.17 · Division V

Reza Khan's Premiership and the Declining Authority of Ahmad Shah

On 28 October 1923 Ahmad Shah appointed Reza Khan prime minister, and shortly afterward the Shah left Iran for Europe. He did not return. The appointment recognized an existing reality: for two years Reza Khan, from the Ministry of War, had been the most powerful figure in government. As prime minister he now held the civil executive as well, while retaining his command of the army. The Shah's departure, whose motives are variously described as health, a desire to escape political pressure, and a wish to avoid a confrontation, left the throne effectively without its occupant, and Ahmad Shah's authority declined steadily during his absence.

Reza Khan's methods of consolidation were varied. He cultivated parliamentary deputies, and the Fifth Majles, which met from 1924, included a bloc of supporters sometimes associated with the Renovation (Tajaddod) group, whose members included men who would become important in the Pahlavi administration. He worked with administrators and politicians who valued order and reform, with landowners who wanted stability, with merchants who wanted security and predictable commerce, and with military commanders whose careers depended on him. His relations with religious authorities were more complicated: he sought clerical support or at least acquiescence while avoiding concessions that would limit his program.

Finances were central. In 1922 the Majles had engaged an American financial adviser, Arthur Millspaugh, as administrator-general of finances, with substantial powers over revenue and expenditure. Millspaugh's reforms aimed to improve revenue collection, regularize budgets, and reduce waste, and they helped to place the government's finances on a firmer basis than they had been in years. Iranian politicians chose an American in part because the United States was not a neighbor and had no history of imperial intervention in Iran, though American oil interests also sought concessions. The tension between Millspaugh and Reza Khan over military spending, which absorbed a large part of the budget, became a persistent issue. The revenue improvements nevertheless financed the expanding army.

The question of how Reza Khan accumulated authority without replacing the constitutional monarchy has an institutional answer. The constitution gave the Shah the power to appoint the prime minister and gave the Majles the power to approve the cabinet. Reza Khan secured both, since the Shah, still in Iran at the time of the appointment but politically weakened, appointed him and the Majles, which he influenced, supported him. He therefore governed within the forms of the constitution while changing its substance. This pattern, in which the formal institutions remained but the balance of power within them shifted, is important for understanding the events of 1925. It suggests that the removal of the dynasty was not the first step of a seizure of power but the last step in a process in which real authority had already passed.

The opening of the Fifth Majles in 1924 illustrates that parliamentary politics retained its own resources. Support for the executive came in part from the Tajaddod (Renovation) faction and modernizing politicians who saw a powerful government as a prerequisite for national reform. Deputies associated with the Socialists could support parts of such a program without endorsing every military intervention; conservative and clerical deputies could favor security while rejecting changes to the constitution. The parties and alignments were not simply equivalent to a formal government and opposition with stable memberships. Patronage, constituency interests, and relationships to ministers also shaped voting. The exercise of military influence in elections and parliamentary disputes brought a new imbalance into this already complex political environment.

Millspaugh's appointment in 1922 reflected the legislature's attempt to revive fiscal administration through a foreign expert whose authority derived from an Iranian law and contract rather than a bilateral scheme like the 1919 agreement. Revenue reforms promised more consistent salaries, tax administration, and accounting. But the military establishment made competing claims on these resources. A budget that assigned a large portion of its receipts to the army could strengthen road security and enforce collection, yet diminish what remained available for courts, schools, infrastructure, and public health. The parliamentary question was not merely whether expenditure increased, but who decided priorities and whether elected deputies could inspect them. A government that collected taxes more effectively was not thereby more representative. Reza Khan's combination of military command and premiership brought this tension into the center of constitutional politics.

Section 80.18 · Division V

The Republican Movement of 1924 and the Politics of Opposition

In early 1924 a movement arose to transform Iran into a republic. Its inspiration came partly from Turkey, where Mustafa Kemal had abolished the sultanate in 1922, proclaimed the Turkish Republic in October 1923, and abolished the caliphate in March 1924. Republicanism appealed to some Iranian intellectuals, journalists, military officers, and politicians who saw in the monarchy an obstacle to reform and in Ahmad Shah an absent and ineffective ruler. A republic seemed to offer a modern state with a strong executive, and it fit the example of Turkey. Reza Khan and his supporters at first encouraged or tolerated the movement, and it gained support in the press, the army, and among some deputies.

Opposition was strong and came from several quarters. Many clerics feared that a republic on the Turkish model would lead to the secularization of the state, and the abolition of the caliphate in Turkey heightened such concerns. Sayyed Hasan Modarres, a leading cleric and deputy, was prominent among the opponents, and religious leaders mobilized public opinion against the project. Constitutionalists such as Mohammad Mossadegh opposed the concentration of power that a republic under Reza Khan would imply. Merchants and bazaar groups feared instability, and some provincial notables were unwilling to see the monarchy replaced. Large demonstrations were held in Tehran against republicanism, and the controversy took on a religious character.

In the spring of 1924 Reza Khan abandoned the project. He traveled to Qom to consult the religious leaders and then made public statements calling for an end to republican agitation, and the movement ceased. Historians have debated whether he sincerely sought a republic or used the movement to test opinion and pressure the monarchy. The evidence does not settle the question. What is clear is that he judged that he lacked sufficient support, in particular among religious leaders and parts of the urban population, to establish a republic, and that the episode demonstrated that an attack on the monarchy, as such, would meet strong resistance.

The failure influenced the course of events in 1925. It showed that a change of regime would need to be presented in a form that did not offend religious sentiment, and that a dynastic solution, with a new monarch rather than a republic, would be more acceptable. It also showed the political lines among opponents. Students should distinguish opposition to republicanism from unconditional support for the Qajar monarchy. Modarres and others who opposed a republic were not necessarily defenders of Ahmad Shah, and many were critical of Reza Khan's growing power. The opposition thus had different motives, and its members would find themselves divided when the question of the dynasty arose in 1925.

Republican advocacy reached the streets and the chamber in March 1924. Supporters circulated petitions and used newspapers and public meetings to associate the republic with national regeneration; military officials' involvement in organizing expressions of support cast doubt on whether every petition reflected voluntary opinion. A republic could mean popular sovereignty and elected office to one advocate and an executive presidency backed by the army to another. This ambiguity made it impossible to infer the political implications of the proposal from the word itself.

On 22 March opponents, including religious leaders and bazaar supporters, gathered at the Majles. An attempt to disperse them produced a confrontation with Reza Khan; the speaker, Mo'tamen al-Molk, warned of parliamentary action against the prime minister. The episode showed that a political assembly which was increasingly constrained could nevertheless confront the executive when opponents mobilized beyond the chamber. The meetings in Qom that followed were also consequential. Abd al-Karim Haeri Yazdi, Mohammad Hossein Naini, and Abu al-Hasan Isfahani pressed against the republican proposal. Their intervention was not necessarily an endorsement of unrestricted government by Reza Khan. Contemporary and later accounts indicate their preference for a monarch whose authority remained constitutionally limited. Reza Khan publicly renounced republican agitation in deference to religious opinion in the spring of 1924. The exact sequence of his proclamation and the scholars’ response is inconsistently dated in published accounts.

The outcome undermines an interpretation in which the establishment of a new dynasty was already predetermined in 1921. The republican project failed, demonstrating the ability of organized opponents to constrain the prime minister, at least on a particular institutional question. The dynastic alternative subsequently offered his supporters a way to retain monarchical symbolism and the established religion while replacing the ruler. For constitutionalists the crucial issue of accountability remained; a monarchy with a commander as its sovereign could be less constitutional in practice than a genuinely limited republic.

Section 80.19 · Division V

Sheikh Khazal, Khuzestan, and the Final Weakening of Qajar Authority

Khuzestan, the southwestern lowland region discussed in Chapter 1, had been home to a distinctive political arrangement for decades. Sheikh Khazal, ruler of Mohammareh and the surrounding Arab population, exercised autonomous authority under nominal Iranian sovereignty. He had close relations with Britain, which valued his cooperation in protecting the Gulf and the oil installations, and with the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, which had leased land and obtained local support through him. His authority depended on his control of territory and tribes, his wealth, and his relationship with the British, and it ran counter to the central government's claim to rule the whole country.

As Reza Khan strengthened the army and the central government, the status of Khuzestan became a test of national authority. The region produced the oil on which the British position depended, and its autonomous ruler represented the kind of local power that centralizers wanted to end. In late 1924 Reza Khan led a campaign into Khuzestan. Britain, whose government did not wish to break with the Iranian government and whose support for the Sheikh was limited, did not intervene on his behalf, and Khazal submitted. On 18 April 1925 army personnel acting through Fazlollah Zahedi seized Khazal and his son Abd al-Hamid aboard his vessel; both were taken to Tehran. Khazal remained under restriction there until his death in 1936.

The campaign mattered for several reasons. It extended the authority of the central government to the oil-producing region, and it demonstrated that Britain would not use force to protect its regional ally against the Iranian army. It enhanced Reza Khan's reputation as a leader who could unify the country, which strengthened his position in the parliamentary politics of 1925. It also showed that Iranian nationalism and state-building could advance without a break with Britain, whose oil interests were not directly attacked.

The meaning of centralization differed for different people. For government officials and nationalists it meant the end of anomalies that compromised sovereignty. For the Arab population of Khuzestan and for local leaders it meant the loss of local authority and its replacement by officials from Tehran, in some cases with little knowledge of local conditions. The history of Khuzestan under Pahlavi rule, including its later oil politics and the position of its Arab population, was shaped by this transition. The episode, like the other regional campaigns, should be seen from both viewpoints: as a stage in the integration of the territory of the state and as the subordination of a locally rooted political order.

Khazal's autonomy had been maintained through fiscal negotiations and relationships with imperial representatives as well as through local military and tribal authority. Britain had given him assurances connected with his cooperation, but those assurances did not guarantee an independent state or unconditional British intervention against the Iranian government. His position became increasingly vulnerable when Tehran pressed for overdue revenues and demanded that its officials exercise jurisdiction in the southwest. The government's claim to collect taxes from a nominally subordinate regional ruler was therefore inseparable from the military campaign. The dispute was not solely about ethnicity or oil; it involved the character of Iranian sovereignty and the practical authority of separate provincial networks.

The British authorities faced a choice between defending an old regional client and accepting a stronger central government with which the oil company could negotiate. Their protection of commercial installations did not require preserving Khazal's political autonomy indefinitely, and intervention on his behalf risked conflict with Tehran. The campaign and the Sheikh's submission consequently did not destroy British participation in petroleum development. They changed the identity of the Iranian counterpart with whom British officials and the company increasingly dealt. Khazal's arrest in April 1925 separated his personal authority from the local institutions and estates that had sustained it. The restoration of formal national control was thus accompanied by the removal of an influential intermediary. The political meaning of the episode differed sharply for the centralizers, the Sheikh's dependents, and Arab communities whose subsequent relationship with the state remained contested.

Section 80.20 · Division V

The Termination of Qajar Rule and Historical Synthesis

The Majles Decision of 31 October 1925

In the autumn of 1925 the final steps were taken. Ahmad Shah remained in Europe. Reza Khan, now with the Khuzestan campaign behind him, held the prime ministership and the army. On 31 October 1925 the Majles voted to terminate the rule of the Qajar dynasty and to entrust provisional government to Reza Khan, pending the decision of a constituent assembly on the question of the monarchy. The resolution invoked public welfare, ended Qajar sovereignty, vested provisional government in Reza Khan within the existing legal framework, and referred the permanent form of government and revision of specified constitutional articles to a constituent assembly.

Opposition was expressed in the Majles. Mohammad Mossadegh and Sayyed Hasan Modarres, among others, argued against the measure. They contended that the procedure was inconsistent with the constitution, that the Majles lacked the authority to depose a monarch and transfer the crown, and that the concentration of power in one person was dangerous. Their arguments were recorded, but the measure passed by a large majority. The debate and the vote must be understood in context. Soldiers were visible in Tehran, the government controlled the machinery of elections and public order, and opponents faced the risk of political retaliation. A formal vote in these conditions does not tell us how deputies would have voted freely, and the historian should distinguish procedural regularity from freely given consent.

The Constituent Assembly of December 1925

A constituent assembly was convened in December 1925. It was elected rapidly and reflected the government's influence. On 12 December it approved amendments to the Supplementary Fundamental Law that transferred the hereditary monarchy to Reza Khan and his male descendants, adopting the dynastic name Pahlavi, and it set out rules for succession. A few days later, on 15 December, Reza Khan took the oath as shah. The assembly also reflected an effort to avoid the religious controversy that had defeated the republican project: the amendments preserved the monarchy and the constitution while changing the occupant.

The constitutional objection concerned the Majles’s competence to depose the dynasty to which the existing Supplement assigned the throne, the deputies’ oath, and the authenticity of popular authorization. Neither the 1906 Fundamental Law nor the 1907 Supplement established an explicit amendment procedure. A constituent assembly was a mechanism adopted to effect the transfer rather than a process prescribed in those texts. The argument in favor was that the constituent assembly was the proper body to amend the constitution and that necessity required it. Reza Khan's supporters also argued that the Qajar dynasty had failed, as shown by the war and its aftermath. The legal and political aspects of the question remain subjects of debate, as the Historical Debate below shows. The coronation, which took place in April 1926, and the development of Pahlavi institutions belong to Chapter 81.

The October vote illustrates how difficult it is to separate formal parliamentary action from the political circumstances that created it. In his telegram to Washington that evening, the American chargé Amory reported eighty in favor, five opposed, and thirty absent. Mohammad Javad Sheikh-ol-Islami’s biography of Ahmad Shah instead records twenty abstentions alongside eighty votes in favor and five opposed. Absence and abstention are distinct categories, and these reports should not be merged into a single reconstructed roll call. The resolution identified Articles 36, 37, 38 and 40 of the Supplement as the provisions to be addressed. Its supporters cited provincial telegrams calling for an end to Qajar rule and invoked earlier electoral changes as precedents for national authority. The minority invoked the deputies' oath, the constitutional assignment of the throne to the Qajars, and the absence of a genuinely expressed popular mandate. These were arguments about the source of political authority, as much as about the merits of Ahmad Shah.

The amended provisions vested the constitutional monarchy in Reza Shah Pahlavi and his male descendants. The heir apparent was to be the eldest son born of an Iranian mother; if there was no son, designation required parliamentary approval and could not favor a Qajar. The rules of regency likewise explicitly excluded Qajars. These provisions are important because the constituent assembly did more than select a ruler: it revised the rules by which future occupants of the monarchy could claim a right to rule. Contemporary diplomatic notification assigns the decision to 12 December 1925, while a specialist constitutional chronology gives 11 December for the amendment act. The biography of Ahmad Shah in Encyclopaedia Iranica reports an assembly vote of 257 to 3; the October parliamentary tally was a separate vote. The oath of office followed on 15 December. The monarchy survived as an institution, but its dynastic basis and the distribution of political power changed. There was no generally accepted pre-existing amendment procedure to resolve the controversy, making the legitimacy of the constituent assembly itself a central question.

The balance sheet of the Qajar period must include achievements as well as failures. The reunification achieved by Agha Mohammad Khan was a major political accomplishment after prolonged dynastic struggles. It did not, however, produce institutions capable of matching the fiscal and military resources of nineteenth-century imperial states. Military defeat accelerated the search for technological expertise and administrative reform, while indemnities and unequal commercial arrangements reduced the resources available for that task. Reform was possible within the dynasty, as the projects of Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir demonstrated, but sustaining it required overcoming opposition within the court, securing stable revenues, and developing institutions that could survive their founders. The expansion of education, publishing, translation, and public discussion likewise generated possibilities that extended beyond royal initiative.

Economic integration and constitutional politics also interacted. Foreign loans and concessions could finance projects that the treasury could not otherwise undertake, yet their conditions constrained independent policy. Merchants, religious scholars, workers, and intellectuals formulated new claims against arbitrary power. The Tobacco Protest established the efficacy of organized pressure; the Constitutional Revolution provided an arena and legal language for nationwide political claims. Those achievements were not erased when the Majles was closed or a new dynasty was proclaimed. Their practical realization required institutions of taxation, courts, and military command that were particularly difficult to build in the presence of occupation and competing armed networks. The late Qajar state's predicament was therefore not an absence of political innovation but the gap between political innovation and enforceable authority.

The war greatly widened that gap. Its damage to subsistence, public health, and local administration increased demand for security while reducing the capacity of elected and appointed officials to supply it. Postwar nationalists offered different institutional solutions, from reforming provincial movements and constitutional ministries to more radical revolutionary projects. A military-centered solution prevailed in the conditions created by foreign withdrawal, armed consolidation, and the failure of rival political coalitions. It resolved some problems of central command while leaving unresolved the revolutionary demand that power itself be subject to law. The end of Qajar rule is accordingly best understood as both a consequence of accumulated pressures and a contingent choice among political alternatives. The Pahlavi chapters will examine how that new balance of authority transformed the societies whose future it claimed to secure.

Synthesis: Why Did the Qajar Dynasty Lose the Capacity to Preserve Its Own Rule?

The question that concludes Volume VI is why a dynasty that had reconstructed Iranian political authority at the end of the eighteenth century could not preserve its rule in 1925. The answer lies in the accumulation of problems across the whole Qajar period, each of which weakened the foundations of the state and which converged in the crisis of 1914–1925.

  • Dynastic reconstruction and territorial consolidation. Agha Mohammad Khan and his successors reunified much of the plateau (Chapter 69), but the state they built depended on personal authority, tribal and provincial alliances, and a modest administrative apparatus, and not on a large standing army or a deep fiscal base.
  • Military defeats and foreign encroachment. The Russo-Persian wars and the treaties of Gulistan and Turkmenchay (Chapters 70 and 71) cost territory and imposed unequal terms, and rivalry between Russia and Britain (Chapter 72) left Iran under repeated outside pressure.
  • Economic restructuring and foreign concessions. Concessions, loans, and customs arrangements (Chapter 75) gave foreign interests influence over revenue and development while leaving the state short of funds and politically exposed.
  • Religious and intellectual transformation. Reform efforts, the Babi and Baha'i movements, new education, and the press (Chapters 73, 74, and 77) created new political languages and new demands that the court did not satisfy.
  • Constitutional mobilization and parliamentary government. The Constitutional Revolution (Chapters 76, 78, and 79) created institutions and expectations of representative government, but it did not create the means to enforce them against foreign pressure and armed rivals.
  • Wartime devastation and the crisis of national sovereignty. The occupations, famine, and epidemic of 1914–1919 further reduced the capacity of the state and discredited the political class.
  • Military centralization and dynastic replacement. The army, built up under Reza Khan after 1921, became the only institution able to impose order, and its commander used the political system to replace the dynasty.

These factors are linked. The state's weakness made it dependent on foreign loans and forces, which in turn undermined its sovereignty and legitimacy, which encouraged both reform movements and regional challengers, which deepened the crisis. Contingency also mattered: the timing of the war, the Russian Revolution, the British decision to withdraw, and the personalities of Ahmad Shah and Reza Khan all affected outcomes. The Qajar dynasty's fall was not inevitable, but the structural conditions made a sustained recovery under the existing arrangements difficult. The dynasty's ending came less through a sudden collapse than through the progressive transfer of effective power to a new center.

Historical Debate

Did Reza Khan Rescue Iranian Statehood or Undermine the Constitutional Revolution?

The question: How should historians judge the significance of Reza Khan's rise for the history of the Iranian state and of constitutional government?

Interpretation A — A rescue of the state: Some historians emphasize that by 1921 Iran faced fragmentation, foreign domination, and social collapse, and that Reza Khan restored order, unified the army, ended regional rebellions, improved finances, and laid the basis for a modern state able to resist foreign control.
Interpretation B — A subversion of constitutionalism: Others stress that the same process ended the autonomy of the Majles, silenced opposition, and replaced a flawed constitutional order with an authoritarian monarchy, so that the achievements of 1906 were lost. The consolidation relied on coercion, and the procedures of 1925 provided a formal cover.
Interpretation C — A longer tension in Iranian politics: Some scholars argue that the episode reflects a recurring pattern in which the need for a strong state and the demand for accountability have been in tension, and that neither a simple rescue nor a simple betrayal explains it.

Evidence: Parliamentary records, memoirs of politicians on different sides, diplomatic reports, financial records, and the course of later Pahlavi policy.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and weighting: how much to credit the benefits of order and administration, and how much to emphasize the loss of political freedoms. It also involves counterfactuals about what other courses were possible.

Why it matters: Views of 1921–1925 shape views of the Pahlavi period and of the Revolution of 1979, since both supporters and opponents of the monarchy have drawn on this history.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Iran from a constitutional monarchy of weak institutions into a new dynastic state between 1914 and 1925. It began with the inherited condition of the Qajar state after the closure of the Second Majles in 1911: a young monarch, unstable cabinets, a depleted treasury, foreign troops on Iranian soil, and two armed forces, the Cossack Brigade and the Gendarmerie, whose loyalties were uncertain.

The outbreak of the First World War did not make Iran a belligerent, but it made Iran a battlefield. The government's declaration of neutrality on 1 November 1914 was legally meaningful and practically unenforceable. Russian and Ottoman forces fought across Azerbaijan, Hamadan, and Kermanshah, British forces landed in the south, and German agents sought to organize resistance. The civilian populations of the northwest, among them Muslim, Armenian, Assyrian, and Kurdish communities, endured violence, requisitions, and displacement.

Iranian political actors were not passive. The nationalist migration of 1915 and the provisional government at Kermanshah showed that some nationalists chose tactical alliance with Germany and the Ottoman Empire to oppose Britain and Russia. Provincial leaders such as Rais Ali Delvari resisted for their own reasons, and German support amplified rather than created resistance. These episodes revealed the central dilemma of the period, that the forces most committed to independence lacked the means to enforce it.

Oil became a strategic resource. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company, born of the 1901 concession and the discovery of 1908, passed in 1914 under the controlling interest of the British government because the Royal Navy needed fuel. The oilfields, pipeline, and Abadan refinery created an industrial enclave with a new labor force and new relationships with regional leaders, while the Iranian state received limited benefit and could not audit the company.

The wartime subsistence crisis of about 1917–1919 and the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 were the period's greatest human catastrophe. Military requisitioning, transport collapse, trade disruption, speculation, weak state capacity, and disease interacted, and the experience varied by region and class. The scale of mortality cannot be established. Historians disagree about numbers and causes, and this chapter has not treated the highest estimates as fact.

After 1917 the Russian collapse and the Soviet repudiation of tsarist policy opened a vacuum that Britain tried to fill through the Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919. The agreement was signed but never ratified, and it became a symbol of the threat of foreign tutelage. Meanwhile regional movements, namely the Jangalis in Gilan, Khiabani in Azerbaijan, Pessian in Khurasan, and Simko in the northwest, proposed different political projects that should not be merged into one story of regionalism.

The coup of 21 February 1921 displaced the government but kept the Shah. British personnel in Iran provided critical assistance and may have initiated the operation in cooperation with Iranian participants; a specific direction from London is not established. Sayyed Zia's government was short-lived, the Soviet-Iranian treaty of 26 February 1921 repudiated tsarist privileges while containing controversial intervention clauses, and the Anglo-Persian Agreement was abandoned. Reza Khan, as minister of war, unified much of the army and suppressed several regional movements, while other actors such as Qavam helped defeat regional challengers; central authority expanded, but at the expense of constitutional accountability.

Reza Khan became prime minister in October 1923 as Ahmad Shah left for Europe. The republican movement of 1924 failed in the face of clerical and constitutionalist opposition, the campaign against Sheikh Khazal extended central authority to oil-producing Khuzestan, and in 1925 the Majles and a constituent assembly ended Qajar rule and transferred the monarchy to the Pahlavi family. These measures used legislative and constituent institutions under military pressure, but their constitutional validity was disputed; formal votes cannot be equated with freely given consent.

The synthesis in Section 80.20 explains the Qajar collapse as the convergence of long-term fiscal and military weakness, foreign encroachment, economic dependence, the unmet demands of constitutional mobilization, wartime devastation, and the rise of a military centralizing force. The chapter ends at the threshold of the Pahlavi state, whose consolidation is the subject of Chapter 81.

The transition should not be understood as the disappearance of constitutional politics. The parliamentary debates over the 1919 agreement, the Soviet treaty, republicanism, and the 1925 resolution show how constitutional claims remained central even as executive and military power expanded. The Qajar period left a territorial state, reform institutions, an expanding public sphere, and the framework of representative government. The Pahlavi regime inherited these achievements together with their unresolved fiscal, regional, and constitutional tensions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a long-established dynasty came to be replaced without a revolution in the usual sense. The Qajar monarchy was not overthrown by a mass uprising. It was hollowed out by war, fiscal weakness, and the failure of civilian governments, and its end was carried out through constitutional forms under the influence of a commander who already held the effective power of the state. Understanding this process helps students see how formal institutions and real power can diverge, and how legal procedures can be used to ratify changes that were produced by other means.

The chapter also shows how foreign intervention and Iranian agency intertwined. Britain, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, Germany, and later the Soviet Union all shaped events, yet none determined them. Iranian nationalists, provincial leaders, clerics, deputies, soldiers, and ordinary people made choices under constraint. The chapter's cautions, about the coup's origins, about mortality estimates, and about the meaning of the 1925 votes, are lessons in how historians reason from incomplete evidence.

Many of the chapter's themes continue. The question of control over oil revenue returns in the Pahlavi period and in the nationalization crisis of the early 1950s. The tension between a strong state and accountable government recurs throughout the following volumes. The memory of foreign domination, whether in the Anglo-Persian Agreement or in the coup, influenced later political narratives, including those of the Revolution of 1979. Students should be wary of reading these later outcomes back into 1925, since contemporaries did not know what would follow.

As the final chapter of Volume VI, it closes the story of the Qajar dynasty and opens the story of its successor. The dynasty that reconstructed Iranian authority in the 1790s ended not because its subjects had ceased to exist as a political community, but because the institutions on which it relied could no longer meet the demands placed on them. What replaced it, examined in Chapter 81 and after, drew on the same problems, of sovereignty, state building, and the relation between rulers and ruled, that the Qajars had faced.
