The Collapse of the Monarchy and Khomeini’s Return

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Overthrowing a monarchy and constructing a successor state are different historical tasks. In the weeks between the Shah’s departure and the eve of the Islamic Republic referendum, Iranians discovered that revolutionary victory answered one question, whether the Pahlavi dynasty would rule, while opening many others: who would govern, under what law, with what army, and for whose purposes.

On the morning of 1 February 1979, an Air France aircraft carrying Ruhollah Khomeini landed at Tehran’s Mehrabad Airport after nearly fifteen years in exile. The crowds that had gathered in the capital and along the route to the city’s main cemetery were among the largest in modern Iranian history, though no reliable count exists and published estimates differ widely. Ten days later, the armed forces announced their neutrality, the government of Prime Minister Shapour Bakhtiar ceased to function, and the Pahlavi monarchy, which had ruled since 1925 and whose last occupant had left the country on 16 January, ended as a working political system. Chapter 91 explained why that collapse occurred. This chapter asks what happened next.

The answer is not a simple passage from one regime to another. In the weeks after 11 February, at least five distinct sources of authority operated at once in Tehran and the provinces. A provisional cabinet under Mehdi Bazargan, appointed by Khomeini on 5 February, held the ministries, the civil service, and the formal responsibilities of government. A Revolutionary Council, whose membership was initially not made public, advised Khomeini, issued decisions, and shaped appointments. Khomeini himself, first in Tehran and from 1 March in Qom, commanded a religious and popular authority that no ministry could match. Neighborhood revolutionary committees, known as komitehs, arrested suspects, guarded buildings, and enforced local order. And armed organizations, workers’ councils, women’s groups, student associations, and regional movements pursued programs of their own. These were not a single hierarchy. Their relations were negotiated, and often contested, day by day.

Historians have described this arrangement in several ways. Some, drawing on the vocabulary of earlier revolutions, speak of dual power, a formal government shadowed by a parallel revolutionary authority. Others point out that the situation was more fragmented than that term suggests, since in matters of security, justice, economic policy, and regional administration there were three or more claimants. Still others emphasize that the confusion was also creative: ordinary people formed organizations, argued in public, and attempted to shape what the revolution would mean. This chapter treats February and March 1979 as a distinct period of institutional experimentation, popular participation, and struggle over sovereignty, not as a brief interlude between a fallen monarchy and an Islamic Republic whose form was already settled.

The chapter is built on a variety of evidence, and its limits should be stated at the outset. Contemporary newspapers, radio and television broadcasts, and diplomatic reports record events as observers perceived them, but observers disagreed, and foreign diplomats and journalists had incomplete access. Official declarations and decrees show what authorities claimed, not necessarily what was enforced. Memoirs by participants, including members of the provisional government and the revolutionary organizations, are indispensable but were written after the revolution’s later conflicts and are shaped by them. Scholarly studies of the provisional government, the workers’ councils, the women’s movement, and Kurdish politics supply analysis but differ in emphasis. Numerical claims about crowds, casualties, weapons, and committee members are especially uncertain, and this chapter gives ranges or declines to give figures where the evidence does not support precision.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first returns to the closing weeks of the monarchy, but from the perspective of succession: how rival claims to legitimate government emerged between 16 January and 11 February. The second examines the provisional government, the Revolutionary Council, the ministries, and the committees. The third turns to street politics, armed groups, workers, and women, whose mobilization shaped, and sometimes challenged, the new authorities. The fourth considers revolutionary justice, the armed forces, property, and regional movements, areas in which the question of coercive power was most acute. The fifth compares the political programs then in competition, the first foreign-policy decisions, and the debates preceding the referendum of 30–31 March, whose result and consequences belong to Chapter 93.

Two cautions govern the whole chapter. First, hindsight is a hazard. Readers know that the Islamic Republic took a particular form, and it is tempting to read every event of February and March as a step toward it. Participants did not know this. Many who joined the revolution expected a republic with elected institutions and civil liberties; others expected Islamic government in a form that remained undefined; others expected a socialist transformation. Second, popular legitimacy, constitutional legality, and administrative capacity are different things. A revolution can possess the first in abundance while lacking the others, and understanding that gap is the central task of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the departure of Mohammad Reza Shah, the Regency Council, and the Bakhtiar government created competing claims to constitutional authority between January and February 1979.
  2. Trace the events of 1–11 February 1979, including Khomeini’s return, the appointment of Mehdi Bazargan, and the collapse of military support for the monarchy.
  3. Distinguish the provisional government, the Revolutionary Council, Khomeini’s personal authority, and the revolutionary committees as separate institutions with different sources of legitimacy.
  4. Analyze how ministries, public employees, and economic institutions continued to function despite political rupture.
  5. Assess the roles of mosque networks, bazaar organizations, armed groups, workers’ councils, and women’s organizations in the post-victory contest for influence.
  6. Evaluate the early revolutionary courts and executions, identifying both the political demand for accountability and the problems of due process.
  7. Describe the early autonomy demands of Kurdish and other regional movements and distinguish autonomy from separatism.
  8. Compare the competing political programs of Islamic government, religious constitutionalism, secular nationalism, Islamic socialism, and Marxism in February–March 1979.
  9. Interpret primary sources, including declarations, diplomatic reports, and press accounts, attending to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  10. Distinguish the February 1979 embassy incident from the November 1979 hostage crisis, and the revolutionary committees from the later Revolutionary Guards.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the early revolutionary state without treating its eventual form as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does it mean for a government to possess legitimacy, and how can legitimacy based on constitutional law, revolutionary mobilization, and religious authority conflict?
  2. Why did a revolution that unified so many groups against the monarchy produce competing institutions almost immediately after victory?
  3. How did a provisional government with experienced administrators and formal responsibilities find its authority limited by bodies it did not control?
  4. Why did revolutionary committees provide real security and at the same time challenge the government’s claim to govern?
  5. How did different social groups, women, workers, bazaar merchants, students, and regional communities, understand the revolution’s promises?
  6. What problems arise when courts created in emergency conditions pass sentences of death, and why did these problems matter politically?
  7. In what ways did the absence of a settled constitution allow multiple interpretations of the revolution to coexist?
  8. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct a period in which institutions were improvised and records were incomplete?
  9. How should historians avoid reading the later Islamic Republic backward into the first weeks of revolutionary rule?

Historical Context

The Pahlavi monarchy, examined in Volume VII, rested on a centralized state that expanded dramatically after the 1953 coup restored royal authority. By the 1970s the monarchy governed through the armed forces, the intelligence and security services (notably SAVAK), a court-centered patronage system, expanded ministries financed by oil revenue, and a single-party arrangement, the Rastakhiz Party, created in 1975. The 1906 Constitution and its 1907 Supplementary Fundamental Laws, products of the Constitutional Revolution discussed in Chapter 78, remained the formal legal framework, but opposition leaders argued that the Shah had hollowed out its representative provisions. The tension between constitutional text and royal practice was a central grievance of the revolution.

Chapter 91 traced how that grievance, combined with economic dislocation, religious mobilization, political repression, and the growth of opposition networks, produced the mass protests and strikes of 1977–1979. By late 1978 oil workers’ strikes, bazaar closures, and large demonstrations had paralyzed the economy and exposed the limits of military rule. Opposition was never a single movement. It included the National Front and its heirs, the Freedom Movement of Iran led by Mehdi Bazargan, networks of senior and junior clergy, students and teachers, Marxist and Islamic guerrilla organizations, labor activists, and merchants. These groups shared a rejection of the Shah but differed on what should replace him. Khomeini, exiled since 1964 and resident in Najaf and then, from October 1978, near Paris, became the movement’s most widely recognized figure and its principal point of unity.

The legal context in January 1979 was ambiguous. Bakhtiar, a longtime National Front politician, had accepted the premiership from the Shah in early January, was expelled from the National Front for doing so, and attempted to govern under the existing constitution while dismantling elements of royal rule. His government relied on a parliament elected under the monarchy and on the armed forces, whose commanders owed their positions to the Shah. Khomeini and his allies rejected this arrangement as illegitimate. They argued that sovereignty derived from the people’s demonstrated will, not from the monarchy’s constitutional machinery, and began creating alternative institutions.

The social context also matters. Iran in 1979 was a society of roughly thirty-five million people, urbanizing rapidly, with a young population, expanded education, a large public sector, a sizeable industrial working class, and a bazaar sector that retained financial and political weight. The revolution had mobilized these groups through mosques, workplaces, universities, and neighborhoods, creating dense networks of organization. When the monarchy fell, these networks did not disband. They became the raw material from which new authority, and new conflicts, were built.

From Royal Departure to Revolutionary Victory

Section 92.1

The Shah’s Departure, the Regency Council, and the Constitutional Crisis

By the first days of January 1979, the Pahlavi monarchy was attempting to survive through constitutional improvisation. After months of strikes, martial law, and mass demonstrations, Mohammad Reza Shah had turned to a figure from the moderate opposition. Shapour Bakhtiar, a veteran of the National Front and a critic of the Shah’s authoritarian methods, accepted the premiership in the first week of January and secured a vote of confidence from the parliament, whose deputies had been elected under the monarchy. The National Front, led by Karim Sanjabi, had already declared that no one could cooperate with the Shah’s government, and it expelled Bakhtiar for accepting the post. His position was therefore unusual: a reformist prime minister with little organized political base, dependent on the monarchy and the military he hoped to restrain.

Bakhtiar’s program was substantial on paper. He announced plans to dismantle SAVAK, pursued the release of political prisoners, promised free elections, and relaxed press restrictions. He also sought changes in foreign and oil policy, including ending sales to Israel and South Africa. Announcements, parliamentary measures and their implementation did not all occur at the same moment. His central idea was that the monarchy could survive as a constitutional institution if the Shah ceased to rule in practice. The difficulty was that the revolutionary movement had already moved beyond reform. Khomeini, speaking from exile, insisted that the monarchy itself was illegitimate and that no government appointed by the Shah could be lawful. For many demonstrators, concessions that would have satisfied the opposition a year earlier now seemed inadequate.

The Shah’s departure was prepared through a constitutional device. Under the 1906–1907 constitutional framework, provision existed for a regency when the monarch was unable to perform his duties. On 13 January 1979 a Regency Council was constituted, composed of senior figures from the political establishment, to exercise royal functions during the Shah’s absence. On 16 January the Shah and his wife left Tehran by air, publicly describing the trip as a temporary absence for rest. The wording mattered. The Shah did not abdicate, and the dynasty’s legal claim to the throne continued. His departure was celebrated in the streets as a victory, yet constitutionally the monarchy remained intact, represented now by a council with limited prestige and an uncertain relationship to the armed forces.

This distinction between departure and abdication shaped the weeks that followed. For Bakhtiar and the Regency Council, constitutional continuity offered a path to a peaceful transfer: if the existing government could remain in office long enough, perhaps a negotiated settlement involving the opposition and the military could be reached. For Khomeini and his allies, continuity was precisely the problem. Recognizing the Regency Council would have meant recognizing the legal order that the revolution had set out to dismantle. Khomeini declared the council and the Bakhtiar government illegal, and he called for obedience to the revolutionary authorities he was preparing to name. Bakhtiar’s hope that Khomeini might accept a compromise, and a proposed visit to Khomeini in Paris that did not take place, failed to bridge the difference.

The standoff displayed what political theorists call a contest between legality and legitimacy. The Bakhtiar government held the buildings, the formal chain of command, and the legal sanction of the existing constitution. The revolutionary movement held the streets, the loyalty of millions of participants, and increasingly the allegiance of ordinary soldiers and officials. Neither side could govern the country without the other’s acquiescence, yet neither would acknowledge the other’s claim. The military, which might have enforced the government’s legal authority, was itself uncertain: senior officers had divergent views, conscripts had relatives among the demonstrators, and the strikes had undermined fuel supplies and administrative routines. Chapter 91 described these pressures. What matters here is the institutional consequence: the monarchy’s law persisted longer than its power.

A further complication concerned the armed forces’ own legal position. The Shah had been supreme commander, and officers had sworn loyalty to him. His departure, without abdication, left the question of whom the military obeyed unresolved. Some officers regarded Bakhtiar as their legitimate superior; others looked to the Regency Council; others began looking toward the revolutionary leadership or simply to the preservation of their units and their country. In this ambiguity, a decision about the Shah’s future became a decision about the loyalty of the armed forces, and a decision about the armed forces became a decision about who would govern. These connected problems would be settled, as the next sections show, not by negotiation but by the events of early February.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional Legality, Revolutionary Legitimacy, Sovereignty, and Dual Power

Definition: Constitutional legality refers to authority derived from established law and recognized procedures. Revolutionary legitimacy refers to authority claimed on the basis of popular mobilization and the overthrow of a prior order. Sovereignty is the claim to ultimate authority within a political community, whether located in a monarch, a people, a religious law, or a constitution. Dual power is a term, associated with analyses of the Russian Revolution of 1917, for a situation in which two institutions with distinct sources of authority operate simultaneously within one state.

Historical context: In January and February 1979, Bakhtiar’s government held constitutional legality while the revolutionary movement claimed legitimacy from mass participation and, for Khomeini’s supporters, from religious authority. After 11 February the same terms apply to the relation between Bazargan’s cabinet and other revolutionary bodies.

Why it matters: These distinctions help explain why a government could possess ministries and still lack authority, or command crowds and still lack a constitution.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that “dual power” accurately describes every institutional dispute of 1979. As later sections show, in security, justice, and regional administration there were more than two claimants.

Section 92.2

Khomeini’s Return and the Symbolism of Revolutionary Leadership

Khomeini’s return was delayed by the Bakhtiar government’s decision to close Iran’s airports to international flights, a measure intended to prevent his arrival and to avoid confrontation. Protests against the closure, and the risk that the army would be unable to hold the capital, led the government to reopen the airports at the end of January. On the morning of 1 February 1979, an Air France aircraft chartered for the occasion brought Khomeini, accompanied by aides, family members, and journalists, from Paris to Mehrabad Airport. He was then in his late seventies. He had left Iran in 1964 after opposing the Shah’s policies, spent more than a decade in Najaf, and had moved to France in October 1978 after Iraqi authorities restricted his activities.

The reception was organized and spontaneous at once. Revolutionary committees, mosque networks, student groups, and volunteer marshals prepared the route and the reception, while crowds that observers estimated in the hundreds of thousands to several million lined the streets of Tehran. These figures are impressionistic; no systematic count was possible, and scholars treat claims of exact numbers cautiously. What is clear is the scale and the density of organization. Marshals managed the movement of Khomeini’s vehicle through the city, and the procession’s destination, the Behesht-e Zahra cemetery south of Tehran, was chosen for its symbolic value. It was the burial ground of many killed in the protests of 1978, and speaking there allowed Khomeini to claim the authority of the dead.

At Behesht-e Zahra, Khomeini delivered an address that set out his claims. He honored those killed in the demonstrations, attacked the monarchy as an illegitimate and exploitative institution, denounced the Bakhtiar government and the Regency Council as unlawful, and announced that he would appoint a government. He also spoke about the dependence of the economy, the armed forces, and the country’s resources on foreign powers, and he appealed to the soldiers to join the people. The speech combined the language of national independence, popular sovereignty, and Islamic obligation. In substance it asserted that legitimacy rested with the people who had demonstrated against the Shah and with the religious leadership that had guided them, rather than with any institution created under the monarchy.

Khomeini’s authority at this moment was exceptional but informal. He held no office. He possessed the religious standing of a senior Shi‘i jurist and the status of a marja‘, a source of emulation to whom believers looked for guidance, although the extent of his following among the religious hierarchy was uneven and other senior clerics, including Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari and Mahmud Taleghani, held independent positions and followings. He was also the symbol of opposition to the monarchy for many Iranians who did not share his religious politics. A secular student, a bazaar merchant, a worker in the oil fields, and a cleric in Qom might each regard him as the embodiment of the revolution while understanding that revolution in different ways.

His return changed the movement’s internal relations. During his exile, the revolutionary leadership inside Iran had operated through informal networks, with messages carried by cassette tapes, couriers, and later telephone and broadcast. Once Khomeini was in Tehran, decision-making could be centralized around him, and his statements became the benchmark by which other leaders’ authority was measured. At the same time, the movement’s practical organization remained decentralized. The people who had organized marches, managed funerals, protected neighborhoods, and raised funds did so through local ties. Khomeini’s presence provided a common reference point but did not automatically create a hierarchy of command between him and these local actors.

The return also posed a question that would structure the next weeks: how could revolutionary charisma be converted into governmental authority? Charismatic leadership can legitimize a regime without defining its institutions. Khomeini’s appeal enabled him to name a prime minister and to expect obedience from millions, but it did not tell anyone how legislation would be enacted, how judges would be appointed, how security forces would be controlled, or how disputes among revolutionary bodies would be settled. The answers were to be worked out under pressure, and the capacity of the new leaders to establish accepted procedures would determine whether the revolution’s popular legitimacy could become durable institutional authority.

Section 92.3

The Revolutionary Council and the Appointment of Mehdi Bazargan

Even before returning, Khomeini had begun building a successor leadership. Following clandestine preparations, in a message dated 12 January 1979 he announced a Revolutionary Council, a body intended to prepare the transition and to coordinate between his religious followers and the wider opposition. [19] Its membership was at first not publicly disclosed, partly because the Bakhtiar government remained in office and partly because some members were still in danger. Later accounts identify figures such as Morteza Motahhari, Mohammad Beheshti, Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, and Mehdi Bazargan among those associated with the council, together with other clerics and religious-nationalist laymen. Because membership shifted and was not recorded in a single authoritative document, historians treat lists compiled later with care, and this chapter does not claim a fixed roster.

The council’s character reflected the alliance within the revolutionary movement. It combined clerical activists with lay professionals from the Freedom Movement and related religious-nationalist circles. These groups shared opposition to the Shah and a commitment to an Islamic framework, but not necessarily a common idea of the state. Many lay members, including Bazargan, had long argued for constitutional government compatible with Islamic values. Some clerics were more interested in establishing a government in which religious scholars exercised direct authority. During the first weeks these differences remained largely implicit, subordinate to the immediate goals of ending the monarchy and preventing a military counter-coup.

In a decree dated 4 February and publicly announced on 5 February 1979, Khomeini appointed Mehdi Bazargan to form a provisional government. The choice was widely understood. Bazargan, born in 1907, was an engineer, a founder of the Freedom Movement of Iran, a figure of the Mossadegh era who had been involved in the early administration of the nationalized oil industry, and a founder of the National Resistance Movement formed after the 1953 coup. He was imprisoned more than once under the Shah. A devout Muslim who advocated representative government and the rule of law, he enjoyed respect among the bazaar, the professional middle class, and many clergy. He was not a cleric and had no personal army, but he possessed administrative experience and a reputation for integrity.

The appointment’s legal basis is the key to understanding the period. Bazargan did not succeed Bakhtiar through a procedure recognized by the existing constitutional order. The decree dated 4 February named him on the Revolutionary Council’s recommendation and invoked Khomeini’s asserted religious and legal authority and the popular support expressed in the demonstrations. At the public introduction on 5 February, Khomeini rejected the standing of the existing regime and asserted the authority of the new government. The written mandate charged Bazargan with administration, a referendum on an Islamic republic, an elected constitutional assembly, and elections for the legislature under the new system. The order of these tasks was identifiable, but the electoral rules, alternatives, constitutional powers, and practical enforcement of the promises remained to be determined. [1] [2]

From 5 February, therefore, Iran possessed two prime ministers. Bakhtiar remained in the prime minister’s office, with the formal machinery of government, the support of the Regency Council, and nominal control of the military command. Bazargan operated from the revolution’s headquarters at the Refah School and nearby locations, with the backing of Khomeini and the revolutionary organizations. Each claimed to be the legitimate head of government. The situation was unsustainable and was resolved within a week, but the manner of its resolution mattered. The Bakhtiar government fell because it lost the military, not because it lost a vote or abdicated. Bazargan’s government came to power not through any process in the old constitution but through the same event, and so inherited a state whose legal foundations had been set aside without being replaced.

Bazargan’s own approach reflected his assumptions. He expected an orderly transition in which the revolutionary movement would hand administrative responsibility to a competent cabinet, which would then organize elections and a constitution before surrendering power to elected institutions. He emphasized that the provisional government was provisional. He did not anticipate that revolutionary committees, armed groups, and the Revolutionary Council would claim independent authority, or that Khomeini would regard the cabinet as one instrument of the revolution rather than as the sole executive. These expectations, and their disappointment, framed his government’s subsequent history, which should not be judged solely by its eventual resignation in November 1979.

Primary Source Window

Creator: Ruhollah Khomeini · Dates: 4 and 5 February 1979 · Places: Refah and Alavi schools, Tehran · Documents: Appointment decree (Sahifeh-ye Emam, VI, pp. 50–51); Public address (VI, pp. 52–56)

Context: The decree preceded the public announcement by one day. Bakhtiar still occupied the premiership. The two documents record related but distinguishable acts of revolutionary authority. The published English translations are used here; readers comparing legal terminology should also consult the Persian originals.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The decree names Bazargan on the Revolutionary Council’s recommendation, invoking religious and legal authority and public support. It assigns administration, a referendum on the Islamic republic, an elected constitution-making assembly and subsequent parliamentary elections. The separate address challenges the legitimacy of the monarchy and its government and presents the proposed cabinet as an authority entitled to obedience.

Analysis: The documents combine religious warrant, a claim to popular consent, and commitments to representative procedures. They reveal a tension between authority asserted before a vote and authority promised through future electoral arrangements. They do not establish whether the population accepted those arrangements or how competing institutions would be reconciled.

Limitations: The document records an intention and a claim, not an enforced reality. It does not tell historians who obeyed, whether the referendum would offer meaningful alternatives, or how disputes between the cabinet and other bodies would be resolved. Translations introduce further uncertainty.

Perspective: The source reflects the viewpoint of its author, a leader asserting authority. Those who rejected the claim, including supporters of Bakhtiar and the monarchy and some secular opponents, are not represented.

Section 92.4

The Final Military Crisis and the Fall of the Monarchy, 9–11 February

The decisive struggle was fought over the military. Through the first week of February the armed forces remained, on paper, the monarchy’s instrument, with several hundred thousand personnel under a command structure built by the Shah. Yet there were signs of erosion: desertions, refusals to fire on crowds, and contacts between lower-ranking personnel and the opposition. Senior commanders, including the chief of staff, General Abbas Gharabaghi, were aware that a clash with the revolutionary movement could fragment the army, and some were in discreet contact with opposition figures. Others favored a harder line, and rumors circulated of a possible military action to preserve the regime.

The crisis broke at air force bases in Tehran. Technicians known as homafars, who maintained aircraft and held relatively low rank, showed support for Khomeini’s return and for the revolution. On 8 February they publicly demonstrated their loyalty to him, an act that violated military discipline. The Imperial Guard, an elite force with direct ties to the monarchy, was sent to quell the disturbance at the Doshan Tappeh air base on the night of 9 February. Fighting followed. Armed civilians, members of revolutionary organizations including the Fedai and Mojahedin, and sympathetic military personnel joined the defenders, and the clash spread into the surrounding neighborhoods.

On 10 February the government imposed an early curfew, apparently as part of an attempt to contain the fighting and restore control. Khomeini’s supporters responded by calling on people to ignore it, and large numbers did so. In the course of that day and the next, groups of revolutionaries and defecting soldiers seized police stations, barracks, weapons depots, and prisons. Weapons, including rifles and automatic arms, passed into the hands of civilians and organized groups on a large scale, though no reliable inventory exists and published estimates differ. The seizure of armaments from military and police facilities had long-term consequences, since it distributed coercive capacity among organizations that did not answer to any single authority.

The final collapse of the monarchy’s military backing came on 11 February. After meeting, senior commanders of the armed forces concluded that they could not and should not attempt to suppress the uprising, and the Supreme Military Council announced that the armed forces would remain neutral in the political dispute. The statement was brief and the reasoning was partly practical: the chain of command had broken down in the capital, units were splitting, and further fighting threatened the army’s unity. Neutrality, however, had the effect of withdrawing the force on which Bakhtiar’s government depended. By that evening, revolutionary forces had occupied key government buildings and broadcasting facilities, and Bakhtiar was in hiding; the Regency Council and the Bakhtiar government no longer functioned in any practical sense.

Distinguishing the end of the monarchy from the end of the Iranian state is essential. On 11 February the royal government ceased to rule, but the ministries, courts, banks, public companies, municipal offices, schools, and military units that made up the state did not disappear. Their personnel still occupied buildings, drew salaries, and awaited instruction. What had collapsed was the political authority at the top of the structure and the military hierarchy that sustained it. The new authorities therefore confronted a paradox: they controlled a vast formal apparatus whose leaders they did not trust and whose staff they had no means of replacing quickly.

This situation meant that victory immediately generated governing problems. Coercive institutions had lost their chain of command, with police and military units unsure to whom they reported. Weapons were dispersed among groups with different objectives. Prisons had been opened, and many prisoners released. The capital’s services depended on workers and officials who now looked to new, uncertain leadership. In many neighborhoods, young volunteers kept watch, directed traffic, and guarded buildings, performing functions that police had previously handled. Whatever one’s view of the revolution, it had to meet the demand for order at once, and different groups answered that demand differently, as the following sections show.

Division II

The Provisional Government and the Revolutionary Authorities

Section 92.5

Bazargan’s Provisional Government and the Restoration of Administration

Bazargan’s cabinet took shape in the days following 11 February. It combined figures from the religious-nationalist milieu of the Freedom Movement with members of the National Front tradition and technically qualified professionals. Among those associated with it were Ibrahim Yazdi, a Freedom Movement activist who had worked closely with Khomeini in exile, who served as a deputy prime minister handling revolutionary affairs; Abbas Amir-Entezam, a deputy prime minister who also served as government spokesman; Yadollah Sahabi, an engineer and planner; and Karim Sanjabi, the National Front leader, who became foreign minister (he would resign on 16 April [17]). Other ministers came from the civil service, engineering, law, and academic life. The composition reflected the government’s intention to demonstrate competence and to bring together religious and nationalist currents of the opposition.

The government’s immediate tasks were enormous. For months strikes had interrupted the operation of ministries, banks, factories, transport, and the oil industry. The cabinet had to restore functioning offices, ensure that public employees returned to work and received salaries, resume the delivery of electricity, water, and fuel, reopen schools and universities where possible, and revive the economy’s productive capacity. At the same time it had to decide what to do with the armed forces, the police, and the intelligence apparatus. Each of these problems required decisions about personnel and authority, and each was complicated by the fact that the government’s own authority was unsettled.

Bazargan approached the task as an administrator. His government’s early statements emphasized the restoration of order, the resumption of work, and respect for law. He called on strikers to return to their jobs, on citizens to return weapons to authorities, and on revolutionary groups to defer to the state’s institutions. This approach reflected genuine convictions as well as practical necessity: a government that could not restore routine services would lose public confidence, while a country that remained in a condition of permanent mobilization could not rebuild its economy. For Bazargan, the revolution’s purpose was the establishment of constitutional government and the rule of law, and he regarded prolonged revolutionary disorder as a threat to that purpose.

Formal responsibility, however, did not equal real capacity. Ministers inherited departments whose senior officials had been appointed by the old regime and whose loyalty could not be assumed, and whose staff often feared dismissal or arrest. Meanwhile revolutionary organizations outside the government made independent decisions about arrests, property, and appointments. A minister might issue an instruction that a local committee ignored, or discover that a subordinate had been detained by a body whose authority he could not verify. The government held the apparatus of administration but lacked the monopoly over enforcement that normally accompanies it. Observers and participants later described this condition in various images; Bazargan himself is often reported to have described his government, in retrospect, as powerless, though the exact wording and timing of such remarks should be checked against his own writings before being quoted.

It would be a mistake to evaluate the provisional government solely by its inability to command. It accomplished a good deal in a short time. Ministries reopened, salaries were paid, essential services were gradually restored, and the oil industry resumed production and exports within weeks. The government also opened relations with foreign governments, began to prepare for the referendum promised in the appointment declaration, and worked to maintain basic public order. Its achievements were often overshadowed by controversies over justice, security, and politics, but they show that the state was being reassembled by people who understood its workings, not simply dismantled. Historians who emphasize the government’s weakness do not necessarily deny these achievements; they stress that administrative competence cannot substitute for settled authority.

The cabinet also faced the question of its own temporary character. A provisional government has no constitutional mandate beyond the task of transition, which makes it vulnerable to the argument that major decisions should await elected institutions. Bazargan’s ministers repeatedly insisted that fundamental changes, such as nationalizations, the restructuring of the legal system, or the reorganization of the armed forces, should be reserved for the constitutional assembly and the legislature to follow. Their critics, including leftists and some clerics, responded that revolutions cannot wait, and that those who had risked their lives to overthrow the monarchy were entitled to immediate action. This argument over timing, caution versus urgency, recurred throughout the period.

Section 92.6

The Revolutionary Council, Khomeini, and the Problem of Competing Executive Authority

The Revolutionary Council, which had been formed as a transitional preparatory body, did not dissolve when the provisional government took office. Instead it continued meeting, and in practice it exercised functions that overlapped with those of the cabinet. It discussed legislation, approved or influenced appointments, issued decisions on the organization of revolutionary institutions, and advised Khomeini on matters ranging from foreign relations to economic policy. Several of its members were also ministers or held positions in the provisional administration, which created close personal links between the bodies. Yet the council was not a government department, and its powers were not defined in any published constitution. In the absence of a legislature, it functioned in effect as a revolutionary lawmaking and supervisory authority.

Khomeini’s personal authority formed a third element. Using the Refah and Alavi school headquarters in Tehran, and relocating to the religious city of Qom on 1 March, he received delegations, issued statements, and gave decisions on questions submitted to him by officials, organizations, and ordinary citizens. His position was unlike that of any office-holder. He had no ministry and no formal staff organization of the kind a head of government commands, yet his pronouncements carried weight that no cabinet decision could equal. When he endorsed a policy, opposition to it became difficult; when he criticized an action, those responsible often retreated. At the same time, he did not intervene in every matter, and he sometimes avoided clarifying disputes between bodies that claimed to act in his name. [22]

The relations among these three centers of authority were never formalized. Bazargan reportedly met regularly with Khomeini and with the Revolutionary Council, and many decisions were reached through consultation. But the cabinet could not always be sure that a decision approved in one meeting would be honored by the council or by the committees. Conflicting instructions reached ministries about security, appointments, and the handling of former officials. Foreign diplomats trying to determine who spoke for Iran found that statements by the prime minister, by Khomeini’s office, and by the council did not always coincide. For ordinary citizens, the question was practical: to whom should they turn for permission, protection, or redress?

The overlapping institutions had causes that lay in the nature of the revolution. The movement that overthrew the monarchy was a coalition, and each part of the coalition had contributed organizations, personnel, and followings. The religious networks contributed mosque-based mobilization and a cadre of clerical activists; the Freedom Movement and National Front contributed administrative experience and a Western-recognized respectability; the guerrilla organizations contributed armed forces; the workers contributed strike committees. No single part could simply absorb the others, and Khomeini, who needed the whole coalition, did not immediately choose among them. Overlapping institutions therefore accommodated competing interests while postponing decisions about who would ultimately govern.

This chapter avoids treating dual power as a complete explanation. In some matters two bodies did compete, as when the Revolutionary Council and the cabinet issued different positions on a policy. But in others the situation was more complicated. On security, the army, the police, the committees, and armed organizations all claimed or exercised authority. On justice, the Ministry of Justice, the regular courts, the revolutionary tribunals, and individual clerics with their own authority acted simultaneously. On the economy, the cabinet, the council, workers’ councils, bazaar interests, and managers made decisions that affected each other. Describing this as a contest between two rival governments would obscure the number of actors and the fluidity of their alliances. The better description is a network of overlapping and sometimes cooperating, sometimes competing, centers of authority.

The reality of shared responsibility was visible in small matters as well as large ones. A ministry official signing a payment, a bank manager approving a loan, or a municipal engineer authorizing repairs might find that his decision was reviewed by an employees’ committee, questioned by a neighborhood group, or countermanded by an instruction whose source was unclear. Such experiences, repeated across thousands of offices, produced caution and delay. They also produced informal accommodations, in which officials and activists negotiated working arrangements that allowed the daily business of government to continue without being formally resolved.

Cause and Effect

How Revolutionary Victory Produced Overlapping Institutions of Government

Long-term causes

A highly centralized monarchy that had suppressed independent political organization, leaving opposition groups to build alternative networks in mosques, bazaars, universities, and workplaces; a coalition opposition that never agreed on the form of a successor state.

Short-term causes

The strikes and demonstrations of 1978, which developed local committees and strike organizations; the creation of a Revolutionary Council and a rival cabinet before the monarchy collapsed; and the Shah’s departure without abdication.

Immediate trigger

The armed forces’ declaration of neutrality on 11 February and the seizure of weapons and public buildings by revolutionary groups.

Transformation

The state’s political head disappeared, while the ministries remained; revolutionary organizations, councils, and committees exercised authority in parallel with them.

Immediate effects

Competing instructions on security, courts, appointments, and property; difficulty for ministries in enforcing decisions; appeals by citizens to multiple authorities.

Long-term consequences

A prolonged contest over which institutions would define the new state, shaping the constitutional debates of Chapter 93 and the consolidation described in Chapter 95.

Historical complexity: Overlap was not simply an accident of confusion. For some actors it was an advantage, because ambiguity allowed them to act without accountability or to postpone commitments. For others, including Bazargan, it was a severe handicap. The same institutional arrangement therefore benefited some political projects over others.

Section 92.7

Ministries, Public Employees, the Economy, and Institutional Continuity

One of the most striking features of the transition was how much of the Iranian state’s machinery continued to operate. The 1970s had produced a large public sector. Ministries of finance, education, health, roads, agriculture, and industry employed hundreds of thousands of salaried officials; the central bank and commercial banks managed the financial system; the National Iranian Oil Company operated the fields, refineries, and export terminals on which government revenue depended; municipalities, utilities, and public transport companies ran urban life. These institutions were staffed largely by trained people who had served under the monarchy and who, with few exceptions, now sought to continue working under whatever government held power.

The new authorities thus faced a dilemma, which was both political and practical. Activists who had risked arrest and violence under the monarchy distrusted officials associated with the old order, particularly those linked to SAVAK, the royal court, the armed forces’ senior ranks, or the Rastakhiz Party. They demanded purges. Yet the country’s economy and services depended on the technical knowledge of those same bureaucracies, and a wholesale replacement of administrators would have paralyzed government. Bazargan’s ministers generally favored retention of competent staff, removing those with clear records of abuse or corruption while keeping the rest. Revolutionary organizations within ministries and companies often favored more aggressive action, forming committees to examine and dismiss colleagues.

The oil industry illustrates these tensions. Oil workers’ strikes in late 1978 had been among the most damaging blows to the monarchy, cutting production and exports and depriving the state of revenue. After 11 February the new government needed oil income, and workers’ committees in the industry, which had organized the strikes, retained considerable influence. Production was restarted, and oil exports resumed on 5 March 1979 [14], but at levels below those of the pre-revolutionary period, and managers, workers, and the government negotiated the terms of resumed operations. Questions about who would run the industry, what role employee committees would play, and how foreign technicians would be treated, were unresolved. The government’s leading figures stressed the importance of reliable oil revenue; strike leaders and some political groups emphasized workers’ rights and national control.

The economy more generally was in poor condition. The strikes and unrest had reduced production in many sectors, capital had left the country, some wealthy families and foreign employees had departed, and private investment slowed. Unemployment, which had been a serious problem before the revolution, was aggravated by the closure or slowdown of many enterprises. At the same time, popular expectations were high. Those who had taken part in the revolution anticipated improvements in wages, housing, and living conditions, and the new government had little capacity to deliver them quickly. It had to meet payroll, ensure that food and fuel reached cities, and keep factories open, often with managers who feared to take decisions and workers who demanded changes.

Several mechanisms complicated routine administration. First, personnel decisions were politicized. Appointments to senior posts frequently depended on political reliability as judged by the Revolutionary Council, religious leaders, or local committees, as well as on competence. Second, workplace committees emerged in ministries, banks, and companies, sometimes helping to maintain order and sometimes interfering with management. Third, suspicion of association with the old regime made officials cautious, and inertia or avoidance of responsibility became common. Fourth, the shortage of trusted administrators meant that many positions were filled by people with little experience. Each of these factors slowed the government’s ability to implement decisions.

The key point is that political rupture did not mean institutional erasure. The state’s infrastructure, from power plants and roads to ministries and data, survived. What changed was leadership, accountability, and the criteria by which people were hired and promoted. This continuity helps explain why Iran did not experience a collapse into chaos, and also why later struggles over the state’s character were so intense: the institutions of the old state were available to be used by new masters, and the question of who would control them was central.

Section 92.8

Revolutionary Committees, Neighborhood Security, and Local Authority

The revolutionary committees, usually called komitehs (from the French comité), emerged from the networks that had organized the demonstrations of 1978. In many neighborhoods, mosque committees and groups of young men and women had arranged marches, protected processions, distributed food and medical supplies, collected funds, and watched for police or army action. When the monarchy’s police apparatus collapsed in February, these groups took on functions of local security: patrolling streets, guarding buildings, controlling traffic, seizing weapons from suspected former officials, and detaining people believed to be associated with the former regime. They were rooted in local communities and, in many cases, worked with the approval of nearby clergy.

Their activities were varied. In many places committee members did provide order at a moment when nothing else did, preventing looting and protecting hospitals, banks, and public offices. In other cases they conducted arrests on the basis of denunciation, searched homes and offices, confiscated property, and held prisoners in improvised detention. They investigated former SAVAK agents, police officers, army personnel, and officials suspected of corruption, and some prisoners were handed over to revolutionary courts. The committees differed widely: some were disciplined and cooperated with local administrators, while others acted on the initiative of their members, sometimes in pursuit of personal or factional rivalries. Generalizations about “the committees” as a single organization therefore mislead.

Efforts to coordinate and regularize the committees began soon after the monarchy’s fall. Mohammad Reza Mahdavi-Kani, a cleric with experience in revolutionary organization, was among those who sought to bring greater coordination and discipline to the local bodies, working with the Revolutionary Council and the provisional government. On 8 March 1979 he issued regulations defining the functions of the committees, as documented in human-rights and documentary collections. The measures indicated an attempt to turn groups formed under exceptional conditions into disciplined organizations operating within a defined framework, and to limit unauthorized arrests and searches. How far the regulations were obeyed is much less certain. Committees in different cities and districts responded differently, and enforcement depended on political relationships on the ground.

The committees raised a fundamental question about the legal and political order. Their members were volunteers acting to maintain order, which gave them a plausible claim to legitimacy. Yet they lacked legal accountability: they were not appointed under any law, were not subject to a clear chain of command, and could not be held responsible for abuses through established procedures. For citizens detained or searched, there was often no way to appeal. For the provisional government, the committees were both useful and threatening, since they performed functions the police could not, but they also acted independently of ministers. Bazargan and his colleagues repeatedly called for committee members to act under the supervision of regular authorities, with mixed results.

It is important to distinguish the committees from the Revolutionary Guards. The Islamic Revolution Guards Corps (IRGC) was established as a formal institution in the following weeks and months, drawing in part on committee members and armed revolutionary groups but with a distinct organizational structure, mission, and legal foundation. In February and early March, the term “Guards” was sometimes used loosely for armed revolutionaries, which has produced confusion in later accounts. This chapter uses komiteh for the local committees and reserves the name Revolutionary Guards for the later institution, whose history belongs to subsequent chapters, especially Chapter 105.

The committees thus presented the problem of the revolution in miniature. They embodied popular participation, local initiative, and security provision. They also embodied unaccountable force. How they were treated, whether abolished, absorbed into regular police and judicial structures, or retained as independent organs, would reveal what kind of state the revolution would build. In late winter 1979 the question remained open, and the provisional government’s attempts to resolve it in favor of regular institutions met resistance from those who saw the committees as essential to defending the revolution.

Division III

Street Politics, Social Movements, and Competing Claims

Section 92.9

Mosques, Bazaars, Neighborhood Organizations, and Popular Participation

The revolution’s organizational foundations did not disappear with the monarchy. Mosques, which had served as meeting places, distribution centers, and sanctuaries from police action, remained focal points of neighborhood life. Local clerics, prayer leaders, and mosque committees continued to give advice, settle disputes, and organize assistance, and in many districts they became the first point of contact for residents seeking help from the new order. Religious associations, charitable funds, and networks of lay preachers extended influence beyond formal worship. Their strength lay in trust built over years, rather than in any legal status.

The bazaar, the traditional commercial center of Iranian cities, had been a pillar of the movement, closing its shops in strikes and funding opposition networks. After the revolution, merchants’ guilds and associations sought assurance that their property would be respected and their interests represented in the new regime. Bazaar leaders had ties to both the religious networks and to the Freedom Movement and National Front tradition, and many supported a government that would protect private commerce while promoting Islamic values. Their concerns about economic policy, particularly proposals for nationalization or redistribution, would shape debates throughout the year.

Students, teachers, and university staff formed another important constituency. Universities had been centers of political activity, and after February student associations, many of them with leftist or Islamic orientations, held meetings, issued statements, and campaigned for their preferred programs. University politics was intense and diverse, ranging from groups loyal to Khomeini to Marxist and nationalist organizations to independent liberal and professional associations. Professional bodies, such as lawyers’, writers’, and physicians’ organizations, also revived or reorganized themselves, asserting a role in shaping legal and cultural policy.

Public life in the weeks after 11 February was marked by an unprecedented volume of political expression. Newspapers, which had been restricted under the monarchy, appeared with new titles and viewpoints, and political organizations that had operated clandestinely opened offices, distributed leaflets, held rallies, and sold publications on sidewalks. Demonstrations continued, both to commemorate the revolution’s martyrs and to press specific demands. The sense that politics belonged to the people, and that ordinary citizens could take part in decisions about their country, was widely shared and was a genuine achievement of the revolution, whatever limits later emerged.

This participation was not evenly distributed. Wealthier northern neighborhoods of Tehran, home to many officials, professionals, and entrepreneurs, experienced the revolution differently from the working-class districts of the south, where factories, small workshops, and migrant settlements were concentrated. Some wealthy residents left the country or withdrew from public activity; others joined committees or supported the new government. University districts were politically charged, traditional commercial quarters organized around the bazaar and its mosques, and provincial towns followed patterns shaped by local elites and religious figures. Understanding the period requires attention to these variations, since an account based only on events in central Tehran would miss much of the country.

The central question these networks raised was how popular participation could contribute to revolutionary legitimacy while complicating the creation of a common administrative framework. Participation gave the new order a mass base and enabled it to claim to rule in the people’s name. But participants often expected their contribution to be rewarded with influence: a voice in local government, a role in selecting officials, or a share of resources. When many groups expect influence, and no procedure exists to allocate it, conflict is likely. The organizational energy of the revolution thus supported and strained the project of building a new state at the same time.

The press also changed. Newspapers that had operated under royal censorship, such as the major Tehran dailies, now reported with new freedom and under new editorial pressure, while an array of party papers and leaflets appeared. Editors and journalists were themselves divided, and some publications faced hostility from committee members or crowds when they printed views regarded as hostile to the revolution. This produced a public sphere that was lively and contested at once, in which the boundary between acceptable criticism and counter-revolutionary activity had not been defined and could be drawn differently by different local authorities.

Section 92.10

Armed Revolutionary Organizations and the Contest over Weapons and Public Order

The armed organizations that emerged from the February fighting differed from each other in origin, ideology, and relation to Khomeini. The Organization of Iranian People’s Fedai Guerrillas had been founded in the early 1970s by Marxist activists who adopted armed struggle against the monarchy; many of its leaders had been killed or imprisoned, and its survivors emerged from prison and hiding as the monarchy collapsed. The Mojahedin-e Khalq, founded in the mid-1960s, advanced a revolutionary interpretation of Islam combined with a critique of social inequality, and also lost many leaders to execution and imprisonment under the Shah. Both groups took part in the February fighting, and both had substantial followings among students and young people. They were not the same organization and did not share a program; the Mojahedin, in particular, saw their Islamic socialism as distinct from both Marxism and the clerical program.

The weapons captured from military and police facilities during 9–11 February gave these and other groups a significant capacity for armed action. Neighborhood committees also possessed arms, as did individuals and local groups who had taken weapons during the fighting. No reliable inventory exists of how many weapons were distributed or retained, and estimates should be treated as impressions. What is certain is that a large quantity of firearms was in civilian hands, and that the new authorities regarded their recovery as essential to establishing order. Khomeini and Bazargan repeatedly called on people to surrender weapons to authorized bodies, and the government set up procedures for collection, with partial success.

Groups differed in their attitudes toward the new authorities and toward the revolution’s purpose. Some armed organizations accepted Khomeini’s leadership and sought integration into the emerging revolutionary institutions. Others, particularly on the Marxist left, regarded the monarchy’s fall as the opening of a wider struggle for social transformation and were reluctant to disarm. Still others, among them the Mojahedin, kept their own organizations and pursued political activity independently, calling for a state in which different currents could compete. Within each group there were disagreements as well; the Fedai in particular were divided in subsequent months over their relationship to the government and to the Soviet-aligned Tudeh Party, which also reappeared in public life.

The provisional government’s attempts to restore control over firearms, military installations, detention facilities, and street security illustrate the difficulty of its position. It could ask armed groups to hand over weapons and could negotiate with their leaders, but it had little ability to compel them. Reliance on persuasion and Khomeini’s moral authority yielded partial results. Some weapons were returned; others were retained by groups who feared that surrender would leave them vulnerable, or who valued arms as a political resource. The persistence of armed organizations outside state control created a continuing risk of violent confrontation, whether among revolutionary groups, between them and the authorities, or in regional disputes.

A basic distinction should be maintained: participation in overthrowing the monarchy did not by itself entitle any organization to control the state. Many groups made sacrifices, and their contributions were genuine, but the revolution had been a collective achievement of millions, and no one organization had a unique claim. Armed groups’ claims to special status rested on their record of resistance, while the claims of the government and the Revolutionary Council rested on Khomeini’s authorization and on the need for a unified state. Neither claim was self-evidently superior, which is why the issue of who might legitimately bear arms became so contentious.

Finally, historians should avoid presenting all leftist organizations as identical or attributing every armed confrontation to one ideological conflict. Disputes among armed groups, between them and committees, and between factions within the clerical and nationalist currents often had local, personal, or tactical causes. Understanding the variety of motivations and the shifting alignments of this period guards against retrospective schemes that divide the revolution into two clear camps, a simplification that its participants would not have recognized.

Section 92.11

Workers’ Councils, Factory Committees, and the Meaning of Economic Democracy

The industrial working class had been a decisive force in the revolution. Strikes in the oil industry, in factories, in transport, and in public services in the autumn of 1978 had crippled the state’s capacity to function and demonstrated that a mobilized workforce could threaten the monarchy’s survival. Strike committees, formed in many workplaces to coordinate walkouts, raise funds, and negotiate demands, gave workers organizational experience. After the monarchy’s fall, many of these committees evolved into workers’ councils, known in Persian as shoras, which sought a continuing voice in the running of enterprises.

It would be wrong to assume that all strike committees became identical councils. Their forms and purposes varied. Some focused on immediate economic demands, such as wage increases, reinstatement of workers dismissed for political activity, payment of back wages, and an end to arbitrary supervision. Others aimed at consultation with management over production and personnel. Still others, particularly where leftist activists were influential, sought workers’ control of the enterprise, or the removal of managers associated with the old regime and the nationalization of firms. The oil installations, large factories, and public sector enterprises each had distinctive histories and balances of power. Scholars who have studied the shoras emphasize this diversity and stress that their development depended on the structure of the industry, the presence of political activists, and the attitudes of management and the authorities.

Differentiating among the demands is important. A demand for higher wages leaves ownership and management unchanged; a demand for consultation modifies decision-making without transferring control; a demand for nationalization changes ownership but does not necessarily empower workers, since a state-owned enterprise may be managed by government appointees; workers’ control places decision-making in employees’ hands; and socialist ownership, in the range of meanings used by Iranian leftists, implies a broader restructuring of the economy. Participants in the councils used these terms differently, and the same word could carry varying meanings in different factories. Historians of the period note that “workers’ control” was claimed by groups with quite different aims.

The relationship between the councils and the provisional government was uneasy. Bazargan’s cabinet viewed the restoration of regular production as a priority. It feared that factory occupations, dismissal of managers, and disputes over authority would reduce output and discourage investment, and it asked workers to return to normal operations and to resolve grievances through established channels. Many council members, in contrast, had gained confidence from their role in the revolution and expected to be consulted about the economy’s direction. Khomeini’s statements emphasized work and national reconstruction, while also affirming concern for the poor and the “oppressed.” Some Islamic activists supported workers’ associations as part of a just Islamic society, while remaining opposed to class conflict. The result was a field of competing expectations rather than a settled labor policy.

Asef Bayat’s study of workers’ control in Iran has shown how interactions between workers’ organizations and the evolving institutions of revolutionary authority shaped the councils’ fate. Councils could be supported when they helped restore order or counter managers associated with the old regime, and opposed when they asserted independence from the emerging structures of authority. Over subsequent months and years, pressures from the government, Islamic associations, employers, and economic hardship reduced the autonomy of many councils, though the process was uneven and cannot be fully described within this chapter’s time frame. For February and March 1979, the important point is that workers’ organizations had real strength, and that their claims raised questions about who should control economic life that the new state had not answered.

A further source of tension was unemployment and the position of workers dismissed before or during the revolution. Many demanded reinstatement and compensation for lost wages, while managers and officials hesitated to rehire people whose conduct they could not easily assess. Some councils formed committees to investigate managers accused of corruption or collaboration with the old regime, and in a number of enterprises senior staff left or were removed. These actions blurred the boundary between legitimate grievance redress and informal purge, and they added to the sense among the cabinet’s members that economic authority was being exercised outside the normal channels of management.

Historical Debate

Did Revolutionary Councils Democratize Economic and Political Life or Fragment Governmental Authority?

The question: Workers’ councils, neighborhood committees, and similar bodies multiplied after February 1979. Historians disagree about whether they should be understood primarily as expressions of democratic participation, as obstacles to the creation of coherent government, or as something between.

Interpretation A — Councils as democratization: Scholars sympathetic to this view stress that councils gave ordinary people an unprecedented voice in workplaces and neighborhoods. They show that workers developed forms of self-organization and collective bargaining, that committees provided services and security when the state failed, and that the revolution’s participatory dimension should be taken seriously rather than treated as mere disorder.
Interpretation B — Councils as fragmentation of authority: Others emphasize that overlapping and unaccountable bodies undermined the provisional government and made it difficult to implement consistent policy. They note that some councils and committees acted arbitrarily, that local bodies could be captured by particular factions, and that the absence of coordination weakened the possibility of representative government resting on law.
Interpretation C — The councils as an arena used by multiple actors: A third approach holds that the councils were neither inherently democratic nor inherently disruptive. Their significance depended on who controlled them and how political authorities treated them; different actors, including leftists, Islamic activists, and the state, sought to use or subordinate them for their own purposes.

Evidence: Historians use factory records, council statements, newspapers, memoirs of activists and managers, interviews, and labor ministry documents. Evidence is uneven: some enterprises are well documented, others barely.

Points of disagreement: The debate involves terminology (what counts as democracy), interpretation of motives, and the scale of councils’ activity. It also involves causation: whether the councils’ decline resulted mainly from state pressure, internal divisions, or economic conditions.

Why it matters: How one understands the councils affects one’s view of whether a democratic outcome was a real possibility in 1979 and who bore responsibility for its failure.

Student task: Select one interpretation and identify the evidence that supports it. Then identify one piece of evidence, from this chapter or elsewhere, that complicates it. Explain what additional sources would help resolve the disagreement.

Section 92.12

Women’s Rights, Political Participation, and the Demonstrations of March 1979

Women had taken part in the revolution in large numbers and in diverse roles. They marched in the demonstrations of 1978, with many wearing the chador as a sign of solidarity with the religious opposition, including women who did not ordinarily do so. They organized in neighborhoods, taught in schools, treated the wounded in hospitals, signed petitions, and took part in strikes as teachers, nurses, and civil servants. Women belonged to nationalist, Marxist, Islamic, and independent organizations. Their reasons for participating were various: opposition to the Shah, religious conviction, hope for improved conditions, and the expectation, among many, that a free Iran would respect their rights.

Women’s expectations differed. Some wanted to preserve and extend rights gained under the Pahlavi state, including the right to vote, access to education and employment, and the protections of the Family Protection Law of 1967 and 1975, which had restricted a husband’s right to unilateral divorce and polygyny. Others, particularly religiously committed women, criticized the Pahlavi family laws as imposed from above, and sought a different understanding of women’s roles grounded in Islamic law and ethics, while also wanting educational and professional opportunities. Women’s positions, in short, did not map neatly onto “secular” and “religious” categories, and individual women could hold views that combined elements of both.

The new authorities’ early actions raised concerns. Within weeks of Khomeini’s return, statements and measures affecting family law, women’s legal status, and public conduct began to appear. On 26 February the Family Protection Law was repudiated as inconsistent with Islamic law; protections concerning divorce and polygyny were subsequently dismantled through changes in law and judicial practice. The announcement and the implementation of a replacement family-law system should not be confused. [7] The state-sponsored women’s organization of the old regime was dissolved. In early March, in remarks reported from Qom, Khomeini said that women working in government ministries should observe Islamic dress. Accounts of the exact wording and the circumstances differ, and the significance of the remarks was disputed at the time, but many women understood them as a signal that compulsory covering might be imposed.

The reaction was swift. On 8 March 1979, International Women’s Day, women’s groups that had planned commemorations held demonstrations in Tehran, and the protests continued over the following days. Participants included students, teachers, lawyers, public employees, and members of independent women’s organizations; they included both women who wore religious dress and women who did not. Reports of the numbers vary, from several thousand to many tens of thousands at the largest events, and no precise count can be established. The demonstrators’ slogans and statements stressed that rights and freedoms should not be curtailed in a revolution made in the name of freedom, and they opposed any compulsion regarding dress. The marches encountered hostile counter-demonstrators in some places, and there were incidents of harassment and violence.

These demonstrations must be described with care. They were not protests against an already enacted, nationwide compulsory-hijab law. No such statute existed in March 1979. The dispute concerned statements by Khomeini and others, proposed requirements for women in government workplaces, institutional changes such as the transfer of family law, and the fear that enforcement by committees and others would follow. In response to the protests, officials of the provisional government and Khomeini’s office, according to contemporary reports, clarified that a mandatory requirement had not been decreed, and the immediate controversy subsided. The dispute, however, did not end. Compulsory covering was introduced later, in stages, as discussed in subsequent chapters.

The disagreements among revolutionary supporters were as important as those between the demonstrators and the authorities. Some leftist men and women supported the women’s protest; others argued that it diverted attention from the main struggle against imperialism and class inequality, or that it risked dividing the revolutionary camp. Some Islamic activists defended Khomeini’s position; others, including women in religious organizations, sought a reading of Islamic principles that would protect women’s dignity without compulsion. Many men and women who had participated in the revolution were uneasy and uncertain. Participation in the revolutionary movement did not imply agreement about gender policy.

The March demonstrations are significant because they were an early public contest over the meaning of revolutionary freedom. They revealed that groups who had joined in overthrowing the monarchy held different expectations about the new order, and they showed that the question of the state’s role in regulating private conduct would be central. The episode also illustrated the limits of revolutionary unity: Khomeini’s authority remained great, but a large number of women were willing to publicly dispute a policy associated with him. This act of dissent is a reminder that the early weeks were a period of genuine debate, and that those debates took place among people who shared a commitment to ending the monarchy.

Section 92.13

Revolutionary Courts and the First Executions of Former Royal Officials

The demand for punishment of officials associated with the monarchy’s repression was widespread and deeply felt. Families of those killed in the demonstrations of 1978, former political prisoners, and many ordinary citizens wanted accountability for torture, killings, and corruption. SAVAK, the military governorships under martial law, and the senior armed forces command were closely associated in public memory with violence against civilians. After 11 February, thousands of officials, officers, and security personnel were detained, some by committees, some by military units, and some by individuals who sought refuge in the custody of revolutionary organizations. What should be done with them was an urgent question that no existing law could settle, since the courts of the monarchy were themselves part of the old order.

The answer was the creation of revolutionary tribunals. Emergency courts were established, outside the regular judicial system, to try former officials. Their judges were generally clerics or others appointed under the authority of Khomeini or the Revolutionary Council; their procedures were improvised, and their rules of evidence and defense were minimal. The principal early figure associated with the tribunals was the cleric Sadeq Khalkhali, who was appointed as a religious judge and presided over the proceedings that produced the first executions. The courts operated in locations such as the Refah School, the revolution’s headquarters, and in committee-run facilities.

The first executions of former senior military and security officials took place on 15–16 February 1979. Those condemned included Nematollah Nasiri, the former head of SAVAK; Mehdi Rahimi, a former military governor of Tehran; Manouchehr Khosrodad, former commander of Imperial Iranian Army Aviation; and Reza Naji, a former military governor of Isfahan. Accounts describe abbreviated proceedings on 15 February and sentences implemented that night; the proceedings should not be treated as a single precisely timed event. Contemporary human-rights investigations questioned the availability of independent defense counsel, the opportunity to challenge evidence, and the absence of meaningful review. Publicity surrounding the executions drew attention both within Iran and abroad. The distinction between bringing former officials to account and guaranteeing a fair trial became an urgent political issue. [6] [20] [21]

The accusations against the defendants included responsibility for the killing of demonstrators, torture, corruption, and betrayal of the nation. Many Iranians regarded these charges as well founded, and the executions were welcomed by some who saw them as overdue justice. But the manner of the trials raised serious issues. Defendants were given little opportunity to prepare a defense, the evidence presented was not tested by established rules, and the jurisdiction of the court was unclear. Observers, including some who had no sympathy for the defendants, questioned whether the proceedings could be called trials in any legal sense. Concerns focused on the absence of procedural guarantees, the possibility of error, and the risk that revolutionary justice could be used for political ends.

Within the revolutionary leadership there was disagreement. Bazargan and members of his cabinet expressed disquiet about the executions and the conduct of the courts, and some pressed for formal procedures, open trials, and the right to defense. Others, including Khalkhali and some in Khomeini’s circle, argued that revolutionary courts needed to act swiftly to dispose of those regarded as enemies of the people and that a delay would risk counter-revolution. Khomeini’s own position was supportive of the principle of punishing the former regime’s officials, while he at times called for attention to the fairness of the process. The dispute over who controlled the courts and what procedures they must follow was an early example of conflict between those who prioritized legal safeguards and those who prioritized revolutionary urgency.

These first executions should be distinguished from the later, much larger system of political trials and executions, which belongs to Chapter 95. In February and March the revolutionary courts were emergency bodies without settled legal foundation, and the number of executions was small compared with what followed, although dozens of people were put to death in the first weeks and months according to reports whose totals differ. Further executions of prominent former officials took place in subsequent weeks. The historical significance of the early tribunals lies in what they revealed: that the revolution would involve violent settling of accounts with the old regime, that legal accountability was disputed, and that the authority to impose death sentences rested with bodies that were not subject to the provisional government.

Contemporaneous human-rights investigations, undertaken by lawyers and organizations that documented arrests, tribunals, and detentions, are an important source for this period, though they too reflect the information available to investigators and the limits on their access. Combined with memoirs and press reports, they allow historians to establish the broad pattern: arrests by multiple authorities, hasty tribunals, and public controversy about legality. They do not always allow precise accounting of individual cases, and students should be cautious about statistics.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolutionary Tribunal, Due Process, Transitional Justice, and Rule of Law

Definition: A revolutionary tribunal is an emergency court created by revolutionary authorities, typically outside the regular judicial system, to try people associated with the former regime. Due process refers to procedural protections such as notice of charges, the opportunity to prepare a defense, the right to counsel, examination of evidence, and the possibility of appeal. Transitional justice is the set of processes, trials, truth-seeking, reparations, and institutional reform, by which societies address past abuses during or after a political transition. Rule of law is the principle that government and citizens alike are bound by publicly known laws applied through established procedures.

Historical context: In February 1979 these concepts were invoked in different ways. Demanding punishment, revolutionaries spoke of justice for victims; critics, including some within the provisional government, appealed to procedural fairness.

Why it matters: The disagreement shows that the idea of justice itself was contested, with some defining it as swift retribution and others as lawful process.

Potential misunderstanding: “Transitional justice” is a modern scholarly term, not one used by participants in 1979. Using it does not imply that the Iranian revolutionary courts followed international models.

Section 92.14

The Armed Forces, Security Services, and the Problem of Rebuilding State Coercion

The condition of the armed forces after 11 February was precarious. The command structure built by the Shah, whose senior officers had been chosen largely for loyalty to the monarch, was broken. Some commanders were arrested or in hiding, others had left the country, and others remained in post under uncertain authority. Large numbers of conscripts left their units and returned home, taking in some cases their uniforms but not always their weapons; equipment and installations had been seized or damaged during the fighting. The air force, in which the homafars had played a central role in the revolution, had a particularly altered internal dynamic, with technicians and junior personnel asserting a new voice in the institution’s affairs.

For Bazargan’s government, restoring a functioning national military was a practical necessity. Iran had long borders, including with the Soviet Union, Iraq, Pakistan, and Afghanistan, as well as significant internal security tasks, among them regional disturbances. The government wished to keep a professional force intact and to prevent its disintegration, which would leave the country defenseless and encourage separatist movements. The new defense leadership, including General Gharabaghi, who had presided over the armed forces’ neutrality declaration, worked to reestablish discipline, and many career officers cooperated, hoping to preserve their institutions and, in some cases, their lives.

Revolutionary activists often regarded the officers with suspicion. Officers had sworn loyalty to the Shah, trained under his system, and served in a military that had killed demonstrators in 1978. Many activists argued that the armed forces needed to be purged, reorganized, or replaced by popular militias, and some leftist groups advocated arming the people. Debate focused on how much of the old officer corps could be retained, how far soldiers’ committees should have a role in command, and what to do about senior officers associated with repression. These arguments overlapped with the dispute about revolutionary courts, since many of those on trial or detained were military men.

In the weeks after the monarchy’s fall, various revolutionary armed formations emerged. These included committee patrols, groups of volunteers under clerical or political leadership, and organizations of fighters drawn from the guerrilla movements. Some saw themselves as the nucleus of a new revolutionary force that would protect the revolution from both foreign and domestic threats. The Islamic Revolution Guards Corps was formally established in the following weeks and developed through the year; but the early February formations were not yet that institution, and the chapter should not present them as one. The IRGC emerged from a process of consolidating and organizing diverse armed groups under Islamic revolutionary leadership, a process that belongs to later chapters.

The central problem was control of the legitimate use of force. In a functioning state, a single authority possesses the recognized right to use coercion, and other bodies exercise force only under its delegation. In February and March 1979, no authority had this monopoly. The army answered to a damaged command, the police were demoralized and in many places replaced by committees, armed organizations followed their own leaderships, and the courts operated under different jurisdictions. The question of how to reconstitute a coherent coercive apparatus, and under whom, was therefore immediate and unresolved. Whoever succeeded in creating dependable armed institutions would hold a decisive advantage in the contest for the state.

The security services posed a related problem. The Bakhtiar government had moved to dismantle SAVAK before its own collapse, but its former personnel, files, and informants remained a subject of intense concern, and revolutionary groups sought out records and individuals associated with it. Regular police were largely absent from the streets in the first days and were only gradually reconstituted, often under the supervision of committees. Whether a new police and gendarmerie should be built from existing personnel, from revolutionary volunteers, or from a combination remained unresolved. Each option carried risks: retaining old officers risked disloyalty, while relying on volunteers risked inconsistency and abuse of power.

Section 92.15

Property, Economic Decisions, and the First Demands for Revolutionary Redistribution

The revolution had been fueled in part by economic grievance: resentment of inequality, corruption associated with the court and its allies, rapid inflation during the oil boom, housing shortages, and the displacement of rural migrants. Once the monarchy fell, these grievances translated into demands for action. Revolutionaries called for the seizure of the property of the Pahlavi family and its associates, for control of major firms, for housing for the poor, for the distribution of land, and for protection of workers’ jobs. The new authorities had to determine how far and how fast to respond.

Different kinds of action should be distinguished. The confiscation of particular assets, such as royal properties, the holdings of the Pahlavi Foundation, and the property of individuals accused of corruption or flight, was a relatively contained measure that enjoyed broad support. Temporary administrative control was another option, in which the state or a revolutionary body took charge of abandoned or disputed enterprises to prevent collapse, without deciding on permanent ownership. Comprehensive nationalization of whole sectors, such as banking, insurance, or heavy industry, was a more sweeping step. This chapter stresses that in February and March 1979 the third had not yet occurred, and the later nationalizations, which followed in the course of 1979, should not be read backward into the first weeks.

Positions on these questions varied. Bazargan’s ministers, many of them engineers and professionals, favored a mixed economy with respect for private property and pressed for orderly legal procedures to deal with the assets of the former regime. Leftist organizations and many workers’ councils advocated more radical redistribution and the nationalization of major industries. Bazaar merchants wanted guarantees for private commerce and a rapid return to stability. Islamic activists who emphasized social justice and the cause of the “deprived” and the “oppressed” proposed measures to assist the poor, such as housing and land programs, and were divided over how far to restrict private property. Khomeini’s own statements combined support for the poor with a general acceptance of lawful ownership, leaving room for different interpretations.

The assets of the former elite presented immediate problems. Many wealthy families and officials left the country, and factories, offices, and homes were abandoned or placed under the control of committees and employees. Authorities faced the task of preventing looting and asset stripping while deciding who should manage the properties. A decree of 1 March ordered confiscation of the Shah’s, his family’s and close associates’ property. On 3 March the Foundation for the Downtrodden (Bonyad-e Mostazafan) was established to administer confiscated assets for the benefit of the poor. The distinction between confiscation and continuing administration mattered: the new foundation provided a separate institutional channel for economic power. Its later development belongs to Chapter 105. [8] For the period examined here, the point is that decisions about property were being made by several bodies without a settled legal framework.

Housing and land showed similar pressures. Poor families and migrants in urban settlements sought to occupy vacant buildings or land, and in some areas rural cultivators pressed claims against landowners. The provisional government’s response was cautious, emphasizing the need for legal processes, while some committees and activists encouraged or tolerated occupations. These conflicts brought out the social meaning of the revolution for lower-income groups, who expected material improvements, and they highlighted the dilemma facing authorities: acting too slowly risked disillusionment, while acting too quickly risked disorder and economic damage.

The analytical focus of this section is therefore on competing visions of economic authority during the uncertain opening weeks. Who had the right to decide about property: the cabinet, the Revolutionary Council, the religious leadership, workers’ councils, or local committees? Could ownership be altered by revolutionary decree before a constitution existed? These questions did not have agreed answers, and the lack of agreement, combined with the pressure of events, produced a series of ad hoc decisions that later shaped the economy of the Islamic Republic.

Section 92.16

Regional Movements, Kurdish Autonomy, and the Limits of Centralized Revolutionary Authority

The revolution was not a single event but a set of events that occurred in Tehran and across the country, and its meaning varied by region. Iran’s population included large communities, Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Arabs, Baluch, Turkmen, Lurs, and others, whose languages, religions, and histories differed from those of the Persian-speaking majority and from one another. Under the Pahlavi monarchy, centralizing policies had restricted the use of minority languages in education and public life, and many communities had grievances concerning political representation, economic development, and cultural recognition. The collapse of the monarchy gave these communities an opportunity to voice demands that had been suppressed.

Kurdish political activity was among the most organized. In the Kurdish-populated areas of the west and northwest, political parties and local groups re-emerged. The Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan, long banned, reorganized publicly, and its leader, Abdul Rahman Qasemlou, returned from exile. Other groups, including the leftist Komala organization, also developed a presence. Kurdish religious leaders, among them Sunni and Shi‘i clergy, played roles in local politics. Kurdish demands varied but commonly included autonomy, meaning self-administration within Iran, recognition of the Kurdish language, representation in governing institutions, and the control of local resources. These demands should be distinguished from separatism, the desire for independence from Iran; most Kurdish parties at this stage advocated autonomy within an Iranian state, though outside observers and government officials sometimes treated the two as equivalent.

Confrontations occurred in March 1979. In Kurdistan, disputes arose over control of military installations, the presence of government-aligned armed groups, local administration, and the status of Kurdish political organizations. In the city of Sanandaj, armed clashes beginning on 18 March involved Kurdish groups, local residents, and forces associated with the central authorities, and the fighting became a symbol of the tension between regional aspirations and central control. [9] Historians of Kurdish politics treat these events as the first major confrontations between the new authorities and a regional movement, though their precise sequence and casualty figures remain difficult to establish from the available sources.

Other regions experienced distinct disputes. Among the Turkmen of the northeast, whose Sunni majority and tradition of pastoralism and agriculture gave them a particular identity, local councils sought land redistribution and cultural recognition. A strike in Gonbad-e Kavus was reported on 11 March; renewed conflict in the town on 26–28 March demonstrated how land disputes, minority rights and competing security authorities converged. [10] [11] In Khuzestan, Arab communities, in an oil-producing region with significant economic grievances, formed organizations calling for recognition of language and a share in the region’s wealth. Baluch communities in the southeast, long among the poorest in Iran, pressed their own concerns. In the northwest, Azerbaijani Turks, the largest minority, were generally integrated into the revolutionary movement, and many looked to the religious leadership of figures such as Ayatollah Shariatmadari, though not uniformly. These communities were not united in ideology or aims; even within them, there were Islamic, nationalist, leftist, and conservative tendencies.

Taken together, these developments established the early territorial and constitutional questions that would continue to trouble the Islamic Republic: whether Iran would be a unitary centralized state or allow regional self-administration, how minority languages and religions would be treated, and who had the authority to negotiate between center and periphery. The sustained conflicts that followed, and the use of force by the central authorities, are the subject of Chapter 95. For present purposes, the important point is that in February and March 1979 the reach of revolutionary authority outside Tehran was limited, that regional movements possessed organized constituencies, and that the response of the center was still being determined.

Azerbaijan illustrated a different pattern. The northwest, with its largely Azerbaijani Turkish population and its commercial city of Tabriz, had been a center of the protests, and its inhabitants were generally integrated into the revolutionary movement. Many looked to local religious leaders for guidance, and cultural demands, such as for the use of the Azerbaijani language in education and publishing, were voiced alongside support for the revolution. These aspirations did not take the form of armed confrontation in this period, but they formed part of the wider question of how a centralized state would accommodate linguistic and cultural diversity.

Competing Projects for Iran’s Political Future

Section 92.17

Islamic Government, Constitutional Republicanism, Socialism, and Political Pluralism

By February 1979, nearly everyone in the revolutionary movement agreed that the monarchy should end, and many spoke of a republic. But the word covered different ideas. A republic could mean a government in which sovereignty belongs to the people and is exercised through elected officials under a constitution protecting individual rights. It could mean a republic governed by Islamic principles, in which elected institutions operated within religious norms. It could also mean a state in which qualified Islamic jurists exercised overarching authority. Each of these conceptions had its advocates, and the differences among them were not resolved when the monarchy fell.

Khomeini’s conception of Islamic government had been set out in lectures delivered in Najaf in the early 1970s and later published under the title commonly translated as Islamic Government. It argued that, in the absence of the Twelfth Imam, qualified jurists were responsible for governing the community according to Islamic law, an idea later known as velayat-e faqih, the guardianship of the jurist. This doctrine was controversial among Shi‘i scholars, many of whom held that clerics should advise rather than rule. Khomeini’s public statements in early 1979 were more cautious than the book’s theory. He spoke of an Islamic republic and of popular consent, emphasized national independence and social justice, and did not specify the powers of the jurist. How far his followers expected a state built on velayat-e faqih in February and March is difficult to establish, and it should not be assumed that all those who supported him shared that expectation.

Bazargan’s outlook, which can be called religious constitutionalism, held that Islam and constitutional government were compatible. He favored an elected legislature, an independent judiciary, civil liberties, and a government accountable to the people, with Islamic values informing public life. Many in the Freedom Movement had long defended the 1906 Constitution against royal abuse, and they regarded the revolution as the realization of constitutional principles. The National Front tradition, rooted in Mossadegh’s nationalism, similarly stressed constitutionalism, national independence, and secular, though not anti-religious, politics, though the Front itself was divided and weakened by the choices of its members, including Bakhtiar’s entry into government.

The Mojahedin-e Khalq articulated a revolutionary Islamic program that combined religious language with an emphasis on social equality and anti-imperialism. They supported the revolution, were respectful toward Khomeini, but retained an independent organization and sought a state in which their ideas would compete. Marxist and socialist groups, including the Fedai and the Tudeh Party, wanted a transformation of property relations and a state serving workers and peasants, with differing views about alliance with Islamic forces. Women’s organizations, professional associations, writers’ and lawyers’ groups, and student bodies sought guarantees of freedom of expression, association, and equality before the law. Ethnic and regional movements added demands for autonomy and cultural rights.

It is important to distinguish what participants said and proposed in early 1979 from positions they adopted later. Some who supported a rapid referendum in March would later become critics of the Islamic Republic’s institutions, and others who were then skeptical would later accept them. The retrospective labels that are now common, such as “liberal,” “hardline,” “reformist,” and “conservative,” do not map neatly onto the alignments of 1979, and applying them uncritically distorts the picture. In February and March the question was not yet a choice between a fixed Islamic Republic and its alternatives but a negotiation over what the term “Islamic Republic” would mean.

The clerical leadership was itself not uniform. Senior religious figures such as Mahmud Taleghani, who had been prominent in the opposition and was respected by many on the left, and Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari, a leading authority with a large following in Azerbaijan, held positions that did not always coincide with Khomeini’s. Both supported ending the monarchy, but their views on the role of the clergy in government and on the limits of revolutionary action differed in emphasis. Their continuing prominence is a reminder that “the clergy” and “the revolution” were not interchangeable terms, and that debates about the state took place within the religious establishment as well as outside it.

Multiple Perspectives

Competing Visions of the Iranian Republic in February–March 1979

Khomeini and clerical political activists: An Islamic republic whose institutions reflect Islamic law and in which religious leadership has an authoritative role, with legitimacy grounded in the people’s demonstrated support and a commitment to independence from foreign powers and to social justice for the deprived.
Bazargan and the Freedom Movement: A democratic, constitutional republic respecting Islamic values, with elected institutions, the rule of law, and gradual reform; the provisional government as an administrative bridge to a constituent assembly and an elected legislature.
National Front and secular nationalists: A parliamentary republic with civil liberties and national independence, in which religion would be respected but would not constitute the basis of the state’s authority; concern that a rushed referendum would foreclose debate.
Mojahedin-e Khalq: A revolutionary Islamic order marked by social equality and anti-imperialism, with political space for their organization to compete for influence.
Marxist and socialist organizations: A transformation of economic and social relations in favor of workers and peasants, regarding the end of the monarchy as an initial stage, with disagreement over how far to ally with Islamic forces.
Women’s, professional, and student organizations: Guarantees of equality, freedom of expression, and association, with some emphasizing secular law and others seeking Islamic frameworks that protected women’s rights.

Note on power: These groups did not possess equal organizational, coercive, or symbolic resources. Khomeini’s authority and the clerical networks’ reach into mosques and neighborhoods gave them advantages that other groups lacked, while the armed organizations had weapons but less popular legitimacy among the religious majority.

Section 92.18

International Recognition, the February Embassy Incident, and Revolutionary Foreign Policy

The provisional government sought international recognition and moved to define its foreign relations in the first weeks. Governments around the world had to decide whether to recognize the new authorities, and most did so rapidly, since the Bakhtiar government had ceased to exist. The United States, which had been the Shah’s principal ally and arms supplier, maintained an embassy staffed by hundreds of Americans and sought a working relationship with the new government, while recognizing that its previous ties to the monarchy complicated matters. The Soviet Union, a neighbor and Cold War rival, offered recognition and observed events closely. European governments, Iran’s neighbors in the Gulf and the wider region, and countries that purchased Iranian oil also established contacts.

The revolutionary leadership articulated principles of independence, summed up for many supporters in the claim that Iran would be free of domination by either superpower. This stance had practical implications. The new authorities reviewed arms contracts, questioned the presence of foreign advisers, and examined cooperation arrangements, including intelligence-gathering facilities in the north that had been operated with American involvement. They also questioned Iran’s alignment within regional security arrangements, a reexamination that resulted in Iran’s announcement of withdrawal from the Central Treaty Organization (CENTO) on 11 March. Pakistan followed on 12 March; the alliance’s dissolution was a related but distinct development. [12] At the same time the government wished to maintain commercial relations, including the sale of oil, and to preserve access to spare parts and technical expertise for the military and industry, which had been built largely on foreign supply.

On 14 February 1979, the first sign of the dangers facing foreign missions came in Tehran. Armed men attacked the American embassy compound, entering the grounds and the chancery building. The assailants are generally described in contemporary reporting as members of armed leftist groups, though the exact identities and motives are less clear in the sources. U.S. Marine guards and diplomats were confined, and the ambassador, William Sullivan, ordered that they cease resistance to avoid bloodshed. The occupation was ended within hours when forces aligned with Khomeini, accompanied by officials of the provisional government such as Ibrahim Yazdi, intervened, secured the compound, and escorted the attackers out. The provisional government apologized, and the embassy resumed operations, with the Iranian authorities pledging to protect it.

This episode must be distinguished explicitly from the seizure of the embassy on 4 November 1979, which Chapter 94 examines. The February incident was an attack by armed groups not under the government’s control, and it was ended by the revolutionary authorities, with the cooperation of Khomeini’s supporters. The November seizure was a different event, carried out by students and ending in a prolonged hostage crisis that the revolutionary leadership endorsed. Conflating the two events obscures the very different positions that Khomeini’s circle and the provisional government took toward the American mission in February and November, and it risks reading the later crisis backward into the earlier period. The February attack is better understood as evidence of the provisional government’s inability to control armed groups and of the early alignment between Bazargan’s officials and the Khomeini network in resolving the crisis.

Policy toward Israel and the Palestinian cause changed quickly. The monarchy had maintained a discreet relationship with Israel that included oil sales and a quasi-diplomatic mission in Tehran. The new authorities ended these ties. On 17 February, Yasser Arafat, the chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization, arrived in Tehran and was received with public enthusiasm; the PLO took over the premises formerly used by the Israeli mission. The move was popular among many revolutionary supporters, who identified with the Palestinian cause and saw it as an expression of anti-imperialist principles, and it signaled a significant change in Iran’s regional alignments.

The broader question is how a revolutionary government attempted to gain international legitimacy while domestic control remained unsettled. Foreign governments dealt with multiple interlocutors: the prime minister and foreign minister, Khomeini’s office, the Revolutionary Council, and, at times, local committees. Diplomats’ reports from the period, like the cable discussed earlier, show their uncertainty about whom to trust. The new authorities, for their part, sought recognition and trade without appearing to abandon their principles, and different factions defined the balance differently. These early choices, made under pressure, set patterns that would be tested during the crisis of the autumn.

Section 92.19

The Road toward the Islamic Republic Referendum

Throughout February and March, a central political issue was what procedure would establish the new state. Khomeini’s decree of 4 February envisaged a referendum on an Islamic republic, a popularly elected assembly to prepare the constitution, and parliamentary elections under the new order. The mandate outlined a sequence, but it did not settle the wording of the ballot, the timetable for constitution-making, or the powers of the resulting institutions. Some opponents preferred a constituent assembly before a vote on the system itself. Questions multiplied: what alternatives would the ballot offer, who would supervise the vote, how would the assembly represent different political constituencies, and how long would the provisional government remain in office? The difference between a declared procedure and an agreed constitutional settlement is crucial. [1]

Within the revolutionary leadership, a view gained strength that the quickest way to consolidate the revolution’s achievements was a prompt referendum on the establishment of an Islamic republic. Khomeini supported this approach, and the Revolutionary Council and provisional government moved to organize it. The date was set for 30–31 March 1979, and the ballot was to present voters with a single question: for or against an Islamic republic. Supporters argued that the people had already shown their will in the demonstrations, that delay would invite counter-revolution, and that a clear popular mandate would resolve doubts about the new government’s legitimacy.

Others raised objections. Groups in the National Front tradition and various secular organizations argued that voters should have a choice among alternatives, and that the term “Islamic republic” had not been defined, so a vote for it would amount to approving an unknown. They called instead for the election of a constituent assembly with the time and freedom to debate the form of the state. Some Kurdish and other regional parties questioned the process and, in some cases, declined to take part. Some leftists regarded the referendum as premature. Some within Bazargan’s circle, though supportive of a republic, were concerned about the lack of detail. Khomeini’s side responded that the question was clear, that the people understood what an Islamic republic meant, and that further elaboration would come through the constitution.

The weeks before the vote were a time of vigorous debate, though under constraints. Newspapers and political groups argued over the referendum in print and in rallies. Freedom of organization and public debate existed in practice to a degree unusual in Iran’s recent past, but committees and groups hostile to certain publications and meetings sometimes interfered with opponents’ activities, and some political gatherings were disrupted. Whether the campaign allowed meaningful competition among positions is an important question for assessing the referendum, which Chapter 93 addresses. This chapter stops before reporting the vote’s result.

Students should distinguish agreement on ending the monarchy from agreement on the legal content of the new state. Nearly all revolutionary participants agreed on the former. The latter, the powers of religious leaders, the role of elected bodies, the independence of courts, the status of rights, and the treatment of minorities, remained undefined. The referendum, as framed, would settle the first but not the second, leaving the content of the republic to the constitutional process that followed. This division between a clear but limited popular decision and a later process of detailed institutional design would shape the contests of the following months.

Khomeini’s side also stressed the clarity of the question. In public statements he insisted that the choice should be framed simply as for or against an Islamic republic, rejecting suggestions that the wording be elaborated or that alternative formulations be added. His supporters presented this as respect for the people’s plain wishes; critics saw it as a way of avoiding detailed debate. The Revolutionary Council and the provisional government administered the arrangements, which meant that the bodies organizing the vote were also parties to the argument about its terms, a circumstance that fueled suspicion among those already doubtful about the process.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Constitutional Revolution of 1906 to the Contests over Sovereignty in 1979

What Changed

  • In 1906 the movement aimed to limit a monarch’s power through a constitution and parliament; in 1979 it aimed to end the monarchy altogether.
  • The 1979 movement was far larger, involving millions in cities and countryside, and was supported by mass communications and a much more developed state apparatus.
  • Religious leadership in 1979 was more directly engaged in political organization, with a single prominent figure commanding wide public allegiance.
  • The state in 1979 was much more centralized, with a large military, bureaucracy, and oil income, and so the stakes of controlling it were higher.

What Remained Continuous

  • The questions of whether sovereignty resided in the people, the ruler, or religious law recurred; the 1907 Supplementary Fundamental Laws had included a provision for clerical review of legislation, which shows that the relation between elected and religious authority was an issue from the start.
  • Coalitions of bazaar merchants, clergy, intellectuals, and urban crowds provided a pattern of mobilization in both episodes.
  • Foreign influence and resistance to it were persistent themes.
  • Disputes among allies after a successful mobilization, as occurred after 1906–1911, were repeated in 1979.

Why the pattern matters: The comparison shows that questions about the source and limits of political authority were not novel in 1979, but it also warns against assuming direct inheritance. Each episode had distinctive actors, and participants in 1979 interpreted the earlier revolution in various ways, some seeing 1979 as completing it and others as departing from it.

Section 92.20

Historical Synthesis: Revolutionary Victory without a Settled Constitutional Order

By 29 March 1979, the day before voting began in the referendum, Iran presented an unusual political picture. The monarchy had gone, and restoration no longer constituted a viable mainstream political program inside revolutionary Iran. A provisional government with competent administrators held the ministries and conducted daily governance. The Revolutionary Council advised Khomeini and shaped decisions. Khomeini, from Qom, exercised an authority that no formal institution matched. Committees patrolled neighborhoods and held detainees; armed organizations retained weapons; workers’ councils sought influence in workplaces; women’s organizations and others pressed for rights; regional movements asserted claims; and political parties and newspapers debated the country’s future. There was no constitution defining how these elements related to each other.

The new situation created real opportunities. Millions of Iranians who had been excluded from political life under the monarchy now took part in public debate, organized associations, and sought to shape decisions that affected them. The release of political prisoners, the reappearance of banned parties and publications, and the expansion of public discussion were significant changes. Many Iranians experienced this period as one of liberation and possibility. It would be a mistake to reduce it to a mere prelude to later restrictions, since the aspirations expressed in those weeks were genuine and did not disappear.

The situation also produced serious conflicts, around legal accountability (the revolutionary courts), public order (the committees and armed groups), labor relations (the workers’ councils), political pluralism (the referendum debate), regional autonomy (Kurdistan and elsewhere), women’s rights (the March demonstrations), and the legitimate use of coercion (the armed forces and the Guards’ precursors). These were not separate problems. They shared a root in the absence of an agreed constitutional framework and a recognized hierarchy of institutions. Each dispute raised the question of which body had the right to decide, and the lack of an answer made every dispute harder to settle.

The ambiguous hierarchy that characterized the period is best understood by distinguishing three kinds of authority. Popular revolutionary legitimacy, the claim to rule on behalf of the people who made the revolution, was widely shared but also widely contested, since many groups invoked it. Constitutional legitimacy, based on a framework of law, was in suspension, awaiting the referendum and constitution. Administrative effectiveness, the capacity to carry out decisions, rested largely with the provisional government and the ministries, which lacked enforcement power. Khomeini and the clerical networks held the strongest claim to the first; Bazargan’s government to the third; and no one yet held the second. The tension among these explains why the period was both promising and fragile.

Historians have interpreted February–March 1979 in various ways. Some see it as a period of exceptional democratic opportunity, in which a pluralistic revolution might have produced representative government. Others see it as a process of clerical centralization, in which Khomeini’s network used its advantages to subordinate other groups. Others emphasize a struggle among several revolutionary projects, none of which was predetermined to win. And many offer combinations of these views. The evidence is consistent with each of them in part, and which one seems most persuasive depends on how one weighs the capabilities and intentions of different actors and the significance of specific events, such as the first executions, the women’s demonstrations, and the structuring of the referendum.

What the evidence does not support is the idea that the eventual outcome was predetermined. In February and March 1979, participants did not know how the contest would end. The balance of power was uneven: Khomeini had unmatched popular standing, the clerical networks had organizational reach, and the armed organizations had weapons but not wide religious legitimacy. Yet the government held the bureaucracy, the National Front and other groups still had constituencies, and mass mobilization could be turned in more than one direction. Historians should avoid reading later victories as the inevitable result of earlier advantages, and should recognize that choices, contingencies, and events, such as those examined in Chapters 93 through 95, shaped the path.

The chapter ends, therefore, with an open question that governs the next: how would the revolution’s competing claims to legitimacy be translated into a constitution and permanent institutions of government? That question concerns who would draft the constitution, what it would say about the powers of jurists, elected bodies, and courts, and how opposition would be treated. The answer, as Chapter 93 shows, would be given in the months following the referendum, through a process that tested the alliances and claims examined here.

Historical Debate

Was the Early Revolutionary State a Transition toward Representative Government or the Beginning of Concentrated Revolutionary Authority?

The question: Did the institutions of February–March 1979 point toward a constitutional, representative order, or did they already contain the elements of a centralized system dominated by a particular religious-political network?

Interpretation A — A transition toward representative government: Proponents point to the appointment of an experienced cabinet, the promise of a referendum and constituent assembly, the flourishing of political parties and newspapers, the presence of multiple groups in public debate, and the stated commitments of Khomeini and Bazargan to popular consent. They argue that the revolution’s early institutions were improvised but oriented toward elections and constitutional government.
Interpretation B — The beginning of concentrated revolutionary authority: Others emphasize that the Revolutionary Council and Khomeini’s network held decisive power, that revolutionary courts and committees acted outside the cabinet’s control, that the referendum offered a single question without defining its content, and that institutions subject to clerical direction were established before elected bodies existed. In this view, concentration of authority began early.
Interpretation C — An open contest with unequal resources: A further view holds that both tendencies existed together and that the outcome depended on decisions made later, including the constitution-making process, the hostage crisis, and the war with Iraq. On this account, the early period is best treated as one of contingency.

Evidence: Declarations, decrees, memoirs, diplomatic reports, newspapers, and the records of the revolutionary bodies. Evidence on internal deliberations of the Revolutionary Council is limited and mostly retrospective.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly interpretation and causation: how much weight to give leaders’ stated intentions compared with institutional arrangements and later events, and how far later outcomes can be inferred from early conditions.

Why it matters: The answer affects judgments about whether alternatives were available and about responsibility for what followed. It also affects how students understand the Islamic Republic’s origins, as a product of a deliberate design or of contested, contingent processes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the weeks between the Shah’s departure and the eve of the Islamic Republic referendum, a period in which the Pahlavi monarchy’s collapse produced not a settled successor state but a contest over who had the authority to govern Iran. Its central argument is that overthrowing a monarchy and constructing a successor state are different tasks, and that in February and March 1979 the first had been accomplished while the second had barely begun.

The first division showed how competing claims to legitimacy arose even before the monarchy fell. The Shah’s departure on 16 January, without abdication, left the monarchy’s constitutional framework in place under a Regency Council and the Bakhtiar government, which held legal authority but little popular support. Khomeini’s return on 1 February gave the revolutionary movement a leader of exceptional standing, though without formal office. His appointment of Mehdi Bazargan on 5 February created a rival government grounded in revolutionary and religious legitimacy, so that Iran briefly had two prime ministers. The armed forces’ declaration of neutrality on 11 February, following fighting in Tehran, ended the monarchy’s practical authority and distributed weapons to civilian and revolutionary groups.

The second division examined the institutions that attempted to govern. Bazargan’s provisional government held the ministries and the formal apparatus of administration and made real efforts to restore services, the economy, and the oil industry. But it operated alongside the Revolutionary Council, Khomeini’s personal authority, and the revolutionary committees, none of which was fully accountable to it. The result was a network of overlapping authorities rather than a simple dual power. The state’s infrastructure and personnel survived the political rupture, which explains why Iran did not collapse into chaos, but the question of who would direct them remained open.

The third division turned to society. Mosques, bazaars, universities, neighborhoods, and workplaces remained mobilized, and participants sought to convert their revolutionary contributions into influence. Armed organizations, including the Fedai and the Mojahedin, held weapons seized in February and differed in their attitudes toward the new authorities. Workers’ councils sought a voice in management and employment, raising questions about economic democracy that the cabinet, concerned with restoring production, did not share. Women who had participated in the revolution demonstrated in March against proposed requirements concerning dress and against threats to legal rights; the demonstrations showed that agreement on ending the monarchy did not imply agreement on gender policy.

The fourth division considered justice, force, property, and region. Revolutionary tribunals carried out the first executions of senior officials on 15–16 February under procedures that raised serious questions of due process, and they exposed disagreement within the revolutionary camp. The armed forces had to be rebuilt amid suspicion, while revolutionary armed formations emerged that were distinct from the later Revolutionary Guards. Demands for the confiscation and redistribution of property competed with the cabinet’s preference for legal procedure, and the major nationalizations lay in the future. Kurdish and other regional movements asserted claims to autonomy, and confrontations in Kurdistan showed that revolutionary authority had limited reach outside the capital.

The fifth division compared the political programs then in competition: Khomeini’s Islamic government, Bazargan’s religious constitutionalism, National Front secular nationalism, the Mojahedin’s revolutionary Islam, Marxist socialism, and the demands of professional and women’s organizations. It stressed that “republic” meant different things to different people and that positions in early 1979 should not be confused with later ones. It also examined the first foreign-policy decisions, including the attack on the American embassy on 14 February, which was ended through the intervention of Khomeini-aligned forces and which must be distinguished from the hostage crisis of November, and the reorientation of policy toward Israel and the Palestinians. Finally, it traced the debate over the referendum, with its single question and undefined content, stopping before the vote.

Throughout, the chapter distinguished three kinds of authority: popular revolutionary legitimacy, constitutional legitimacy, and administrative effectiveness. In late March no institution possessed all three. Khomeini and the clerical networks held the strongest claim to revolutionary legitimacy, the provisional government to administrative competence, and the constitutional order was awaiting construction. This distribution explains both the exceptional opportunities of the period and its fragility.

The evidence for the period is varied but uneven. Declarations, decrees, newspaper reports, diplomatic cables, human-rights investigations, memoirs, and scholarly studies allow reconstruction of the main developments, but numbers of participants, weapons, and casualties are uncertain, and accounts written later are often shaped by the conflicts that followed. The chapter has therefore offered ranges and qualifications where the evidence requires, and has distinguished claims by historical actors from established facts.

Historians interpret the period in different ways: as an episode of democratic opportunity, a process of clerical centralization, a struggle among several revolutionary projects, or a combination. This chapter has argued that the outcome was not predetermined. The balance of resources was unequal, but choices, contingencies, and later events, including the constitution-making process and the crises of the following year, shaped what followed.

The chapter closes with the question that governs Chapter 93: how the revolution’s competing claims to legitimacy would be translated into a constitution and permanent institutions of government. The answer was not yet known on 29 March 1979, and understanding why it remained open is essential to understanding how the Islamic Republic took shape.

The cabinet appointed by Khomeini on 5 February 1979 under Mehdi Bazargan, responsible for administration during a transition toward a constitutional order.

A body of Khomeini’s allies and advisers, publicly announced on 12 January 1979, that influenced decisions and appointments during the transition; its membership was initially undisclosed.

The recognized right of an authority to rule, which may rest on law, popular support, religious standing, or other grounds.

Authority derived from established law and procedures; in early 1979 it was associated with the monarchy’s framework and the Bakhtiar government.

The claim to ultimate political authority, located variously in a monarch, the people, religious law, or a constitution.

An analytical term for coexisting institutions with different sources of authority; useful but insufficient for 1979, when more than two claimants existed.

The large cemetery south of Tehran where Khomeini addressed crowds on 1 February 1979, chosen for its association with those killed in the revolution.
Marja‘
A senior Shi‘i jurist whom believers follow for religious guidance; Khomeini held this status but his authority in 1979 went beyond it.
Homafar
A junior air force technician; air force technicians’ show of support for Khomeini helped trigger the February fighting.

Shora
A workers’ council or workplace committee seeking a role in management, employment, or production.

Self-administration within a state; distinguished from separatism, which seeks independence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the end of a regime is not the same as the creation of a new one. The weeks between the Shah’s departure and the referendum were a period in which the revolution’s coalition was still intact but already divided, and in which institutions were improvised under pressure. The questions that arose, about legitimacy, coercion, justice, rights, and regional diversity, did not belong to a later stage. They were present from the start.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in modern Iranian history: the recurring problem of the relationship between popular mobilization and constitutional order, which links 1979 to the Constitutional Revolution of 1906–1911; the importance of networks of mosques, bazaars, and neighborhoods in shaping political action; and the persistence of administrative institutions through political rupture. These patterns help explain why the Islamic Republic took the form it did and why its institutions embodied tensions between popular sovereignty and religious authority.

Later developments built directly on this period. The constitution-making process, the hostage crisis, the consolidation of revolutionary institutions, and the war with Iraq would all be shaped by the organizations, claims, and disputes that emerged in February and March. Yet the chapter has argued that none of these outcomes was fixed in advance. Recognizing contingency means treating participants as actors who made choices under uncertainty, and not simply as figures in a story whose ending is known.

For the larger textbook, Chapter 92 develops the opening themes of Volume VIII by demonstrating that the Islamic Republic’s history begins not with a finished institution but with an unsettled contest. Chapter 93 turns to how that contest was addressed through a referendum and a constitution, and the chapters that follow trace how the new order was consolidated, challenged, and tested.
