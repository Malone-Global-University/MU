Railways, Oil, Industry, Education, and the Developmental State

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between the late 1920s and 1941, the Iranian state built a railway across two mountain systems, negotiated and renegotiated the terms of the country's largest foreign enterprise, founded factories, banks, and a university, and sent students to Europe. It did so with limited revenue, scarce skills, and an authoritarian method, and the results were real, uneven, and unequally shared.

In August 1938 a railway that ran from the Caspian Sea to the Persian Gulf was formally opened. Its builders had driven it through the Alborz and Zagros mountains, over high bridges and through tunnels, and across a country in which, only a generation earlier, most goods had moved by animal caravan. The line is commonly given a length of roughly 1,400 kilometers. Its political meaning was at least as important as its freight capacity: it joined Tehran to both seas, it was financed largely from domestic taxation rather than foreign loans, and it was presented as proof that the state could carry out projects that earlier governments had only imagined. That presentation, and the evidence for and against it, is one of the central subjects of this chapter.

The railway was one part of a wider program. Chapter 81 examined how Reza Shah built the army, bureaucracy, and monarchy through which this program was carried out, and Chapter 82 examined the legal, cultural, and nationalist projects of the same state. This chapter turns to material development: transportation, petroleum, industry, banking and trade, and modern education. These were not separate enterprises. Railways needed steel, engineers, and money; money came from taxes, customs, and oil payments; engineers and administrators came from the new schools or from abroad; and the factories needed protected markets, credit, and workers who had to be recruited from villages and cities that were themselves being rearranged.

The history of these years is easily told as a story of heroic construction, and it can as easily be told as a story of coercion and waste. Neither version is adequate. The evidence shows a government that acquired new capacities, that depended heavily on foreign firms, foreign technicians, and one great foreign concession, and that distributed the costs of development more visibly than its benefits. Taxes on everyday goods fell on ordinary consumers. Workers in new enterprises labored under strict political controls. Large parts of the countryside saw little change, while Tehran and a few corridors changed rapidly.

This chapter also examines the special case of oil. Petroleum production in Khuzestan was run by the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, renamed the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1935, under a concession first granted in 1901. In 1932 Reza Shah's government cancelled that concession, and in 1933 it accepted a new agreement that extended the company's rights while changing the terms. How a government that proclaimed sovereignty came to sign that agreement, and how historians have judged it, forms the core of Sections 83.5 and 83.6.

Historians reconstruct this period from several kinds of evidence: Iranian laws and budgets; the records and published histories of companies such as the Anglo-Persian Oil Company; engineering and contractor reports; British, American, German, Soviet, and other diplomatic dispatches; memoirs of ministers, engineers, teachers, and students; newspapers and photographs controlled by a state that prized its own image; and, increasingly, studies of prices, wages, and labor. Each source type has a bias. State publications celebrate achievement, company records emphasize company interests, and diplomatic reports reflect the concerns of foreign governments. The reader should ask whose voice is missing, above all the voices of laborers, peasants, and women.

The chapter closes at the edge of the Second World War. The same railway, ports, and refinery that symbolized Reza Shah's achievements made Iran strategically valuable to the Allied powers in 1941. That sequence of events belongs to Chapter 84; this chapter explains the institutions that made it possible and describes their limits.

Historical Context

When Reza Khan came to power in 1921 and became shah in 1925, Iran's modern economic institutions were thin. The Qajar state, examined in Chapter 75, had financed itself through land revenue, customs, and the sale of concessions, and it had borrowed abroad. Tariffs on imports were restricted by nineteenth-century treaties, and foreign powers enjoyed extraterritorial privileges. Modern transport was limited to a few short railways and a small number of carriage roads, and the sea and caravan routes linked the country's regions less closely than they linked each region to neighboring markets. A British- and later Anglo-Iranian-run oil industry in the southwest was by far the largest modern enterprise.

The new government's first tasks were military and fiscal. It needed an army and a revenue system to pay for it, and in the early 1920s it relied on an American financial mission headed by Arthur Millspaugh to reorganize tax collection and accounting. After Millspaugh's mission ended in 1927, the state took over more of that work itself. The legal ending of the capitulations and the recovery of tariff autonomy in 1928 gave the government the freedom to set import duties, and with it a new instrument for raising revenue and protecting local production.

Other conditions framed the decade. The world economic depression after 1929 lowered the value of Iran's exports, disrupted the exchange rate, and encouraged governments everywhere to intervene in trade. Iran's long northern border with the Soviet Union, whose own state-directed industrialization was under way, gave the country a model, a trading partner, and a source of anxiety. Britain, with its stake in Khuzestan, remained the leading foreign power in the south. Germany, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Italy, and other European countries supplied engineers, machinery, and advisers, and those relationships grew steadily in the 1930s.

The country's society was overwhelmingly rural and pastoral. Most Iranians lived in villages or moved with their herds, and landlord-peasant relations, tribal structures, and local markets determined daily life. The cities, led by Tehran, were small by later standards, and bazaar merchants and artisans dominated commerce. Literacy was low. It is this society, not a modern industrial one, that the state's development program tried to reshape, and the difference between plan and reality is one of the chapter's organizing problems.

Division 1 — Finance and Economic Policy

Section 83.1 · Division 1

The Developmental State: Aims, Resources, and Limits

Historians often describe the Pahlavi government of the 1930s as a "developmental state," meaning one that used public authority to organize investment in infrastructure, industry, and education. The label is useful because it draws attention to what the state tried to do, but it carries risks. It can suggest a coordinated plan where the evidence shows improvisation, and it can imply success where outcomes were mixed. This textbook uses the term analytically, and keeps in view the gap between the state's aims and its capacity.

The aims were several and overlapping. Reza Shah and his ministers wanted national strength: a modern army requires roads, rails, and industry. They wanted political integration: a railway or a telegraph line makes it easier to govern provinces that earlier governments reached with difficulty, an aim linked to the campaigns against tribal autonomy examined in Chapter 82. They wanted economic independence from foreign creditors and concession holders, which explains both the early preference for domestic financing and the later emphasis on state banks and monopolies. And they wanted international respect, which explains the importance given to visible symbols such as stations, bridges, factories, and the university campus.

The resources were constrained. Revenue came mainly from customs, taxes on land and consumption, state monopolies, and, increasingly, payments from the oil company. The number of Iranians with technical and administrative training was small. Capital markets were undeveloped, and private investors often preferred land, trade, or foreign currency to industrial ventures. The state therefore became the principal investor, which meant that investment followed the decisions of a small group around the shah. There was no comprehensive national plan in the later sense; the first formal Seven-Year Development Plan belongs to 1949, after Reza Shah’s reign's setting. Projects were chosen, financed, and supervised by individual ministries and, at critical moments, by the shah himself.

The political setting shaped economic decisions. The Majles continued to meet, but it no longer functioned as an independent check, and the press and organized labor were restrained. Officials who succeeded could rise rapidly, and those who displeased the shah could fall suddenly, as the fates of Teymurtash and Davar show. That insecurity affected economic policy as much as political life. Decisions could be taken swiftly, and mistakes were not easily debated.

Reza Shah's own wealth also belongs to this story. During his reign he acquired extensive agricultural lands, especially in the Caspian provinces, and investors and officials understood that his personal interests and state policy were closely connected. Historians disagree about how to weigh this fact against the achievements of the period, but all agree that the line between public and private resources was porous, a point revisited in the Historical Debate below.

The chronological limits of this argument matter. The cabinet did not possess an integrated five- or seven-year investment plan of the sort that emerged after the Second World War. Ministries assembled budgets and construction schedules, and the Shah intervened personally in conspicuous undertakings. Yet their activities increasingly became mutually dependent. Rail construction called for bridges, surveyors, imported rails, and reliable finance; industrial plants needed foreign exchange and protected markets; a national banking institution was expected to supply credit and handle government accounts. These relationships amount to a development strategy, even without a single master plan. To assess it, historians must distinguish stated priorities, actual allocations, physical outputs, and long-term social outcomes.

The background was not institutional emptiness. Constitutional-era reformers had imagined railways and schools before Reza Shah, merchants had experimented with banking, and industrial workshops and oil facilities had operated under the Qajars. What changed after the 1921 coup and the founding of the Pahlavi monarchy was the state's ability to mobilize compulsory revenue, enforce contracts, discipline officials, and implement projects across provincial boundaries. That increase in administrative capacity is demonstrated by completed works, not merely by official speeches. But coercion and concentrated discretion were built into the same system. A merchant excluded from import permits and a farmer paying indirect taxes could perceive a project differently from the official inaugurating it.

A further difficulty concerns the expression 'national independence.' Domestic finance for a railway reduced dependence on foreign lenders, while imported locomotives, steel, contractors, and expertise still connected construction to the international economy. Nationalization of banking powers could coexist with extensive trade through British and Soviet institutions. Industrial protection might encourage local ownership yet raise prices for consumers. For historians, these are not simply contradictions to expose. They are the actual constraints under which a comparatively poor state attempted capital-intensive transformation. The chapter's central method is therefore to follow resources through institutions and into particular households, workplaces, and regions.

Historical Vocabulary
Developmental State

Definition: A scholarly label for a government that takes the leading role in directing investment and institution-building to produce economic growth and national strength, rather than leaving such tasks mainly to private enterprise or foreign firms.

Historical context: The concept was developed by later social scientists, partly to describe twentieth-century East Asian states. Reza Shah's officials did not use it of themselves. Applied to Iran in the 1930s, it describes a state that built railways, banks, factories, and schools under authoritarian direction, with state revenue and foreign expertise.

Why it matters: The label helps compare Iran with other countries that pursued state-led modernization, such as Turkey and the Soviet Union, while reminding students that the Iranian state's capacity was narrower than the concept implies.

Potential misunderstanding: The term does not mean that development was successful, broadly beneficial, or democratic. It names a method and an aim, not a result.

Section 83.2 · Division 1

Financing Development: Consumption Taxes, Customs, and Public Accounts

Behind every project stood a revenue question. The government's most distinctive financial choice was to finance the railway primarily by dedicated taxes on sugar and tea, established from 1925, rather than by a foreign loan. This avoided the political burdens of borrowing, which Iranian nationalists had long associated with the Qajar concessions and foreign creditors. It also meant that a substantial share of construction costs was borne by consumers of two staple goods, including poor households. Consumption taxes of this kind are regressive: they take a larger share of income from those with less income. Contemporary supporters regarded them as a practical way to raise steady funds, and critics saw them as a burden placed on those least able to protest.

Customs also mattered. The restoration of tariff autonomy in 1928, together with the end of the capitulations, allowed the government to raise duties and to use tariffs deliberately to encourage domestic production. In the 1930s it supplemented tariffs with a stricter instrument. The Foreign Trade Monopoly Law of 1931 required that imports and exports be conducted under state-controlled licenses and quotas, and it was intended to defend the currency, regulate scarce foreign exchange, and protect developing industries. In practice, it also gave officials great discretion over merchants. Bazaar traders, long central to the economy, found themselves dependent on permits, and state trading companies and monopolies took over parts of the market. Some larger merchants adapted and prospered, and many smaller ones did not.

The state also acquired control of monetary institutions. The 1931 monetary-unit legislation established the rial as the national currency, with note-issue implementation following in 1932, and the national bank, discussed in Section 83.3, took over note issue from foreign-owned institutions. The Depression had made exchange-rate stability a pressing issue, since Iran earned foreign currency from oil payments and exports but needed it to import machinery, rails, and military equipment.

Oil payments were a growing, though politically sensitive, source of revenue. They were especially valuable as foreign exchange, which could be used to buy imported capital goods. Because royalties depended on the company's production, prices, and accounting, the amount was beyond the Iranian government's control, a weakness that helps explain the confrontation of 1932 described in Sections 83.9–83.11.

The fiscal system as a whole expanded the reach of the state. More taxes were collected in cash, and more officials were needed to measure, record, and enforce. Land registration advanced, and the cadastral and tax apparatus extended into provinces where it had been weak. Historians stress that these changes served the state's aims first. Landed elites and officials often retained favorable treatment, while ordinary villagers and consumers bore burdens that were visible and persistent.

The dedicated sugar-and-tea levy followed parliamentary measures adopted in 1925, and a subsequent railway-construction authorization gave the program a defined fiscal purpose. Such a tax could be collected across a broad population without establishing a sophisticated income-tax apparatus. That was administratively attractive to a state with limited knowledge of households' actual incomes. It was also distributively blunt. A laborer purchasing tea paid into the same national project as a wealthy landowner, but the expense took a larger proportion of the laborer's income. A railway's apparent independence from foreign borrowing must accordingly be evaluated alongside the domestic costs through which independence was purchased.

Public revenue was not a single freely available sum. Funds might be earmarked for construction, military procurement, provincial administration, or servicing commitments. Ministries competed for money, while foreign exchange was scarce because the equipment required for ambitious projects could not be produced domestically. Receipts recorded in rials did not themselves supply sterling or other currencies needed for rails, locomotives, turbines, or machine tools. Customs duties and petroleum payments therefore played a dual role: they supported ordinary expenditure and helped obtain the external purchasing power needed for industrial capital goods. The precise proportion allocated to each object varied sharply over the period and should be calculated from the same year's accounts rather than inferred from an undated statement that 'development spending increased.'

A historian should also distinguish an announced tax from its incidence. Merchants collected or passed on indirect taxes, households adjusted consumption, and officials decided how aggressively to pursue arrears. Monopoly revenues, meanwhile, could combine a tax element with the profits of a state trading operation. The boundary between the public purse and privileged private transactions remained blurred where concession contracts, royal estates, and ministry procurement overlapped. Budget expansion demonstrated improved extraction and accounting; it did not by itself establish that spending was efficient or equitably distributed.

The surviving fiscal evidence includes ministerial budgets, bank reports, customs series, foreign diplomatic assessments, and later statistical reconstructions. Because accounting categories changed and some expenditure moved through special accounts, different scholars' percentages are not always directly comparable. Students examining the railway should ask whether a quoted 'cost' includes imported equipment, administrative expenses, debt, maintenance, or only contracts for the track itself. Similar questions apply to claims about the share of the budget assigned to the army or education. The developmental state's fiscal story is one of growing means, but also of priorities and trade-offs.

Section 83.3 · Division 1

National Banking, Credit, and Monetary Reform

A modern economy needs credit and a stable currency, and Reza Shah's government built institutions to provide them. Bank Melli Iran was founded in the late 1920s as a national bank under state sponsorship, with initial German technical assistance. Over the following years it took over functions previously held by the British-owned Imperial Bank of Persia, including the issue of banknotes and the management of government accounts, so that by the early 1930s Iran had a national central-banking institution in a limited sense. The 1931 currency-unit legislation established the rial, with practical changes continuing in 1932.

A more precise institutional sequence shows how banking authority was transferred. The Majles authorized Bank Melli on 4 May 1927; the bank was organized in 1928 and opened for business on 8 September of that year. It was intended both to mobilize domestic savings and to handle public finance. German bankers and technical staff, including Kurt Lindenblatt, played important roles in its early organization. Their employment demonstrated that an institution could be nationally owned while relying upon foreign experience. Bank Melli's provincial branches, payment arrangements, and government accounts gradually reduced the practical importance of foreign-controlled banking services, though merchants continued to use established foreign and informal credit networks.

The transfer of the Imperial Bank of Persia's banknote privilege was arranged by agreement in May 1930; the first Bank Melli notes appeared in 1932. Monetary legislation in 1931 made the rial the basic unit, while the operational changes were implemented across 1931–1932. Those are separate dates and should not be compressed into the claim that a single law of 1932 created the currency. The process involved compensation to the Imperial Bank, reserve arrangements, the exchange value of silver, and the challenge of obtaining confidence in nationally issued paper money. When the rial displaced the qeran as the formal unit, familiar everyday accounts and older currency terminology did not disappear overnight.

The government's goals extended beyond symbolism. A national branch network could transmit funds between provinces, accumulate deposits, make payments for railway contracts, and advance credit to favored enterprises. The same functions gave authorities knowledge of economic activity and increased the dependence of merchants and contractors on public institutions. Concentrating credit in a government bank could facilitate construction but also crowd out borrowers who lacked influence or acceptable security. Branch numbers and aggregate deposits thus describe institutional growth; they are not complete measures of how accessible banking became to villagers or small firms.

Institutional names must be handled carefully. The Agricultural and Industrial Bank, established by parliamentary action in June 1933, belonged to this era and combined aims of agricultural and industrial credit. It was associated with the separation and expansion of existing agricultural credit facilities. This institution should not be confused with the much later Industrial and Mining Development Bank founded in 1959. The Mortgage Bank developed during the late 1930s and similarly directed specialized finance toward selected sectors. These initiatives were genuine attempts to fill financing gaps, but their first years and limited funds did not create a comprehensive rural credit system.

Across all of these changes the government was not simply replacing foreigners with private Iranian entrepreneurs. It was creating a banking structure unusually close to executive policy, through which public borrowing, monetary administration, and development credit became linked. The independence achieved in note issue could therefore coexist with reduced independence for bank managers and borrowers. That distinction will recur whenever national ownership is treated as equivalent to an autonomous or broadly accessible financial system.

Section 83.4 · Division 1

Trade Monopoly, Protection, and the Managed Economy

A protected economy required instruments beyond ordinary customs duties. The Foreign Trade Monopoly Law of 1931 gave the government extensive authority over the movement of goods across Iran's borders, and subsequent regulations and legislation in 1932 increased discretion over import licenses, quotas, and the exchange obtained from export earnings. Officials could decide which commodities were essential, which enterprises should receive scarce foreign exchange, and which traders might operate in specific branches of commerce. In a country reliant on imported machinery, this was an influential form of industrial policy. It also created a premium on access to ministries rather than on commercial efficiency alone.

Trade controls emerged in the setting of the world depression. Iranian exports of carpets, dried fruits, cotton, and agricultural products faced changing demand and difficult prices, while machinery purchases required payment in external currencies. Policymakers feared that unchecked imports would drain reserves and undermine the rial. Licensing and bilateral clearing arrangements offered one means of controlling the balance. The government also operated trading companies and monopolies dealing in staples or export commodities. The legal forms differed: direct state purchase, an exclusive wholesale concession, and a private importer's license should not all be treated as identical 'nationalization.'

Merchants experienced these measures unequally. Larger firms with capital, political connections, and the ability to manage paperwork could adjust to new permissions; smaller traders confronted uncertainty and lost older networks of cross-border exchange. Foreign companies found that purchases by Iran's growing state sector could be negotiated within bilateral trade relationships, opening opportunities for Germany and other European suppliers. Such commerce did not eliminate Iran's reliance on Britain in oil or on the Soviet Union for northern trade. The resulting economy was diversified in trading partners but not free from external dependence.

For domestic factory owners, the policy could transform a prospective venture into a viable one. A textile manufacturer importing equipment but competing against established foreign cloth required affordable machinery, permission to obtain currency, and some assurance that imports would not immediately undercut the plant. Tariffs, quotas, and government orders could provide that assurance. Consumers, however, might pay more for cloth or sugar, and protected producers might face less pressure to improve quality. The same law thus had different effects depending on whether one examines industrial employment, the treasury, household budgets, or merchant competition.

A key analytical distinction is between economic administration and planning. The Pahlavi regime possessed instruments to direct trade, finance, and investment, but it did not have the nationwide statistical systems or consolidated planning apparatus subsequently associated with the Plan Organization founded after the war. Decisions were often reactive, particularly when prices or foreign-exchange supplies changed. Even when factory output increased, protection did not guarantee long-term technical independence: spare parts, fuel, engineers, and replacements for foreign machinery still had to be obtained abroad. Students should compare policy ambitions with measurable outcomes and then ask who bore the costs of transition.

Division 2 — Transportation and Infrastructure

Section 83.5 · Division 2

Planning and Financing the Trans-Iranian Railway

The idea of a railway across Iran was older than the Pahlavi state. Qajar governments had considered lines, but foreign rivalry, especially Russian and British, had discouraged construction; each power feared that a line built under the other's influence would advance its rival's interests. Under Reza Shah the line became a national project. Work began in the late 1920s, with early contracts involving foreign firms including American and German companies, and was affected by the Depression. The main phase of construction was entrusted from 1933 to Kampsax, a Danish consortium, which used large numbers of Iranian laborers and a smaller corps of foreign engineers and supervisors.

The terrain made the line difficult and costly. The route had to climb the Alborz range between the Caspian lowlands and the plateau, and cross the Zagros between the plateau and Khuzestan. Engineers built a large number of tunnels and bridges, including spiral and curving alignments in the mountains. The Veresk Bridge, completed in 1936 in the Alborz, became a symbol of the project. Sources give widely varying counts of tunnels and bridges, and students should treat the more specific figures with caution unless they cite engineering records.

The southern terminus was Bandar Shahpur on the Persian Gulf, and the northern terminus was Bandar Shah on the Caspian. Between them, the line passed through Tehran, Qom, Arak, and Dezful's region before reaching the Khuzestan plain. Its official opening in August 1938 was marked with ceremony. Later governments extended the network to other cities, including Tabriz and Mashhad, and those later additions mean that the 1938 line should be understood as the foundation of the national system rather than the completed system.

Labor conditions were harsh. Tens of thousands of workers, many of them recruited from rural areas, built the line, and accounts speak of difficult working conditions, accidents, and disease. Systematic figures for deaths and injuries are lacking, and students should be wary of precise numbers. What is clear is that the project required a large, mobile labor force and the cooperation, or coercion, of provincial authorities, and that it introduced many Iranians to wage labor and to engineering as an occupation.

The railway's purposes remain debated. For the state it demonstrated technical and fiscal capacity and strengthened control over distant provinces. For the army it offered mobility. Economically, the early line traversed regions with limited population and trade, and critics noted that it connected the Gulf and the Caspian more than it connected the main agricultural and commercial areas of the country. Its capacity to carry grain, oil, and other goods was lower than its political prestige suggested. Defenders respond that national railway systems commonly need decades to develop traffic, and that the line created the possibility of national integration. The Historical Debate below sets out these positions. Its military significance in 1941 is a topic for Chapter 84, which takes the completed railway as its starting point.

The financing law itself was part of the politics of sovereignty. A railway built largely with domestic taxes was harder for foreign governments to use as security for debt, and officials celebrated the absence of a foreign creditor directing the project. Yet Iran still purchased rails, rolling stock, machinery, and specialized engineering abroad, generally through state contracts with several firms. The earliest construction phase involved different foreign enterprises and exposed administrative weaknesses, changed terms, and delays; it should not be compressed into the later appointment of the Kampsax consortium. Kampsax coordinated the major remaining construction work from 1933 with a network of contractors rather than building every section itself.

The geographical selection was also a policy choice. A north–south route through Tehran and across difficult mountain country created a visible territorial axis and offered a possible military and administrative connection between the Caspian and the Gulf. Some merchants preferred east–west or other routes linking large commercial cities and productive regions more directly. Advocates of the chosen line argued that a railway should create traffic and integration, not merely follow existing trade, and that a central state needed routes independent of traditional corridors. Such disputes cannot be settled by observing the line's later strategic importance alone. A historian must ask what traffic forecasts, budgets, and competing routes were available to policymakers in the 1920s.

The completed line was inaugurated on 26 August 1938, connecting Bandar Shah on the Caspian with Bandar Shahpur on the Persian Gulf over approximately 1,394 kilometers. The network expanded thereafter, but those later additions were not part of the inaugurated trunk. This distinction matters for evaluating claims that the new railway immediately reached Iran's major cities. Important commercial centers, including Tabriz, Mashhad, and Isfahan, remained outside the original corridor. Geography placed real limits on how rapidly a single line could change the national distribution of transport costs and access to markets.

Section 83.6 · Division 2

Mountain Engineering, Kampsax, and the Railway Workforce

The achievement can be understood only by examining the work that lay between the ceremonial terminals. The Alborz north of Tehran and the Zagros to its south required extensive surveying, gradients suitable for locomotives, tunnels, retaining walls, drainage works, and bridges capable of carrying trains over deep valleys. Engineers had to choose alignments that traded shorter distance for gentler grades and greater stability. A train could not simply follow the straightest road across the mountains. Track-bed preparation and bridge foundations depended on geological conditions, rainfall, and the availability of stone and other materials. Mistakes could mean landslides, expensive reconstruction, or dangerous operation long after the first ceremony.

Kampsax's task after 1933 was to coordinate this complicated program, drawing on Scandinavian direction and contractors and specialists of several nationalities. The UNESCO historical record emphasizes a collaborative project involving many separate firms rather than a single foreign builder. Iranian laborers performed much of the physically demanding construction; surveyors, mechanics, drivers, masons, and foremen acquired skills as the undertaking advanced. Those who supplied engineering knowledge were neither a substitute for the workforce nor proof of complete national technical self-sufficiency. The project produced both infrastructure and a new set of occupational experiences.

The Veresk Bridge, completed in 1936 in the Alborz, became one of the best-known symbols of this engineering effort. A long elevated bridge spanning a mountain valley could be photographed, circulated in newspapers, and placed in an official narrative of national revival. The bridge was genuinely difficult to build, but a photograph does not reveal its full cost, the conditions on adjacent tunnel works, or the risks borne by crews. Popular stories about individual engineers and the first train should be checked against contemporary construction records before being taught as fact. The engineering history is impressive without embellishment.

Workers faced exposure to heat and cold, dangerous excavation, injuries, illness, and uncertain access to medical care. Laborers arriving from villages or regions distant from the construction sites could lose established sources of support. Camp supply, disease control, wages, transport, and the employment of subcontractors varied along the line. Later summaries sometimes repeat precise death or injury totals without demonstrating a national accident register; where adequate records are unavailable, numbers should be attributed rather than asserted. The absence of reliable statistics does not erase the hazards: it calls for stronger attention to contractors' records, memoirs, and local reports.

Financing and maintenance complicate evaluation. A final construction bill could be expressed in rials, gold or sterling equivalents, or the value of completed contracts, but not all series include identical expenses. Once opened, the railway still required track repair, locomotives, trained crews, spare parts, bridges inspected after flooding, and fuel. Initial economic returns must therefore be evaluated against ongoing operating expenditure, not only against the act of completion. By distinguishing engineering feasibility from commercial profitability, historians can recognize the project's scale without treating the railway as proof that every development choice was optimal.

The completed railway also had an intellectual and cultural significance that cannot be inferred from engineering metrics. Training in surveying and route design introduced technical concepts into government offices and schools; discussions of gradients, bridge span, rolling stock, and cost appeared in newspapers and political debate. The language of engineering became part of a wider claim that an Iranian government could organize collective projects across difficult geography. Images of bridges and stations helped create a public identity for the state, while the expense of construction encouraged critics to demand explanations of contracts and revenue. Scholarship on the railway emphasizes that it reorganized ideas of space and movement as well as physical routes.

The construction chronology itself was uneven. Work was not equally advanced along northern and southern mountain sections, and a date attached to a company contract does not establish that trains were running through the relevant district. Temporary tracks, imported materials, local supply roads, and housing for engineers were often needed before permanent rail could be laid. A local community might experience a long construction disruption years before receiving passenger or freight service. That gap between construction and operation helps explain why different regions remembered the project differently. The formal completion of the trunk in August 1938 closed an engineering campaign; it did not mark the end of maintenance, training, or the gradual expansion of rail-dependent commerce.

Section 83.7 · Division 2

Roads, Ports, and Motor Transport

The railway was only the best-known transportation project. In the same years the government extended motor roads, improved ports, and promoted truck and bus travel. Roads were more flexible than the railway: they could connect towns and villages to markets and to the provincial garrisons that held the countryside. Their construction relied on corvée-like local labor, financial contributions from provinces, and engineering departments of the state, and standards varied widely. Many roads were passable only in good weather.

Motor vehicles changed trade and the balance among regions. Merchants and carriers adopted trucks where roads allowed, and the caravan trade declined on competing routes, although animal transport persisted in mountain and desert areas. Roads and vehicles also made it easier for the army and gendarmerie to reach rural populations, including pastoral groups whose seasonal routes were increasingly constrained. In this sense, transport improvement served both commerce and the state's coercive power, which is why it fits the centralization narrative of Chapters 81 and 82.

Ports expanded as well, especially on the Gulf, where state-controlled facilities such as Bandar Shahpur complemented oil facilities controlled by the company. The state's control over customs and ports strengthened its fiscal position. The southern ports, however, also tied Iran's trade to the Gulf and to British-dominated shipping, and the new Caspian port tied it to Soviet trade routes. Foreign trade policy in the 1930s, including the trade monopoly and bilateral agreements, repeatedly had to balance these links.

New transport also reshaped cities. Tehran's streets were widened, new avenues cut, and older city walls and gates removed, which made room for motor traffic and symbolized a break with the Qajar past. Such redevelopment displaced residents and shopkeepers and favored new commercial and official districts. Historians reading urban change should consider whose property was demolished and whose land values rose.

Road building required an administrative geography different from that of a trunk railway. A single national rail corridor linked selected terminals; a usable road network needed many connections between market towns, villages, military posts, provincial capitals, and international frontiers. Officials therefore pursued routes with varied objectives. Some helped trucks move food and manufactured goods; others allowed the army to reach districts previously accessible only through seasonal tracks. Their quality ranged from improved mountain roads and bridges to stretches whose usefulness changed with the rains. Counting total kilometers without distinguishing paved, gravel, and seasonal routes would exaggerate the speed of transformation.

Motor transport did not spread evenly. A merchant with capital might purchase trucks and displace caravan carriers on a profitable route, while isolated villages remained dependent on pack animals. Improved roads increased the radius from which perishable goods could reach an urban market, but also made it easier for tax officials, police, and recruiters to exercise power. Competition between buses, trucks, carts, and railways changed the economics of travel and the fortunes of towns bypassed by new routes. Some households benefited from access; others lost transport employment or faced compulsory road labor and requisitioning.

Ports reveal another dimension of the policy. Bandar Shahpur was the planned southern outlet of the trunk railway, while Khorramshahr, Abadan, and other Gulf facilities served different commercial, customs, and petroleum functions. On the Caspian, Bandar Shah connected the railway to a maritime environment strongly influenced by Soviet shipping and trade. These were not interchangeable ports: draught, loading facilities, ownership, petroleum pipelines, and available rail sidings shaped their roles. The state sought customs and logistical capacity, but the equipment and ocean shipping on which trade depended were largely international.

Urban roads were equally political. Street widening in Tehran altered property relations, moved shops, and created new ceremonial spaces that displayed government offices and modern vehicles. It sometimes improved circulation and sanitation while erasing older markets or neighborhoods. In evaluating these changes, the crucial question is not whether 'roads were modern,' but which journeys became cheaper, whose buildings were removed, and whose mobility remained constrained. Such measures belonged at once to the material developmental program and to the cultural and coercive transformation explored in Chapter 82.

Section 83.8 · Division 2

Railway Geography, Economic Impact, and Competing Alternatives

The railway's map demonstrates why infrastructure does not distribute benefits uniformly. Its northern terminal reached the Caspian shore, but that did not mean the line automatically connected to every Caspian trading center. Its southern approach crossed sparsely populated stretches before reaching the Gulf. The line passed Tehran and important nodes such as Qom and Arak, yet missed established centers of textile, agricultural, and bazaar activity. Merchants could use feeder roads to reach a station, but the price of loading, waiting, reloading, and hauling goods by truck might determine whether rail carriage was actually economical. A line drawn across a national map can overstate integration if one ignores its local connections.

Commercial traffic depended on the relationship between rates, distance, the size of shipments, and competing methods of transport. Large standardized loads might favor rail, while short trips or dispersed crops often remained better suited to trucks or animal caravans. In the first years, limited rolling stock and inexperienced operating personnel also constrained the service. The railway might therefore have considerable military value while carrying less civilian freight than planners had anticipated. Supporters regarded such a gap as normal for a new national system; critics read it as evidence that the chosen route favored prestige and strategic control over immediate economic needs.

The domestic-tax financing question also belongs in any cost-benefit assessment. The expenditure generated construction wages, demand for local supplies, and trained technical labor. At the same time, taxing common commodities reduced households' disposable income and could depress consumption elsewhere. A complete account cannot treat wages paid to builders as a net benefit while forgetting that much of their financing came from other Iranians. Nor should it assume that an asset's initial commercial return exhausts its public value: interregional administration, emergency transport, and national engineering capacity were outcomes the state deliberately sought.

International comparison has limits. States in Europe and Asia used railways to consolidate territory, and rulers presented stations and bridges as symbols of order. Iran's difficulties were magnified by mountains, limited capital, and foreign rivalry that had blocked earlier schemes. The government chose to solve one of those difficulties—outside financial leverage—by taxing consumers rather than borrowing abroad. Its foreign engineers demonstrate the cost of avoiding financial dependence without possessing a large domestic technical corps. Thus the same project can be understood simultaneously as a significant public work, a heavy tax burden, and an imperfect answer to national transport needs.

The most productive historical debate asks about alternatives available at the time. Could a less costly road program have served more agricultural regions? Would rail links from ports to major cities have generated commercial traffic faster? Would foreign borrowing have provided capital at an unacceptable sovereignty cost? Contemporaries did not know that the line would later become critical in the Second World War; its wartime uses belong to Chapter 84 and cannot be substituted for evidence of planners' original intentions. Modern judgments should acknowledge unforeseen consequences without reading them backward into the 1920s.

The ultimate test must remain chronological. Freight volume during the first operating years, estimates of maintenance expenditure, and contemporary complaints about rates are better evidence of early commercial performance than the line's much later heritage status. Its international recognition today confirms the scale and technical importance of the engineering achievement, not the profitability of every original routing decision. Separating those questions makes both praise and criticism more rigorous.

Historical Debate

Foundation of Modern Iran or Prestige Project of an Authoritarian State?

The question: Did Reza Shah's developmental program, symbolized by the railway, lay the foundations of modern economic and social institutions in Iran, or was it mainly a set of costly prestige projects whose benefits were limited and unequally shared?

Interpretation A — Foundation: Some historians emphasize that the program created national institutions, including the railway, a national bank, a university, and a larger industrial and administrative base, which later governments built upon. They point out that before 1921 these institutions barely existed, and that the achievements were made with limited resources and without foreign loans.
Interpretation B — Prestige and coercion: Others stress that the benefits were narrow and the costs, borne by consumers, workers, peasants, and pastoral groups, were heavy. They argue that projects were chosen for political and symbolic reasons, that economic returns were low, and that authoritarian rule suppressed the debate and the organizations that might have improved outcomes.
Interpretation C — Constrained development: A third position holds that the program must be judged against the constraints of the time: weak revenues, limited skills, foreign dominance in oil, and the Depression. On this view, the state achieved real but partial results, and both celebratory and condemnatory accounts overlook the contingency of choices and the possibility of other paths.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on budgets, contractor and engineering reports, freight and passenger statistics, company records, diplomatic dispatches, memoirs, and studies of prices and wages. Much of this evidence originates from the state, foreign firms, or foreign governments, and comparable data on rural incomes and living standards are scarce.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and weighting. Historians agree that the railway was built and that taxes on sugar and tea helped pay for it. They differ on which indicators matter most, on how to compare Iran with other states, and on the importance of political context.

Why it matters: How the period is judged affects how later developmental policies, such as those of Chapters 88 and 90, are understood, and whether authoritarian modernization is treated as a necessary stage, an avoidable detour, or a mixed legacy.

Section 83.9 · Division 3

The D’Arcy Concession and the Political Economy of Iranian Oil

The oil industry stood at the center of Iran's economic relations with the outside world. The D'Arcy concession of 1901, examined in Chapter 75, gave a British entrepreneur rights over a large area of the country in exchange for payments and a share of profits. Oil was discovered at Masjed Soleyman in 1908, the Anglo-Persian Oil Company was formed in 1909, and the Abadan refinery began operating in 1912. In 1914, the British government became the company's majority shareholder, linking the enterprise directly to British naval and imperial policy. By the 1920s the company's operations in Khuzestan were large, and its payments to Iran were important but disputed.

Iranian officials had long complained about the terms. They questioned how the company calculated profits, the limited share Iran received, the vast area covered by the concession, and the lack of Iranian oversight. The Depression sharpened the dispute: company profits and thus Iranian royalties fell in 1931, at the same time as the government needed funds. In November 1932 Reza Shah's government announced the cancellation of the D'Arcy concession. The step was dramatic, not least because the shah had been closely associated with the earlier arrangements; it was followed by the fall of Teymurtash, who had managed the file, though historians continue to debate how directly the two events were connected.

The original D'Arcy concession, granted in 1901, was one of the most consequential arrangements inherited by the Pahlavi government. It offered exploration rights over a vast part of Iran and gave the Iranian side a share of reported annual net profits rather than direct operational control. Once oil was found at Masjed Soleyman in May 1908, exploration became an industrial system: wells, storage, pipelines, refinery units, loading facilities, international sales, and accounting structures. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company was incorporated in 1909, and the Abadan refinery began operations in 1912. British government ownership acquired in 1914 connected what had begun as a commercial concession to the security interests of the Royal Navy.

The political significance of the contract lay partly in what its figures could not settle. Iran's share depended on the meaning of 'net profits,' a term affected by how the company organized its subsidiaries, valued transactions, deducted costs, and distributed earnings. Iranian officials contested whether the calculation covered the full value of an integrated international enterprise. Company managers, meanwhile, argued that large investments and geological risks entitled them to contractual protection. The parties thus disagreed not only over the desired distribution but also over which accounts and legal interpretations could establish a fair distribution in the first place.

Oil also posed a geographic problem. The producing and refining centers of Khuzestan were connected to overseas markets by pipeline and shipping, but were weakly linked to many Iranian manufacturing districts. Royalty receipts could finance government projects in Tehran or elsewhere without automatically raising household incomes in the districts where extraction occurred. Local communities encountered the company through employment, land use, housing, transport, and access to water, and they experienced the Iranian government's attempts to increase its authority in the southwest at the same time. Such conditions created overlapping company, state, and regional interests.

During the 1920s the government sought clearer payments and greater access to the company accounts, while retaining reliance on a foreign enterprise with technical, financial, and marketing capabilities it could not readily replace. The unresolved 1920 oil settlement and other disputes inherited from the war shaped this relationship. When world prices and company distributions deteriorated during the Depression, the underlying disagreement became acute. The state had increasingly ambitious spending commitments but no dependable mechanism for converting those ambitions into a larger share of petroleum value. The confrontation of 1932 must be understood against that longer history, not as an isolated outburst by Reza Shah.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession and Royalty

Definition: A concession is a grant by a government to a person or company of exclusive rights to explore or exploit resources or operate a service within a defined area and period. A royalty is a payment made to the grantor, commonly calculated from the quantity or value of what is produced or from profits.

Historical context: Iran's nineteenth- and early twentieth-century governments granted concessions to foreign entrepreneurs to raise revenue and obtain services they could not provide themselves. The D'Arcy concession and its 1933 successor are the most consequential examples.

Why it matters: The terms of the concession and the method of royalty calculation determined how much of the oil industry's value reached the Iranian treasury and how much control Iranians had over their own resources.

Potential misunderstanding: A concession is not a sale of territory, and the concession holder did not become the sovereign. However, in practice its extensive operational control gave it an influence in the southwest that the Iranian government could not easily match.

Section 83.10 · Division 3

Cancellation, the League of Nations, and the 1933 Negotiations

Britain rejected the cancellation as a unilateral breach and took the case to the Council of the League of Nations in December 1932. Iran, which was a member of the League, contested the Council's competence. In the following months, the League mediated, but the decisive step was direct negotiation. Sir John Cadman of the company met Reza Shah in Tehran in the spring of 1933 and the two sides reached a new agreement, signed in April and approved by the Majles in May.

Why did a strongly nationalist government accept these terms? Historians identify several pressures: Britain's political and legal leverage, the League's procedures, the Depression and Iran's dependence on oil money, the shah's unwillingness to risk an open confrontation with Britain, and the practical fact that Iran lacked the technology, marketing, and tanker fleet to run the industry. Whether Iran might have secured better terms remains a matter of judgment, and the evidence permits more than one reading. The affair showed both the state's willingness to assert itself and the limits of its leverage, a lesson later Iranian politicians drew on in the very different circumstances of Chapter 86.

The crisis intensified when the royalty due for 1931 appeared exceptionally low to Iranian decision-makers, despite large company operations. The government had long demanded renegotiation and greater certainty, and the Depression made its revenue needs more urgent. In November 1932 the Iranian authorities announced cancellation of the 1901 concession. Cancellation expressed the government's view that the concession had become intolerable; it did not by itself give Iran operational control of the wells, refinery, tanker network, or overseas sales. The company rejected the validity of the step, and Britain took the issue to the League of Nations in December 1932.

The League proceedings placed a commercial concession within the contested field of international law and diplomacy. Iran maintained that the disagreement concerned rights between the government and company and resisted British efforts to present it as an international claim. Britain asserted that protection of the company's rights justified diplomatic intervention. The League sought to prevent escalation and favored further negotiations. The dispute demonstrated how a sovereign state might formally control its natural resources while facing difficult economic and diplomatic consequences when challenging a contract held by a powerful foreign-backed enterprise.

Personal politics compounded the crisis. Abdolhossein Teymurtash, a leading architect of early Pahlavi state-building and a central intermediary in petroleum negotiations, fell from royal favor in 1932 and later died in custody. Historians disagree about the precise connection between his fall, the oil negotiations, and Reza Shah's increasingly arbitrary treatment of ministers. It is safer to describe the timing and the mechanisms of political power than to present an undocumented private conversation as the decisive cause. The weakening of trusted negotiators may itself have affected the government's capacity to sustain a coordinated bargaining strategy.

Sir John Cadman, chairman of APOC, met Reza Shah in Tehran on 24 April 1933. Iranian ministers including Hasan Taqizadeh, Mohammad Ali Foroughi, and Ali Akbar Davar then participated in working out the agreement. It was signed on 29 April, approved by the Majles on 28 May, and granted royal assent on 29 May 1933. These stages matter: negotiations were not a two-person confrontation whose legal result existed before ministerial drafting and parliamentary action. Yet the rapid passage through a compliant parliament also did not amount to unconstrained legislative bargaining.

The question of alternatives remains difficult. Prolonging cancellation risked reduced payments and diplomatic escalation; accepting new terms could secure immediate fiscal relief while surrendering future bargaining opportunities. Iran had petroleum in the ground but lacked a complete independent organization for producing, transporting, refining, insuring, and selling it abroad. APOC had those capabilities and strong diplomatic support. The resulting balance of power explains why a government publicly committed to sovereignty might sign a long-duration agreement it expected to be politically defensible, and why opponents later regarded the same bargain as deeply disappointing.

Section 83.11 · Division 3

The 1933 Oil Agreement: Payments, Territory, and Sovereignty

The terms are commonly summarized as follows. The concession area was reduced to a far smaller zone, a point supporters of the agreement emphasized. The concession's term was extended, to 1993. The royalty was fixed at a stated sum per ton of oil, with minimum annual payments and a share in certain distributions to shareholders. Iran gained a clearer formula but continued to rely on company accounting. The agreement also contained restraints on future cancellation. Because later nationalist critics focused on the extended duration and the continued limited oversight, it is the most criticized part of the arrangement; defenders replied that Iran had won a smaller area, guaranteed payments, and a settlement that ended an international dispute.

The text of the 1933 concession should be read as a set of interlocking provisions, not as a single headline figure. The company undertook to surrender much of the original concession territory: a claimed area of approximately 480,000 square miles was to be reduced to at most 100,000 square miles by the end of 1938. This was a substantial geographic contraction, but the company could choose the areas it retained within contractual limits. The reduction therefore did not produce an equivalent reduction in access to known productive fields. A map of the surrendered territory and the retained oil-bearing zone is more informative than an area percentage alone.

The revised concession was to run until 31 December 1993, extending the end date of the original sixty-year grant by approximately thirty-two years. Supporters argued that a stable term was necessary to induce investment in reservoirs, pipelines, and refinery facilities. Critics pointed out that the longer duration restricted the bargaining power of future governments. Those two claims could both be sincerely held. The central historical issue is how much value Iran received in exchange for extending a monopoly over future petroleum development and for accepting contractual restraints on cancellation.

The principal payment formula was four gold shillings per metric ton of oil sold in Iran or exported, supplemented by participation in specified company distributions. The additional provision concerned twenty percent of distributions to ordinary shareholders or transfers to reserves above a threshold of £671,250; it was not a direct twenty-percent ownership share of worldwide production or a straightforward twenty-percent tax on all reported net profits. The agreement guaranteed Iran at least £750,000 annually and included a £1 million settlement of outstanding claims. Separate provisions concerned payments in lieu of taxes, which later critics considered modest. The differences between a per-ton royalty, a dividend-linked payment, and a general profit share are indispensable to understanding the controversy.

The agreement also offered certain advantages to Iranian consumers and officials. It provided discounts on petroleum sold within Iran, including a larger discount for government purchases, and acknowledged a government delegate with access to information defined by the agreement. Provisions called for increasing the employment and training of qualified Iranian personnel and gradually replacing foreign staff where practicable. These clauses did not guarantee equal access to high technical positions or independent scrutiny of every affiliated company account. Their practical value depended upon implementation, information flows, and who interpreted obligations when disagreements arose.

Most historical assessments recognize improved near-term fiscal terms compared with the D'Arcy concession, while sharply criticizing the prolonged duration, restrictions on cancellation, tax limitations, and inadequate access to company profits. A historian should avoid treating later oil nationalism as proof that the 1933 negotiators knew all its eventual consequences. At the same time, the complaints about duration and fiscal control were visible within the agreement itself and were not invented retrospectively. This document is therefore particularly useful for teaching the difference between a contract's apparent numerical gains and the longer-term politics of bargaining power.

A final method of comparing the contracts is to examine their risks. Under the original arrangement Iran's principal profit-linked revenue could fall if the company's accounting showed reduced net profits. Under the newer arrangement, the volume royalty offered some protection against changes in reported profit but exposed the state to production fluctuations, while the distribution-based supplement still depended on company policy. The minimum payment created a lower bound, not a promise that receipts would rise with oil prices. A government expecting petroleum revenue to finance long-lived schools or railways thus faced uncertainty under either arrangement. The choice of a royalty formula helped allocate risk between state and company; it did not remove risk from the Iranian budget.

Revenue history should likewise distinguish current payments from ultimate ownership. A concession holder might owe taxes, royalties, training obligations, and reporting duties while retaining extensive authority over investment and production. The Iranian government's delegate gained certain defined informational rights, but the company continued to manage key commercial decisions. Students should read those clauses alongside the provisions for arbitration and cancellation to identify the legal mechanisms available when disagreements arose. This approach is more revealing than labeling the concession simply a national victory or defeat, because it shows the concrete areas in which sovereignty was asserted and the areas in which it remained constrained.

Historical Debate 2 — The 1933 Concession

Did the Agreement Advance Economic Sovereignty or Extend Foreign Control?

Interpretation A — Negotiated improvement: The smaller concession area, clearer volume-based royalties, minimum guarantee, claims settlement, and training obligations improved Iran’s immediate position compared with 1901. The country lacked independent facilities and access to international markets, and prolonged litigation could have jeopardized essential revenue.
Interpretation B — Lost opportunity: Extending rights until 1993, limiting cancellation, and tying some payments to company decisions constrained future governments. The state traded durable leverage for short-term relief, despite its proclaimed commitment to sovereignty.
Interpretation C — A conditional bargain: The same agreement could improve immediate revenue and remain structurally unequal; the significance of the terms depended on enforcement, accounting, and changing oil prices.

Section 83.12 · Division 3

Abadan and the Oil Enclave: Workers, Housing, and Politics

The oil region in Khuzestan was an enclave economy, meaning that its main enterprise was oriented to foreign markets and linked weakly to the rest of Iran's economy. The company built pipelines, a refinery, housing, hospitals, schools, and shops. Abadan grew from a small settlement into a major industrial town, and Masjed Soleyman and other fields drew workers from across the south and from other regions. The company hired Iranians, as well as British and Indian staff, and training programs produced a skilled Iranian workforce over time.

The enclave was also sharply divided. British staff lived in well-equipped residential areas with services unavailable to most Iranian employees, who lived in more crowded accommodations, and the gap in pay, housing, and promotion became a lasting grievance. Contract and casual workers lived in even poorer conditions. Contemporary and later accounts differ on how serious the conditions were and how much they improved over time, and company records, state records, and labor testimony do not always agree.

Labor protest occurred. A strike of Abadan oil workers in 1929 is the best-known early example, and it was suppressed; later legislation, such as the 1931 law against communist and collectivist activity, constrained union organization throughout the country. Historians note that the combination of company power, state control, and the enclave's physical separation made independent labor organization difficult, but that grievances persisted and found expression after 1941 in the organizing described in Chapter 84 and Chapter 85.

The enclave's relationship with the central state was complex. The Iranian state collected payments and regulated aspects of the company's work, and in the 1930s it also extended its administration into Khuzestan, including the settlement of Arab and Bakhtiari populations. The province's pastoral and tribal communities, whose lands lay partly in the oil region, experienced the combined weight of company operations and government policy. Because most of the oil industry's output was exported, its direct effects on the rest of the economy were limited to payments, some employment, and local purchasing. This is the structural reason why oil income, though important, did not by itself transform the Iranian economy, a pattern that reappears in the oil boom analyzed in Chapter 90.

Abadan's expansion created not merely a factory town but a distinctive hierarchy of residence, work, and authority. Company housing, medical care, recreation grounds, and schools were distributed unevenly among European, Indian, and Iranian personnel. Categories of employment shaped promotion opportunities, wage levels, and access to amenities. Some skilled Iranian employees gained training and stable earnings; many casual or contract workers lived under difficult conditions, while foreign managers retained disproportionate control of technical and administrative decisions. The social landscape could therefore appear simultaneously as evidence of industrial progress and as a highly visible system of exclusion.

The refinery depended on an urban and regional labor market. Workers arrived from different parts of Iran and from nearby countries, bringing varied languages, religions, occupations, and household circumstances. Recruitment created demand for housing, food, transport, and small businesses beyond the company's formal installations. Families depended on wages but also on informal trade and the labor of women and children. Where a worker was dismissed or injured, the effects extended beyond the individual; a household could lose accommodation and access to company services. The company town therefore deserves analysis as a social formation as much as an engineering plant.

The May 1929 strike showed that workers were political actors rather than passive recipients of development. Demands included wages, employment conditions, and the right to organize, but collective action also drew upon the wider urban community. Stephanie Cronin's detailed study describes the participation of workers' wives in demonstrations, including actions outside the refinery gates after arrests. The government suppressed the strike and cooperated with the company's restoration of operations. The episode exposed the contradictions of a nationalist state that celebrated modern Iranian workers while policing their independent organizations.

The labor relationship extended into the 1933 concession, which called for progressive Iranianization of technical and commercial staff according to stated qualifications. The critical questions are who defined qualification, how rapidly skills were transferred, and whether employees had effective channels to challenge discrimination. Schools and apprenticeships provided genuine training, but promotion to senior posts remained constrained. It would be inaccurate to describe Abadan as wholly separate from Iran: company purchases, workers' remittances, customs, petroleum products, and government authority linked it to the country. The term 'enclave' instead captures how unusually large portions of the production system remained controlled from within a company network oriented to foreign markets.

The wider Khuzestan setting also matters. The oilfields, refinery, shipping installations, and associated roads operated in a province inhabited by Arab communities, Bakhtiari groups, farmers, merchants, and townspeople whose interests did not coincide. Oil development intersected with land claims and the Pahlavi government's provincial centralization policies. A comparison between company engineering reports and oral histories from workers and residents can reveal quite different definitions of progress. The permanent significance of the oil region lay in this combination of an industrial workforce, foreign corporate authority, national revenue, and local claims that no single government contract could settle.

Division 4 — Industry and Education

Section 83.13 · Division 4

State Industry and Import-Substitution Manufacturing

Modern factory industry before 1925 was small. In the 1930s, the state invested in new enterprises, including textile mills at Isfahan, Mazandaran, and Tehran; sugar refineries; cement, glass, match, and tobacco works; and other plants. Some were owned directly by the state, others were financed through state-connected banks, and a number were set up by private entrepreneurs under protection. The aim was import substitution: producing goods domestically that Iran had previously imported, so as to save foreign exchange and build national capacity.

Protection made this possible. Tariffs, trade licensing, and state monopolies on goods such as sugar, tea, and tobacco shielded domestic producers from foreign competition and gave the state revenue. The system favored a small number of large enterprises and well-connected investors. Consumers paid higher prices for protected goods, and merchants who had imported the same products lost business.

The new plants depended on foreign skills. Firms and technicians from Germany, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Italy, the Soviet Union, and other countries designed factories, supplied machinery, and trained workers. This created a paradox: a government that sought independence relied intensively on foreign expertise, and the dependence grew as the factory program expanded. Choosing among suppliers was also a diplomatic act, since each foreign relationship carried political implications. By the late 1930s, Germany's commercial position had grown considerably, a development relevant to the diplomatic crisis of 1941 described in Chapter 84.

The scale should be kept in proportion. Factory employment grew from a very low base to a few tens of thousands, a small fraction of the population. Much production remained in workshops and in the households of artisans, and carpet weaving, a large export industry, remained dispersed. Some state plants were costly, operated below capacity, or suffered from management problems, and evaluations of their economic efficiency vary. At the same time, they trained a generation of Iranian managers, technicians, and workers and created industrial centers around which later growth took place.

The growth of factory industry did not follow a single ownership formula. Some plants belonged directly to ministries, some were financed through government-controlled banks, and others were private enterprises sheltered by tariffs, procurement, or privileged access to foreign exchange. A useful example is the Shahi textile mill in Mazandaran, established in the early 1930s with investment associated with Reza Shah and Bank Melli. Its financing shows how royal, state, and commercial interests could converge in a way that was productive yet difficult to scrutinize independently. Other enterprises in Isfahan, Tehran, and provincial centers operated under different combinations of public and private capital.

Industrial policy favored manufactures that could supply the domestic market or meet government orders. Textile plants reduced dependence on certain imported cloths, while cement supported construction and sugar processing served a large market. State tobacco and other monopolies were expected to collect revenue as well as influence supply. The import-substitution rationale was not inherently irrational: a domestic enterprise might acquire skills and equipment that an unconstrained importer would never invest in. But productive capacity could be expensive to establish in a country where skilled workers, spare parts, electricity, and long-term finance were scarce.

Growth statistics require special care. Official enumerations might count a machine workshop, spinning mill, or food-processing plant as 'industrial,' despite enormous differences in workforce, capital, and output. Increases in the number of factories do not automatically establish that domestic value added grew at the same rate, or that consumer prices fell. Industrial employment was concentrated in a few cities, while the vast majority of households continued to live from agriculture, crafts, and small trade. Comparisons are strongest when they use the same definitions across years and identify whether they measure plant openings, operating output, employment, or capital invested.

The state's involvement in the labor process was also direct. In a protected enterprise, the government could simultaneously be employer, regulator, financier, and the police authority that suppressed strikes. Laborers gained cash incomes and new occupational skills; they also experienced strict discipline, workplace injuries, and limited independent representation. The industrial program transformed some urban and provincial locations, yet it did not amount to nationwide industrialization. Its historical importance lies in creating the first larger institutional and technical bases from which later industrial development proceeded.

Section 83.14 · Division 4

Foreign Technicians, Technology Transfer, and Industrial Capacity

Foreign technicians were indispensable to much of the new industrial economy. Iranian ministries purchased machines from firms in Germany, Czechoslovakia, Britain, Italy, and elsewhere, and hired engineers, chemists, machinists, railway specialists, architects, and industrial managers to install and operate them. Such partnerships were practical responses to shortages of domestic expertise. They were also a persistent test of the government's language of independence. State ownership of a factory was incomplete evidence of national technical control if essential repairs, designs, and components could be obtained only from outside suppliers.

Training arrangements took several forms. A foreign engineer might supervise an Iranian assistant on a shop floor; a government student could study at a European technical institute and return to a ministry; a German-Iranian vocational school could combine classroom instruction with apprenticeships. The result was a growing domestic technical stratum. But a handful of engineers could not instantly replace the knowledge embodied in an entire construction industry. The pace of transfer depended on language, equipment, teachers, wages, promotion opportunities, and whether employers regarded Iranian staff as future decision-makers or permanent auxiliaries.

A factory's viability also depended on power, transport, and reliable supplies. Textiles needed cotton or wool, water and electricity; cement needed fuel and steady construction demand; sugar refining needed a market and access to inputs. The railway made some movement easier but did not provide uniform national distribution. Imported machines could lie idle when parts were unavailable or a ministry delayed purchasing materials. A statistical inventory of installed equipment should therefore be compared with records of actual operation and output. The developmental state's ability to purchase capacity was often greater than its ability to use that capacity consistently.

The international political setting shaped procurement. Bilateral trade and clearing arrangements could channel Iranian exports toward partners able to provide machinery without requiring scarce convertible currency. German firms became increasingly prominent as technical and commercial suppliers during the 1930s, while Britain retained the dominant position in the petroleum enterprise. The Soviet Union influenced northern commercial routes. This plurality of outside relationships allowed Tehran some bargaining room without eliminating dependence on the international economy. None of these trade connections by itself proves that Iran had adopted the foreign power's ideology or had become its military partner; that distinction becomes especially important in Chapter 84.

Economic historians also ask why the state preferred particular enterprises. National security, import substitution, expected revenue, the wishes of ministers, and royal prestige all played roles. Without independent press scrutiny and effective parliamentary challenge, the selection of projects could not be tested publicly in a sustained way. Some initiatives succeeded in establishing enduring facilities and occupational skills; others were expensive, inefficient, or exposed to political favoritism. The proper comparison is not with an imaginary world of effortless private investment, but with plausible alternatives under the same constraints of capital, knowledge, and foreign exchange.

Section 83.15 · Division 4

Modern Schooling and the University of Tehran

Education was both an instrument of development and an arena of ideology. The nineteenth-century foundations, including Dar al-Funun (Chapter 73) and the new schools and press of the constitutional era (Chapter 77), had created a small modern educational sector, and the Pahlavi state greatly enlarged it. The Ministry of Education centralized administration, set a national curriculum, issued standardized textbooks, and made Persian the language of instruction, a policy that fit the nationalist program described in Chapter 82. Enrolment grew significantly from a low starting point, and girls' schooling expanded, but rural areas, pastoral groups, and many minority communities remained poorly served. Literacy remained low by the end of the period.

The state also reduced the influence of competing educational providers. Traditional religious schools and local maktabs lost ground to state schools, and foreign and minority schools came under tighter regulation and, by the end of the 1930s, were largely restricted or taken over. This change expanded state control of education, though it also removed institutions that had educated many students. Interpretations of this policy depend on whether it is viewed mainly as national integration or mainly as a loss of pluralism, and both elements were present.

The central institutional achievement was the University of Tehran. Established by law in 1934 and organized around existing professional colleges such as medicine, law, and the teachers' college, it was housed on a new campus designed with the advice of the French architect André Godard. The university gave the state a national institution for training physicians, lawyers, engineers, teachers, and officials. In 1936 women were admitted, an important step given the limited opportunities for women's higher education elsewhere in the region. Other specialist institutions, including technical and agricultural schools, supplemented the university.

The social effects were significant but selective. Education opened careers in the bureaucracy and professions to families that could afford schooling and that lived in or near cities. For many families, it became a route to status, and the growing group of graduates would later supply political leaders, critics, and activists on all sides. At the same time, the state's tight control of curricula and political expression meant that students were expected to serve the state, not question it. Tensions between educational expansion and political limits emerged forcefully after 1941.

The educational figures reveal the scale and limits of change. Scholarship records an increase in primary schools from several hundred early in the Pahlavi era to more than two thousand by 1941, and growth in secondary institutions from a few dozen to several hundred. Such numbers demonstrate a striking expansion from a small base. They do not mean that most children attended school. Rural distance, household labor requirements, inadequate buildings, shortages of teachers, and local language barriers continued to restrict participation. Families who lived near urban schools and could forgo their children's work were better placed to benefit.

Reza Shah's educational project combined professional training with national integration. Textbooks and curricula emphasized standardized Persian, Iranian history, citizenship, and forms of public conduct that fit the state's broader cultural program. For many pupils, literacy and numeracy opened opportunities previously limited to the sons of established notables. Girls' schooling expanded, and women's access to higher education began to change during the 1930s. But government supervision also narrowed the independence of teachers, religious schools, foreign institutions, and student organizations. Expansion of educational opportunity and reduction of educational pluralism occurred together.

The University of Tehran illustrates this dual character. Its foundation was authorized by legislation in 1934 and it was inaugurated on 4 February 1935. The original institutional organization joined existing colleges and professional traditions with new faculties. Medicine, law, sciences, letters, engineering, and theology were brought within a university framework supervised by the Ministry of Education. The university's capacity to offer research and specialist training depended heavily on returning graduates and a smaller number of foreign teachers. Although official language invoked institutional independence, political oversight and the professional obligations expected of graduates limited the freedom of scholarly life.

The government's policy toward foreign schools should also be described chronologically rather than as their uniform disappearance. Earlier regulations required greater oversight of languages and curricula. In 1939 the government adopted measures to close or acquire remaining foreign schools; specific establishments, including Isfahan's Stewart Memorial College, passed into state hands during 1940. Minority institutions were not all identical to foreign missionary schools, and the impact varied by community. National oversight could make certification and curricula more uniform while eliminating schools that had served particular populations for decades.

The expansion of adult education reveals a related measurement problem. The government promoted adult literacy classes in the later 1930s, but figures for enrollment can exceed those for regular attendance or completion. Reports of increased spending similarly need to be interpreted alongside the number of children reached, teachers trained, and geographic distribution of institutions. The strongest assessment recognizes the durability of the university and school network without imagining that these institutions had already produced a literate or educationally equal society by the eve of war.

Section 83.16 · Division 4

Students Abroad, Technical Training, and the New Professions

A law of 1928 authorized the government to send a number of students each year to study abroad at state expense, primarily in Europe. Many went to France, and others to Germany, Belgium, and elsewhere, and they studied engineering, medicine, law, sciences, and military subjects. On their return, most were employed by the state as officials, teachers, and technical specialists. The program was an investment in expertise that Iran could not yet produce at home, and it remained one of the principal channels of foreign intellectual influence.

The returning students formed part of a wider new professional class that included engineers, physicians, teachers, civil servants, military officers, and managers. These groups had diverse outlooks. Some were committed to the state's nationalist and modernizing program, some admired Soviet or European social democratic ideas, and some were critical of authoritarian rule. The state kept watch. The arrest of a group of young intellectuals in 1937, associated with a Marxist circle, showed the limits of political tolerance, and the earlier law against collectivist activity had made such circles illegal.

Technical training also developed at lower levels. Vocational and craft schools, railway and port training, army technical programs, and apprenticeship in state factories produced skilled workers and foremen. These workers were often the first to enter new occupations such as locomotive driver, telegraph operator, machinist, and refinery technician. Their experience helped create an urban working population with different skills and expectations from agricultural laborers or bazaar artisans, a change that shaped political life in the next decade.

Evidence for this subject includes official education statistics, the records of the overseas student program, memoirs of returnees, and educational publications. Students should treat enrolment figures cautiously, since reporting varied, and should note that most evidence concerns male, urban, and elite experiences; the voices of rural children, tribal families, and women outside the urban middle class are comparatively sparse.

The 1928 law concerning students sent to Europe required a government scholarship program of at least one hundred students annually. Specialist studies identify roughly 640 students sent abroad between 1928 and 1933. Their destinations and disciplines varied, but medicine, engineering, science, law, teacher training, and administrative expertise figured prominently. These figures should be used with care: the legal annual target is not identical to the number departing in every calendar year, and the cited total concerns a defined period rather than the entire reign. Earlier Qajar and constitutional governments had already sent selected students overseas; the Pahlavi measure expanded and regularized an existing practice.

The students' experiences were not uniform. A young man preparing for engineering in France faced different language and training demands from a physician enrolled in Germany or a legal scholar comparing civil codes. Their family backgrounds often afforded educational advantages unavailable to rural and poor households. Many graduates returned to positions in ministries, hospitals, schools, and public works. Yet an overseas qualification did not necessarily guarantee career security: a centralized administration could promote technically skilled officials rapidly and dismiss them for political reasons. The program helped create modern professions while leaving their members dependent on state employment.

International study also exposed students to political movements and ideas beyond the government's preferred curriculum. Some admired administrative efficiency and European science; others encountered socialism, liberal constitutionalism, student unions, and critiques of authoritarianism. Contacts abroad could produce loyal state technicians or committed critics. The government's growing concern with oppositional circles, exemplified by the arrests associated with Taqi Arani in 1937, showed how technical modernization could create educated people whose political independence the regime feared. The emergence of post-1941 intellectual and party organizations cannot be understood without this history of state-sponsored education.

Technical learning also occurred inside Iran. Railway apprenticeships, industrial workshops, military training, and vocational schools produced drivers, mechanics, surveyors, bookkeepers, and skilled operators. This lower tier of expertise was indispensable; no number of highly trained engineers could operate the growing number of factories without competent workers and foremen. Many trainees combined learning with paid labor under difficult conditions. Such occupations opened routes to urban mobility but also created a workforce exposed to safety risks and strict workplace supervision.

A final distinction concerns 'human capital' as a retrospective economic category. Education increased skills, but its value depended on jobs, wages, public-health conditions, and the freedom to apply knowledge. The same government that trained a professional class directed its careers and restricted political organizing. A university or scholarship program should therefore be evaluated not merely by the number of graduates produced but by the institutions and society in which those graduates were expected to work.

Division 5 — Unequal Development and Historical Synthesis

Section 83.17 · Division 5

Industrial Workers, Labor Politics, and Household Livelihoods

The developmental economy created a growing industrial working class, but employment conditions differed greatly between the oilfields, government factories, railway works, and small workshops. In Abadan, the foreign-owned company regulated recruitment, housing, and promotion. On the railway, laborers moved between temporary construction camps and sections controlled by contractors. In textile and cement plants, managers imposed shift systems and mechanized production on workers who might recently have been agricultural laborers or artisans. A single label such as 'factory worker' conceals the different risks and resources attached to these workplaces.

Wages were attractive to some households because cash earnings could pay debts, buy food, or support relatives remaining in villages. Yet nominal pay alone says little without prices, hours, housing costs, deductions, and the reliability of employment. A higher wage in an urban factory might be consumed by rent and food. Dangerous machinery, excavation accidents, dust, heat, and exposure to chemicals imposed costs that contemporary official statistics poorly captured. When companies provided clinics or housing, those benefits might also become instruments of discipline because dismissal could jeopardize them. The category of industrial progress is incomplete without labor history.

The organization of workers was contested. The 1929 Abadan strike was an early major instance of collective mobilization in which workers and family members demanded recognition and better conditions. The 1931 anti-collectivist legislation, police surveillance, and restrictions on independent associations greatly narrowed the channels for subsequent action. The government could praise the industrial worker as a symbol of the modern nation while prohibiting independent bargaining over wages or safety. The labor system thus mirrored the wider political structure described in Chapter 81: public institutions expanded while autonomous organization contracted.

Workers also learned new forms of cooperation and conflict from the labor process itself. A railway crew depended on coordinated shifts and common safety procedures; refinery operations placed hundreds or thousands of people within a connected industrial system; state schools taught technical vocabulary to young employees. These experiences encouraged occupational identities that differed from guild or village ties. Such identities were not necessarily revolutionary or partisan. Many workers sought stable employment and advancement before political confrontation, while others drew upon socialist and nationalist arguments when available.

Women's contributions need careful recovery. Women worked in agricultural processing, carpet production, domestic service, and family businesses, and their labor also sustained the households of paid male workers. The participation of women in demonstrations connected with the Abadan strike shows that the boundaries of industrial labor politics extended beyond the factory gate. Company accounts and state employment statistics commonly underrepresent such activity. Everyday Life analysis should therefore include buying, cooking, caring, and maintaining household income as well as the wage contract.

The growth of wage labor remained modest compared with the total population. It nevertheless created lasting infrastructure for postwar politics: unions, parties, professional associations, and expectations of labor protection expanded after Reza Shah's fall. Chapter 84 examines that opening under wartime occupation. The relevant continuity is not that prewar workers enjoyed collective freedom, but that the economic institutions of the 1930s brought people together in settings where collective demands became possible, even when suppressed.

The state's handling of wages and industrial relations also complicates the vocabulary of 'modernization.' A modern factory may have standardized shifts and machine-powered production while maintaining paternalistic authority and denying employees independent representation. Conversely, a laborer's demand for better housing or a safer worksite need not imply rejection of industry. Much of the evidence consists of employer and police descriptions, which can portray strikes as disorder and omit the content of workers' grievances. The historian should reconstruct the demands from the widest available sources and distinguish political affiliations attributed by police from the purposes workers expressed themselves. This is particularly important when later nationalist and leftist parties claimed earlier strikes as precursors to their own movements.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work and Household in a Developing State

For many families, the developmental program appeared in daily life as new demands and new opportunities. A village household might send a son to work on railway construction or in a textile mill, gaining cash wages but losing his labor on the family's land and exposing him to dangerous work. A family in Tehran might send a daughter to a state school, an opportunity opened by the expansion of girls' education, while paying more for sugar and tea because of consumption taxes. An oil worker's household in Abadan might have a regular wage, company housing, and access to a clinic, and also live under supervision and with conditions inferior to those of foreign staff.

Women's experiences varied by class and place. Urban middle-class women gained access to schools and, in a few cases, the university and some professions, while the great majority of rural and working women worked in agriculture, carpet weaving, and household production, often without public recognition. The evidence is uneven: official and company sources record male employment more fully, and historians reconstruct women's economic roles from memoirs, school records, and occasional government surveys. Children, too, experienced change unevenly, with schooling an option in towns but rarely in remote villages.

Section 83.18 · Division 5

Agriculture, Village Economies, and Pastoral Communities

Most Iranian households did not encounter development as employees of a railway or bank. They lived in villages, cultivated land, kept livestock, produced carpets, or traded in small markets. Agriculture remained essential to food supply and employment, yet the government concentrated many of its most visible investments in transport, industry, and the administrative capital. Agricultural credit measures and selected research institutions were introduced, but irrigation, tenancy, landlord power, and access to land changed unevenly. The absence of a comprehensive land redistribution program under Reza Shah is significant: the agrarian policies later associated with the White Revolution belong to another political era.

Peasant livelihoods depended on local arrangements with landlords and village intermediaries, as well as on rainfall, livestock, credit, and the prices received for crops. A new road could reduce a merchant's travel cost and still leave a tenant farmer with little control over the sale price of produce. Cash taxes and conscription increased the state's direct claims on households. Consumption levies could affect villagers as purchasers of tea and sugar even where no major public works reached them. Counting the construction of a road as a rural 'benefit' without asking who could afford to use it risks confusing government access with household opportunity.

Pastoral communities faced a more coercive transformation. Forced settlement and restrictions on migration, treated in Chapter 82, disrupted herd movement and the seasonal allocation of labor. Loss of livestock could convert a household's productive wealth into debt or dependence on wage work. Such policies cannot be measured solely by the number of settlements established. Their economic meaning lay in the ability to maintain herds, grazing rights, and relations with markets. In some districts ordinary villagers also suffered from army requisitioning or the costs of garrison construction, even while improved security along roads helped traders.

Government agricultural programs should not be dismissed as nonexistent. The establishment of agricultural credit institutions in 1933 and of specialist teaching and veterinary initiatives provided starting points for later changes. Some farmers benefited from crop improvement, disease control, or local marketing opportunities. But a state-owned bank was not synonymous with loans available to all cultivators. Security requirements and administrative location could favor larger producers and merchants, and the scale of agricultural transformation remained limited compared with the prominence given to the railway and factories.

The environmental dimensions of development also matter. Roads, railways, new industrial plants, and expanding urban water requirements altered land use, demand for timber and fuel, and pressures on local communities. The evidence for nationwide ecological change is patchy, and a university textbook should resist unsupported aggregate claims. Local examples can nevertheless show that engineering projects have consequences for pastures, drainage, water access, and urban neighborhoods. Reza Shah's state pursued a more connected national territory but did not produce a uniformly improved rural economy. Its material achievements and agrarian limits must be analyzed together.

The distinction between agrarian improvement and agrarian reform provides a useful concluding test. Better seed varieties, veterinary services, or agricultural credit could increase production without changing who owned land or controlled irrigation. Land redistribution would have affected property rights and the bargaining positions of landlords and tenants; this was not the central policy of the 1930s government. Measures under the Land Development Act of 1937 produced limited results in part because implementation depended on existing landholders. The subsequent prominence of rural reform in Iranian political debates reflects how much remained unsettled despite the industrial and transportation achievements of the period.

Section 83.19 · Division 5

Regional Inequality and the Uneven Distribution of Development

The benefits and burdens of the developmental program were not evenly distributed. Wage labor in railways, factories, and the oil industry expanded, but the labor force remained small compared with the rural population. Workers were subject to state controls: unions were suppressed, strikes were dangerous, and labor legislation providing protections was limited. Working hours, pay, and safety depended largely on employers, and factory discipline was often strict.

Most Iranians lived in villages, where the effects of state development were indirect. Taxes and conscription reached them, as did roads and, in some areas, schools or gendarmerie posts. The main structures of rural life, however, continued: landlords or the shah's own estates held much of the land, and peasants cultivated it for a share of the crop. Land reform was not a goal of the 1930s state; it appears as a policy only in the programs described in Chapter 88. Pastoral and nomadic groups faced pressure of a different kind through forced settlement and disarmament, whose consequences included loss of herds and displacement.

Regional inequality was marked. Tehran grew rapidly as the site of government, universities, banks, and many factories. Isfahan, parts of Mazandaran, and a few other centers gained industrial plants. Khuzestan's oil economy was productive but isolated from the rest of the country. Some peripheral regions, including parts of the northwest and east whose trade had depended on cross-border commerce, were affected by trade restrictions and shifts in foreign trade policy. These patterns of concentration influenced later migration, political grievances, and regional movements.

A geographical comparison helps reveal the uneven pattern. Tehran attracted ministries, bank headquarters, technical schools, roads, and private investment associated with state demand. Isfahan gained textile enterprises and educational institutions; parts of Mazandaran saw factories, railway works, and the development of lands associated with the Shah. Khuzestan acquired large oil installations and some of the country's most modern industrial facilities, but their profits, technical control, and exports remained tied to a foreign company. Peripheral border regions often experienced development primarily through army roads, customs supervision, and restrictions on customary trade.

One consequence was an emerging hierarchy among cities. Residents near universities, administrative offices, and factories had greater access to salaried occupations and education. Other towns were bypassed by the main railway or received improvements too modest to overcome distance from markets. A provincial student could obtain higher education in Tehran only by bearing the costs of travel and residence; a merchant faced both the opportunities of a larger national market and new licensing barriers. Migration toward new employment centers began to alter households even before the much larger movements of the postwar decades.

Class differences cut across regions. Large landowners retained control over much cultivable land, while tenants bore uncertainties of rent, water, debt, and crop yield. Urban professionals gained credentials and state employment, whereas laborers often had limited rights. Contractors profited from public works, but small merchants could be harmed by monopolies. Women obtained growing access to certain schools and professions in cities, yet many rural and working women continued to perform labor not measured as paid industrial employment. These examples show why a single indicator such as bank deposits, railway mileage, or factory numbers cannot establish the population-wide distribution of development.

The Shah's extensive acquisition of estates further complicates an account centered on public purpose. Estate ownership could overlap with state power over taxation, policing, and administrative appointments. Modernization sometimes increased the market value of land while leaving tenants' bargaining position weak. The historian should distinguish changes in national productive capacity from changes in the distribution of property and income. They are related but not interchangeable outcomes, and the surviving statistics are much stronger for official investment than for the living standards of ordinary households.

An adequate regional assessment therefore needs different types of evidence. Maps indicate access to roads and railways; government reports list schools, factories, and loans; company archives describe wages and housing; village histories and oral testimonies disclose what happened to land, work, and household choices. Each source has selective visibility. By comparing them, students can see why the developmental state looks different from the office of a minister in Tehran, the workshop of a machinist in Isfahan, the home of an oil worker in Abadan, and a tenant village far from the new transport corridors.

Cause and Effect

Why the Pahlavi State Invested in Railways, Industry, and Schools

Long-term causes

Qajar weakness and foreign concessions, the experience of foreign occupation in World War I, nationalist reform thought from the constitutional era, and the wish for military and fiscal strength.

Short-term causes

Tariff autonomy in 1928, the Depression after 1929, the dispute with the oil company, the availability of consumption-tax revenue, and the shah's personal commitment to particular projects.

Effect

A railway, national banks, protected industries, a university, and a larger bureaucracy and professional class, financed by regressive taxes and operated under authoritarian controls.

Longer-term consequences

Concentration of development in Tehran and a few corridors, dependence on foreign expertise and oil income, strategic exposure in 1941, and a new educated and working population with political expectations the state did not fully meet.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the program. Fiscal opportunity, nationalist ideology, the Depression, military needs, and personal decisions all interacted, and the same projects served several purposes at once.

The Limits and Legacies of the Developmental State, 1938–1941

By 1938 the achievements were visible. The country had a railway, a national bank, a university, factories, and a larger bureaucracy and army. The state collected more revenue and exercised more control than any Iranian government in a century. Nonetheless, the same years revealed structural limits.

The first limit was finance and capacity. Large projects absorbed available funds, and the state depended on consumption taxes, customs, and oil payments, whose size it could not fully control. The supply of trained people was inadequate, and projects often relied on foreign specialists. The second limit was political. Authoritarian rule allowed rapid decisions but discouraged criticism, so mistakes were hard to correct, officials sought to please the shah, and the line between public interest and personal advantage was unclear. The third limit was distributional: costs and benefits fell unequally, and the most visible gains were concentrated in the capital, the new industries, and the elites connected to the state.

A fourth limit was external. The developmental program depended on international trade, foreign technicians, and a concession whose operations Iran could not control. As Europe moved toward war, these dependencies became strategic liabilities. Germany had become a leading trade and technical partner, Britain retained its position in the oil industry, and the Soviet Union bordered the country. The assets the state had built, particularly the railway, the ports, and the oil facilities, were now of interest to the great powers. The consequences for Iran, which proclaimed neutrality when the war began in 1939, are the subject of Chapter 84.

Assessing the period, therefore, requires holding together two judgments. The Pahlavi state of the 1930s created institutions and infrastructure that shaped Iran for decades, and it did so through coercive and unequal methods and with limited ability to withstand external pressure. The debates among historians turn on how to weigh these elements.

The final assessment should avoid assuming that a new institution necessarily fulfilled its stated purpose. Bank Melli mobilized domestic finance but directed a large share of credit toward public priorities; the railway crossed the country but initially omitted many commercial centers; a university trained professionals while political controls curtailed academic freedom. The industrial program produced factories and skilled workers but relied heavily on imported machinery. The oil settlement improved some immediate payments yet prolonged a concession whose limits would become a major source of future conflict. These are not signs that nothing was accomplished. They are the substantive findings of a balanced assessment.

A second question concerns capacity beyond construction. States can build a factory or bridge by concentrating resources for a few years, but maintain them only through continuing budgets, engineers, supplies, and institutions that can identify failures. The absence of independent public criticism under the Shah reduced the avenues through which costly mistakes might be challenged. Patronage and fear could influence reports to ministers, while personal intervention sometimes accelerated decisions at the price of institutional predictability. Development based on command may therefore generate impressive early outputs and long-term vulnerabilities simultaneously.

A third question concerns the alternative uses of scarce funds. Officials could spend on a trunk railway, feeder roads, irrigation, primary schools, hospitals, or additional military equipment. Some projects helped more than one goal, but choices still excluded others. Economic analysis requires asking what a unit of public expenditure might have achieved elsewhere, while acknowledging that historical actors faced incomplete data and pressing sovereignty concerns. The comparison cannot assume the easy availability of private capital, foreign loans on acceptable terms, or a large trained workforce that Iran did not possess.

A fourth question is what survived. The banking network, railway, University of Tehran, industrial facilities, and technical professions remained important after the end of Reza Shah's reign. Some became the basis for postwar expansion; others required reforms or new investment to operate effectively. The first formal Seven-Year Development Plan came in 1949, after the events of Chapter 84 and during the subsequent political period, not under Reza Shah and not as an achievement of the White Revolution examined later. The distinction between investment projects and a formal national planning regime is crucial to a coherent history of Iranian economic policy.

Finally, economic strength and geopolitical security must be separated. The same railway, ports, industrial plants, and refinery that symbolized state-building were attractive to outside powers once war transformed the strategic map. Reza Shah declared neutrality in 1939, but the presence of foreign economic interests and technicians and the country's location between the Gulf and the Soviet Union limited his freedom to remain outside the conflict. Chapter 84 explains the Allied invasion and occupation; this chapter has shown what had been built, who made it possible, how its benefits were divided, and which dependencies persisted. The history of Iran's developmental state is therefore neither a triumphal chronicle nor an account of wasted effort. It is a history of institutional creation within unequal social relations and an international economy the Iranian government could not command.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Qajar Economy to the Pahlavi Developmental State

What Changed

  • The state took the lead in investment, replacing the earlier pattern of selling concessions to foreign entrepreneurs
  • Tariff autonomy, a national bank, and a national currency replaced foreign-controlled instruments
  • A national railway and expanded roads linked regions to the capital
  • A state school system and a national university supplemented religious and foreign institutions

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on foreign expertise and on the oil concession
  • A predominantly rural society with landlord-peasant relations largely intact
  • Reliance on regressive taxation and a small circle of decision-makers
  • Regional unevenness in the benefits of change

Why the pattern matters: Change was rapid in institutions and infrastructure but gradual in the social structure of the countryside. The mismatch between the two helps explain the political tensions described in Chapters 84 through 91.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the material side of Reza Shah's centralizing state: the railway, roads, oil, industry, banking, and education that the government developed between the late 1920s and 1941. It argued that the "developmental state" label is helpful as a description of the state's aims and methods, but must be balanced by attention to its limited resources, authoritarian controls, and unequal results.

Financing was the starting point. The government raised revenue through customs, consumption taxes on sugar and tea, monopolies, and oil payments, and it used newly regained tariff autonomy and the 1931 trade monopoly to protect domestic production. These measures made large projects possible without foreign loans, but they placed significant burdens on ordinary consumers and gave officials extensive control over commerce.

The Trans-Iranian Railway, completed in 1938, was the central achievement. Built across the Alborz and Zagros mountains, largely by Iranian labor under a Danish consortium's direction, it joined the Caspian and the Persian Gulf through Tehran. It was an engineering success and a political symbol, and its economic effects were more limited than its prestige. Roads, ports, and motor transport extended state reach and changed trade, while also serving the army and administration.

Oil remained the largest foreign enterprise. The cancellation of the D'Arcy concession in 1932 and the agreement of 1933 revealed both the government's willingness to assert itself and the limits of its bargaining power. The 1933 terms reduced the concession area and clarified payments but extended the concession to 1993, and later critics focused on that extension. In Abadan and the Khuzestan oil region, an enclave economy with sharp hierarchies and limited links to the rest of the country, workers' grievances and state controls shaped labor relations.

The state also built industry and financial institutions. Factories, protected by tariffs and monopolies, relied on foreign technicians and machinery. National banks consolidated the currency and provided credit, mainly to state-favored enterprises. These institutions strengthened state control of the economy but did not reach most rural Iranians.

Education expanded greatly from a low base. The Ministry of Education centralized schooling and curricula, the University of Tehran was created in 1934, women were admitted from 1936, and students were sent abroad. The new professional class that resulted would become politically significant, even though its members' political expression was restricted.

Development was unevenly distributed. Tehran and a few corridors gained most, the countryside experienced mainly taxes and conscription, and pastoral groups often suffered from state policy. Workers faced strict controls, and unions were suppressed. These inequalities were part of the pattern, not exceptions to it.

Interpretations differ. Some historians see the program as the foundation of modern Iran, others as costly and authoritarian prestige building, and others as a constrained but real transformation. The evidence supports elements of each view, and the choice among them depends on which indicators and which groups are emphasized.

By 1938 the state possessed institutions that earlier governments had lacked, and it also possessed vulnerabilities. Dependence on foreign expertise, trade, and an external oil concession meant that the assets created in the 1930s would attract the great powers' strategic interest once the Second World War began. That sequence leads directly to the invasion and abdication examined in Chapter 84.

Bank Melli’s authorization in 1927, opening in 1928, 1930 note-issue agreement, and the currency reforms of 1931–1932 illustrate the staged transfer of financial authority. The Agricultural and Industrial Bank established in 1933 should be distinguished from industrial-and-mining institutions created much later. The 1933 oil concession reduced the area but prolonged the term to 1993, paired a four-gold-shilling royalty with a defined distribution-based component, and guaranteed minimum annual payments. Labor conflict in Abadan, expanded scholarship training, and the unequal conditions of village and urban life reveal how development was distributed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a modern state was built in a country with limited resources, and how the methods of construction shaped the outcome. The railway, the national bank, the factories, the university, and the new oil agreement were not simply technical achievements. They were products of political choices about who would pay, who would decide, and who would benefit.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs in Iranian history: the interaction of internal state-building and external dependence. Iran's rulers repeatedly sought to strengthen national control over resources and institutions while relying on foreign capital, expertise, or markets. The oil agreement of 1933 and the use of foreign technicians illustrate this tension, which reappears in later chapters on oil nationalization and the development of the 1960s and 1970s.

Continuities extend beyond 1941. The institutions of the 1930s, including the railway, banks, university, and administrative apparatus, survived the abdication of Reza Shah and supplied the framework for postwar politics. So did the social and regional inequalities, and so did the grievances of workers and professionals whose expectations the authoritarian state had raised and restricted. Chapter 84 shows how the war exposed the limits of this state, and Chapter 85 examines the political competition that followed.

For students, the chapter offers a way of reading development stories critically. Large projects invite celebration or condemnation. The more useful historical question is what the project achieved, for whom, at what cost, and under what constraints, and how we can know.
