The Khwarazmian Empire and Iran on the Eve of the Mongol Invasions

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Within about a century and a half, a family founded by a Turkic slave commander of the Seljuk sultans passed from governing a single oasis province to ruling a realm that stretched from the Syr Darya to the western Iranian plateau and eastward toward the Indus. Within a few years of reaching that extent, the realm had been broken by the Mongols. The Khwarazmian story is therefore both a history of imperial construction and a history of political fragility, and it closes the third volume of this textbook.

Consider an episode that later historians placed at the hinge of the dynasty's fortunes. In 1218, according to the principal narrative traditions, a caravan of merchants traveling from territory under the authority of Chinggis Khan reached Otrar, a city on the middle Syr Darya, then part of the domains of the Khwarazmshah Muhammad II. The governor of the city detained the party. Most accounts report that the merchants were killed and their goods seized, and that the diplomacy which followed failed. The episode is widely regarded as the immediate occasion of the Mongol invasion of 1219–1221. Yet its significance depends on what lay behind it: an empire only a few years old in its largest form, assembled by conquest from the wreckage of Seljuk, Ghurid, and Qarakhanid power, and governed through a combination of Persian-language bureaucracy and Turkic military households.

This chapter follows the Anushteginid dynasty from its origins under the Seljuks in the late eleventh century to the death of its last effective claimant, Jalal al-Din, in 1231. It begins in Khwarazm itself, the irrigated lower basin of the Amu Darya, because the dynasty's wealth, its commercial horizons, and some of its political vulnerabilities all had a geographic foundation. It then traces the gradual passage from hereditary provincial governorship under Seljuk sovereignty to autonomy under Atsiz and Il-Arslan, and from autonomy to imperial expansion under Tekish and Muhammad II. A central division of the chapter examines how this empire was governed: its secretaries and chanceries, its army and the Qangli–Kipchak networks on which it relied, the independent court of Terken Khatun, the cities and countrysides that paid for it, and its quarrel with the Abbasid caliphate.

The governing question is a pointed one. How did a provincial dynasty establish the largest territorial power in the eastern Islamic world of its day, and why did that rapid expansion fail to produce enough political cohesion to withstand the Mongol invasion? The chapter's answer resists two simple narratives. One treats the Khwarazmian collapse as the inevitable result of Mongol military superiority. The other attributes it to a single blunder, usually the events at Otrar. The evidence supports neither. The dynasty was neither powerless nor doomed before 1219, and its defeat arose from the interaction of external force, internal political divisions, and choices made under pressure by rulers who could not know the outcome.

Terminology deserves care from the outset. The Khwarazmshahs of this chapter were of Turkic military origin, and their dynasty is conventionally classed among the Persianate states of the period: its chancery and much of its court culture used Persian and Arabic, and it inherited Seljuk administrative practices that were themselves shaped by Iranian bureaucratic families, a subject examined in Chapters 38 and 39. This textbook's Part VII is titled "Iranian Dynasties and the Persianate Renaissance," and the Khwarazmians belong there in the sense that they governed Iranian lands and relied on Persianate institutions, not in the sense that their rulers or soldiers were Iranian-speaking. Likewise, the population of their realm was linguistically, religiously, and socially diverse, and the dynasty's Turkic military identity should not be mistaken for the identity of the cities, farmers, scholars, and merchants it governed.

The chapter's evidence is uneven, and students will meet this unevenness repeatedly. For the dynasty's rise, historians depend on Seljuk-era chronicles, later compilations, coins, and the letters collected by Khwarazmian secretaries. For the administration, the surviving chancery collections are invaluable but cannot be used to reconstruct every office. For the Mongol invasion, the principal narratives are Muslim histories written after the events, most famously by Ata-Malik Juvayni under Mongol rule, by the contemporary historian Ibn al-Athir in Mosul, and by Shihab al-Din al-Nasawi, who served Jalal al-Din. Each had distinct vantage points and purposes. Where these sources disagree on dates, motives, or numbers, the chapter says so rather than choosing one version silently.

Finally, the chapter observes a boundary with its neighbors in the textbook. The Seljuk conquest and the Persianate state it inherited belong to Chapters 38 and 39; Sunni and Shiʿi institutions to Chapter 40; the Nizari Ismailis to Chapter 41; and Khwarazm's scholarly world, including al-Biruni, to Chapter 43. The Mongol conquest as a whole, including its military organization, its devastation of Iranian regions, and the Ilkhanid state that followed, belongs to Volume IV, beginning with Chapter 45. This chapter explains the Mongol invasion from the standpoint of the Khwarazmian empire it destroyed, and it ends in 1231 with the death of the last Khwarazmshah to command an army.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the geographic, agricultural, and commercial foundations of Khwarazm and how they supported a regional political center.
  2. Distinguish hereditary provincial governorship, political autonomy, and full imperial sovereignty in the Anushteginid dynasty's development between c. 1077 and 1200.
  3. Trace the dynasty's expansion under Tekish and Muhammad II into western Iran, Khurasan, Afghanistan, and Transoxiana, identifying the political inheritances of the Seljuks, Ghurids, and Qarakhanids.
  4. Assess the role of the Qara Khitai in both enabling and constraining Khwarazmian power.
  5. Analyze how Persianate bureaucratic practice and Turkic military networks combined in Khwarazmian government, and evaluate the limits of the surviving administrative evidence.
  6. Evaluate Terken Khatun's administrative and political authority without reducing the empire's collapse to her influence.
  7. Interpret the conflict between the Khwarazmshahs and the Abbasid caliphate as a question of sovereignty and legitimacy.
  8. Compare competing narrative traditions concerning the Otrar incident, distinguishing established events from contested motives and responsibility.
  9. Explain why the Khwarazmian empire collapsed quickly in 1219–1221 by weighing structural vulnerability, Mongol operations, and the decisions of Muhammad II.
  10. Evaluate Jalal al-Din's campaigns of 1221–1231, including both their military achievements and their political costs.
  11. Assess continuity and change between the late Seljuk world and early Mongol domination, recognizing that the end of the dynasty did not end Iranian society.

Historical Context

By the late eleventh century the political map of the eastern Islamic world had been redrawn by the Seljuk Turks. Having overcome the Ghaznavids at Dandanqan in 1040 and entered Baghdad in 1055, the Seljuk sultans ruled, in various forms, over Iran, Iraq, and much of Central Asia and Anatolia. Their government combined a Turkic military aristocracy, whose members received revenue assignments, with a Persianate bureaucracy whose viziers and secretaries kept accounts, wrote correspondence, and supervised taxation. Chapters 38 and 39 examine this synthesis. What matters here is its fragility: after the death of Malik Shah in 1092 the Seljuk realm divided among rival branches, and the authority of the eastern ruler, Sultan Sanjar, depended heavily on provincial governors who might, under pressure or opportunity, act for themselves.

Khwarazm, the irrigated region along the lower Amu Darya, was one such province. It had a long history before the Seljuks. Its rulers had borne the title Khwarazmshah since pre-Islamic times, and the region had been under a succession of dynasties, among them the Afrighids, the Ma'munids, and, after 1017, governors for the Ghaznavid sultans (Chapter 37). The Seljuks incorporated it in the 1040s. The later Anushteginid rulers therefore inherited an old title, a prosperous region with its own scholarly tradition, and, crucially, an established place within Seljuk imperial government.

Beyond the Seljuks stood other powers. To the east and north, the Qarakhanid rulers of Transoxiana, Muslim Turkic dynasts descended from the polity that had overthrown the Samanids (Chapter 35), controlled cities such as Samarkand and Bukhara. In the Hindu Kush and Afghan highlands, the Ghurids rose in the twelfth century and would become formidable rivals. The Nizari Ismailis, examined in Chapter 41, held fortresses in northern and eastern Iran. After 1141 a new force entered the region: the Qara Khitai, a Khitan-led Buddhist and multiconfessional polity that had migrated west from northern China. Their victory over Sanjar at Qatwan in that year permanently altered the balance of power in Transoxiana.

To the west lay the Abbasid caliphate, whose caliphs had regained practical political agency in Iraq in the late twelfth century under al-Nasir li-Din Allah (r. 1180–1225), and beyond it Egypt and Syria under the Ayyubids, and Anatolia under the Seljuks of Rum. Further east, the Mongols were being united under Chinggis Khan in the early thirteenth century. The Khwarazmian empire thus occupied a vast middle space between powers on every side, and its history must be read against the shifting alliances and rivalries of a region where no ruler controlled all of the resources required to hold it.

Section 44.1

Khwarazm: Geography, Irrigation, Trade, and Political Importance

Khwarazm (Chorasmia in classical sources) occupies the lower basin and delta of the Amu Darya, the river the Greeks called the Oxus and Arabic writers the Jayhun. South and west of the river lie the Karakum desert and the plateaus leading toward Khurasan and the Caspian; to the east lie the Kyzylkum steppe and, beyond it, the cities of Transoxiana; to the north the river approaches the Aral Sea. The region is naturally a district of oases, and its settlement has always depended on canals drawing off the river. The course of the lower Amu Darya and the extent of the Aral Sea have both shifted over time, and medieval Khwarazm was shaped by a landscape that differs from the modern one; students should avoid reading the present-day Aral basin back into the twelfth century.

Irrigation made Khwarazm productive. Networks of canals supported grain, orchards, melons, and cotton, and they sustained dense settlement along the river. Such systems required continuous labor and administration, since silt, salinity, flooding, and canal repair demanded collective effort, and a state that could organize that labor and protect the headworks could prosper. They were also vulnerable: breaching a dam or canal could ruin farmland and flood cities, a fact grimly demonstrated at Gurganj in 1221 (Section 44.16). Geography here created possibilities and risks. It did not determine the political outcomes discussed in this chapter.

The region's commercial position was equally important. Khwarazm lay between the Iranian plateau and the steppe. Caravans linked it westward across the desert to the Caspian and the Volga region, northward to the Kipchak steppe, southward to Khurasan, and eastward to Bukhara and Samarkand. Goods that moved through such networks included horses, furs, hides, livestock, slaves, textiles, metalwork, and agricultural products, and merchants from Khwarazm had long maintained relations with peoples on the northern steppe. This brokerage position brought revenue from tolls and markets and gave the region's rulers access to steppe soldiers, a connection that proved politically decisive (Section 44.10).

Khwarazm's main cities reflected its history. The older capital was Kath, on the eastern bank of the river, associated with the Afrighid dynasty, whose rulers bore the title Khwarazmshah well before the Islamic conquest, and with the scholar al-Biruni, a native of the region (Chapter 43). Under the Ma'munids, who displaced the Afrighids in 995, the center of gravity moved to Gurganj (Arabic Jurjaniyya, later Urgench), on the western bank, which remained the principal city under the Anushteginids. It was also an intellectual center. Scholars connected to Khwarazm in this period include the philologist and Qurʾan commentator al-Zamakhshari (d. 1144), who spent considerable time at Gurganj, and the Sufi master Najm al-Din Kubra, who was killed during the Mongol siege of the city in 1221.

The point for this chapter is that the Anushteginids did not create Khwarazm's political importance. They inherited a wealthy, urbanized, and literate region that had previously been ruled by Afrighid, Ma'munid, Ghaznavid, and Seljuk authorities. Their distinctive achievement was to turn that regional base into the springboard for a much larger state.

Historical Vocabulary
Khwarazmshah

Definition: "Shah of Khwarazm," the title of the ruler of the Khwarazm region. It is attested long before the Islamic period and was borne by the Afrighid dynasty and later claimed or conferred under other regimes.

Historical context: When the Seljuks appointed Anushtegin Gharchai and his son as governors of Khwarazm, they used a title with deep regional associations. The Anushteginid rulers were Khwarazmshahs under Seljuk authority; from the later twelfth century they also claimed the title "sultan," marking a claim to a wider, sovereign authority.

Why it matters: The title is a reminder that the dynasty inherited, rather than invented, Khwarazm's political identity. It also illustrates how titles can lag behind or precede real power: the same title could describe a subordinate governor in 1100 and an imperial ruler in 1215.

Potential misunderstanding: "Khwarazmshah" does not mean the empire's rulers were from an ancient Khwarazmian family or that they were ethnically Khwarazmian. The Anushteginids were Turkic in origin and drew their authority from Seljuk service.

Section 44.2

From Seljuk Governors to a Hereditary Dynasty, c. 1077–1127

The Anushteginid line began in the service of the Seljuk sultans. Anushtegin Gharchai was a Turkic slave commander, a ghulam, in the household of Sultan Malik Shah (r. 1072–1092). Sources identify his court office as tashtdar, keeper of the sultan's washing bowls, a post of trust within the royal household. Around 1077 he was appointed nominal Khwarazmshah, a provincial title under Seljuk sovereignty; the extent of his direct day-to-day rule is not fully documented. The appointment brought a trusted military retainer into Khwarazm's government. Nothing about the arrangement suggests sovereignty.

The importance of this origin is that it set the political style of the dynasty. The Seljuk empire was a family confederation that relied on revenue assignments and personal loyalty, and slave commanders were its characteristic agents. The Anushteginid rulers inherited that culture of military service. They would later rely on slave commanders and steppe troops of their own, and they would learn from the Seljuks how to combine Turkic military power with a Persian-language administration.

After Malik Shah died in 1092, the Seljuk empire fell into succession disputes among his sons and their supporters, and provincial appointments became bound up with factional struggle. Anushtegin's date of death is not securely known. In 1097 another military commander, Ekinchi ibn Qochqar, briefly held the office of Khwarazmshah and was killed soon afterward. The governor of Khurasan, Amir-e Dad Habashi, then appointed Anushtegin's son Qutb al-Din Muhammad. The sequence matters: the father and son did not simply succeed one another without interruption, and Muhammad's appointment still required Seljuk authorization. A hereditary principle was emerging within a system in which the Seljuk sovereign retained the right to confirm, and in theory to remove, his governor.

Qutb al-Din Muhammad ruled until 1127 and, in the sources, appears as a dutiful governor. He served Sultan Sanjar, the Seljuk ruler of the east, whose authority was recognized in Khurasan and Transoxiana, and Khwarazm remained a loyal province. His court at Gurganj attracted scholars and physicians. The best-known example is the Persian medical encyclopedia known as the Dhakhira-yi Khwarazmshahi of Isma'il Jurjani, composed in this period and dedicated to the Khwarazmshah. Such works show that the Khwarazmian court was already a center of patronage and that Persian was becoming a language of learned prose there, a development related to the broader Persianate revival examined in Chapters 33 and 35.

This division of the dynasty's history requires careful use of terms. Hereditary succession in a provincial governorship is not the same as autonomy, and autonomy is not the same as imperial sovereignty. In 1127 the Khwarazmshah was an important and prosperous governor, but he remained subordinate to a Seljuk sultan who could summon him to campaign, collect revenue from him, and, at least in principle, replace him. The dynasty's later claims to a larger role were still unimaginable.

Section 44.3

Atsiz and the Struggle for Independence, 1127–1156

Qutb al-Din Muhammad was succeeded in 1127 by his son Atsiz, who ruled until 1156 and is the first Khwarazmshah to appear as a determined seeker of greater independence. The sources, many of them written from the Seljuk or later Khwarazmian perspective, portray him as ambitious and restless. His relationship with Sultan Sanjar combined service, rebellion, submission, and renewed rebellion, and its repeated reversals illustrate how much a determined provincial governor could achieve and how much he could not.

Atsiz's ambitions pointed in several directions. He sought to extend his authority along the Syr Darya and toward the steppe, where Khwarazm's northern commercial connections lay, and, as Sanjar's grip weakened, toward Khurasan. Sanjar responded by campaigning against him. According to the chronicle tradition, in 1138 Sanjar invaded Khwarazm and forced Atsiz to flee, installing a relative of his own as ruler. Atsiz returned and recovered the province, but the episode shows how directly Seljuk military power could still intervene in the province, and the same Sanjar who dislodged him in 1138 would also be the sovereign Atsiz sought to outmaneuver for the rest of his reign.

A larger event reshaped the field. In 1141 Sanjar was decisively defeated near Samarkand, at the battle of Qatwan, by the Qara Khitai under their ruler, the Gur-khan. The defeat has often been taken as a turning point. It deprived the Seljuks of a substantial portion of their eastern army, cost them standing in Transoxiana, and established the Qara Khitai as the most powerful non-Muslim ruler in the region. For Atsiz it created an opportunity. He moved against Khurasan and, in the sources' account, briefly seized Merv and Nishapur. But Sanjar recovered, retook the territory, and campaigned against Khwarazm again, and Atsiz found himself compelled to submit once more.

Atsiz did not attempt to resist the Qara Khitai. Instead, as later sources report, he accepted their overlordship and paid tribute in return for security on his northern and eastern frontiers. The arrangement is easy to misread. It does not show weakness alone, since it was a recognized method by which a mid-sized regional ruler could avoid being ground between two larger powers; but it does show that the Khwarazmshah's position was defined by relationships with outside powers. In effect, Atsiz navigated competing claims from Sanjar and the more distant Qara Khitai; their demands overlapped rather than forming a clean exchange of one overlord for another. This combination of diplomacy, tribute, and opportunism would recur throughout the dynasty's early history.

Atsiz also cultivated the machinery of an independent court. His secretary and court poet, Rashid al-Din Watwat, wrote prose and verse in Persian and Arabic and became one of the most important literary figures associated with the dynasty (Section 44.9). When Atsiz died in 1156 he had not made Khwarazm independent, yet he had established patterns of leverage and diplomacy that his successors would use.

Section 44.4

Il-Arslan and the Decline of Great Seljuk Authority, 1156–1172

Atsiz was succeeded by his son Il-Arslan (r. 1156–1172). By this time Great Seljuk authority in the east had all but evaporated. Sanjar had been captured by Oghuz Turkmen bands in 1153 and held for several years; he escaped in 1156 and died at Merv in 1157. The Oghuz plundered Khurasan, and the region fragmented among local commanders and rival claimants, among them former commanders of Sanjar who held cities such as Nishapur. There was no longer a Seljuk sultan in the east who could command the loyalty of governors.

This vacuum shaped Il-Arslan's reign. His principal tasks were defensive and opportunistic: to preserve Khwarazm's position against the Oghuz and other Turkic groups, to maintain relations with the Qara Khitai, and to extend his influence into the eastern fringes of Khurasan where there was no dominant authority. He intervened in Khurasan's politics and in Transoxiana, but the evidence for his campaigns is less full than for his father's or his successors', and historians should speak cautiously about their scale. What is clear is that the dynasty continued to pay or acknowledge tribute to the Qara Khitai, and that its rulers regarded the northern and eastern frontiers as places of both danger and recruitment.

The reign is nevertheless important for what it reveals about the gradual character of independence. There was no declaration of independence in the 1150s or 1160s; rather, the Khwarazmshahs continued to act as autonomous rulers while the structure that had made them subordinate dissolved around them. Coins, titles, and diplomatic correspondence changed more slowly than power did. A historian who looks only for a single moment of sovereignty will miss the combination of tribute, local control, ceremonial deference, and calculated ambition that characterized the dynasty from Atsiz onward.

Il-Arslan's death in 1172 plunged the dynasty into the succession conflict that opens the next division. By then the dynasty had survived the collapse of its Seljuk overlord and the arrival of a new regional hegemon, and it had acquired the resources, experience, and military connections that made expansion possible. Whether it would be able to turn those assets into something more than a regional principality remained entirely uncertain.

Section 44.5

Tekish, Sultan Shah, and the Struggle for the Throne, 1172–1193

Il-Arslan's death in 1172 opened a contest between two of his sons. Tekish, the elder, had been governing in the northeast, in the region of Jand on the Syr Darya, an appointment that placed him in close contact with the steppe and its military groups. His younger brother Sultan Shah was supported by the court circle at Gurganj, which, in the sources, included their mother. When Sultan Shah took the throne, Tekish fled to the Qara Khitai, and the Gur-khan provided him with troops. With that support Tekish returned and secured Khwarazm. Sultan Shah withdrew to Khurasan, where he established a rival power base.

The resulting division of the dynasty lasted until 1193. For two decades, Tekish ruled Khwarazm, while Sultan Shah held parts of Khurasan, and the brothers fought, negotiated, and sought allies. These struggles illustrate a feature of the dynasty's politics: succession was not governed by a strict rule of primogeniture, and several members of the family, together with their backers among the military and the court, could advance plausible claims. The same pattern would recur in 1220 (Section 44.16).

Tekish's use of outside military support deserves emphasis. The Qara Khitai intervention was costly in obligation but decisive in effect, and it shows that the dynasty's rise depended on patrons as well as conquests. Tekish also drew on the northern Turkic military groups of the Syr Darya and the steppe. His marriage to Terken Khatun, a woman of Qangli (Kipchak) background, tied him into one of these networks, and Qangli commanders became increasingly important in the army (Section 44.10). In this way Tekish assembled a military base that did not rely on the landholding and revenue structures of the settled regions.

Tekish's advance into Khurasan occurred in stages through the 1180s and early 1190s, as he extended control over cities previously held by local commanders and by his brother. The final resolution came in 1193, when Sultan Shah died and Tekish took over his territories. Chronology for these years is less secure than for later periods, and students should treat individual dates for conquests in Khurasan, as reported by the chronicles, as approximate. The larger point is clear enough: by the early 1190s the Khwarazmian dynasty controlled both Khwarazm and much of Khurasan, and it was positioned to look westward.

Section 44.6

The Conquest of Western Iran and the End of Great Seljuk Rule, 1194–1200

West of Khurasan lay the territory known in this period as Iraq-i Ajam, the central and western Iranian plateau, which included Rayy, Hamadan, and Isfahan. This region had been the core of the Seljuk sultanate of Iraq, whose last sultan, Tughril III, ruled in the 1180s and 1190s, but his authority was threatened by Turkic atabegs who controlled Azerbaijan and by a powerful Abbasid caliph, al-Nasir, who wished to curb Seljuk influence in Iraq and Iran. In 1194 Tekish marched west. Near Rayy he defeated Tughril III, who was killed in the battle. The Great Seljuk line in western Iran ended with him.

The victory transformed the Khwarazmian position. Tekish took possession of Rayy, Hamadan, and much of western Iran and thereby inherited the symbolic standing of the Seljuk sultans, along with a large territory. Sources describe him as taking the title sultan. The occupation was also a source of problems. The western Iranian cities had their own elites, Turkmen emirs with armed followings, and traditions of local government, and they did not become obedient provinces simply because a Khwarazmian army had defeated their previous sultan. Garrisons had to be maintained, revenue had to be collected, and local magnates had to be induced to cooperate or be replaced.

The Abbasid caliph proved a particular complication. Al-Nasir had been willing to see Tughril weakened, but he did not welcome a powerful new sultan in Iran, and relations between Tekish and the caliphate worsened. Disputes over control of Hamadan and other cities arose. Abbasid diplomats and armies sought to limit Khwarazmian gains, while Tekish sought recognition as the sultan of the east. The conflict that would later culminate in Muhammad II's attempt to challenge the caliph himself (Section 44.12) began here, as a competition for control of the heartlands of the former Seljuk state.

Tekish was also involved in wars on other fronts, including contests with the Ghurids over Khurasan and encounters with the Ismailis of northern Iran. These commitments divided his forces. He died in 1200 while on campaign. His career thus ended with the dynasty's territory greatly enlarged but its integration incomplete. The western conquests, in particular, had been won by a military expedition that defeated a single enemy; consolidating them would require a bureaucracy, garrison system, and political settlement of a kind the dynasty was only beginning to build.

Section 44.7

Muhammad II and the Expansion into Khurasan and Afghanistan, 1200–1215

Tekish's death brought his son Muhammad II (ʿAlaʾ al-Din Muhammad) to the throne in 1200. His mother, Terken Khatun, and the Qangli commanders associated with her were significant supporters. The accession was not uncontested in the sense that the new ruler faced immediate challenges from commanders and rivals, but the dynasty held together, and Muhammad turned to the problem that dominated the first decade of his reign: the Ghurids.

The Ghurids, a dynasty from the highlands of central Afghanistan, had expanded during the later twelfth century. Under Ghiyath al-Din and his brother Shihab al-Din (Muʿizz al-Din) they conquered Ghazna from the descendants of the Ghaznavids, advanced into northern India, and contested Khurasan with Khwarazm. In the early years of the thirteenth century the two powers clashed repeatedly. In 1204–1205 Shihab al-Din Ghuri invaded Khwarazm and besieged Gurganj. The sources report that the Qara Khitai, as overlords of Muhammad, came to his aid, and Ghurid forces suffered a serious defeat at Andkhud. It is a reminder that the Qara Khitai continued to function as Khwarazmian protectors even as Muhammad II expanded.

The decisive change came in 1206, when Shihab al-Din Ghuri was assassinated, and the Ghurid empire, held together by his personality and the loyalty of Turkic slave commanders, began to dissolve. Members of the Ghurid family and their commanders fought among themselves, and powerful slave commanders established their own principalities, including at Ghazna. Muhammad II exploited the disintegration. Between about 1206 and 1215 he extended his authority over Ghurid-held territories in Khurasan and Afghanistan, taking Herat and other cities, subduing Ghur proper, and, around 1215, driving the commander Taj al-Din Yildiz out of Ghazna. Exact dates for these conquests vary among sources.

Absorbing such territories demanded more than military victory. Muhammad needed troops to garrison cities, administrators to collect revenue, and commanders trusted enough to be given provincial authority. He assigned provinces to his sons and to commanders, and, as the empire grew, the number of such appointments multiplied. Each appointment raised the question of whether the governor would remain loyal. Revenue assignments (iqtaʿ) were granted to maintain troops, but these could create local interests that competed with the center. The empire's fiscal basis was also stretched: each new province required garrisons that cost more than the province might yield in its first years.

It would be misleading to portray these conquests as easy. Muhammad II's victories depended on the Ghurids' internal collapse, on diplomacy with local rulers, and on the continuing tolerance of the Qara Khitai. The Ghurid case also shows how opportunistic much Khwarazmian expansion was: the dynasty won not because it had a permanent superiority over its neighbors but because it was well placed to profit when neighboring states fractured.

Section 44.8

Transoxiana, the Qarakhanids, and the Decline of Qara Khitai Power

North and east of the Amu Darya, the Khwarazmshah's ambitions ran into a tangled political landscape. The cities of Transoxiana, among them Bukhara and Samarkand, were governed by Qarakhanid rulers who were themselves vassals of the Qara Khitai. The tribute that Khwarazm had paid since the time of Atsiz symbolized the dominance of the Gur-khan. As Muhammad II's power grew, he came to resent his status as a tributary, and around the first decade of the thirteenth century he refused to continue paying it, or sought to throw off Qara Khitai supervision.

The sources for the conflict that followed are narrative accounts written after the event, and they differ on details. They agree that Muhammad II fought the Qara Khitai in the region of the Talas and Syr Darya, around 1209–1210, and that he succeeded in weakening their hold in Transoxiana. A tradition preserved by later historians holds that he was at one point captured by the Qara Khitai and escaped unrecognized, an anecdote whose details students should treat with caution. Whatever the specifics, his campaigns won him standing as a protector of Muslim Transoxiana against a non-Muslim overlord, a role with political value.

The Qarakhanid ruler of Samarkand, Uthman, shifted his allegiance between the Qara Khitai and Muhammad II according to circumstance, and the sources report that he was drawn into a marriage connection with Muhammad. Relations soured, and around 1212 Muhammad took Samarkand, executed Uthman, and incorporated the city and its region directly into his realm. This action ended a Qarakhanid dynasty, replaced a local ruler with a Khwarazmian administration, and demonstrated that Muhammad II intended to rule Transoxiana directly rather than through vassals. In 1211 a Naiman prince, Kuchlug, had seized power among the Qara Khitai, further weakening that polity and making Muhammad's task easier.

For the populations of Transoxiana the change was ambiguous. A Muslim ruler replaced a distant non-Muslim overlord, but the new regime brought its own demands: taxes, garrisons of Turkic troops, and the requisitioning of supplies. The sources also reveal resentment among urban elites toward the new Khwarazmian garrisons, a theme that would reappear in 1220 when the cities of Transoxiana faced the Mongols. The consequence of these campaigns was to carry the Khwarazmian frontier to the Syr Darya and the borders of the steppe world, immediately adjacent to the expanding Mongol polity. By about 1215 the dynasty's territory reached its greatest extent, and its commanders faced the question of how to rule it.

Section 44.9

The Persianate Administration: Viziers, Chanceries, Finance, and Provincial Rule

The Khwarazmian empire was governed through institutions it largely inherited. The Seljuks had developed a system in which a vizier headed the civil administration, specialized offices known as diwans handled correspondence, finance, the army, and inspection, and provincial governors received appointments that combined military command and responsibility for revenue. Chapter 39 examines the Seljuk model in detail. The Khwarazmians adopted it with local adaptations, and it is useful to think of their administration less as an invention than as the Seljuk system operating under a new dynasty and across a larger territory.

The central chancery, the diwan of correspondence (diwan al-insha'), was the most visible institution, since its products survive. Secretaries drafted letters to rulers, governors, and religious figures, as well as decrees conferring offices or revenue, and their prose followed elaborate stylistic conventions with a strong Arabic and Persian literary flavor. The surviving evidence for this practice is a collection of letters and a handful of literary works rather than a body of original documents. The two figures most often cited are Rashid al-Din Watwat (d. c. 1182–1183), who served Atsiz, Il-Arslan, and Tekish and who also composed a treatise on Persian poetics, and Baha al-Din Baghdadi, associated with Tekish's chancery, who compiled a collection of chancery documents called al-Tawassul ila al-tarassul. Both show that the Khwarazmian court valued skill in prose and verse as an instrument of statecraft, and that secretaries formed an educated service class.

Financial administration relied on land revenue, on taxes on commerce and urban production, and on extraordinary levies in wartime. The diwans of finance and of the army maintained the registers by which revenue assignments were granted. Provincial governors were appointed from the ruler's family, from military commanders, and occasionally from civilian administrators, and their duties included maintaining order, collecting revenue, and supplying troops. Beyond this outline, historians know comparatively little. The sources do not permit a full reconstruction of every office, the size of the revenue, or the day-to-day working of the provincial system, and it would be misleading to assign precise duties to offices for which the evidence is thin.

What the evidence does support is a picture of continuity. The Khwarazmian chancery drew on Seljuk practices, and many of its secretaries came from the same milieu of literate administrative families that served the Seljuks and, before them, the Samanids and Ghaznavids. This continuity helped the new dynasty govern lands that had experienced several changes of regime, and it meant that political upheavals at the top did not necessarily remove the clerks and tax collectors below. It also meant that the Khwarazmian state could not easily distance itself from the Seljuk model, and the same bureaucratic apparatus was the one through which a powerful queen mother would later exercise independent authority (Section 44.10).

Diwan and Iqtaʿ

Definition: A diwan was an administrative bureau or its register, such as the diwans of correspondence, finance, or the army. An iqtaʿ was an assignment of the revenue of a district, usually to a military commander, in return for service.

Historical context: The Seljuks made the iqtaʿ a central means of supporting armies, and the Khwarazmians continued the practice. Recipients were entitled to collect revenue rather than, in principle, owning the land.

Why it matters: Iqtaʿ grants allowed a state to pay troops without a large cash treasury, but they also created local interests and made commanders dependent on territory they might wish to retain.

Potential misunderstanding: The iqtaʿ should not be equated with European feudalism. Its legal and practical features varied across time and region, and historians continue to debate how it operated in particular states.

Section 44.10

The Army, Qangli–Kipchak Networks, and Terken Khatun's Political Authority

The Khwarazmian army was a composite force. It included slave commanders and soldiers, Turkmen and other Turkic troops, local levies, and above all cavalry drawn from the steppe peoples north of Khwarazm, among them the Qangli, a group associated with the wider Kipchak confederation. The dynasty's early reliance on these steppe connections, which began with Tekish's years in Jand and the Qara Khitai support he received, grew steadily under his successor. Qangli commanders and their followers were recruited in large numbers, and many received appointments as governors and iqtaʿ holders.

This reliance had advantages and costs. Steppe cavalry gave the dynasty a mobile and formidable force, and the personal ties between Qangli commanders and the ruling family bound them to the dynasty. But those ties were also the basis of rival loyalties. The commanders' first allegiance was often to their kin and clan and to the individuals who had promoted them, rather than to the dynasty as an abstract institution. A commander's followers might see themselves as owing obligations to him rather than to the sultan, and the sultan could not always count on obedience. The army's cohesion therefore depended on constant political management.

At the center of this web stood Terken Khatun—a court title rather than a securely preserved personal name—Tekish's wife and Muhammad II's mother, a woman of Qangli origin whose kin occupied important commands. She was far more than a ceremonial queen. According to the sources, she maintained a separate court and a diwan of her own at Gurganj, issued documents under her own seal, controlled a body of officials and military clients, and influenced appointments throughout the empire. Her authority was such that sources describe the Khwarazmian state as in practice divided between the sultan's administration and the queen mother's. A kinsman of hers, Inalchuq, governed Otrar at the time of the Mongol incident (Section 44.14).

Her influence extended to succession. Muhammad II's eldest son, Jalal al-Din, was reportedly not Terken Khatun's candidate, and the sources associate her with the promotion of another of Muhammad's sons, Uzlaq Shah, as heir. In this reading the succession question became entangled with the competition between Qangli networks and other groups at court. The narratives, mostly written after the fact and often by those who suffered from the dynasty's collapse, may exaggerate this rivalry. Students should treat accounts of the queen mother's role as evidence of real power while remaining cautious about attributing the empire's fall to her.

The right question is a structural one. An empire with two courts, two chanceries, and two sets of patronage relationships had a built-in problem of coordination. When the Mongols arrived, the empire's divided authority, its commanders' uncertain loyalties, and Muhammad II's apparent distrust of some of his own forces compounded one another. That was a weakness of the political system, not simply of a single individual.

Administrative and Military Study

Terken Khatun, Military Patronage, and the Court

The institutions. A diwan of her own gave Terken Khatun the machinery to issue orders, grants, and appointments. Her seal authenticated documents. These details are among the best-attested features of her authority and reflect a recognized form of queenly power in the late medieval Persianate and Turkic world.

The networks. Her kin and clients held commands, including important city governorships. Inalchuq at Otrar is the most famous example. Appointments of this kind turned family ties into military and territorial control.

The tension. A sultan's authority in this system depended on the loyalty of his commanders, some of whom owed their positions to his mother. The sources suggest that Muhammad II distrusted at least some commanders and that, in turn, some officers did not regard him as their sole master.

Section 44.11

Cities, Agriculture, Commerce, Taxation, and Everyday Life

Behind the dynasty's campaigns stood a society of cultivators, artisans, merchants, soldiers, scholars, and women and men of every rank whose lives are far less visible in the sources. The most prominent cities of the empire were Gurganj, the dynasty's home; Bukhara and Samarkand in Transoxiana; Merv, Nishapur, Herat, and Balkh in Khurasan and Afghanistan; and Rayy, Hamadan, and Isfahan in the west. Each had its own urban elites, crafts, market institutions, and religious communities. Nishapur and Merv, in particular, were old centers of learning and commerce and home to Sunni legal schools whose scholars sometimes competed for influence.

The agricultural base differed by region. In Khwarazm, Transoxiana, and parts of Khurasan, farmers depended on canal irrigation or on the underground channels known as qanats, which tapped groundwater and carried it to fields. In other regions rainfall agriculture or pastoralism predominated. Land revenue formed the largest fiscal resource, and it was collected through officials and iqtaʿ holders. The distribution of land and the terms on which peasants held it varied, and sources are generally thin on peasant life.

Commerce thrived on the networks described in Section 44.1. Merchants carried textiles, metalwork, paper, leather, furs, horses, and other goods through the empire's cities, and rulers drew revenue from tolls, market taxes, and mint profits. Merchants and the empire's rulers shared an interest in security but did not always agree on terms. The Otrar episode shows how a local official's action toward traders could trigger a crisis far beyond the city. Crafts, too, flourished in towns, and scholarship found patrons in courts and in religious endowments.

The costs of imperial expansion fell heavily on these populations. A state that maintained a large army required revenue, and the sources hint at extraordinary levies, requisitioning of supplies and animals, and billeting of troops, especially in wartime and in newly conquered regions. Garrisons of Turkic troops in cities such as Samarkand could be a source of both protection and resentment. Many urban notables seem to have accepted Khwarazmian rule as an alternative to disorder, but there is little evidence of deep loyalty to the dynasty, and when the Mongols appeared some cities chose negotiation rather than resistance.

Everyday Life and Social History

Irrigation, Trade, Taxes, and Military Demands

For a farmer in the Khwarazm oasis, the central fact of life was the canal: its clearing, repair, and fair division of water. Labor for canal maintenance was a recurring obligation, and any disruption could ruin a harvest. For a merchant in Bukhara or Samarkand, the central concern was safe passage and predictable tolls across a wide territory that, until recently, had been divided among several rulers. For an artisan in Nishapur or Merv, markets and the patronage of courts and mosques supplied demand.

For soldiers, the obligations were different. Commanders held iqtaʿ and maintained followers; ordinary troopers depended on their patrons for pay, food, and fodder. For town residents, the presence of an army could mean security, but also the requisition of supplies. Scholars, jurists, and Sufis occupied a distinct social space in the cities and could influence public opinion. Women's roles are visible mainly for elite figures such as Terken Khatun; the lives of non-elite women are poorly documented, and historians should avoid generalizing from the queen mother to women of all ranks.

Because the surviving sources were written by educated administrators, historians and court chroniclers, rural populations and the poor appear mostly indirectly, as taxpayers, laborers, and victims of campaigns.

Section 44.12

The Khwarazmshahs and the Abbasid Caliphate: Sovereignty, Religion, and Rivalry

In an influential Abbasid–Seljuk model of Sunni political legitimacy, a sultan could seek caliphal recognition through investiture, mention in the Friday sermon (the khutba), and the use of the caliph's name on coins; actual sovereignty and relations with the caliph varied greatly. Seljuk sultans had operated within this framework, even as the caliphs lost real power. The Khwarazmshahs, as Sunni Muslim rulers, sought recognition in the same terms, and their relations with the caliphate shaped both their legitimacy and their policy.

By the late twelfth century the caliphate had changed. Under al-Nasir li-Din Allah (r. 1180–1225) the Abbasid court pursued an active policy in Iraq and western Iran, built up an army, cultivated ties with religious networks, and sought to prevent any sultan from again dominating Baghdad as the Seljuks had. This policy was bound to conflict with the ambitions of Tekish and Muhammad II, who claimed the legacy of the Seljuk sultans. After 1194 the two sides quarreled over control of Hamadan and western Iran and over the formal recognition that the caliph might grant or withhold.

Muhammad II eventually pursued a more radical course. Around 1217 he challenged the caliph's standing, reportedly seeking opinions from jurists and scholars that questioned al-Nasir's right to the caliphate and promoting a descendant of the Prophet's son-in-law Ali as an alternative. The proposal reflected both political calculation and a wider strand of thought that held the Abbasid claim to be illegitimate or to have been compromised by the caliph's policies. The sources make clear that it was controversial. Many Sunni scholars regarded the Abbasid caliph as the proper center of Sunni legitimacy, and Muhammad's initiative did not command universal support, even in his own territories.

The practical result was an expedition westward in 1217–1218. Muhammad II marched toward Baghdad with a large army, but heavy snow and difficult conditions in the Zagros passes, together with harassment by local forces, turned the campaign into a failure, and he withdrew without accomplishing his aim. The reverse cost him prestige and resources and distracted his attention from the east at a critical moment.

A further tradition requires caution. Some later sources claim that al-Nasir actively invited the Mongols to attack Muhammad II. The claim is widely repeated in later literature, but contemporary evidence for it is weak, and many historians treat it with skepticism. It is safer to say that the caliph and the Khwarazmshah were rivals, and that the Abbasid court had every reason to watch Khwarazmian difficulties with satisfaction, than to assume a documented conspiracy. The episode as a whole shows how Muhammad's search for legitimacy had become a source of weakness: in challenging the caliph he alienated potential supporters among the Sunni religious establishment.

Section 44.13

The Khwarazmian Empire at Its Height: Territory, Power, and Structural Vulnerability

By about 1215–1218 the Khwarazmian empire reached its greatest extent. From the Syr Darya and the Fergana approaches in the northeast, it extended across Transoxiana, Khurasan, and Afghanistan, and from the Amu Darya delta in the north to central and western Iran in the southwest and west. Some regions were ruled directly, others through governors or relatives of the sultan, and still others, such as parts of southern Iran and Azerbaijan, were held by local dynasties that acknowledged his suzerainty to varying degrees or remained independent. The empire's boundaries were therefore not fixed lines but zones of differing control.

At this size the empire faced a set of structural problems. The first was distance. Communication between Gurganj, Samarkand, Nishapur, Ghazna, and Hamadan took days or weeks, and no single center could supervise all of them. The empire had several capitals and courts, including that of Terken Khatun, and the ruler's personal presence was needed in many places at once. The second was loyalty. Local elites in newly conquered territories, whether former Qarakhanid officials, Ghurid commanders, or western Iranian notables, had been incorporated by force within the preceding two decades, and the sources do not suggest that they felt strong commitment to the dynasty.

The third was military. The army was large but dispersed among garrisons, and its commanders' loyalties were divided, as discussed in Section 44.10. The fourth was fiscal. The cost of garrisons, campaigns, and court patronage placed heavy demands on a revenue system that had been extended rapidly and had not had time to mature. Finally, there was the question of legitimacy. Muhammad's conflict with the caliph deprived him of some religious support, and the competing power of his mother's court weakened the appearance of unified command.

None of this means the empire was a hollow shell. It commanded substantial resources, and its military record before 1219 was strong. Historians should note that the empire's structural weaknesses were visible mainly in hindsight and that its rulers could have taken steps to remedy them had circumstances differed. But when pressure came, the empire's integration was shallow, and the contest with the Mongols would test exactly those weaknesses.

Historical Debate I

Did the Khwarazmshahs Build a Durable Imperial State or a Military Expansion System Dependent on Personal Power?

The question: Was the Khwarazmian empire a developing state with real institutions that might have matured, or a rapidly assembled conquest system whose cohesion depended on the ruler, his army, and the queen mother's networks?

Interpretation A — an incipient durable state: Supporters of this view point to the inherited Seljuk bureaucracy, the functioning chancery and diwans, the continuity of secretarial personnel, the wealth of the regional economy, and the dynasty's century and a half of growth. The empire, in this reading, was young rather than hollow, and the Mongol invasion destroyed a state that had not had time to consolidate.
Interpretation B — a personal and military system: Others emphasize the shallowness of integration. Conquered regions retained their own elites, commanders held divided loyalties, there were two courts, no stable succession rule, and the empire's cohesion depended on a single army and ruler. Seen this way, the empire's size outstripped its institutions.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same limited evidence: chancery collections, narrative accounts by historians writing after the collapse, coins, and the course of events in 1219–1221. The scarcity of evidence on internal administration means that neither view can be decisively proved.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and the weight given to scarce evidence, rather than any dispute about the basic sequence of events. A further difficulty is that the narrative sources were written after the collapse and may project failure backward.

Why it matters: If the empire was an incipient state, its destruction was a loss of institutional potential. If it was largely a personal system, its quick collapse was more predictable. The two readings also bear differently on how much responsibility to place on the decisions of 1218–1220.

Section 44.14

The Mongols, Diplomacy, and the Otrar Incident of 1218

In the first decades of the thirteenth century, Temüjin, proclaimed Chinggis Khan at a great assembly in 1206, united the steppe peoples of Mongolia and then turned outward, campaigning against the Tangut state and the Jin dynasty in northern China. In 1218 his generals defeated Kuchlug, who had seized the Qara Khitai throne, and the Mongol realm thereby reached the borders of the Khwarazmian empire in the Semirechye and Syr Darya region. For the first time the two powers were direct neighbors.

Initial contacts, as the main narrative sources report, were commercial and diplomatic. Chinggis Khan sent envoys to Muhammad II proposing peaceful relations and trade between their domains. Juvayni, writing under Mongol rule, preserves a report that the Mongol message addressed the Khwarazmshah in terms that Muhammad resented, but the exact wording and tone cannot be verified. Muhammad received the envoys and, according to the sources, agreed to commercial relations. Mongol interest in trade with Central Asian merchants was real, since the Mongol rulers valued access to luxury goods and to merchants who could supply them.

The crisis came with a caravan. The sources report that a trading party traveling under Mongol auspices arrived at Otrar in 1218. Its governor, Inalchuq, a kinsman of Terken Khatun, detained the party. Most accounts report that its members were killed and its goods seized. The reasons given vary. Some say Inalchuq suspected the traders of spying; others point to greed; still others emphasize the sultan's role. Whether Muhammad II ordered the killings, approved them after the fact, or merely failed to repudiate them is disputed. What is generally accepted is that the event happened, that it caused a major diplomatic crisis, and that Chinggis Khan sent envoys demanding redress, including the surrender of the governor. According to the narrative tradition, Muhammad rejected the demand and the envoys were killed or humiliated, an act that left little room for settlement.

The chapter's task is to present these events with source-critical care. The Muslim sources that provide the detail were written after the destruction that followed, and they had reasons to explain the catastrophe through a clear chain of cause and effect. The Mongol side is less fully documented in sources available to Muslim authors. Historians therefore treat the events at Otrar as firmly attested in outline but uncertain in their precise motives, scale, and chain of responsibility.

Section 44.15

The Mongol Invasion of Transoxiana, 1219–1220

In 1219 Chinggis Khan led a large army toward the Khwarazmian frontier. The Mongol forces advanced by several routes at once. One force, including Chinggis Khan's sons, besieged Otrar. Another moved down the Syr Darya, taking its cities one by one. A principal force under Chinggis Khan himself crossed the Kyzylkum toward Bukhara, a march that brought it to the city from an unexpected direction, behind the Syr Darya defenses. The Mongols excelled at coordinating separate columns, at using intelligence supplied by local guides and captives, and at siege warfare, including the use of conscripted prisoners to build siegeworks and fill moats. Details of the scale of the forces are uncertain, and the large figures given in the sources should not be treated as reliable.

Muhammad II's strategy, as best it can be reconstructed, was to garrison the main cities and avoid a decisive field battle, relying on the strength of walls and the dispersion of troops. Explanations offered for the choice include the strength of Mongol cavalry, distrust of his own commanders, a belief that fortified cities could withstand a siege until the invaders were worn down, and an unwillingness to risk his army. Historians cannot recover his reasoning with certainty. What is clear is that dispersing the army left each garrison to face the enemy alone and prevented a coordinated response.

Otrar held out for several months before it fell, and its governor was reportedly executed. Bukhara fell in early 1220. The Turkic garrison attempted to break out, and the city's population, as reported, surrendered while the citadel held out for a short time before being taken. The city suffered fire and plunder. Samarkand, which had a large garrison and was a place of great importance, fell in the spring of 1220, apparently after a brief siege, and the sources report that soldiers were killed and townspeople subjected to massacre, ransom, and the deportation of skilled artisans. Accounts of both cities are drawn mainly from Muslim writers, some of whom wrote under the Mongols, and their details are difficult to confirm.

This chapter does not reproduce the immense casualty figures found in medieval sources. Such numbers are generally rhetorical or unverifiable, and they obscure what can be established: that sieges caused substantial death and destruction, that populations were displaced or enslaved, that artisans and scholars were deported or killed, and that urban economies were disrupted. The broader history of the Mongol military system and of the consequences for Iran is the subject of Chapters 45 and 46.

Historical Debate II

Was the Mongol War Caused Principally by the Otrar Incident, or Did Wider Imperial Tensions Make Conflict Increasingly Likely?

The question: Did one provocation produce the war, or was it the result of broader dynamics between two expanding powers?

Interpretation A — Otrar as the principal cause: The narrative sources present a causal sequence: seizure of merchants, refusal of redress, killing of envoys, invasion. On this reading, a failure of diplomacy at a particular moment turned a possible commercial relationship into war.
Interpretation B — Otrar as a trigger within a broader context: Others stress that the two empires had just become neighbors after the Mongol defeat of Kuchlug, that both were expansionist, that the Mongols had already shown a willingness to conquer large states, and that Mongol interest in the wealth of Central Asia was substantial. In this reading, Otrar provided the occasion or pretext, and a war might well have come for other reasons.

Evidence: Both views draw on the narrative sources for the incident and on the broader pattern of Mongol expansion. The Mongol side's own motivations are poorly documented in sources available to the Muslim historians.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and the weight of contingency against structure. It also concerns evidence, since the sources on Mongol decision-making are limited.

Why it matters: If a single incident caused the war, the invasion looks like a preventable tragedy. If wider tensions made war likely, then it appears more as a collision between two expanding powers, and responsibility is more widely distributed.

Section 44.16

Muhammad II's Retreat, Death, and the Collapse of Central Authority, 1220–1221

After the fall of Samarkand, Muhammad II withdrew southwest through Khurasan. Mongol detachments under experienced commanders pursued him, and he moved from city to city without establishing a stable base. By the account of the narrative sources, he lost confidence in his ability to hold the cities, split his remaining forces, and ultimately fled toward the Caspian. Explanations for this retreat include the collapse of morale, the fear of treachery from his own commanders, the loss of the Transoxiana heartland, and the difficulty of organizing a defense once the army was dispersed. Historians cannot recover his thinking with certainty, and caricatures of cowardice are as unsupported as defenses of a considered strategy.

He reached the Caspian coast, apparently near Abaskun in the Gurgan region, where he took refuge on a small island. He died there in December 1220 (some sources place the death in early 1221). Narrative accounts report that he was ill and had lost his possessions, and that before his death he named Jalal al-Din as his successor. The latter claim, preserved by al-Nasawi, a partisan of Jalal al-Din, should be treated with care. Whatever the circumstances, the empire's head died outside any city, without an army, and without an agreed successor.

In Gurganj, the situation was unstable. Terken Khatun, with the royal household and members of the family, withdrew from Gurganj toward Mazandaran in early 1220, before Muhammad II's death; she was eventually captured by the Mongols in 1221, and several of Muhammad's younger sons were killed. Among those at Gurganj, the Qangli commanders and members of the court disagreed over leadership, and the succession of Jalal al-Din was challenged. Mongol forces, including those under Chinggis Khan's sons Jochi, Chaghatai, and Ögedei, besieged Gurganj through the winter and early spring of 1221. The siege was reportedly hampered by disputes among the Mongol commanders, but the city fell around April 1221. Accounts of the siege and its aftermath report the breaking or failure of embankments and flooding from the Amu Darya, but they differ about whether deliberate diversion or the collapse of neglected waterworks caused the inundation, and about its timing. The Sufi master Najm al-Din Kubra was among those who died.

Meanwhile, Mongol forces swept through Khurasan in 1221, attacking Merv, Nishapur, Herat, and other cities. The destruction was severe in many places, though its scale is difficult to measure from the surviving sources, and Chapters 45 and 46 treat it in detail. What must be stressed here is that the sultan's defeat was not the same as the experience of every community. Some cities surrendered, others resisted and suffered, and some, such as Herat, rose in revolt after an initial submission and were punished. Local elites made their own decisions about whether to cooperate, flee, or fight.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of Khwarazmian Central Authority, 1219–1221

Long-term causes

Rapid expansion by conquest; weak integration of Khurasan, Afghanistan, Transoxiana, and western Iran; reliance on personal and tribal loyalties; the persistence of local elites and rival courts; absence of a stable succession rule.

Short-term causes

The conflict with the Abbasid caliphate and failed expedition of 1217–1218; the dispersal of the army among garrisons; distrust between the sultan and some commanders; and the sudden proximity of a new, formidable neighbor after 1218.

Immediate trigger

The Otrar incident of 1218 and the failure of the diplomacy that followed, leading to Chinggis Khan's decision to invade in 1219.

Event

The loss of Otrar, Bukhara, and Samarkand in 1219–1220; Muhammad II's flight and death in December 1220; and the fall of Gurganj in 1221.

Immediate effects

Destruction of cities and irrigation, death and displacement of populations, disintegration of imperial command, a contested succession, and the rise of local resistance.

Long-term consequences

The end of the Khwarazmian state, the incorporation of Central Asia and eventually Iran into the Mongol empire, and the political reorganization described in Volume IV.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the collapse. Mongol military capability mattered, as did Khwarazmian political weaknesses, strategic choices, and contingency. Chronology alone cannot establish causation, and historians weigh these factors differently.

Section 44.17

Jalal al-Din's Succession, the Battle of Parwan, and the Indus, 1220–1221

Jalal al-Din Mingburnu, also rendered Mankubirni, was Muhammad II's eldest son. His position at Gurganj in 1220–1221 was precarious. According to al-Nasawi, the leading Qangli commanders and members of the court favored another son, Uzlaq Shah, and Jalal al-Din learned of a plot against him and left the city with a small following. The account comes from his own secretary, and the specifics of the plot cannot be independently confirmed, but the underlying fact is not in doubt: the dynasty's military and court elite did not unite behind the strongest candidate at the moment when unity mattered most. Uzlaq Shah and other members of the family were captured by the Mongols and killed.

Jalal al-Din moved through Khurasan and across the Hindu Kush to Ghazna, the former Ghaznavid and Ghurid center, where he was able to gather troops. His new army was a coalition of Turkic commanders, Khalaj and Turkmen fighters, former Ghurid soldiers, and others, numbers uncertain. The evidence indicates that he was an energetic organizer who could attract and hold a following. In 1221, at Parwan, north of Kabul, his forces defeated a Mongol army commanded by Shigi Qutuqu, a senior commander and adopted kinsman of Chinggis Khan. The victory was significant: it showed that Mongol forces could be beaten in the field, and it became a symbol of Muslim resistance.

Success did not translate into durable recovery. According to the narrative tradition, a quarrel over the division of spoils, and in particular a dispute between commanders over a horse, led one of Jalal al-Din's senior commanders to withdraw with his troops, shrinking the army before the Mongols responded. Chinggis Khan then moved against Jalal al-Din in person. In late 1221 he caught up with him at the Indus, and in the ensuing battle the Khwarazmian army was routed. Jalal al-Din, according to the sources, escaped across the river with his horse, an act later celebrated in the narrative literature. The story is told in different forms, and details such as the height of the bank or the number of survivors should be treated with caution.

The sequence from Parwan to the Indus illustrates a pattern. Jalal al-Din could win battles and inspire loyalty, but his coalition was fragile. The commanders who joined him did so for varied reasons, and quarrels over spoils and rank could dissolve the army. The absence of an agreed political structure, an established bureaucracy, or a secure territorial base meant that a military victory gave only temporary advantage.

Section 44.18

Exile, India, and the Return to Iran, 1221–1225

After the Indus, Jalal al-Din crossed into the Indian subcontinent, where he spent roughly three years, from about 1221 to 1224. The sources, again principally al-Nasawi, describe a period of maneuver. He gathered survivors and recruited local troops, raided in the Punjab and Sind region, and sought alliances with local rulers. He sent an embassy to Iltutmish, the sultan of Delhi, seeking refuge or alliance, but the sultan, wary of a powerful and unpredictable fugitive and of the Mongols who pursued him, declined to commit himself. Relations with other local rulers, including those of the Khokhar groups and the ruler of Multan and Uch, were shifting, and Jalal al-Din's army lived partly off the land.

A Mongol detachment pursued him, and he defeated it in a skirmish, but the Mongol threat remained. By about 1223–1224, having concluded that he could not build a durable base in India, he turned westward and returned to Iran through the southern route by way of Makran and Kirman. The reasons for the return are mixed. India offered little prospect of a permanent state, while Iran offered the chance to recover territory that had once belonged to his father. The Mongol armies had withdrawn from much of Iran after their campaigns of 1220–1223, leaving a political vacuum filled by local rulers and ambitious princes.

On his return Jalal al-Din's objectives shifted. His campaigns in Iran in the following years combined an effort to organize resistance with the aim of establishing himself as a ruler. He subordinated his brother, Ghiyath al-Din Pir-Shah, who held parts of western Iran, and he dealt with local dynasts, including the atabegs who governed Fars and Azerbaijan. The goal of a defensive coalition against the Mongols gradually gave way to the practical pursuit of a kingdom, a transition that shaped the quality of his rule and his relations with neighbors.

Section 44.19

The Last Khwarazmshah: Azerbaijan, Georgia, Anatolia, and the End in 1231

In 1225 Jalal al-Din occupied Tabriz, the principal city of Azerbaijan, where the local atabeg dynasty had collapsed, and made it his base. From there he turned against the Christian kingdom of Georgia, which had been weakened by earlier Mongol raids but remained a significant regional power. He defeated a Georgian army in 1225 and took Tbilisi in 1226. The campaigns brought plunder and prestige but also deepened the devastation of the Caucasus, and the sources report destruction and the exaction of heavy tribute from conquered populations. The point here is that Jalal al-Din's wars fell on the same frontier societies that the Mongols would later subjugate.

His relations with Muslim neighbors worsened. He fought with the Ayyubid rulers of Syria and Mesopotamia and with the Seljuk sultanate of Rum in Anatolia, whose ruler, Kayqubad I, regarded him as a threat. The siege and capture of Akhlat (Khilat) in 1230 inflamed relations. In August 1230 a coalition of Seljuks of Rum and Ayyubids defeated Jalal al-Din near Erzincan. The defeat shattered his army and undermined his position. At the same time, Mongol forces, now under Chormaghan, advanced into Iran and Azerbaijan, and Jalal al-Din fled. He was killed in August 1231 by a local man, reportedly a Kurd, in the region near Mayyafariqin. With his death the Anushteginid dynasty effectively ended as a political force, though bands of Khwarazmian soldiers survived for some years, serving in Syria and Mesopotamia and, in the 1240s, taking part in warfare among the Ayyubid princes.

Jalal al-Din's career deserves a balanced assessment. He showed real military and organizational abilities, won an important victory at Parwan, rebuilt armies more than once, and resisted the Mongols when many others submitted or fled. He was not the only person to resist, and he has been remembered in later literature as a hero. But his wars against Georgia and neighboring Muslim states consumed resources and goodwill that a defensive coalition against the Mongols would have needed. Whether a successful coalition was possible is uncertain. The Ayyubids and Seljuks had their own reasons to fear a powerful, restless army on their borders, and Jalal al-Din's own decisions contributed to their hostility. The result, in 1230, was a battle in which Muslim powers defeated a Muslim leader who had fought the Mongols, while the Mongols were advancing.

Section 44.20

Chapter Synthesis and Review: Imperial Expansion, Political Fragmentation, and the End of Pre-Mongol Iran

The history of the Khwarazmian dynasty can be read in three stages. In the first, from about 1077 to 1172, a family of Seljuk slave commanders governed an important province, acquired hereditary tenure, and, as Seljuk power ebbed after 1141, learned to bargain with larger powers, above all the Qara Khitai. In the second, from 1172 to about 1215, a succession struggle, the use of outside support, steppe military connections, and a series of opportunistic victories over the Seljuks of western Iran, the Ghurids, and the Qarakhanids produced a very large territorial state. In the third, from 1218 to 1231, the empire encountered the Mongols, collapsed within a few years, and was succeeded by an effort at resistance that ended with Jalal al-Din's death.

The dynasty's government combined Persianate bureaucracy and Turkic military power. The secretaries and diwans inherited from the Seljuks gave it administrative continuity and a vocabulary of legitimacy; the Qangli and other steppe networks gave it an army. But the empire also contained competing centers of authority, loosely integrated provinces, and an unresolved relationship with the Abbasid caliphate. These features made it a powerful state without making it a cohesive one. The Mongol invasion exploited and exposed these weaknesses, but it was not the only reason for the collapse, and it was not merely the result of Mongol superiority or of one diplomatic mistake.

The consequences were large. The Khwarazmian collapse removed the main Muslim power between the Mongols and the lands to the west, opened the Iranian plateau to Mongol conquest, and devastated cities and irrigation systems. Yet the dynasty's end did not end Iranian society. Cities survived, often damaged; local elites negotiated new arrangements; the Persianate chancery tradition continued under Mongol rule; and writers such as Juvayni, who had served the Mongols, preserved the memory of the earlier world. Volume IV, beginning with Chapter 45, explains how the Mongols conquered Iran and how a new state emerged from the ruins.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Late Seljuk World to Mongol Domination

What Changed

  • The ruling dynasties of the eastern Islamic world, from Seljuks, Ghurids, and Khwarazmshahs to Mongol khans.
  • Many cities, irrigation systems, and populations suffered destruction, displacement, or death, with severity varying by place.
  • The imperial framework shifted from Sunni sultanates acknowledging the caliph to a non-Muslim imperial power whose rulers later converted (Chapters 47 and 48).
  • Political units grew larger, and the relationship between steppe and settled regions was reorganized.

What Stayed the Same

  • Persianate chancery and administrative practice, and the educated service class that maintained it.
  • The role of Turkic and steppe military power in the region.
  • Commercial networks across Central Asia and Iran, and the importance of cities.
  • The dependence of settled society on irrigation and on the revenue of cultivated land.

When and for whom: Change was abrupt in the cities that fell in 1220–1221 and more gradual elsewhere. Those who experienced it most directly were the inhabitants of besieged cities, deported artisans, and scholars who fled or died. Secretaries and administrators who entered Mongol service often experienced greater continuity.

Why the pattern matters: It prevents two errors: treating 1219–1231 as the total destruction of Iranian civilization, and treating it as a minor change of rulers.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Khwarazmian dynasty from its origins as a Seljuk provincial governorship to its destruction by the Mongols. It argued that the dynasty rose gradually, that its independence emerged from the weakness of its overlords, and that its empire, though large and in some respects well governed, was insufficiently integrated to survive the crisis of 1219–1221.

Khwarazm, the irrigated lower basin of the Amu Darya, provided the dynasty's base. Its canals, cities, and commercial position between the Iranian plateau and the steppe gave it wealth and military connections. The title Khwarazmshah long predated the Anushteginids, who inherited it along with a prosperous region and a scholarly tradition.

The family began with Anushtegin Gharchai, a Seljuk slave commander appointed governor around 1077, and his son Qutb al-Din Muhammad, who ruled from 1097 as a loyal governor. These were hereditary governors, not sovereign rulers. Atsiz, from 1127, sought more independence but was constrained by Sultan Sanjar and, after 1141, by the Qara Khitai, to whom the dynasty paid tribute. Il-Arslan ruled as the Seljuk order in the east dissolved.

Tekish, with Qara Khitai help and steppe military connections, won the succession struggle against his brother Sultan Shah. In 1194 he defeated Tughril III and extended Khwarazmian power into western Iran, bringing the dynasty into conflict with the Abbasid caliph. Muhammad II, who succeeded in 1200, exploited the collapse of the Ghurids after 1206 and the weakening of the Qara Khitai to absorb Khurasan, Afghanistan, and Transoxiana.

The empire was governed through a Persianate administration inherited from the Seljuks, in which secretaries wrote in ornate Persian and Arabic and diwans managed correspondence and finance, though the evidence for its inner workings is limited. The army rested on Turkic and especially Qangli–Kipchak commanders. Terken Khatun maintained a separate court and diwan and wielded substantial influence, creating a divided structure of authority that complicated coordination, though it should not be treated as the sole cause of collapse.

The empire's cities and countryside bore the costs of expansion through taxes, requisitions, and garrisons. Urban populations accepted Khwarazmian rule without deep loyalty. Muhammad II's challenge to the Abbasid caliph, culminating in the failed expedition of 1217–1218, cost him religious support and resources and illustrated the risks of seeking legitimacy through confrontation.

The Otrar incident of 1218 and the diplomatic failure that followed led to the Mongol invasion of 1219. Historians agree on the core events but differ on motives, responsibility, and whether broader tensions made war likely. Muhammad's strategy of dispersing garrisons, the empire's political divisions, and the effectiveness of Mongol operations combined to produce the fall of Otrar, Bukhara, and Samarkand and the sultan's flight and death in December 1220. Gurganj fell in 1221.

Jalal al-Din, Muhammad's son, organized resistance, won at Parwan, and was defeated at the Indus in 1221. After years in India he returned to Iran, established himself at Tabriz, and campaigned in Georgia and Anatolia. His wars against neighboring states and a coalition of Seljuks of Rum and Ayyubids brought defeat in 1230 and, in 1231, his death. His career shows both energy and the limits of a coalition without a secure political base.

The end of the dynasty transformed political authority but not Iranian society. Cities, commercial networks, chancery traditions, and intellectual life continued under new conditions. Volume IV takes up the Mongol conquest and the formation of the Ilkhanate, beginning with Chapter 45.

Khwarazm

The title of the Qara Khitai ruler.

The 1141 battle near Samarkand in which the Qara Khitai defeated Sultan Sanjar.

Payment made by a weaker ruler to a stronger one in return for recognition and protection.

An administrative bureau or its register, such as the diwans of correspondence, finance, and the army.
Chancery (diwan al-insha')
The bureau that drafted correspondence and decrees, staffed by secretaries trained in literary prose.
Iqtaʿ
An assignment of the revenue of a district, usually to a commander, in exchange for military service.
Vizier
The chief civil minister of a ruler.
Atabeg
A Turkic title for a guardian or tutor of a prince that became the title of regional rulers, as in Azerbaijan and Fars.
Qangli
A steppe group associated with the Kipchaks, whose commanders formed a core of the Khwarazmian army and court.
Kipchak
A confederation of Turkic-speaking pastoralists of the western steppe.
Terken Khatun
The queen mother of Muhammad II, of Qangli origin, who ran a separate court with its own diwan.
Ghurids
A dynasty from the highlands of Ghur in Afghanistan that conquered Ghazna and parts of northern India and collapsed after 1206.
Qarakhanids
Turkic Muslim dynasties of Transoxiana, including the rulers of Samarkand absorbed by Muhammad II.
Transoxiana
The region beyond the Amu Darya, including Bukhara and Samarkand.
Khutba
The Friday sermon in which a ruler's name was mentioned as a sign of recognition.
Abbasid caliphate
The Sunni caliphate centered at Baghdad, whose caliph al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225) pursued an active policy toward Iran.
Otrar incident
The 1218 seizure and killing of a trading party at Otrar and the failed diplomacy that followed, the occasion of the Mongol invasion.
Parwan
The 1221 battle north of Kabul in which Jalal al-Din defeated a Mongol army.
Persianate
A modern scholarly term for cultures and administrative traditions shaped by Persian language and bureaucratic practice, including in states ruled by Turkic dynasties.

The use of blockade, assault, engineering, and conscripted labor to capture fortified cities, central to the campaigns of 1219–1221.
Qanat
An underground channel carrying groundwater to the surface for irrigation and settlement.
Suzerainty
Overlordship in which a subordinate ruler retains local authority while acknowledging a superior.

Chapter Reflection

The Khwarazmian empire matters because it shows how quickly political structures could be assembled and how quickly they could break. It was built from the remains of the Seljuk, Ghurid, and Qarakhanid worlds by rulers who combined Persianate administration with Turkic military power, and for a short time it dominated a very large area. Its history illustrates the possibilities and the limits of rule by conquest in a region where distances were great, elites were powerful, and loyalty depended on personal ties.

The chapter also shows why careful historical reasoning matters. A simple story would say the Mongols were unstoppable, or that Otrar doomed the empire, or that Terken Khatun or Muhammad II failed. The evidence points to a more complex picture in which external force, institutional weakness, and contingent decisions combined. It also shows how much depends on sources written by survivors, secretaries, and historians who explained catastrophe in retrospect.

The chapter closes a volume. The Persianate administrative and literary traditions that this volume has followed from the Samanids and Ghaznavids to the Seljuks survived the dynasty's fall, carried by secretaries, scholars, and merchants into the new Mongol order. Volume IV begins with the invasions themselves in Chapter 45, with the question of damage and demographic change in Chapter 46, and with the Ilkhanate in Chapter 47, and it asks how a society that had been so profoundly disrupted reorganized itself under new rulers.

The story of Jalal al-Din, finally, is a reminder that the end of a dynasty is not the end of a society. The cities, languages, institutions, and communities of Iran and Central Asia continued, damaged but not extinguished, and the next chapters examine how.
