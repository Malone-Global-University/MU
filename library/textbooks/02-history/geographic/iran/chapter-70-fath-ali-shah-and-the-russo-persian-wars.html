Fath Ali Shah and the Russo-Persian Wars

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In October 1827 Russian troops entered Tabriz, the seat of Crown Prince Abbas Mirza and the base from which the Qajar monarchy had fought for over two decades to hold the Caucasus. The occupation was the culmination of a long contest, not its beginning, and it exposed how far the balance of power between two empires had shifted.

The entry of Russian forces into Tabriz in October 1827 is the right place to begin a chapter on Fath Ali Shah, because the city condenses the reign's central tensions. Tabriz was the capital of Azerbaijan province, a commercial entrepôt, and the residence of the heir to the throne. It was also the place where Qajar commanders had experimented with new infantry, artillery, foreign instructors, and imported weapons. Contemporary and later sources agree that the occupation was not accompanied by sustained fighting inside the city. They also agree that it was a severe political and psychological blow, and that it brought the second Russo-Persian war to the point of negotiation. The details of the occupation, discussed in Section 70.15, are less important at this stage than the question it raises: how had a dynasty that had reunified most of the plateau in the 1790s arrived at a position in which its second city could be occupied by a foreign army?

Answering that question requires returning to June 1797, when the founder of the dynasty, Agha Mohammad Khan, was killed in the Caucasus during a campaign, and his nephew Baba Khan succeeded him as Fath Ali Shah. The chapter follows Fath Ali Shah's reign, which lasted until 1834, in five movements. It first examines the monarchy he inherited: its court, its distribution of provincial authority among royal princes, and its military system. It then follows the first Russo-Persian war (1804–1813), the diplomatic search for European assistance, and the defeats of 1812 and 1813. A third movement examines the unstable interwar years and the conditions that led to renewed fighting in 1826. The fourth reconstructs the second war, which ended with the Treaty of Turkmenchay in February 1828. A final movement studies the political and social consequences of defeat, the killing of the Russian envoy Alexander Griboyedov in Tehran in 1829, the death of Abbas Mirza in 1833, and Fath Ali Shah's own death in 1834.

The chapter takes a particular stance toward the military record. It is tempting to treat the two wars as a straightforward story of European technological superiority and Iranian backwardness, and many older narratives, European and Iranian alike, did so. Historians now generally approach the subject with more attention to specific causes. The Russian Empire possessed larger and more reliable revenues, a more centralized command structure, and, by the late 1820s, battle-hardened forces freed from other commitments. But the Qajar state's difficulties also arose from logistical constraints, divisions among princes and commanders, the uncertain loyalties of Caucasian rulers, the unreliability of foreign allies, and the pressure of simultaneous conflicts with the Ottomans. Iranian rulers and commanders made deliberate decisions in response to these circumstances. Some were mistaken, some were reasonable gambles that failed, and some were responses to genuinely limited options.

The Caucasus themselves were not an empty arena. The region comprised Georgian kingdoms, Armenian communities under a variety of rulers and ecclesiastical authorities, Muslim khanates in Karabakh, Ganja, Shirvan, Baku, Shaki, and Talysh, Dagestani highland societies, and the Armenian-populated khanate of Yerevan. Their rulers calculated their own interests, shifted allegiances, and suffered the consequences of war. A history that treats them only as prizes would repeat the error, noted since Chapter 1, of reading the region from the perspective of the empires that contested it. This chapter therefore pays attention to Georgian, Armenian, and Azerbaijani-speaking communities throughout.

Evidence for the period is abundant but uneven. It includes Persian court chronicles and campaign histories written under royal or princely patronage, among them Mirza Fazlollah Khavari Shirazi's Tarikh-e Zolqarnayn and 'Abd al-Razzaq Donbuli's Ma'athir-e Soltaniyyeh, a history of the first war. It includes treaties and diplomatic correspondence in Russian, British, French, and Persian archives; travel accounts and memoirs by European diplomats and officers such as James Morier, Sir John Malcolm, and Sir Robert Ker Porter; Russian military and administrative publications; and the material record of royal painting, architecture, and rock reliefs. The sources were produced by court historians, imperial officers, and diplomats, each with distinct purposes. The voices of peasants, townspeople, pastoralists, and women are far harder to recover, and the chapter points out where they can be glimpsed only indirectly.

The chapter is bounded by its neighbors. Chapter 69 treated the formation of the Qajar state and Agha Mohammad Khan's conquests, including the 1795 Georgian campaign, and this chapter begins with the inheritance of 1797 rather than retelling them. Chapter 71 owns the detailed analysis of the Treaties of Gulistan and Turkmenchay: their territorial provisions, indemnities, commercial and legal clauses, and the long-term Iranian response. Chapter 72 will treat the larger Anglo-Russian competition in Asia, and Chapter 73 will examine reform institutions, including the military and administrative programs associated with Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir. Where this chapter touches on those subjects, it does so only as far as they shaped decisions during Fath Ali Shah's reign.

Finally, the chapter treats monarchy as an institution that continued to function through military failure. Fath Ali Shah's court produced paintings, poetry, buildings, and ceremonies that projected an image of imperial majesty, even as its army lost fortresses and provinces. The tension between that image and the strategic reality is one of the reign's most revealing features, and it frames the question with which the chapter ends: how did a monarchy that survived two major defeats confront the problem of preserving independence in a changing international system?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Describe the structure of the Qajar court, including ceremony, patronage, royal women, and dynastic representation, and explain its political functions.
  3. Analyze the system of princely governorships and the relationship between Tehran and provincial centers such as Tabriz, Shiraz, and Kermanshah.
  4. Assess the organization, recruitment, financing, and limitations of Qajar military forces at the start of the nineteenth century.
  5. Trace the origins of the first Russo-Persian war from the Russian annexation of eastern Georgia through the capture of Ganja in 1804.
  6. Evaluate Iranian diplomacy toward France and Britain, including the Treaty of Finkenstein and its collapse after Tilsit, as responses to strategic threat.
  7. Reconstruct the military reverses at Aslanduz and Lankaran and explain how they shaped the settlement of 1813.
  8. Distinguish the military end of a war from the political dispute over its settlement, using the interwar years of 1814–1825.
  9. Compare competing interpretations of responsibility for the outbreak of the second war in 1826, using the evidence available.
  10. Interpret the campaigns of 1826–1828 and explain why the capture of Yerevan and Tabriz proved decisive.
  11. Analyze how war affected agriculture, taxation, trade, civilians, and frontier communities, and how experiences differed across groups.
  12. Evaluate the consequences of defeat for the court, the clergy, and political culture, including the Griboyedov crisis and the succession of 1834.

Historical Context

By 1797 the Qajar dynasty had absorbed the legacy of nearly seventy years of fragmentation after the death of Nader Shah (Chapter 68). Agha Mohammad Khan had defeated the Zand successors, subdued Khurasan, and reasserted claims over the Caucasus. In 1795 his forces sacked Tbilisi, an act intended to punish the Georgian king Heraclius II for his alignment with Russia, and in 1796 he was crowned shah. The Qajars were originally a Turkmen tribal group associated with the Safavid military, and their rise rested on tribal military support, control of the northern provinces, and the elimination of rivals. Chapter 69 examined these developments. Students should retain from them the fact that the dynasty's authority in 1797 was real but young, built on conquest, and unproven in transfer.

The Caucasus were already a contested zone between Iranian, Ottoman, and Russian power. As Chapter 1 explained, the northwest provided routes toward Anatolia, Armenia, and the South Caucasus, and the Safavids and Ottomans had fought over the region in earlier centuries. From the early eighteenth century, the Russian Empire expanded south, and in 1783 the Treaty of Georgievsk placed the Georgian kingdom of Kartli-Kakheti under Russian protection. For Iranian rulers, Georgia and the neighboring khanates had been, in varying degrees, dependencies whose tribute, military service, and political loyalty were claimed by the shah. That understanding of sovereignty, rooted in Safavid and Afsharid practice, was not the same as the territorial exclusivity that Russian officials and European diplomats were beginning to apply. The difference between layered suzerainty and exclusive territorial sovereignty will recur throughout this chapter.

The Qajar state's institutional equipment was modest. It had a court and a bureaucracy of secretaries and fiscal officials inherited from earlier regimes, land revenue collected through governors and local notables, and tribal and household military forces. It did not possess a standing professional army of the type that European powers had developed, nor a bureaucracy that could assess and extract revenue uniformly across the provinces. The monarchy relied on personal relationships: kinship ties to Qajar tribal chiefs, patronage of provincial elites, marriages that bound families to the dynasty, and the cooperation of religious scholars and merchants. These arrangements could be remarkably resilient, but they were also hard to expand quickly in times of crisis.

European interest in Iran intensified in this period. The French Revolution and Napoleonic wars made India's northwestern approaches a strategic concern for Britain, which sought agreements with Iran in 1801 and later. France, in turn, explored an overland route to India and sought Iranian cooperation against Russia and Britain. The Ottoman Empire, itself under pressure from Russia, was both a rival and a potential partner. This web of relationships meant that the Russo-Persian wars were never simply bilateral contests; they were shaped by changes in European diplomacy, in Anglo-Russian relations, and in the Ottoman-Russian conflict. At the same time, as Chapter 72 will show, Iranian leaders were not merely reacting to European decisions. They pursued their own aims, and they judged each alignment according to local threats.

Division I · Fath Ali Shah and the Early Qajar Monarchy, 1797–1804 · Section 70.1

The Succession of 1797 and the Consolidation of Fath Ali Shah's Authority

Agha Mohammad Khan was killed in June 1797 at Shusha in Karabakh, where he had stopped during a campaign to reassert Qajar authority in the Caucasus. Accounts agree that he was murdered by members of his own entourage, but they differ on the immediate motives, which sources attribute variously to personal fear of punishment and to wider resentment. What matters for this chapter is the political situation that followed. The shah had no son. He had previously designated his nephew Baba Khan, then governor of Fars, as his heir, and the transfer of power depended on whether that designation would be honored by the army in the field, by Qajar chiefs, and by provincial commanders who had recently submitted to Qajar authority.

Baba Khan was in Shiraz when the news arrived. He moved north, and his claim was contested by rivals, including relatives who saw an opening in the founder's death. Sadeq Khan Shaqaqi, a chief of the Shaqaqi tribal grouping who had served Agha Mohammad Khan, challenged the new shah and was defeated; other relatives and commanders were pacified, neutralized, or incorporated through a mixture of force, pardon, and marriage ties. Baba Khan was crowned in Tehran in 1798 as Fath Ali Shah, taking a throne name that evoked victory, and he continued to treat Tehran as the principal Qajar capital. The speed of this consolidation should not obscure its fragility. The new shah had no record of conquest equal to his uncle's, and the dynasty's army, a coalition of tribal and household forces, owed its loyalty to a particular family and set of relationships rather than to an impersonal state.

The early years of the reign also involved changes in the central administration. Agha Mohammad Khan's governing circle, which included the long-serving administrator Hajji Ebrahim Shirazi, who had played a major role in the dynasty's formation, did not survive the transition intact. In 1801 Fath Ali Shah moved against Hajji Ebrahim and his family, and he was replaced by other ministers, a pattern that illustrates how the new shah asserted control over a government inherited from his predecessor. Historians note that purges of this kind were common in the early reigns of new dynasties, serving both to remove entrenched figures and to demonstrate the sovereign's authority. They did not, however, create a stable bureaucracy. Ministers rose and fell with the shah's favor, and administrative continuity depended on the families of secretaries and accountants who served successive rulers.

The succession mattered strategically as well. Agha Mohammad Khan's death in the Caucasus left unfinished business there, and the Qajar claim to Georgia, reasserted after the sack of Tbilisi in 1795, was not accompanied by durable control. Russian policy toward Georgia was developing at the same moment. Within a few years, Russian rulers moved to annex eastern Georgia directly, as Section 70.5 explains. Fath Ali Shah therefore faced simultaneous tasks: consolidating authority at home, creating institutions that would let the dynasty reproduce itself, and responding to Russian initiatives he could not ignore. It is a useful reminder that early Qajar rule was not a period of calm that war interrupted; the conditions that produced war were present from the outset.

The competing claimants at the beginning of the reign also exposed the difference between court recognition and effective provincial obedience. A governor who acknowledged the new shah might still postpone forwarding taxes, mobilize soldiers for private purposes, or negotiate with a neighboring chief. The shah's authority had to be demonstrated by appointments, rapid messages, exemplary rewards, and, when necessary, campaigns. News itself moved at the pace of mounted messengers; what seemed an instantaneous succession in later court histories was a sequence of decisions made by distant actors who lacked a complete picture of events in Tehran. That experience shaped Fath Ali Shah's later preference for distributing provinces among princes whose family interests could be aligned with his own, although doing so created rival regional power centers.

Section 70.2

The Qajar Court: Kingship, Ceremony, Dynastic Politics, and Royal Representation

Fath Ali Shah's court was an instrument of rule as well as a household. Its rituals, buildings, and images were designed to express a claim that the shah was the rightful successor to Iranian kingship, a claim made necessary by the dynasty's tribal origin and recent accession. Courtiers used the title shahanshah, "king of kings," a designation with Achaemenid and Sasanian associations (Chapters 9 and 23), and the shah styled himself in poetry under the pen name Khaqan. At the great annual audiences, above all at Nowruz, the Persian new year, courtiers, governors, and visitors appeared in a ceremony known as the salam, offering gifts, peshkesh, that symbolized loyalty and, in practice, supplied revenue. European envoys were received with equal ceremony, and the manner of their reception, including questions of dress, seating, and gifts, was itself a subject of diplomatic negotiation.

The architecture of the reign extended this language. Fath Ali Shah expanded the royal complex at Tehran that later became known as Golestan Palace, commissioned a marble throne in the early nineteenth century, and enlarged gardens and pavilions. A gilded throne in the palace associated with the reign is conventionally called the Peacock Throne (Takht-e Tavus); tradition links the name to Tavus Khanum, the shah's favored wife, though the connection is a matter of tradition rather than a settled fact. Rock reliefs carved at sites with older royal associations, including near Rayy, positioned the shah in a landscape layered with Sasanian memory. The monarchy thus drew on pre-Islamic imagery, Shi'i devotional practice, and Persianate court culture at the same time. It was not unusual for a single ruler to appear in the same year as a Kayanid hero of the Shahnameh and as a patron of Muharram mourning rituals.

Literary patronage was also political. A circle of poets at court, including the poet laureate Fath Ali Khan Saba, composed odes and a long epic poem, the Shahanshahnameh, in the manner of Firdawsi (Chapter 42), recounting the shah's reign in heroic terms. This work belonged to a wider literary movement sometimes called the literary return (bazgasht-e adabi), which looked back to the classical Persian poetry of earlier centuries. Court historians, such as Khavari, wrote chronicles that praised the monarch and interpreted events within a framework of royal legitimacy. Students should read such texts as evidence of how the court wished to be seen. They are also valuable sources of administrative detail, but they were written by clients of the dynasty.

Women were integral to court politics and not merely a feature of the royal household. The shah's household was very large, and contemporary and later accounts report numbers of wives and children that vary widely and cannot be fixed with confidence; the shah had scores of children. Marriage connected the dynasty to Qajar tribal chiefs, provincial notables, and Georgian and other elite families, and the status of a prince's mother mattered for succession. Abbas Mirza, for example, was chosen as heir in preference to an older brother, Mohammad Ali Mirza, in part because his mother belonged to the Qajar line, while the older brother's mother did not. Royal women also managed households, controlled resources, patronized religious foundations, and acted as intermediaries. Their voices survive mostly through male writers and European observers, so reconstruction requires care.

Ceremonial magnificence had costs. The shah's household, gifts, and buildings consumed revenue that might have supported military reorganization, and European visitors often remarked on the contrast between court splendor and provincial poverty. Yet it would be a mistake to interpret the court as mere extravagance. Display communicated that the monarch was powerful enough to command resources, that the dynasty was secure, and that its authority extended over a hierarchy of governors and notables. In a state that lacked the means to enforce obedience uniformly, ceremonial hierarchy helped maintain relationships. Its limits, however, were exposed when the monarchy faced a determined and well-supplied external enemy.

Historical Vocabulary
Shahanshah, Salam, and Court Patronage

Definition: Shahanshah is the Persian title "king of kings." Salam refers to a formal audience at which participants offered greetings and gifts to the shah. Court patronage designates the support the monarch gave to poets, painters, architects, and scholars in return for praise and service.

Historical context: The Qajar shahs used the title shahanshah to invoke a long Iranian tradition of kingship, while performing salam audiences on fixed occasions, especially Nowruz. Patronage tied artists and writers to the court and produced images and texts that presented the ruler as heroic, just, and magnificent.

Why it matters: These practices show how a dynasty without a bureaucracy capable of uniform control used ritual, gift exchange, and cultural production to build and signal political relationships.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat the title as evidence of actual imperial power equal to the Achaemenids or Sasanians. The title expressed a claim and a tradition; the Qajar state's real reach was far narrower.

Section 70.3

Royal Princes, Provincial Governors, and the Administration of the Monarchy

A defining feature of Fath Ali Shah's government was the assignment of provinces to his sons. Instead of relying primarily on tribal chiefs or local notables to govern the provinces, the shah installed princes as governors, supported by ministers, secretaries, and military retinues. This arrangement had precedents in Safavid and earlier practice, and its logic was clear. Princes were loyal by kinship, had a personal stake in the dynasty's survival, and could represent royal authority in distant areas. It was also a way of providing for the large number of the shah's sons. But it created a set of semi-autonomous princely courts, each with its own resources and ambitions, and it did so in a dynasty without a firm rule of succession.

The central figure in this system was Abbas Mirza, designated crown prince (vali'ahd) around 1799 and installed as governor of Azerbaijan, with Tabriz as his seat. His older half-brother Mohammad Ali Mirza, known as Dowlatshah, received the governorship of Kermanshah and the western frontier with the Ottomans, where he commanded forces and fought Ottoman and Kurdish opponents. Hosayn Ali Mirza governed Fars, the dynasty's southern base, and other sons governed provinces such as Khurasan, Isfahan, and elsewhere. Each prince collected revenue, appointed officials, raised troops, and remitted tribute and peshkesh to the shah. In theory the arrangement tied the provinces to Tehran through the royal family. In practice the flow of revenue and the discipline of governors depended on the shah's ability to monitor and, when necessary, discipline them.

Tabriz was particularly important. As the capital of a frontier province, it combined commercial functions with strategic ones. Its merchants linked Iran to Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Russian Empire; its position near the Aras River placed it close to the theater of war. Abbas Mirza's court there, staffed by capable secretaries and ministers, including the minister Mirza Bozorg Qa'em-maqam, became a center of administrative and military experimentation. The crown prince's position was also exposed. He had responsibility for the war against Russia but depended on the shah for money, troops, and policy decisions, and the other princes and courtiers had their own views on strategic priorities.

This pattern generated tensions that shaped events. Revenue was never sufficient for the army that war required, and governors competed for resources. Court officials sometimes favored caution; some princes and military men favored action. The shah's habit of balancing factions and dispensing favors could preserve his authority while impeding coordinated policy. Whether princes honored requests for troops, whether the northwest was adequately funded, and whether campaigns were coordinated across provinces, all depended on negotiations that were personal and political. Later in the chapter, we will see how these limits affected both wars. The central point here is that the same system that tied provinces to the dynasty also limited the capacity to mobilize the kingdom for a sustained campaign.

A princely governorship was not merely a title bestowed on a royal son. Each household had to sustain secretaries, tax officers, military commanders, servants, and clients whose expectations could compete with those of the royal treasury. Provincial notables might approach the prince to obtain protection from officials or confirmation of an endowment, while Tehran expected the governor to transmit revenue and deploy forces when summoned. Correspondence could produce a formal order, yet compliance might depend on the timing of harvest receipts and the willingness of district chiefs to cooperate. This was an administrative system adapted to a broad and socially varied monarchy, not simply evidence that the shah failed to understand government. Its weakness emerged when coordinated campaigns required several princely courts to make costly sacrifices at once.

Section 70.4

The Qajar Military System: Tribal Forces, Artillery, Revenue, and Mobilization

The army Fath Ali Shah inherited was a composite force. Its core was cavalry drawn from Qajar and other tribal groups, mobile, experienced in raiding and field warfare, and led by chiefs whose loyalty depended on patronage and kinship. Alongside the tribal contingents were household troops attached to the shah and to princes, provincial levies of musketeers (tufangchi), artillerymen, and garrisons in fortresses. Recruitment relied on a mix of tribal obligation, regional quotas, and personal retinues; pay came partly in cash, partly in assignments of revenue or land, and partly in the expectation of booty. Supplying large forces over long distances required the cooperation of local officials and communities, whose grain, fodder, and animals were requisitioned.

The system had real strengths. Tribal cavalry could move quickly across the plateau and the frontier, raid enemy supply lines, and defend mountainous terrain. It was cheap to maintain in peacetime, because many troops were mobilized only in campaigns. The dynasty had used it successfully to reunify much of the country under Agha Mohammad Khan. In conflicts with Ottoman neighbors, Afghan rulers, and internal rivals, it remained serviceable. But its limitations became apparent against an opponent that fielded disciplined infantry formations, mobile field artillery, and a logistical system capable of sustaining sieges and winter campaigns. Irregular cavalry was not well suited to holding fortified positions against volley fire, nor to storming or defending walled towns against modern artillery. Tribal contingents also dispersed after campaigns, making sustained operations and reorganization difficult.

Artillery and infantry were therefore the areas of greatest concern. Qajar forces possessed guns, including the light camel-mounted swivel guns known as zanburak, but their field and siege artillery were limited in numbers, standardization, and training. Infantry musketeers were often levied for a campaign, with variable training. Commanders understood these deficiencies, and experiments with newly trained infantry began in the early 1800s under Abbas Mirza, using deserters, foreign advisers, and recruits from the northwest. This early experimentation is introduced here only as context for the war narrative; the full history of the Nezam-e Jadid and of related reforms belongs to Chapter 73.

Revenue was the underlying constraint. The Qajar treasury depended on land revenue, customs, and gifts, and collection was uneven. Warfare increased costs at the very moment when disruption cut revenue from affected provinces. Pay arrears could erode discipline and loyalty. Even a well-conceived reform required steady funding, which the shah and governors found difficult to guarantee. Foreign subsidies, discussed in Section 70.8, were thus attractive: they promised money, equipment, and instructors without a wholesale restructuring of the state's finances. Evidence for military strength, however, comes largely from outside observers and official narratives, and numerical estimates of army sizes in this period vary so widely that the chapter avoids treating them as precise.

One distinction of importance is between the number of armed men who could be summoned and the number who could be maintained in the field. A provincial chief might promise cavalry whose horses and riders supplied much of their own equipment, but a long winter campaign required stored grain, cash payments, fodder, spare mounts, powder, and secure transport. The costs fell on governors, villages along army routes, merchants extending credit, and ultimately on the crown. If troops dispersed when their obligations expired, a fortress taken in one season might be lost the next. By contrast, an enemy able to rotate garrison battalions and provision artillery over successive months could turn limited battlefield victories into permanent territorial occupation. These differences were logistical and political, not an inherent distinction between Iranian and European soldiers.

Division II · The First Russo-Persian War and the Struggle for the Caucasus, 1804–1811 · Section 70.5

Russian Expansion, Georgia, Ganja, and the Outbreak of War, 1801–1804

The immediate background to the first war was the fate of eastern Georgia. After the Treaty of Georgievsk in 1783, King Heraclius II of Kartli-Kakheti had sought Russian protection, but Russian help had not prevented the sack of Tbilisi in 1795. Heraclius died in 1798, and the succession in the kingdom was contested within the royal family. In December 1800 Tsar Paul I issued a manifesto announcing the incorporation of Kartli-Kakheti into the Russian Empire, and his successor Alexander I confirmed the annexation in September 1801. The Georgian dynasty was set aside, and Russian administrators governed the territory. Some Georgian nobles accepted the change, while others resisted; Georgian princes, including members of the royal house, sought support in Iran and elsewhere. Georgian perspectives were therefore not unified, and the annexation was neither simply welcomed nor simply rejected.

For Fath Ali Shah, the annexation challenged a claim of suzerainty that Agha Mohammad Khan had pressed by force. The Qajar court regarded Georgia and the khanates as dependencies whose rulers owed obedience and tribute; the Russian government regarded Georgia as a Christian kingdom that had voluntarily sought protection and was now part of the empire. The two positions were not reconcilable by negotiation alone. Moreover, Russian policy under the commander Pavel Tsitsianov, appointed to the Caucasus in 1802, was assertive. A general of Georgian noble origin who had served in the Russian army, Tsitsianov sought to extend Russian control over the neighboring khanates and approached their rulers with demands for submission, backed by military force.

The decisive step came in January 1804, when Tsitsianov besieged and stormed Ganja, the capital of a khanate ruled by Javad Khan. Javad Khan was killed in the assault, the city was renamed Elizavetpol, and the incident demonstrated that Russia would take territory by force. Russian forces then moved toward Yerevan, the Armenian-populated khanate whose governor owed allegiance to the Qajars. In the summer of 1804 Tsitsianov's expedition besieged Yerevan, but a combination of Iranian resistance, heat, supply problems, and the arrival of an army under Abbas Mirza forced a Russian withdrawal. Fath Ali Shah, for his part, had already ordered mobilization. By mid-1804 a state of war existed, even if no single formal declaration marks its beginning in the sources.

This opening phase set patterns for the war. Local rulers calculated whether to resist, to submit, or to play the empires against each other. Population experience varied: Armenian communities in Yerevan and elsewhere faced the burdens of both Iranian and Russian armies and, in some cases, sympathized with Russia as a Christian power; Muslim communities in the khanates faced conquest and the loss of their rulers. Russian officers, many of whom regarded the campaign as one of civilization and order, and Iranian court writers, who described Russian advances as aggression against the shah's dominions, offered competing narratives that historians must treat as sources of evidence, not as neutral summaries.

The Georgian annexation was especially complicated because Georgian dynastic claims and Russian assertions did not exhaust the region's political identities. Some Georgian nobles regarded protection by Russia as a defense against repeated invasions, while others objected to the loss of a separate crown and to the treatment of the Georgian church. Armenian ecclesiastical institutions and merchant families maintained connections across competing jurisdictions. Khanate rulers in the eastern Caucasus possessed their own revenue, patronage, and military networks; an oath of allegiance signed under pressure could be reinterpreted when another army approached. Fath Ali Shah's advisers therefore confronted an interconnected set of local political decisions, not a single frontier that could be defended or relinquished at one stroke.

Section 70.6

The Campaigns of 1804–1806: Ganja, Karabakh, Shirvan, and Baku

After the failure at Yerevan in 1804, Russian operations shifted to a strategy of coercing the khanates one by one. Tsitsianov pressed rulers to accept Russian suzerainty, and several did so, sometimes after military pressure. In 1805 Ebrahim Khalil Khan of Karabakh, whose stronghold of Shusha was the site of Agha Mohammad Khan's death, concluded an agreement with Russia, an example of a local ruler choosing alignment with the stronger neighbor. Other khans, such as those of Shaki and Shirvan, also negotiated with Russian commanders at various points. These submissions were rarely stable. Rulers tested the balance of forces, and when Iranian armies approached, some changed sides, while others suffered reprisals from whichever power they had abandoned.

Iranian operations during these years were energetic but unevenly coordinated. Abbas Mirza advanced into the contested regions with forces drawn from Azerbaijan and tribal contingents, and he won some successes, as well as suffering reverses. A recurring difficulty was that holding territory required garrisons, supplies, and local cooperation, which were difficult to sustain across dispersed fortresses. Russian forces were smaller but better supplied from their bases in Georgia, and Tsitsianov's methods, which relied on rapid movement and disciplined fire, proved effective against irregular opposition. The campaigns also involved considerable violence against civilians, including sieges, requisitions, and the destruction of crops and villages, and accounts from both sides emphasize atrocity by the other.

In February 1806, Tsitsianov traveled to Baku to receive the surrender of its khan, Hosayn Qoli Khan. During a parley outside the city walls, he was killed. Accounts of the circumstances vary, and the episode has been read both as a treacherous ambush and as a confrontation that escalated from a tense meeting. What is clear is that the death of the Russian commander in chief deprived Russian operations of a driving figure, and the Russians withdrew from Baku for the time being. Baku itself was later taken by Russian forces and became part of the empire's holdings in the settlement of 1813. For the Qajar court, the episode was a reminder that local rulers could act effectively on their own; for Russian officers, it confirmed a view that the region required firm control.

Tsitsianov was succeeded by General Ivan Gudovich and then by other commanders, and the Russians pursued their aims in a more cautious style, with Russian attention divided by wars in Europe and against the Ottoman Empire, which began in 1806. In 1808 Gudovich's attempt to take Yerevan again failed, and the Iranian hold over the fortress of Yerevan, defended by the sardar Hosayn Khan Qajar, was a notable success. These years were in many respects a stalemate in which neither power could force a decision. The question for historians is why the stalemate eventually broke in Russia's favor. Section 70.10 returns to that problem, but part of the answer lies in the diplomatic maneuvers examined in Section 70.8 and in the shifting commitments of the Russian army.

The 1805 arrangements with khanate rulers created problems that military reports often conceal. A khan who accepted a Russian protectorate could seek to preserve local jurisdiction, yet Russian officers increasingly intervened in succession, garrison access, and external relations. Dependents of a ruler displaced or killed under Russian authority could then appeal to Tehran, while families fearful of an Iranian return sought security from a Russian commander. These competing petitions mattered because an army crossing a mountain route needed guides, fodder, and information before it could seek battle. In the Karabakh case the killing of Ebrahim Khalil Khan by Russian troops in 1806, amid suspicion and disputed reports of his intentions, deepened uncertainty about what Russian guarantees meant for a local ruler. Treating all such rulers as either loyal allies or traitors would obscure their restricted options.

Section 70.7

Abbas Mirza and the Conduct of the War: Command, Adaptation, and Military Learning

Abbas Mirza was in his teens when the war began, and he emerged as an active field commander as well as an administrator. He led armies in person, negotiated with local chiefs, and sought ways to improve his forces. His interests were practical. Encounters with Russian troops had shown the effectiveness of disciplined infantry fire and of mobile artillery, and in response he began to recruit and train infantry in the European manner, at first using Russian deserters and later foreign officers sent under diplomatic agreements. Among the deserters in Iranian service was Samson Makintsev, who rose to command a unit of Russian deserters. These men were valuable instructors but were also treated with suspicion, since their loyalty and status were uncertain.

The conduct of the war reflected both adaptation and constraint. Abbas Mirza used cavalry for scouting, raiding, and flanking, and he used fortified positions and artillery for defense. Intelligence networks drawn from local communities provided information, and he exploited the mobility of his troops to threaten Russian communications. But he also faced problems that training alone could not solve. Pay was uncertain; the army depended on the seasonal availability of tribal contingents; and the shah and court sometimes demanded actions that did not fit the crown prince's strategic judgment. Importing weapons or instructors did not by itself create a coherent institution. A new-style battalion needed uniforms, arms, ammunition, regular pay, officers, and a place in the army's command structure, and tribal chiefs whose status depended on traditional cavalry could resent a rival force.

Observers noted both the energy and the limitations of this early reform. European visitors who met Abbas Mirza often praised his intelligence and openness, while remarking on difficulties of discipline. Iranian writers associated with the court, such as Donbuli in the Ma'athir-e Soltaniyyeh, celebrated the prince's leadership and the army's efforts. These accounts are valuable but partial, and they were composed within a framework that emphasized the heroism of the dynasty. Students should therefore compare them with Russian official reports and British diplomatic correspondence, each with its own interests. The detailed institutional story of the reforms, including schools, factories, and fiscal measures, belongs to Chapter 73; the point here is that Abbas Mirza treated the war as a school and that adaptation was real but incomplete.

The use of foreign instructors illustrates the difference between learning a drill and constructing an institution. Translating commands, obtaining muskets of usable caliber, paying instructors, standardizing powder, and finding a reliable method to replace absent or killed recruits were separate tasks. Even where a battalion performed well at inspection, its effectiveness during a march depended on animals, pack routes, and officers who could communicate orders between cavalry contingents and infantry squares. The prince's administration in Tabriz had to negotiate with district leaders to obtain conscripts and with Tehran to sustain the treasury. Such practical constraints help explain why new-style infantry could become an important part of Qajar warfare without transforming the military balance automatically.

An additional complication rarely captured in descriptions of the new-style army was the movement of individual soldiers across the frontier. Deserters from Russian forces could be accepted into Iranian service, bringing familiarity with drill and weapons as well as creating diplomatic disputes about repatriation. Their presence complicates the picture of two neatly separated national armies, just as Armenian, Georgian, and Muslim officers could serve in changing configurations of authority. The Qajar military was not an isolated institution waiting for Europeans to introduce skills; it already contained accumulated practices from Safavid and Afsharid service and learned selectively from different sources. What it lacked was a consistently financed framework capable of integrating this knowledge at scale.

Section 70.8

Napoleon, Britain, and the Diplomatic Search for Military Assistance

Facing a stronger opponent, the Qajar court sought allies. Its first European partner was Britain. In 1801, John Malcolm, sent from British India, concluded political and commercial agreements with Fath Ali Shah, aimed at countering French influence and Afghan threats to India. The agreements provided little help against Russia, but they established a channel of relations. As Russian pressure increased, the court looked elsewhere. France under Napoleon had its own interest: an overland route to India and pressure on Russia and Britain. French emissaries reached Tehran in the mid-1800s, and an Iranian envoy, Mirza Mohammad Reza Qazvini, traveled to Napoleon's headquarters.

The result was the Treaty of Finkenstein, concluded on 4 May 1807 at a castle in East Prussia. In it, France promised to recognize Georgia as Iranian territory and to work toward Russian withdrawal, and to supply arms and military instructors; Iran undertook to break with Britain and to facilitate French operations toward India. It was a significant diplomatic achievement for the Qajars, and it reflected a reasonable assessment: France was a major land power at war with Russia, and its commitment seemed to offer practical support. But within weeks the strategic landscape changed. After French victories over Russian forces, Napoleon and Tsar Alexander I concluded the Treaty of Tilsit in July 1807, creating a Franco-Russian understanding. France no longer had reason to press Iranian claims against Russia.

General Claude Matthieu Gardane arrived in Tehran at the end of 1807 with a mission that included officers and engineers, and the French helped to train troops and to survey fortifications. But with Tilsit having transformed the context, the mission could not deliver the promised Russian withdrawal. Its members offered technical help but little political leverage, and Iranian officials gradually turned back to Britain, which now sought to restore influence. In 1808 Malcolm visited again without success, and in 1809 Harford Jones concluded a preliminary treaty under which Britain promised subsidies or assistance if Iran were attacked by a European power; Gore Ouseley's embassy followed, and a definitive treaty was concluded in 1812. The British connection brought arms, officers, and money, and it eventually led to British mediation of the peace of 1813.

Iranian diplomats were not naive. Their decisions followed the logic of balance: when a threat comes from one state, seek help from its rivals. The fault lay in the instability of European alignments, which could change within months, and in the fact that neither Britain nor France regarded Iranian security as its principal interest. The lesson, drawn by many Iranian statesmen of the period, was that foreign guarantees were conditional and could not substitute for domestic strength. It also became clear that diplomacy itself had costs: envoys, gifts, and missions consumed resources, and Iranian officials had to learn the languages, protocols, and legal categories of European diplomacy. Some of these lessons fed into the later reform discussions of Chapter 73, and the broader Anglo-Russian context belongs to Chapter 72.

Diplomatic missions also changed the information available to Iranian decision-makers. An envoy might report on military roads, the financial scale of a European treasury, or the ceremonial standing accorded to an Iranian sovereign, but reports had to compete with rumors arriving from agents in the Caucasus and with the interests of men who had promoted one alliance over another. Gardane's officers provided training even after the political foundation of their mission had weakened; British envoys, by contrast, linked military assistance to restrictions shaped by Britain's relations with Russia. A commitment drafted in Europe could thus be simultaneously genuine as a promise and precarious as a means of defense. This gap between formal diplomacy and enforceable military aid became visible again when war resumed in 1826.

Prolonged Warfare: Fortresses, Supply, Geography, and Regional Resistance

Between 1807 and 1811, the war continued at a lower intensity than in its first years. Both sides were exhausted and distracted: Russia was engaged against the Ottoman Empire and, increasingly, against the threat of Napoleonic invasion; Iran had to manage the fallout of its diplomatic shifts and the demands of the western frontier. Operations focused on fortresses and the control of valleys and passes. The Qajar position in Yerevan, Nakhchivan, and the approaches to Azerbaijan held, while Russia maintained its control in Georgia and over several khanates. This was not a static front. Commanders conducted raids, besieged fortresses, and negotiated with local rulers, and the contest between the empires was matched by contests within the khanates.

Geography shaped each side's options. The Aras formed a natural barrier whose crossing points were limited; the highlands of Karabakh and the mountains around Lake Sevan restricted movement; the lowland Mughan steppe permitted maneuver but exposed armies to summer heat and disease. Winter closed passes and slowed supply. Fortified towns, with their walls, wells, and stored grain, could resist for weeks against forces without heavy artillery, but a determined siege with modern guns could succeed. For Abbas Mirza, a recurring problem was that the army could concentrate for a campaign but could not easily sustain operations over months, especially far from Tabriz. For the Russians, the long, thin line of communication through Georgia and the Caucasus road, together with the need to garrison conquered territory and to guard against highland resistance, imposed limits of its own.

Regional resistance complicated the war for both. Highland communities in Dagestan, Georgian rebels such as those who rose in Kakheti in 1812 with the support of an exiled Georgian prince, and Muslim and Armenian populations in the khanates each pursued their own aims. Russia faced insurgency behind its lines; Iran faced the unreliability of allies whose support depended on the apparent balance of forces. These dynamics were not secondary to the war. They meant that every advance and withdrawal changed the local political balance, and that fortress commanders and khans had to guess which empire would prevail. A narrative that follows only the armies of the two empires would miss this layer of the conflict.

Foreign assistance entered the picture again. After the British treaties of 1809 and 1812, British officers, including Captain Christie and others, trained and sometimes led Iranian infantry and artillery, and Britain provided arms and subsidies. This help was real but also had political consequences: it gave Britain influence over Iranian military and diplomatic decisions, and it made Iran dependent on British good will at the moment Britain was Russia's ally against Napoleon. By 1812 these dependencies would matter a great deal.

The administrative geography of the war was therefore as important as the military geography. Soldiers who held a fortress needed access to neighboring fields and water, but cultivation could not be assumed where repeated marches had removed livestock and grain. A khan's envoys might assure Abbas Mirza that a valley would provision his advance, yet the same valley might already have been assessed by Russian officials or damaged by raiders. Conversely, Russian troops carrying standardized weapons did not escape hunger, disease, or desertion, particularly when garrisons lay far from the main Caucasus road. The uncertain frontier encouraged both governments to seek quick operations that could force a diplomatic concession, but every indecisive season increased the fiscal cost and embittered the population on whom future operations depended.

Section 70.10

Aslanduz, Lankaran, and the Military Defeats of 1812–1813

The strategic situation changed in 1812. Napoleon's invasion of Russia in June of that year tied down the bulk of Russian forces, but it also made Russia more anxious to settle accounts in the south. The tsar's Caucasus commander, General Nikolay Rtishchev, and his field officer Pyotr Kotlyarevsky pursued an energetic campaign. Abbas Mirza, meanwhile, had assembled an army that included new-style infantry, artillery, and cavalry, along with British officers serving under the terms of the Anglo-Persian arrangements. In the autumn he crossed the Aras and advanced toward the Mughan steppe, intending to push Russian forces from positions in the Talysh region.

On the night of 31 October (Gregorian dating) 1812, Kotlyarevsky, with a force much smaller than Abbas Mirza's, attacked the Iranian army near the Aras crossing at Aslanduz. Accounts of the battle differ in detail, but they agree on its outline: the Russians used a surprise approach, and in the ensuing fighting the Iranian army, which included the new-style infantry, was broken and forced to retreat across the river. The British officer Christie was killed in the fighting. Russian sources describe a decisive victory against a numerically superior enemy; Iranian and British accounts emphasize confusion, the loss of artillery, and the difficulty of coordinating tribal and new-style units. Casualty and troop estimates vary so widely that they cannot be treated as precise, and students should regard numbers in the literature with caution.

The war's second major blow came in January 1813, when Kotlyarevsky stormed the fortress of Lankaran on the Caspian coast, which was defended by a garrison under the Iranian commander Sadeq Khan. After refusing a call to surrender, the defenders were overwhelmed in a bloody assault in which Russian sources report heavy casualties on both sides and the death of Sadeq Khan. The capture of Lankaran removed a key Iranian position in the Talysh region and signaled the Russian ability to take a fortress by assault. The British government, which had agreed to assist Iran if attacked, also found that its alliance with Russia against Napoleon limited its freedom of action. British officers were recalled from Iranian service in some cases and the terms of British support were questioned.

Together, these defeats damaged Qajar bargaining power. They destroyed much of the army that had been trained since 1804, dispelled the hope that the new-style infantry could by itself change the military balance, and showed that Iranian commanders would have to rely on diplomacy. The human costs were severe, and the battles had consequences for families and communities that supplied soldiers and provisions. Historians continue to discuss the causes of the defeats, and the next box frames that discussion.

Aslanduz also illustrates the danger of inferring a whole military system's worth from one engagement. A surprise attack against a camp, especially at night, tested sentries, the spacing of units, the readiness of horses, and commanders' capacity to rally separated formations. It did not establish that every soldier trained by foreign officers was ineffective. Nor did the Russian success mean that Russian forces had overcome every operational weakness on the southern frontier. At Lankaran the attackers paid heavily for a storming operation; the outcome depended on persistence in a confined space and the inability of defenders to obtain relief. In both cases military losses mattered politically because they deprived Abbas Mirza of experienced troops and field artillery he could not replace quickly through the treasury.

Historical Debate

Explaining the First Russo-Persian Military Defeat

The question: Why did Iran lose the first war, and in particular why did battles such as Aslanduz and Lankaran go against forces that were numerically larger or that included newly trained infantry?

Interpretation A — Military and technological disparity: Some accounts emphasize the superiority of Russian discipline, artillery, and tactics, together with the limits of Iranian training and equipment. On this view, even reformed units were too new and too few to match veteran Russian troops.
Interpretation B — Logistics, command, and politics: Other accounts stress structural problems: uncertain pay and supply, the difficulty of coordinating tribal and new-style forces, divisions among princes and chiefs, and the instability of allies in the khanates. On this view, technology mattered, but organization and political context were decisive.
Interpretation C — The wider international context: A third emphasis falls on the diplomatic environment: the collapse of French support after Tilsit and the constraints on British assistance meant that Iran fought without the backing it had expected, while Russia's ability to concentrate forces improved as the Napoleonic threat changed.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on Russian military reports, British officers' accounts, Persian court chronicles, and diplomatic correspondence. Each source reflects the interests of its author, and the Russian and Persian narratives of particular battles often cannot be reconciled in detail.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ chiefly on relative weight, not on whether any single factor was irrelevant. Evidence for casualty figures, army sizes, and exact dispositions is limited and contested.

Why it matters: If defeat was primarily technological, the obvious remedy was imitation; if organizational and political, reform required changes in finance, command, and state structure. These contrasting diagnoses shaped debates among Iranian officials about reform, taken up in Chapter 73.

Section 70.11

The End of the First War and the Political Crisis of 1813–1814

By mid-1813 the strategic situation favored Russia, whose forces had defeated Napoleon's army and whose attention could turn more fully to the Caucasus. Iran, for its part, sought a settlement that would limit losses. The British ambassador Gore Ouseley, then in Iran, offered mediation, and negotiations took place with Rtishchev. The resulting Treaty of Gulistan, signed on 24 October 1813 in a village in Karabakh, ended the war. Its terms, examined in detail in Chapter 71, transferred to Russia a large part of the territory in dispute, including Karabakh, Ganja, Shirvan, Baku, and others, and confirmed Russian control over Georgia and neighboring areas. The treaty also contained provisions on trade, the Caspian Sea, and provisions concerning recognition of the legitimate Qajar succession.

The response within Iran was far from uniform. For Fath Ali Shah the treaty was a painful necessity, but he and his court sought to reinterpret it as a pause rather than a permanent renunciation. Abbas Mirza had fought the war and had to answer for it; he pressed for military preparation and for the reform of the army, while seeking to preserve his standing and the treaty's guarantee of Russian recognition of his succession. Others at court blamed commanders or foreign advisers; some elites regarded the defeat as evidence that Iran needed new institutions, while others saw it as a result of mistakes in strategy. These views were not stable categories, and individuals shifted among them as circumstances changed.

Provincial elites and local communities also reacted. Rulers in the ceded khanates faced the choice of accepting Russian authority, moving to Iran, or resisting; many families migrated southward, and Russian administrators appointed new officials. In the territories that remained under Qajar control, the war's costs prompted additional taxation and requisition, a point examined in Section 70.17. The political crisis of these years was therefore not a crisis of dynasty but a crisis of policy and resources: the monarchy survived, but its strategic position and its relations with its commanders and subjects had changed.

Negotiation in 1813 should be read in light of what each government feared might happen if it refused to sign. The Iranian side faced the prospect that another campaign would expose still more towns and create further disaffection among provincial forces. The Russian command had occupied territory but had to retain garrisons while much of the empire was at war with Napoleon; it too could value a settlement. British mediation supplied a channel between adversaries, not an independent means of compelling an equitable bargain. For communities whose rulers had once negotiated tribute to Isfahan or Tehran, a change of sovereignty was already being experienced through taxes, courts, military posts, and the appointment of new officials before treaty language formalized it. The agreement therefore concluded a military phase without resolving political allegiance on the ground.

The human meaning of the treaty differed from the diplomatic meaning of possession. A family whose land lay on one side of a newly recognized frontier might have business partners, seasonal pasture, or kin on the other. Local courts and commanders did not necessarily change practice immediately when an agreement was signed far away. Russian administrators and Iranian envoys contested what had actually been conceded, while residents continued to seek protection from the authority most able to safeguard their property. These discrepancies between paper settlement and government on the ground help explain why the first peace left room for grievances that endured into the next war.

Cause and Effect

From Battlefield Defeats to the Peace of 1813

Long-term causes

Russian expansion into the Caucasus, the weakness of Qajar revenue and military organization, and the uncertain loyalties of Caucasian rulers.

Short-term causes

The collapse of French support after Tilsit, British constraints during the Napoleonic wars, and the defeats at Aslanduz and Lankaran.

Immediate context

Napoleon's retreat from Russia freed Russian attention and resources, while the Qajar court faced the possibility of further losses.

Effects

A treaty that transferred territory to Russia, a political reassessment within the Qajar court, and a renewed commitment to military reform, along with a lasting dispute over the settlement.

Historical complexity: The peace was shaped by European diplomacy as well as by battlefield results. A purely military explanation overlooks British mediation and the Napoleonic wars; a purely diplomatic one underestimates the effect of Aslanduz and Lankaran on Iranian bargaining power.

Section 70.12

The Interwar Years: British Diplomacy, Border Disputes, and Renewed Tensions, 1814–1825

The peace did not settle the frontier. Gulistan referred to boundaries in language that left room for dispute, and its provisions on the demarcation of lines, notably in the regions around Lake Gokcha (Sevan) and the Talysh, were contested. Iranian officials argued that the treaty's wording had been misapplied; Russian officials argued that Iran was seeking to evade its terms. The result was recurring friction. Russian administration in the Caucasus was often harsh: General Aleksei Yermolov, who commanded from 1816, favored a policy of military pressure on both the khanates and the highland societies of Dagestan and Chechnya. His diplomatic mission to Tehran in 1817 failed to resolve disputes, and Iranian observers interpreted his posture as evidence of Russian intentions.

British diplomacy sought to stabilize the situation. In 1814 Britain and Iran concluded a definitive treaty that replaced earlier arrangements. Its terms modified British obligations: assistance was linked to Iran's being attacked and not to disputes in which Iran was judged the aggressor, and Britain would provide subsidies in specified circumstances. Iranian officials understood the limits of this commitment, and later events showed that the British government would interpret the treaty narrowly. Meanwhile, British officers remained in Iranian service, and Iranian envoys, including Mirza Abu'l-Hasan Khan, traveled to London in 1809–10 and again in 1819, expanding Iranian knowledge of Europe. The wider consequences of Iranian-European contact belong to Chapters 72 and 73.

Frontier pressures were not limited to the north. In 1821 a war broke out with the Ottoman Empire, in which Abbas Mirza led forces on the western front against Ottoman and allied forces; epidemic disease affected the armies, and a peace at Erzurum in 1823 restored the status quo. The Ottoman war diverted resources, reminded the court of competing demands, and showed that the northwestern army could still operate effectively in some conditions. At the same time, Mohammad Ali Mirza's death in 1821 removed the governor of Kermanshah and altered family politics, reinforcing the position of Abbas Mirza as heir.

Perceptions of Russia were shaped by merchants, scholars, and officials. Merchants in Tabriz and elsewhere dealt with Russian trade restrictions and with the movement of goods through the Caucasus. Religious scholars, some of whom had written on the duty to defend Muslim lands, debated the status of Muslims under Russian rule and the obligations of the community. Diplomats and statesmen assessed the possibility of renewing the conflict. By 1825 the frontier was a place of friction, the peace was widely regarded as provisional, and the court was divided over whether to seek revision by arms or by negotiation. That division, examined in Section 70.13, became decisive in 1826.

Two specific sources of tension persisted through these years. First, surveying a boundary required officials to decide which villages, pastures, and waterways were intended by earlier diplomatic formulas; communities using seasonal grazing grounds might not recognize the line now being drawn across their customary routes. Second, military commanders on each side could harden their government's position by occupying a disputed post or supporting a dissatisfied local leader. The government in Tehran faced demands to redress grievances from displaced families and religious figures; Russian officers in Tbilisi faced pressure not to surrender positions secured at great cost. Disagreement was therefore reproduced by administrators as much as by monarchs. A durable settlement required a measure of local trust that neither empire was well placed to provide.

Historical Vocabulary
Suzerainty, Sovereignty, Frontier, and Diplomatic Guarantee

Definition: Suzerainty is overlordship in which a dependent ruler retains local authority while owing tribute or allegiance. Sovereignty, in European diplomatic usage, denotes exclusive authority within a defined territory. A frontier in this period could be a zone of overlapping claims rather than a precise line. A diplomatic guarantee is a promise by one state to support another in specified circumstances.

Historical context: Qajar claims over the khanates rested on suzerainty; Russian treaties and European diplomacy emphasized exclusive sovereignty and surveyed boundaries. Treaties often combined the two vocabularies, which created ambiguity.

Why it matters: The disputes of 1814–1825 and the arguments over responsibility in 1826 turned partly on these differing concepts, as well as on the interpretation of specific treaty clauses.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that either vocabulary was more "modern" in every respect or that Iranian officials were unaware of European legal categories. They learned them, and used them, over the course of the period.

The Second Russo-Persian War, 1826–1828

A disputed frontier, renewed mobilization, unequal military campaigning and diplomacy under occupation.
Division IV · The Second Russo-Persian War, 1826–1828 · Section 70.13

Why War Returned in 1826: Frontier Disputes, Court Politics, and Religious Mobilization

The immediate sources of the second war lay in the unresolved frontier. In 1825 Russian forces occupied a district near Lake Gokcha (Sevan) whose status was disputed under the terms of Gulistan, and Iranian officials protested. Further incidents followed on the Talysh and Karabakh frontiers, and each side accused the other of violating the peace. Tsar Alexander I died in December 1825, and the accession of Nicholas I, which was followed by the Decembrist uprising in St Petersburg, created a period of uncertainty in Russian politics. Iranian officials differed over whether this moment offered an opportunity or a danger. The Russian government dispatched Prince Aleksandr Menshikov to Tehran in 1826 to negotiate, with the aim of resolving the Gokcha question and preventing war; Iranian authorities received him but the talks failed to produce agreement.

Within the Iranian court, opinion was divided. Abbas Mirza, who knew the military balance firsthand, was cautious about renewing the war but was under pressure from several directions. Court factions, including ministers who favored action, argued that Russia's internal troubles and the grievances of Muslim communities in the ceded khanates created an opening. Clerical scholars joined the debate. Influential mojtaheds, among them Sayyed Mohammad Mojahed, who traveled from the shrine cities of Iraq to Iran in 1826, issued rulings calling on the shah to wage jihad against Russia and describing the loss of Muslim territory as a religious wrong. Such rulings carried moral weight with the public and placed pressure on the shah and crown prince. Historians debate how far they initiated the decision for war and how far they amplified a decision already favored by court factions.

Fath Ali Shah, who had personally avoided direct leadership of the northern campaigns, ultimately authorized military action, and in the summer of 1826 Iranian forces crossed the frontier into the disputed districts. The decision involved a gamble. Iranian leaders hoped to recover territory quickly and to present Russia with a fait accompli, exploiting local discontent. They also hoped for British support, though the 1814 treaty's terms made such support uncertain. These expectations proved mistaken. The tsar's government interpreted the Iranian advance as aggression, and Britain, citing the treaty, did not provide the subsidy Iran sought.

The dispute over the eastern shore of Lake Sevan, widely called Gokcha in contemporary accounts, shows why the categories of aggression and defense were themselves contested. Russian officials asserted that posts occupied under their interpretation of Gulistan belonged to the empire; Iranian officials treated some of the same positions as encroachments upon districts still owing allegiance to the Qajar shah. The seizure of Mirak in the Yerevan region in 1826 sharpened those complaints. Cartographers, surveyors, khans, and frontier commanders all supplied competing information. Diplomatic exchanges in the capital could not cancel the consequences of a new garrison established on a pasture route or of villagers moved under duress. The unresolved character of the frontier was a specific institutional failure of the peace, not merely an abstract grievance about lost national territory.

The politics of calling for war were similarly layered. Religious leaders who spoke in the language of defending Muslim communities had their own standing with pilgrims, merchants, and local judges; their demands need not have been invented by the palace to be politically useful to factions there. Abbas Mirza had reason to doubt the sustainability of another long war, but also faced criticism if he appeared unable to defend the territories for which his office in Azerbaijan was responsible. Fath Ali Shah's court had to weigh prestige against the likelihood of Russian reinforcement. The arrival of Menshikov during this climate did not produce a neutral arbitration of the border: both sides arrived with prior commitments about what they could concede. Scholars differ in the emphasis they place on Russian provocations, Iranian preparations, religious pressure, and expectations of Russian weakness; a sound interpretation keeps these causes in view together.

Historical Debate

Responsibility for the Outbreak of the Second War

The question: Who bears primary responsibility for the war of 1826: an Iranian government that sought to reverse Gulistan by force, or a Russian administration whose frontier policy made war likely?

Interpretation A — Iranian initiative: Russian and British contemporaries, and many later writers, held that Iran crossed the frontier first and was the aggressor. On this view, court factions and clerical agitation pressed a reluctant crown prince into a war the government could not win.
Interpretation B — Russian frontier pressure and Iranian defensive calculation: Some recent scholarship argues that Russian actions, such as the occupation of the Gokcha district and harsh frontier administration, left Iranian leaders with few alternatives, and that Iranian decision-making should be read as defensive or reactive rather than gratuitously aggressive.
Interpretation C — Mutual escalation: A third approach emphasizes that both governments pursued policies that made conflict likely, and that attributing "first" responsibility depends on which incidents are treated as the starting point.

Evidence: Diplomatic correspondence in Russian, British, and Persian archives; treaty texts; the reports of Menshikov and other envoys; and accounts by court chroniclers and clerical writers. Each is shaped by the interests of its author, and the British refusal to grant a subsidy reflected a legal and political judgment, not neutral fact.

Points of disagreement: The disputes concern chronology (which incident came first), terminology (the meaning of treaty clauses on boundaries), and interpretation (whether the decision for war was rational given what the court knew).

Why it matters: The question affects how we assess Iranian agency. If the war was simply reckless aggression, the failure was one of judgment; if it was a response to pressure, the story is partly one of constraint. Neither view requires denying the mistakes that were made.

Section 70.14

The Iranian Offensive of 1826 and the Reversal at Ganja

In the summer of 1826, Iranian forces advanced into Karabakh and toward Ganja, and Abbas Mirza's army besieged Russian-held positions, including the fortress of Shusha. The early phase brought real gains. Parts of the Talysh and the Muslim populations of several districts rose or declared for Iran, and Iranian troops recovered Lankaran. These successes demonstrated that the Russian hold on the khanates remained fragile and that some Muslim communities preferred Iranian to Russian rule or at least responded to Iranian military presence. But the advance was also vulnerable. It depended on the quick collapse of Russian resistance, and it left the army stretched across fortresses and districts that could not all be supplied or defended.

The Russian command, initially under Yermolov, was slow to respond, and the Tsar sent General Ivan Paskevich to the Caucasus as a trusted commander who soon took effective control. In September 1826 Russian forces defeated Iranian detachments at Shamkhor and then confronted Abbas Mirza's main army near Ganja in a major battle in late September. The Russians, outnumbered, used concentrated fire and decisive maneuvers, and the Iranian army was forced to retreat. Russian sources describe a complete victory, while Iranian accounts, more guarded, stress the difficulties of the terrain and the lack of coordination among contingents. Casualty figures differ widely.

The Ganja reversal shifted the strategic initiative. Iranian forces fell back across the Aras, the territories briefly regained were lost, and Russia moved from defense to planning an offensive. Abbas Mirza's strategic objectives, to exploit local discontent and recover Karabakh and Ganja before Russia could mobilize, had failed. The reasons included the tendency of tribal contingents to disperse, difficulties of supply, and the speed with which Russia concentrated forces. The shah and court, which had counted on a short war, now faced a longer conflict without British subsidies and with the army exhausted.

The episode also illustrates the difficult position of Caucasian Muslim and Armenian communities. Those who had joined the Iranian cause or welcomed it faced Russian retaliation, and many left with the retreating army. Those who stayed had to adapt to renewed Russian authority. The movement of people during these months foreshadowed the larger displacements of the following year.

The opening offensive achieved results because Russian authority in a number of khanates was still recent and unevenly accepted. Local commanders could furnish horses and intelligence, while the appearance of an Iranian army encouraged some former ruling families and their dependents to reconsider their allegiance. Yet the very dispersion that made the advance impressive on a map imposed severe difficulties. Contingents sent toward Shusha, Ganja, and the Caspian shore could not support one another quickly, and each besieged fortress required stores and men to prevent Russian relief. A victorious advance also called forth reprisals and counter-reprisals in districts where neighbors had supported opposing authorities; the campaign cannot be reconstructed merely as two regular armies exchanging positions.

The battle near Ganja should be analyzed through the sequence that preceded it. Russian commanders recovered the initiative at Shamkhor and then concentrated on the main Iranian force before the Qajars could establish a secure line through the region. Abbas Mirza faced the problem of combining units trained for line combat with cavalry whose commanders exercised substantial autonomy. Russian officers, for their part, had to commit forces before all reinforcements arrived and could not disregard resistance behind their positions. The defeat did more than end a single march: it removed the prospect of compelling concessions through rapid occupation and left the Iranian court responsible for funding a prolonged defensive struggle. Those who had welcomed Iranian forces were now exposed to the return of Russian military administration.

Section 70.15

The Campaign of 1827: Abbasabad, Yerevan, Nakhchivan, and Tabriz

In 1827 Paskevich, who replaced Yermolov as commander in the Caucasus, planned a systematic campaign to capture the fortresses that guarded the approaches to Azerbaijan. Russian forces used the ecclesiastical complex of Echmiadzin as a military station and place for wounded men during the campaign; in July they took the fortress of Abbasabad near Nakhchivan, a modern fortification that had been designed with the advice of European engineers. Abbas Mirza attempted to relieve or threaten these positions, and in August fighting near Ashtarak (Oshakan) brought an Iranian tactical victory against the Russian relief force, although surviving Russians reached Echmiadzin and the larger Russian offensive continued. In these campaigns, the Russians relied on disciplined infantry, effective artillery, and a logistical system able to sustain siege operations through the summer.

The central objective was Yerevan. The fortress, defended by Hosayn Khan Sardar, had resisted Russian attacks in 1804 and 1808. In 1827 the situation was different: Iranian field forces had been defeated, relief was unlikely, and Russia brought heavy artillery. After a siege and assault in the autumn, with the nearby fortress of Sardarabad also taken, Yerevan fell in early October. The capture was of great symbolic and strategic importance. It removed the principal Iranian stronghold in the Armenian highlands and opened the way for operations south of the Aras.

With Nakhchivan and Yerevan in Russian hands, Russian forces under General Eristov advanced into Azerbaijan and entered Tabriz in October 1827. Accounts agree that the city was not defended by a major battle at its walls. The crown prince's army had been weakened, and local leaders, merchants, and clerics negotiated the terms of occupation. The occupation brought requisitions, the presence of foreign troops in a major commercial city, and the emotional shock of seeing the heir's capital under foreign control. The Russian advance continued into early 1828, when Urmia and Ardabil were occupied; the seizure of manuscripts from Ardabil's shrine of Shaykh Safi al-Din belongs to that later phase, not to the fall of Tabriz.

For civilians, military occupation meant uncertainty. Households faced billeting, requisitions of food and animals, interruptions to trade, and the risk of violence. Armenian and Azerbaijani communities experienced the occupation differently: some Armenians, in Tabriz and elsewhere, saw the Russians as co-religionists and protectors, while many Muslim residents feared reprisals and cultural dislocation. The records of these experiences are thin, and they come largely from officials and observers; later sections discuss what can be recovered about everyday life.

The events around Echmiadzin require precise separation. The monastery was an important Armenian ecclesiastical center, not simply a fortress. Russian wounded and troops were placed there in 1827 despite an earlier recognition of its neutral standing, and Abbas Mirza moved against the position as part of a wider attempt to break the Russian advance. On 29 August (17 August Old Style), his army engaged a Russian relief force near Ashtarak or Oshakan. The specialist account in Encyclopaedia Iranica describes the action as a Persian battlefield victory, with Russian formations suffering heavily; other narratives emphasize that surviving Russians reached the monastery and that the Iranian success did not destroy the Russian campaign. Thus tactical success, the fate of an immediate relief operation, and the year's eventual strategic result are distinct questions. The engagement should not be retrospectively turned into a Russian victory merely because Yerevan fell later.

The subsequent capture of the fortress of Yerevan was possible because Paskevich renewed the siege effort with artillery and concentrated forces after the earlier difficulties. Local defenses could hold against an improvised expedition yet fail once siege batteries, trenches, and infantry were systematically applied. The fall of Yerevan did not make an occupation of Azerbaijan automatic: Russian commanders still had to cross routes toward Tabriz, secure supplies, and obtain submission or cooperation from local political intermediaries. The occupation of the crown prince's residence city was especially damaging because its political value exceeded the number of troops lost at its gates. It made the court's chief military center a bargaining instrument in Russian hands.

Chronological precision also changes the account of the final advance. The Russian occupation of Tabriz took place in October 1827, but incursions farther into Iranian Azerbaijan continued in early 1828, while negotiations were under way. Urmia and Ardabil belong to that later stage; Ardabil was entered in January 1828. Russian forces removed manuscripts from the shrine of Shaykh Safi al-Din there, transferring cultural property through the imperial administrative network toward St Petersburg. This was an act of wartime seizure with a longer history in the ownership and custody of manuscripts, not a detail to place ahead of the fall of Tabriz. The episode also demonstrates that warfare against provincial cities endangered religious and historical collections as well as troops and commercial wealth.

Section 70.16

The Defeat of 1828 and the Termination of the Second War

After the fall of Tabriz, the Iranian position was desperate. The army was in disarray, the treasury was empty, and Russian forces held the northwest. Fath Ali Shah, in Tehran, had to choose between continuing resistance and negotiating on the terms Russia offered. Britain, citing the 1814 treaty's provisions, withheld assistance. Abbas Mirza opened talks with Paskevich, and negotiations were carried out with the participation of ministers including Allahyar Khan Asef al-Dowleh, who came from Tehran. Paskevich demanded territorial concessions and a large indemnity, and he used the occupation as leverage; the Iranian negotiators had little room to bargain.

The Treaty of Turkmenchay, signed in February 1828 at a village near Tabriz, ended the war. Its provisions were severe. They included the cession of the Yerevan and Nakhchivan khanates, a substantial indemnity, and commercial and legal terms that gave Russian subjects privileges in Iran. The details, and their long-term consequences for sovereignty, trade, and population, are the subject of Chapter 71 and are not analyzed here. What this section emphasizes is the process of decision. The Qajar court accepted terms that it considered punitive because the alternative was continued occupation and the possible collapse of the northwest, and because the shah's government had no prospect of relief from Britain or any other power.

Reactions within Iran were mixed. Some officials regarded the treaty as a catastrophe and sought to minimize its consequences; others emphasized the need to adapt. Abbas Mirza, who had signed, bore both responsibility and the hope that his succession would be protected. The immediate consequences, which included the payment of the indemnity, the departure of Russian troops from Tabriz and other towns as payments were made, and the movement of populations, will be treated in the next section and in Chapter 71. The Russian capture of Yerevan and Tabriz, followed by a negotiated settlement under duress, had changed the strategic setting: Iran had lost more territory, its northwestern defenses, and a measure of independent freedom of action.

The course of negotiations was not a single meeting at which a vanquished court surrendered all arguments. The Qajar government attempted to use changes in the European political environment and the prospect of conflict between Russia and the Ottoman Empire to improve its position. Russian commanders, facing the costs of holding Tabriz and an approaching new theater of operations, nevertheless judged that the occupation gave them decisive leverage. Payment guarantees mattered as much as formal consent: withdrawal from occupied towns depended upon the staged fulfillment of commitments that the Iranian treasury struggled to meet. Court officials had to decide which provincial treasuries, merchants, and precious objects could be drawn upon without making collection itself politically destructive.

To distinguish military history from the following chapter's treaty analysis, the central issue here is why the shah accepted peace. Keeping the war going threatened additional occupations, but raising funds for concessions also risked unrest and reduced resources for future defense. Abbas Mirza remained the crown prince expected to defend the northwest, even as his defeat made his relationship with the royal court vulnerable to rivals. British nonintervention further narrowed his options, although British diplomats still monitored a settlement that would change the regional balance. The decision to sign therefore illustrates the constraints of an unequal negotiation more effectively than a catalogue of clauses would. Chapter 71 can then examine how those clauses restructured trade, legal privileges, borders, and population movements.

Division V · Political, Social, and Institutional Consequences, 1828–1834 · Section 70.17

War and Society: Displacement, Taxation, Trade, and Frontier Communities

Two periods of war, together with the intervening instability, affected society across the northwest and beyond. The most direct effects fell on the people of the frontier zones. Villages in Karabakh, Ganja, Yerevan, Nakhchivan, Talysh, and Azerbaijan lay on the paths of armies. Campaigning seasons coincided with the agricultural calendar, and requisitions of grain, fodder, draft animals, and labor reduced what households could plant, harvest, or sell. Fortress sieges produced hunger and disease, and retreating and advancing armies alike seized supplies. Because tax assessments often did not decline when production fell, the burden on surviving cultivators could be heavy. Evidence for these conditions comes from official reports, travelers' accounts, and later memory, and numerical claims about depopulation or mortality in this period are rarely reliable.

Taxation and war finance extended the burden beyond the war zones. The Qajar government and princely governors raised additional levies, demanded forced loans or gifts from merchants and notables, and debased or manipulated payments when necessary. Merchants in Tabriz and other cities faced disruptions of trade routes through the Caucasus and the effect of Russian customs and commercial policies. At the same time, some merchants profited from military supply, and Armenian and Muslim traders in border towns sought to maintain commerce across changing frontiers. The effects were therefore uneven, and different groups gained and lost in different ways.

Population movement was one of the war's lasting consequences. Muslim families, elites among them, left Russian-held territory for Iran; Armenian families moved in the opposite direction or within the region as they sought safety and opportunity; Georgian nobles and soldiers joined Iranian or Russian service. Some of these movements were voluntary, others coerced, and some were organized after the treaties, an aspect treated in Chapter 71. The immediate wartime pattern was one of flight, return, and resettlement, as armies advanced and retreated. In addition, the plague and cholera epidemics that affected Iran in the late 1820s and early 1830s, including the plague in Azerbaijan and Gilan around 1830, compounded the losses; disease and war together produced demographic disruption whose scale is difficult to measure.

The experiences of Caucasian populations differed by religion, status, and location. Armenian communities faced both the pressures of Iranian and Russian armies and the opportunities of Russian protection; Georgian elites divided over loyalty; Muslim communities in the khanates faced conquest, relocation, and the loss of their rulers. Women and children, whose labor sustained households, bore the costs of absent men and of requisition, and some were taken captive in the fighting. Their voices survive mostly through male officials and foreign observers, so any reconstruction must acknowledge its limits.

The cost of war was often transmitted through institutions that are otherwise hard to see. A village headman could be ordered to supply grain or fodder and then redistribute that demand among households; a merchant might advance cash against future customs receipts; a family might seek protection through a cleric or provincial official when soldiers seized animals. Such exchanges did not affect all subjects equally. Wealthier households had more means of bargaining or moving property, while landless laborers and people dependent on seasonal work faced immediate exposure when markets closed. War also altered the timing of weddings, planting, pilgrimage, and caravan travel. These experiences cannot be quantified from campaign chronicles, but they follow from the documented systems of requisition and disruption, and should be presented as cautious historical reconstruction rather than invented testimony.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Displacement, Taxation, and Household Survival

For most inhabitants of the northwest, war was experienced as a series of disruptions to the rhythms of work. A farming household in Azerbaijan or Karabakh planned its year around planting, irrigation, harvest, and the movement of flocks. An army passing through could take seed grain or draft animals; a siege could cut a village from its market. Households responded in several ways: hiding grain, sending some members to relatives in safer areas, seeking patrons among local notables, or moving altogether.

Artisans and merchants in cities such as Tabriz depended on stable routes and credit. War interrupted both, and occupation brought the additional burdens of billeting and requisition. Religious institutions, including mosques, churches, and shrines, served as places of refuge, charity, and organization, and religious leaders mediated between communities and authorities. Historians must read official records, which stress taxes and troop movements, alongside travelers' descriptions and later local narratives to recover how households managed. Wherever possible, they should ask what such sources omit, including the work of women and the experiences of the poorest.

Section 70.18

The Shah's Court after Defeat: Authority, Religious Institutions, and Political Culture

The court that emerged from the second war faced severe fiscal pressure. The indemnity and the costs of the war strained the treasury, and the shah's government turned to extraordinary levies, the sale of offices, and borrowing against future revenue. Governors competed for resources, and the principle of princely governorship continued, but with the additional strain of managing payments to Russia. Courtiers and officials argued over who was responsible for the defeat, and the shah's own authority, though not seriously challenged as a matter of dynastic legitimacy, was affected by a sense of failure. The monarchy responded by continuing the ritual and artistic production that sustained its image: royal portraits, palace building, poetry, and ceremonies were maintained, and royal patronage continued to provide employment for artists and scholars.

The ulama occupied a delicate position. Clerical scholars had urged war in 1826 and were associated with its failure. Their authority, however, was not necessarily diminished: they remained leaders of communities, judges, and distributors of charity, and the defeat did not end their influence. Some leading mojtaheds argued that the loss of Muslim lands was a calamity caused by lapses in unity and leadership; others turned toward the consolidation of their own institutions. The relationship between the monarchy and religious leaders continued to be a negotiation, with each side needing the other, and it would remain a major feature of Qajar politics, as later chapters on the Tobacco Protest and the Constitutional Revolution show.

Elite discussion of the defeat took several forms. Some officials and writers connected with Abbas Mirza's circle argued that Iran needed more systematic military and administrative reform; some looked to European models of education and organization. Others regarded the defeats as the result of avoidable mistakes or of the shah's reliance on poor advisers, and many continued to rely on customary methods of rule. Iranian society did not reach a single conclusion about reform, and it would be wrong to portray the period as a straightforward awakening. The debates, which Chapter 73 takes up, reflected differences of interest, ideology, and experience.

The court's capacity to project authority nevertheless remained significant. Appointments, ceremonies, and marriage alliances continued, and the shah's household remained at the center of political life. In the provinces, governors continued to collect revenue and maintain order, with varying success. The monarchy had survived, but within a changed environment: it operated under the shadow of Russian power and the knowledge that the frontier could not be altered by force in the near term.

Religious institutions had material as well as intellectual stakes in the outcome. Endowments depended on the security of fields, shops, and caravan roads that generated their revenue; the displacement of communities affected donations and the operation of schools and shrines. Legal scholars could criticize a particular minister or military commander while continuing to support the institution of monarchy, and some patrons at court could fund religious establishments even while disputing the political claims of influential clerics. The more lasting change was that foreign military power became a recurrent factor in how officials discussed revenue and provincial defense. Not every minister advocated imitation of Europe, and the survival of customary offices shows that reform and continuity were concurrent rather than successive phases.

Section 70.19

The Griboyedov Crisis, Abbas Mirza's Death, and the Succession of 1834

In 1829 a new crisis threatened to renew the war. Alexander Griboyedov, a well-known playwright and Russian diplomat who had participated in the negotiation of Turkmenchay, arrived in Tehran as Russia's envoy. One of his tasks was to see to the implementation of the treaty's provisions concerning the return of prisoners and of Georgian and Armenian captives and subjects who had been in Iran. This policy aroused resentment in some quarters of Tehran, where the treatment of people claiming to be covered by it, including women in households, appeared to many a violation of customary rights. The affair was inflamed when individuals who sought refuge in the Russian mission, including a court official of Armenian origin, were taken under Russian protection.

In February 1829, a crowd attacked the Russian mission, and Griboyedov and most members of the legation were killed. Accounts differ about the degree to which clerics, court factions, or officials encouraged the mob, and about whether the government could have prevented it. The Iranian government, fearing a Russian military response, acted quickly to repair the damage: it sent an apology mission to St Petersburg led by Prince Khosrow Mirza, a son of Abbas Mirza, and conveyed gifts, including a famous diamond, to the tsar. Nicholas I, who had other priorities, accepted the apology, and a third war was avoided. The episode showed both the fragility of Iranian security after Turkmenchay and the diplomatic skill with which the court managed it.

Abbas Mirza's later years were spent trying to restore the northwestern army and to extend Qajar authority elsewhere. He took part in a campaign in Khurasan in the early 1830s, aimed at consolidating royal control, and died in Mashhad in October 1833. His death deprived the monarchy of its heir and its most experienced military and diplomatic leader. Fath Ali Shah, now elderly, confronted competing claims while Abbas Mirza's son Mohammad Mirza acquired his father's military and provincial responsibilities. His formal confirmation as successor came after further court deliberation in June 1834.

The shah died in Isfahan in October 1834. His death opened a succession struggle. Other sons and relatives asserted claims, including a prince in Tehran who proclaimed himself ruler and a governor in Fars, while Mohammad Mirza moved from Tabriz toward the capital with support that included Russian and British diplomatic backing. The challenges failed, and Mohammad Mirza was acclaimed as shah at Tabriz in November 1834; his formal coronation in Tehran took place on 14 January 1835. The conflict shows both the fragility and the resilience of the system: succession remained contested, but the dynasty held together, and foreign support was an important factor in the outcome. The events of this reign thus ended with a transfer of authority under conditions that would continue to shape Qajar politics.

The succession after Abbas Mirza's death was not settled by a single immediate proclamation. Mohammad Mirza inherited an influential position in Azerbaijan, but other princes had their own provincial troops, revenues, and claims. Fath Ali Shah delayed a final public settlement before an assembly on 20 June 1834 confirmed Mohammad Mirza's position as deputy and intended successor, amid diplomatic pressure and the possibility of rival claimants. After the shah died in October, Mohammad Mirza was acclaimed at Tabriz in November 1834 and moved toward Tehran with the support of his ministers and British and Russian representatives. His position as shah from 1834 must be distinguished from the formal coronation held in Tehran on 14 January 1835. The distinction reveals how title, recognition, possession of a capital, and ceremony could occur on different dates in a contested succession.

Section 70.20

Historical Synthesis: Monarchical Continuity, Military Defeat, and Iran's Changing International Position

The reign of Fath Ali Shah is marked by an apparent contradiction. The monarchy endured: it passed to a new shah in 1834, maintained courts and provincial governments, expanded its diplomatic contacts, and continued to project an image of Iranian kingship in painting, poetry, and architecture. At the same time, the monarchy lost two wars to Russia, ceded contested Caucasian territories, accepted an indemnity and commercial terms imposed under occupation, and found its freedom of action constrained by external power. The two facts are not contradictory once the nature of the Qajar state is understood. It was a dynastic monarchy sustained by kinship, patronage, ritual, and negotiation with provincial elites and religious leaders, rather than by a centralized bureaucracy or standing army. Such a system could survive defeat because its legitimacy rested on relationships that a battlefield loss did not necessarily destroy, but it could not easily mobilize the resources that the contest with Russia demanded.

The wars themselves had multiple causes and cannot be reduced to technological backwardness. Russian expansion in Georgia and the khanates produced a direct collision with Qajar claims of suzerainty. Qajar revenue, organization, and command structures limited the ability to sustain campaigns, while the dispersal of authority among princes and tribal chiefs complicated coordination. European diplomacy proved unreliable: France's alliance dissolved at Tilsit, and Britain's commitments were conditioned by its own interests. Local rulers and communities in the Caucasus acted on their own calculations. And the Russian Empire, once free of the Napoleonic threat, brought greater resources and military organization to the frontier. Iranian leaders, including Abbas Mirza, adapted and experimented, but adaptation was incomplete when the decisive campaigns came.

Students should distinguish three outcomes. The first is the military defeats themselves, at Aslanduz, Lankaran, Ganja, Yerevan, and Tabriz, which were battlefield and siege results shaped by logistics, command, and numbers. The second is the legal and territorial settlements that followed, the Treaties of Gulistan and Turkmenchay, whose terms Chapter 71 examines. The third is the longer-term movement toward institutional reform, in which defeat prompted debate about military training, administration, education, and the relationship between state and society, discussed in Chapter 73. These outcomes are connected but not identical, and treating them as one obscures the contingency of the period.

The wars also changed the human and political landscape. Communities in the Caucasus were divided by new frontiers, and populations moved; the Qajar court's relationship with Russia and Britain was reconfigured; and the ulama, merchants, and officials of the northwest developed new understandings of foreign power. At the same time, the period did not produce a single, uniform Iranian response. Some saw the need to reform, some sought to restore earlier arrangements, and some focused on the practicalities of survival. The Qajar monarchy, with its strengths and weaknesses, would carry these debates into the reign of Mohammad Shah and beyond.

The question that confronts the next stage of Qajar history is therefore this: how did a monarchy that survived two major military defeats confront the challenge of preserving political independence in a changing international system? The answer, as the following chapters show, would involve treaty settlements and their consequences, a new phase of imperial competition over Iran, and a series of reform attempts of uneven success.

Chapter Summary

Fath Ali Shah succeeded his uncle Agha Mohammad Khan in 1797 and by 1798 had secured the throne, defeating rival claimants and reshaping the central administration. The succession tested a dynasty that had been built by conquest, and its resolution demonstrated that the Qajar monarchy could outlast its founder. It did not, however, resolve the Caucasian question that Agha Mohammad Khan had left unfinished.

The Qajar court operated as an instrument of rule. Ceremony, architecture, poetry, and painting presented the shah as heir to Iranian kingship, and royal women and marriage alliances bound families to the dynasty. This display communicated authority in a state without a large bureaucracy, but it also consumed resources. Provincial government was entrusted largely to the shah's sons, with Abbas Mirza at Tabriz as crown prince; the system tied provinces to the dynasty through kinship but created rival princely courts and limited central coordination.

The military system combined tribal cavalry, levied musketeers, household troops, and limited artillery, funded by uneven revenue. It was effective in mobile warfare and against internal and Ottoman opponents, but it faced difficulty against Russian disciplined infantry, artillery, and logistics. Abbas Mirza began to create new-style infantry, using Russian deserters and later British and French instructors, though the full reform program belongs to Chapter 73.

The first war (1804–1813) grew from the Russian annexation of eastern Georgia in 1801 and the capture of Ganja in 1804. It was fought across a landscape of khanates whose rulers shifted allegiance, and across the Aras and the Armenian highlands. Iranian diplomacy sought help from France, producing the Treaty of Finkenstein in 1807, and then from Britain, but European alignments proved unstable, as the Treaty of Tilsit showed. The defeats at Aslanduz in 1812 and Lankaran in 1813 led to the Treaty of Gulistan, with British mediation.

The interwar years were marked by disputes about the interpretation of Gulistan, harsh Russian frontier administration, British treaty arrangements that limited actual support, and the Ottoman war of 1821–1823. Historians disagree over responsibility for the renewed war of 1826. Some emphasize Iranian initiative and clerical and court pressure; others stress Russian frontier pressure and Iranian defensive calculations. The evidence supports neither a simple nor a one-sided account.

The second war (1826–1828) began with an Iranian offensive that gained ground and then collapsed after Russian counterattacks at Shamkhor and Ganja. In 1827 Paskevich captured Abbasabad, Yerevan, and, in October, occupied Tabriz. The war ended with the Treaty of Turkmenchay in February 1828, which imposed severe terms analyzed in Chapter 71.

War affected rural and urban communities through requisition, taxation, displacement, disease, and the disruption of trade. Armenian, Georgian, Muslim, and other populations experienced the conflicts differently, and evidence for their experiences is limited and mostly mediated by elites. After 1828, the court faced fiscal strain, the ulama retained influence, and elites debated reform without reaching consensus.

The Griboyedov crisis of 1829 was managed by an apology mission that avoided a third war. Abbas Mirza died in 1833 and Fath Ali Shah in 1834, and Mohammad Mirza prevailed in the succession with foreign diplomatic support. The monarchy had survived defeat, but its international position, its frontier, and its internal debates had changed, and the questions that remained are taken up in Chapters 71 through 73.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced a reign in which the continuity of monarchy and the experience of military defeat coexisted. Fath Ali Shah's court preserved and projected a tradition of Iranian kingship, while the state he led could not match the resources of an expanding empire. The chapter matters because it shows how states under pressure adapt, fail, and persist, and it resists both triumphal and fatalistic readings.

The wars also reveal the Caucasus as a region of many actors. Georgians, Armenians, and Muslim rulers and communities shaped events and bore their costs, and their experiences cannot be reduced to the strategies of two empires. This view reinforces the commitment, set out in Chapter 1, to treat borderlands as historically formed spaces rather than as prizes.

The reign shaped later developments directly. The treaties examined in Chapter 71, the reform debates of Chapter 73, and the imperial competition of Chapter 72 all grow out of this period. Habits of mind introduced here, such as separating military result from legal settlement and from long-term institutional change, remain useful for the chapters that follow.

Finally, the chapter invites students to ask how historians know what they know. Court chronicles, imperial reports, diplomatic correspondence, and visual sources each illuminate part of the story and conceal another. Reading them together, and noticing whose voices are absent, is the central skill the chapter aims to build.
