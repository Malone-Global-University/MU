The Origins of the Iran–Iraq War

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic · Principal scope 1968–December 1980

Chapter Opening

A frontier dispute can endure for generations without producing full-scale war. To understand why war began in 1980, we must examine both the conditions of conflict and the decisions that transformed them into invasion.

On September 17, 1980, Saddam Hussein announced that Iraq no longer recognized the 1975 Algiers settlement governing the Iranian-Iraqi frontier. Five years earlier, as vice president of Iraq, he had personally participated in the negotiations that made the settlement possible. Now, as president, he challenged the bargain his own government had accepted. Five days later Iraq initiated a full-scale invasion of Iran. The contrast between the two moments provides the central question of this chapter: why did an arrangement negotiated to limit conflict become the subject of a war that would last eight years? [6], [11], [20]

The answer cannot be found in a single territorial claim or a single ideological disagreement. The two countries had inherited a complicated frontier from earlier empires. The political geography of the Shatt al-Arab, the petroleum economies of the borderlands and the connections among communities across the boundary gave both states strong reasons to concern themselves with the policies of the other. Those same conditions made cooperation valuable. Before 1980 governments had repeatedly negotiated arrangements that allowed ordinary relations to continue even when important disagreements persisted.

The political transformation of Iran after 1979 altered the conditions of rivalry. The Pahlavi monarchy disappeared, the Islamic Republic took shape, and the relationship between Tehran and Washington changed dramatically. Iraqi leaders confronted a neighboring state that presented an ideological challenge to established governments. At the same time the Iranian Revolution disrupted institutions upon which the shah’s regional position had depended. This combination of perceived danger and perceived opportunity is essential to understanding the Iraqi decision, but it cannot be used to establish the private motives of every official involved.

The chapter follows the dispute through five levels of explanation. First it examines the geography and legal history of the frontier. Second it considers the rivalry of the Pahlavi and Baʿathist governments and the negotiated settlement of 1975. Third it reconstructs the political transformation following the Iranian Revolution. Fourth it situates the developing crisis within the regional and international environment. Finally it explains the change from escalating hostility to full-scale war and the immediate failure to achieve a rapid settlement.

These subjects require attention to several distinct kinds of evidence. A treaty establishes the obligations the parties accepted; it does not prove that every obligation was implemented. A government statement supplies evidence of a publicly asserted claim; it does not independently verify the claim. An archive of diplomatic records may disclose contemporary assessments while still leaving gaps concerning private deliberations. Later scholarship can compare these sources, but hindsight must not be mistaken for information available at the time.

The chapter also distinguishes historical explanation from legal responsibility. Reciprocal accusations, border incidents and revolutionary rhetoric contributed to the deteriorating relationship. They do not make the full-scale invasion of September 22 an inevitable or legally justified outcome. The contemporary response of the UN Security Council and the later Secretary-General’s assessment of responsibility are examined as different documents with different institutional purposes. [20], [24]

Chapters 92–95 explained how the Islamic Republic emerged, acquired its constitutional form and consolidated authority. Chapter 96 returns to the years before September 1980 to examine the origins of the international conflict that shaped much of that domestic history. Its narrative concludes principally in December 1980. Chapter 97 will examine the conduct of the war, mobilization, civilian experiences and its enduring social memory.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Distinguish river sovereignty, navigation rights, delimitation, demarcation and the thalweg principle.
  3. Analyze the economic geography of Khuzestan and southern Iraq without equating ethnicity with political allegiance.
  4. Compare the political systems and regional interests of the Pahlavi and Baʿathist governments.
  5. Trace the relationship between the 1969 boundary dispute, Kurdish autonomy and the 1975 Algiers settlement.
  6. Differentiate the March 1975 communiqué, the June treaty and the subsequent ratification process.
  7. Assess the consequences of Khomeini’s Iraqi exile and the Iranian Revolution for regional political relationships.
  8. Explain the Iraqi government’s regime-security concerns and the limits of generalizations about Iraqi Shiʿis.
  9. Evaluate continuity and disruption in Iran’s military institutions after 1979.
  10. Distinguish the interests of neighboring governments and major international powers from evidence of authorization for invasion.
  11. Trace the September 1980 decision for full-scale war and explain why a rapid political settlement did not follow.
  12. Compare Resolution 479 and the 1991 Secretary-General’s report, distinguishing causation from legal responsibility.

Historical Context

Iran and Iraq entered the late twentieth century with national economies profoundly affected by petroleum, governments concerned with territorial sovereignty and populations whose social and religious connections extended across international boundaries. The frontier was a particularly significant point of contact because it separated both mountain regions and a shared commercial waterway. The political interests attached to these environments differed, and a negotiated settlement had to address more than a single line on a map.

Under the Pahlavi monarchy, Iran’s foreign policy relied on substantial petroleum income and an important strategic relationship with the United States. Iraq’s governments changed dramatically after the overthrow of the monarchy in 1958. The establishment of durable Baʿathist government in 1968 created a strengthened political competitor in a region where British withdrawal and expanding oil revenues were changing the possibilities for independent state action. Their rivalry intensified during the early 1970s, but diplomatic bargaining in 1975 temporarily reduced important sources of conflict.

The Iranian Revolution transformed the existing balance in a short period. The overthrow of the shah brought changes to international relationships, military command and domestic political organization. In Iraq the formal succession of Saddam Hussein to the presidency in 1979 further concentrated authority. Political repression and competition concerning religious and ethnic movements increased the importance of perceived threats from beyond the frontier. The relationship between domestic insecurity and external policy must be assessed without assuming that all members of a community shared one political allegiance.

The hostage crisis and the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan added further international pressures. Washington reassessed its regional strategy; Moscow maintained an important relationship with Baghdad while confronting new problems of its own; Gulf governments adopted policies reflecting their distinct domestic and economic interests. These circumstances created opportunities for diplomatic maneuvering, but the presence of multiple outside interests does not establish that an outside government ordered or authorized the Iraqi invasion.

The chronological overlap with Chapter 95 is intentional. That chapter follows domestic consolidation to 1983, whereas this chapter traces the international causes of war to the end of 1980. Students should recognize that historical processes operate simultaneously: the formation of institutions inside Iran affected its external position, and the war in turn affected the struggle over domestic political authority. The two explanations are complementary rather than sequential in every respect.

Section 96.1

The Ottoman–Persian Frontier and the Formation of Modern Iraq

The boundary that separated Iran from Iraq in 1980 was not an ancient ethnic dividing line. Its history lay in the interaction of two empires, the negotiations of their successors, and the development of the modern territorial state. For centuries the territories of western Iran and Mesopotamia were linked by commerce, pilgrimage, pastoral migration and political competition. A family could have commercial interests on both sides of a frontier, and a religious scholar could study in one empire and teach in the other. Neither the language spoken by a community nor the religious allegiance of its inhabitants necessarily determined which government exercised authority over it. The emergence of a precisely surveyed international boundary changed the political significance of these connections, without extinguishing them.

The Ottoman and Safavid empires fought repeatedly over control of Mesopotamia and the adjoining highlands. The Ottoman conquest of Baghdad in 1638 and the Treaty of Zuhab, also known as Qasr-e Shirin, in 1639 established an enduring framework for the division between the empires. It would be misleading, however, to imagine that negotiators in 1639 drew the modern border on a map. Their agreement dealt with places, districts and claims of authority in a world in which effective control frequently diminished with distance from administrative centers. Mountain communities, mobile pastoral groups and settlements around rivers were not easily assigned to an exclusively governed territory. The later history of the frontier therefore involved the interpretation of earlier understandings as well as the negotiation of new ones.

During the nineteenth century, the increasing reach of central governments and the involvement of Britain and Russia made boundary definition a more sustained diplomatic concern. The two Treaties of Erzurum, concluded in 1823 and 1847, formed important stages in this process. The second addressed competing claims concerning places such as Mohammerah, the port later called Khorramshahr, and the southwestern river frontier. Yet textual commitments did not settle every question. A description of a district could be read differently by the governments concerned; a claim about the bank of a river might leave ambiguous the practical rights of boats using its channel. The interests of outside powers also entered negotiations, especially where commercial access and imperial influence were at stake. This was a continuing process rather than the implementation of a single agreement.

The Constantinople Protocol of 1913 and the work of the boundary commission in 1914 represented a more technical phase. Surveyors and commissioners translated agreed principles into lines, marks and descriptive records. Their work became a reference point for subsequent diplomacy, although the outbreak of the First World War and the collapse of Ottoman rule changed the political setting in which the boundary would be administered. Later treaties could adopt these earlier materials as a basis while making revisions to particular disputed questions. The legal history is thus cumulative: a twentieth-century instrument might incorporate records produced under empires that no longer existed. The account of this development in Encyclopaedia Iranica provides a useful guide to the successive instruments and their interpretations. [1]

The modern state of Iraq emerged from Ottoman provinces under British influence after the First World War. A monarchy was established in 1921, and Iraq gained formal independence and entered the League of Nations in 1932. Iran now faced an independent neighboring government rather than the Ottoman administration. The new relationship inherited the frontier but also created new problems. Baghdad and Tehran maintained different positions concerning the validity and meaning of the earlier boundary arrangements. Oil production and expanding port commerce gave legal control of waterways a significance that had not existed in the same form during the seventeenth century. The institutional setting of disputes had changed as well: diplomatic notes, international organizations and treaties were instruments through which states advanced competing claims.

Several conclusions follow. The frontier was a product of political arrangements, not a natural division between Persians and Arabs. Many communities situated around it maintained connections across the line. Moreover, the existence of a longstanding dispute did not make a later war inevitable. Previous governments had repeatedly negotiated arrangements and lived with unresolved claims without initiating a war on the scale of 1980. The origins of the Iran–Iraq War must therefore be traced through the choices made by modern states as well as through their inherited territorial geography. The border furnished subjects of conflict; it did not itself determine when or how those conflicts would be pursued.

Section 96.2

The Shatt al-Arab–Arvand Rud Waterway

The Shatt al-Arab, known in Iran as the Arvand Rud, is a river system of exceptional importance to the political economy of the northern Persian Gulf. It is formed by the meeting of the Tigris and Euphrates at al-Qurnah in Iraq and flows toward the Gulf. Basra lies upstream from the section where the waterway forms the international frontier, while the Iranian ports of Khorramshahr and Abadan stand along or near its lower reaches. The Karun River connects the Iranian interior to this commercial system. These geographical relationships made the waterway more than a line dividing two jurisdictions. It was also a shared environment, a transport corridor, a source of livelihood and an outlet for internationally traded goods. [2]

A student examining a map should distinguish three separate questions: where the river runs, which parts are subject to the international boundary, and what legal rights vessels possess. The Tigris and Euphrates do not themselves form the entire Iran–Iraq frontier, and the Shatt al-Arab does not divide the two countries along its entire length. The boundary concerns the lower shared stretch; upstream portions are within Iraq. Similarly, the fact that a vessel uses a navigable channel does not by itself establish the nationality of that channel. Navigation can be regulated through treaty arrangements that grant access irrespective of the placement of a boundary. Failure to separate these issues has produced misleading descriptions of both the 1937 and 1975 settlements.

The legal term thalweg, derived from German, is central to this history. In navigable rivers it generally refers to the line following the principal channel of navigation. The river protocol signed by Iran and Iraq on 13 June 1975 provided its own exact technical definition: the median of the main navigable channel at the lowest navigable level. This is not necessarily a geometric line halfway between the banks. A river can have shallow areas, shifting sediment, several branches or an uneven cross-section. In practice, determining the relevant channel requires hydrographic measurements and agreed charts. A boundary drawn near one bank and a boundary following the channel may therefore allocate jurisdiction quite differently even when the same boats continue to sail along the river. [3]

These distinctions mattered economically. A government responsible for the navigation administration of the river might collect charges, issue rules governing pilotage and determine the conditions under which vessels operated. Commercial users required reliable access, particularly in the era of expanding petroleum production and refining. The Iranian government regarded an arrangement placing most of the shared reach within Iraqi jurisdiction as a limit on sovereign control over access to Iranian ports. Iraqi governments emphasized their historical administrative role and the importance of the river to Basra. The disagreement was consequently not simply a contest over the ownership of water. It concerned the practical exercise of governmental authority and the distribution of risks associated with commercial dependence.

In addition to defining the border, the 1975 river protocol addressed the shared use of the waterway. Its provisions included non-discriminatory commercial navigation, arrangements for common regulation and surveys, and rules concerning changes in the navigable channel. These provisions demonstrate why technical cooperation remained necessary even when the governments agreed on a territorial line. Rivers change in ways that roads and surveyed land plots generally do not. Sedimentation can modify a channel, and administrative decisions concerning maintenance on one side can affect conditions on the other. A workable settlement therefore requires institutions as well as a map.

The economic and legal consequences of the river dispute should not be confused with the much larger geopolitical contest over the entire Persian Gulf. Iraqi governments sought favorable access to the Gulf, but Iraq also possessed territory and commercial connections outside the Shatt al-Arab system. Iran, with an extensive Gulf coastline, had other ports and routes, yet its investment in Abadan and Khorramshahr made the river dispute especially important. In both countries the waterway acquired a symbolic significance as a measure of sovereign equality. This explains the intensity of bargaining, while the fact that both states negotiated common rules in 1975 illustrates the possibility of arrangements that could accommodate the interests of both.

Section 96.3

Khuzestan, Mesopotamia, and the Political Economy of the Borderlands

The southwestern Iranian province of Khuzestan occupies a distinctive place in the geography of modern Iran. Its plains and rivers differ from the mountains that enclose much of the Iranian plateau. The region contains agricultural land, substantial urban settlements and historically important petroleum installations. Its proximity to southern Iraq made the two areas part of an interconnected commercial and social environment even after international boundary arrangements divided them. The historical names and political claims associated with parts of Khuzestan became controversial, especially in the nationalist rhetoric of the twentieth century. Those claims cannot be substituted for the history of the population itself.

The development of the petroleum industry transformed the economic significance of the region. Oil was discovered in commercial quantities at Masjed Soleyman in 1908, and the refining complex at Abadan subsequently became one of the central institutions of Iran's oil economy. Petroleum extraction, processing and export created a network of enterprises, workers' settlements and transport connections. The industry's relation to foreign ownership and control became a central issue in Iran's nationalization movement in the early 1950s. By the late Pahlavi period the oil sector funded a large share of government spending, and Khuzestan had acquired a national significance far exceeding the size of its population. When relations with Iraq deteriorated, the economic interdependence of the borderlands had to coexist with the governments' competing security policies.

Southern Iraq was similarly important to the Iraqi economy. Basra was a major commercial center with a maritime connection to international markets. Iraq's petroleum industry also extended into northern regions, and over time it developed pipelines and export arrangements that did not depend exclusively on a single waterway. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that the Shatt al-Arab was Iraq's only possible route for exporting oil. Nevertheless, the country's relatively limited coastline and the importance of its southern economic centers increased the political sensitivity of questions concerning Gulf access. Both governments had compelling reasons to secure dependable commercial connections, and these interests could produce incentives for either cooperation or competition.

The population of Khuzestan was diverse. Arabic-speaking Iranians, Persian speakers, members of different tribal and urban communities, migrants attracted by industrial employment, and other groups inhabited the region. The province was not an ethnically uniform territory whose political affiliation could be read directly from the language of its inhabitants. In the aftermath of the revolution some activists demanded recognition of Arab cultural and political rights, while the central authorities feared challenges to national unity. Some organizations advocated more radical political objectives. Neither their demands nor government accusations about them permit a historian to assume that the population as a whole favored Iraqi sovereignty. The regional disputes examined in Chapter 95 belong to this context, but they must be distinguished from Iraq's interstate claims.

A similar methodological problem arises in the northern borderlands. Kurdish-speaking communities were divided among several states, and their demands for autonomy varied by organization, period and local circumstances. Iranian and Iraqi governments attempted to influence Kurdish movements across the border even while restricting movements within their own territories. These policies are evidence of a recurring conflict between national governments and transnational communities, but also of the autonomy of the actors themselves. Kurdish organizations possessed their own leadership, constituencies and political programs. Their interests did not simply coincide with those of a government offering them assistance.

The borderlands consequently illustrate a paradox. Economic interdependence and social connection could make peaceful cooperation highly valuable, yet they also heightened the political significance of competing sovereign claims. During a period of tension, dependence on shared waterways and markets could be interpreted as exposure to pressure; ordinary cross-border movement could become a subject of security suspicion. Government policies that treated entire populations as unreliable also risked alienating citizens whose political preferences were varied. The 1980 war imposed immense costs on the people and productive institutions of these regions. Before examining that outcome, students should understand the distinction between the economic value of an area, the legal status of its territory, and the political loyalties of those who live there.

Section 96.4

The 1937 Boundary Treaty and the Limits of Diplomatic Settlement

The 1937 boundary treaty was an effort to resolve an interstate controversy that survived the transition from Ottoman to Iraqi government. The dispute had already been raised before the League of Nations in the 1930s. Iraq defended the boundary arrangements inherited from the Ottoman era, while Iran sought a larger share of jurisdiction over the common reach of the Shatt al-Arab. Negotiation took place against the background of continuing British influence in Iraq and concern over stability in the region. The treaty concluded in Tehran on 4 July 1937 was therefore both a legal instrument and a political compromise. Its terms cannot be understood solely through the map that resulted from it. [4], [27]

The central difference between the settlement of 1937 and the later one of 1975 concerns the river frontier. Under the earlier arrangement, the line generally lay on or close to the Iranian bank, but particular stretches, including a limited zone near Abadan, were subject to an exception based on the navigable channel. The instrument also contained provisions concerning navigation. A description that assigns all of the river without exception to Iraq is inaccurate, as is a description that presents the 1937 treaty as already establishing the thalweg throughout the common reach. The arrangement gave Iraq substantially greater jurisdiction over the shared waterway than Iran would later obtain under the revised settlement. It did not eliminate Iran's practical interest in using the river.

The relationship between legal authority and economic dependence explains why the issue remained politically significant. A commercial ship bound for an Iranian port might need to navigate waters principally under Iraqi jurisdiction. For Tehran, the question of equal sovereign status was intertwined with the need to secure access to its own territory and facilities. For Baghdad, the earlier distribution of jurisdiction was a legally recognized feature of its territorial inheritance. Both governments had an incentive to maintain trade, but both also feared the political effects of dependence on the other. The result was an agreement that reduced the immediate controversy without removing the underlying grounds of disagreement.

The 1937 settlement should also be viewed in relation to the wider diplomatic environment. In the same year Iran, Iraq, Turkey and Afghanistan concluded the Saadabad Pact, a regional agreement concerned with peaceful relations and noninterference. The existence of these diplomatic efforts challenges any claim that interstate hostility between Iran and Iraq was permanent. During much of the following period, disagreement over the boundary did not prevent the two governments from conducting ordinary relations. The Iraqi monarchy and the Iranian monarchy later participated in the Baghdad Pact, established in 1955, as governments that shared certain Cold War security interests despite the unresolved history of their common frontier. These arrangements were contingent on the domestic and international political circumstances of their time.

The political changes that followed the overthrow of Iraq's monarchy in 1958 altered the relationship. Differences over alliance policy, Arab nationalism, the treatment of communities with connections to Iran and the relative strength of the two governments made earlier arrangements increasingly difficult to sustain. Iran's rising petroleum revenue and military modernization enlarged the shah's diplomatic ambitions. The Baghdad government, meanwhile, defended its territorial claims amid political upheaval and later consolidated Baʿathist authority. The significance of the 1937 treaty changed not because its original clauses were rewritten, but because the political balance within which those clauses operated was changing.

International boundary treaties are normally intended to create stability. A state's dissatisfaction with an arrangement, even if grounded in economic or historical grievances, does not by itself provide authority to use armed force to revise it. Later Iranian and Iraqi denunciations of treaty obligations therefore raise legal questions distinct from the political circumstances that produced them. The historian must reconstruct each government's stated position while preserving this distinction. The events of 1969 and 1975 show how the dispute was reactivated and then negotiated anew. The events of September 1980 demonstrate why the failure of a political commitment to a settlement cannot be equated automatically with lawful termination of a boundary or justification for war.

Section 96.5

The Iranian Monarchy and the Rise of Baʿathist Iraq

The political rivalry of the 1960s and 1970s took shape against the very different trajectories of the two neighboring states. Iran retained the Pahlavi monarchy, whose government emphasized centralization, modernization and the recovery of international prestige. Iraq's monarchy was overthrown in July 1958 by military officers led by Abd al-Karim Qasim. The revolution destroyed the domestic basis of Iraq's participation in the Western-oriented Baghdad Pact, and the republican government withdrew from the pact in 1959. Iran remained in the reorganized alliance known as CENTO. The divergence of the two states' foreign policies gave their territorial disputes a wider Cold War significance, although neither government's political choices can be reduced to the policies of its outside partners.

Iraq experienced several changes of government after 1958. The Baʿath Party participated in the overthrow of Qasim in February 1963 but was removed from power later that year. Its return in July 1968 established a more durable political system under Ahmad Hassan al-Bakr. The party combined Arab nationalism with a commitment to state-led economic development and a powerful security apparatus. Saddam Hussein, who was not yet president, increasingly exercised authority through his positions in the party and the Revolutionary Command Council. Political control extended beyond public offices: government institutions, party membership, patronage and security surveillance contributed to the leadership's ability to marginalize rivals. This institutional background helps explain the concentrated character of decision-making in Baghdad by 1980.

The Pahlavi government, for its part, drew substantial revenue from oil and pursued a major program of industrial and military modernization. Its foreign policy emphasized Iran's importance as a sovereign power in the Persian Gulf. The shah cultivated close relations with the United States, purchased large quantities of foreign military equipment and presented Iran as a central participant in the preservation of regional order. His government also maintained its own diplomatic interests and did not simply execute American decisions. The dispute with Iraq over the Shatt al-Arab was particularly important because the shah interpreted the earlier treaty as inconsistent with Iran's desired regional status. [4]

The ideological language of the governments sharpened the rivalry. Baʿathist leaders presented Iraq as part of a wider Arab nation and frequently criticized Iranian policies through that framework. The Iranian monarchy drew legitimacy from Iranian nationalism, monarchical continuity and its program of modernization. These claims could make concessions to the other government appear politically embarrassing. Yet ideological contrast did not prevent practical cooperation when leaders considered it advantageous. The same Iraqi and Iranian governments that competed over the Gulf later negotiated the Algiers agreement. Political ideology shaped their interpretations of the dispute but did not necessarily dictate a single course of foreign policy.

Both states also sought to extend their authority within their own territories. Kurdish autonomy was a major challenge to successive governments in Baghdad. The shah viewed Iranian Kurdish opposition with suspicion while treating the Iraqi Kurdish movement as a useful source of pressure on his neighbor. Similarly, the Iraqi government had reasons to cultivate opposition to the Iranian monarchy even as it suppressed opponents at home. The difference between a government's claims about national sovereignty and its treatment of cross-border movements was an important feature of the rivalry. Appeals to the rights of a minority abroad were not necessarily accompanied by equivalent protections for communities inside the state making those appeals.

By the early 1970s, rising petroleum income made both states more capable of pursuing independent objectives. Iraq's nationalization of the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1972 strengthened the state's control over a major source of revenue. The oil price increases of the following years expanded the resources available to regional governments. But material capacity was not the same as political stability. The shah had to confront growing opposition to his rule, and the Iraqi government faced a renewed Kurdish conflict. Each state had objectives that could be frustrated by developments in the other. The bilateral relationship therefore combined economic competition, domestic political insecurity and sharply unequal assessments of the existing frontier arrangements.

Section 96.6

Border Disputes and Regional Competition, 1969–1974

The renewed dispute over the Shatt al-Arab in 1969 illustrates the relationship between changing power and the reinterpretation of older treaty arrangements. The Iranian government declared the 1937 treaty invalid on April 19, invoking changed circumstances and its allegations concerning Iraqi conduct. Baghdad rejected Tehran's position. Its representatives brought the controversy before the United Nations Security Council through diplomatic communications, while Iran continued to insist that the boundary should follow the navigable channel. These actions established competing legal positions rather than a mutually accepted revised boundary. The historical importance of the Iranian declaration lies in the reopening of a settled but contested subject at a moment when the shah believed he possessed greater bargaining power. [1], [5]

The confrontation concerned the way governments exercised authority over commercial navigation, as well as their claims to the river itself. Disputes over national flags, pilotage and the enforcement of port regulations acquired a political significance disproportionate to the administrative procedures involved. A merchant's immediate interest might be the predictable movement of a cargo, but an official could interpret the same transaction as an assertion of sovereign authority. During periods of political tension, these different levels of meaning were easily conflated. This helps explain why the relationship required a negotiated legal framework rather than repeated unilateral assertions of jurisdiction.

The regional setting was also changing. In 1968 Britain announced its intention to withdraw its principal military commitments east of Suez, including those in the Gulf. Its withdrawal from the Gulf in 1971 was accompanied by the emergence of newly independent states. Iran sought to be recognized as a leading regional power, while Iraq resisted arrangements that it believed would consolidate Iranian predominance. The differences extended beyond their common frontier. The disputes over Abu Musa and the Greater and Lesser Tunbs, which involved Iran and the emirates that formed the United Arab Emirates, became a subject of Arab regional politics. Iraq criticized Iranian actions. Those islands, however, were not territory claimed by Iraq as part of the Iran–Iraq boundary dispute; their relevance lay in the wider contest over Gulf authority and legitimacy.

Iraq's international relationships developed in a different direction from Iran's. In April 1972 Baghdad and Moscow signed a Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation. The Soviet Union became an important partner in Iraqi economic and military development. Iran's close relationship with Washington did not imply an equally simple alignment against Moscow, since Tehran also conducted trade and maintained diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union. Likewise, Baghdad's relationship with Moscow did not eliminate its ability to bargain with Western European countries or pursue objectives independent of Soviet preferences. In both states, external assistance enlarged governmental capacity but could not determine all of the government's foreign policy decisions.

The conflict with Iraqi Kurdish organizations became increasingly intertwined with the river dispute. Iran's support for the Iraqi Kurdish movement gave Tehran a means of applying pressure without direct full-scale war. Baghdad regarded this intervention as a major obstacle to consolidating its authority in northern Iraq. The failure to implement a durable arrangement for Kurdish autonomy helped produce renewed conflict in 1974. The Iraqi leadership faced the costs of an internal war sustained in part through external assistance, while the shah faced the possibility that the bilateral competition might develop into a more direct confrontation. The American diplomatic records of the period show the importance attached to the Kurdish issue in negotiations over the frontier. [6]

Despite the increasing severity of these disputes, the years from 1969 through 1974 did not form a simple progression toward the war of 1980. Each side attempted to use the other government's difficulties as a source of bargaining strength. When the costs of the conflict increased, the possibility of an exchange of concessions became more attractive. For Tehran, a river boundary recognized by Baghdad was particularly valuable. For Iraq, ending support for its Kurdish opponents offered a way to recover domestic control. The existence of these partially complementary priorities created a diplomatic opportunity. The Algiers settlement demonstrated how the governments could exchange commitments across apparently different policy areas, although it also exposed the vulnerability of populations whose interests were not directly represented in their negotiations.

Section 96.7

The Kurdish Question and Cross-Border Political Intervention

Kurdish political movements were not merely an accessory to the rivalry between Iran and Iraq. They emerged from a long history of demands concerning language, political representation and the relationship between local communities and central governments. Modern state boundaries divided Kurdish-inhabited territories among several countries. In Iraq, the movement associated with Mustafa Barzani and the Kurdistan Democratic Party became one of the most important challenges to the concentration of authority in Baghdad. Its leadership sought recognized Kurdish rights and an autonomous political arrangement. Successive governments alternated between negotiation and repression, and the ability of Kurdish organizations to obtain meaningful guarantees remained uncertain.

In March 1970 the Iraqi government announced an agreement with the Kurdish leadership providing for autonomy within Iraq. The declaration created hope for a settlement but left fundamental questions unresolved, including the territorial extent of autonomy and the distribution of political authority and economic resources. The Kurdish leadership and the government disagreed over implementation. In 1974 Baghdad introduced an autonomy law that the Kurdish movement rejected as inadequate, and the conflict resumed. [31] This was not simply a disagreement about the interpretation of ethnic identity. It concerned whether a central government could impose the terms of political decentralization without obtaining the agreement of the population and organizations affected by it.

Iran's involvement reflected the shah's regional strategy. By supporting the Iraqi Kurdish movement, Tehran increased the costs to Baghdad of continuing the border rivalry and refusing the concessions Iran sought. American and Israeli involvement also formed part of this broader Cold War and regional political environment. The external governments did not necessarily share the Kurdish leadership's long-term objectives. Declassified American records place the beginning of United States assistance in 1972. [28] Assistance that enabled a movement to survive or increase its bargaining power could be withdrawn if the interests of its supporters changed. For a movement seeking institutional recognition, this introduced a serious political vulnerability.

The diplomatic negotiations of 1975 exposed that vulnerability. Iraq accepted concessions concerning the river boundary while Iran agreed to measures ending cross-border intervention. Tehran withdrew its support for the Kurdish insurgency, leaving the Iraqi Kurdish movement in a much weaker position. The contemporary American assessments contained in the Foreign Relations of the United States collection show that the settlement was understood as involving a trade between border concessions and the termination of assistance to Iraqi Kurdish forces. These assessments provide evidence of how outside officials interpreted the agreement; they do not represent the perspectives of all participants, and they are especially limited as evidence for the experiences of Kurdish civilians. [6], [7]

The consequences extended beyond the military fortunes of the movement. Many people fled toward Iran, while the leadership faced the loss of a strategy that had depended heavily on external support. Families had to decide whether to remain in areas newly brought under more effective government control or seek security elsewhere. The outcome generated deep distrust of international assurances. It also illustrated a general problem in the politics of autonomy: an arrangement made between two sovereign governments may improve relations between those governments while leaving the claims of a regional population unresolved. Negotiating states are not necessarily obliged by their political objectives to represent the interests of groups that are not parties to the agreement.

Iranian Kurdish movements must be distinguished from the Iraqi Kurdish organizations involved in the 1974 conflict. They had distinct leaders, political programs and relationships with Tehran, even where kinship, language and political sympathies connected them. After the Iranian Revolution, demands for autonomy in Iranian Kurdistan generated a different series of confrontations with the Islamic Republic, examined in Chapter 95. The ability of Baghdad and Tehran to support movements in one another's territories did not resolve these domestic controversies. It frequently complicated them by making demands for local rights vulnerable to accusations of foreign influence. A sound historical account thus maintains two levels of analysis: Kurdish political agency and the policies of the neighboring governments. The two were connected, but the first cannot be reduced to the second.

Section 96.8

The Algiers Agreement and the Temporary Settlement of 1975

The March 1975 settlement was a significant diplomatic accomplishment precisely because the questions separating Iran and Iraq had become so difficult. At the summit of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries in Algiers, Algerian President Houari Boumédiène facilitated negotiations between Mohammad Reza Shah and Saddam Hussein, who represented Iraq as vice president. On March 6 the parties issued a joint communiqué identifying the principles of a new arrangement. Its political importance lay in linking the river and land frontier questions with security commitments on both sides of the boundary. The agreement addressed different priorities of the negotiating governments rather than assuming that each dispute could be settled in isolation. [6]

The communiqué was not the entire legal settlement. On June 13, 1975, the governments signed the Treaty Concerning the State Frontier and Neighbourly Relations in Baghdad, together with three protocols concerning the river frontier, the land frontier and security along the border. The treaty and associated instruments entered into force following the exchange of ratifications in Tehran on June 22, 1976. The United Nations Treaty Series registration identifies the agreement and the related documentary record. This chronology matters: negotiations in March established the broad bargain, the instruments of June set out its legal arrangements, and subsequent procedures gave those arrangements effect. It is incorrect to use the March communiqué alone as a substitute for the complete treaty framework. [3], [8]

The land and river provisions adopted different methods appropriate to their geography. The land frontier drew upon earlier records of the Constantinople Protocol and the 1914 commission, supplemented by agreed technical work. The shared river frontier was defined according to the thalweg under the river protocol. The parties recognized the need for continued cooperation in navigation and in the administration of changes to the waterway. These were not concessions of ownership over all the rivers flowing through either country. They concerned the specifically defined border and associated arrangements. The documents also described the settlement as an integrated whole, linking the frontier and security commitments in a way that made the political success of each part depend on the broader bargain.

For Baghdad, the major practical benefit was the termination of Iranian assistance to the Kurdish insurgency. For Tehran, the central achievement was Iraqi recognition of a revised river boundary and a more equitable basis for navigation. The governments also agreed to address subsequent controversies through peaceful mechanisms. Article 6 of the main treaty set out a staged dispute-resolution framework involving bilateral negotiation, good offices of a friendly third state and, if the specified procedures failed, arbitration. Such provisions reveal that the parties anticipated possible disagreements during implementation. Their agreement was designed not merely to declare a border but to create a means of administering relations around it. The existence of a mechanism did not guarantee that later governments would use it in good faith. [3]

Contemporary diplomatic assessments emphasized both the potential for improved relations and the political costs of the compromise. Saddam Hussein publicly accepted the agreement at a moment when Iraq's internal conflict limited its freedom of action. The shah accepted the termination of support for a movement that had previously supplied him with leverage. Neither government relinquished all of its broader regional ambitions. Indeed, the benefits of the arrangement depended on their continuing willingness to judge cooperation more valuable than renewed competition. The agreement did not create a political alliance, nor did it establish a common understanding of the future of the Gulf. It provided a workable framework for regulating a central aspect of the bilateral relationship.

The settlement offers a revealing contrast with September 1980. In 1975 the two governments believed they could obtain important objectives through an exchange of concessions. Five years later, the Iraqi leadership declared the arrangement invalid and sought to revise the relationship through force. The intervening changes were primarily political: Iran's monarchy had fallen, revolutionary leaders had assumed authority, and Saddam Hussein had become Iraq's president. The territorial geography had not undergone a comparable transformation. A historian can therefore use the two moments as a comparative inquiry into how perceived power and political vulnerability influence adherence to diplomatic arrangements. The Algiers settlement of 1975 must also be distinguished from the unrelated Algiers Declarations of January 1981, which resolved the American hostage crisis discussed in Chapter 94. Algeria's participation in both settlements does not make them the same legal instrument.

Section 96.9

Khomeini's Iraqi Exile and the Revolutionary Challenge

The political relationship between Iran and Iraq cannot be understood without recognizing the importance of religious networks that extended across their common frontier. Najaf, the site of the shrine of Imam Ali, had long been a major center of Shiʿi religious scholarship, while Karbala occupied a distinctive place in the religious memory of the community. Students, scholars and pilgrims moved among these cities and centers of learning in Iran. Religious authority was not organized along the same lines as territorial state sovereignty. A believer could follow the legal guidance of a scholar resident in another country without thereby becoming a political subject of that country's government. The existence of transnational religious authority was a feature of the region long before the Islamic Republic was established.

Ruhollah Khomeini's own career illustrates these relationships. After his opposition to the shah's policies and his exile from Iran in 1964, he was permitted to move from Turkey to Najaf in September 1965. He remained in Iraq until October 1978. In Najaf he taught religious students, maintained connections with supporters in Iran and developed his argument for Islamic government, including the doctrine of the guardianship of the jurist examined in Chapter 93. The lectures delivered in 1970 were an important stage in the development of his political thought. They were not representative of a unanimous position among senior Shiʿi jurists. Khomeini's conception of the political responsibilities of Islamic jurists was contested by others who understood religious authority and government differently. [9], [10]

The Iraqi political setting complicated his position. The Baghdad government sometimes regarded the presence of an opponent of the Iranian monarchy as politically useful, particularly during periods of tension with Tehran. Yet its own ideological commitment to Baʿathist authority and its suspicion of independent religious organizations limited the scope of such tolerance. Khomeini did not become a dependable instrument of the Iraqi state. His hostility to the shah was accompanied by criticism of political systems he regarded as incompatible with Islamic government. The relationship thus differed from a simple alliance between an exile and the government that hosted him. Both the Iraqi government's priorities and Khomeini's religious-political objectives could change the character of the arrangement.

Following the improvement of Iranian-Iraqi relations in the mid-1970s, Baghdad's reasons for tolerating Khomeini's political activities diminished. As the revolutionary crisis in Iran intensified in 1978, the Iraqi authorities placed greater pressure on him. In October he left Iraq, was unable to enter Kuwait, and proceeded to France. From the Paris area he continued to communicate with the revolutionary movement in Iran until his return in February 1979. The episode connects the history of Iraqi-Iranian diplomacy to the history of the Iranian Revolution. It also demonstrates how a neighboring government's decision to restrict an exiled political leader could have consequences different from those intended. Exile did not prevent Khomeini from acquiring greater political influence in Iran.

The overthrow of the monarchy transformed the implications of these earlier networks for Iraq. A religious scholar who had spent years as an exile in Iraq now occupied the central leadership position in a neighboring state. The Islamic Republic's political message included appeals to Muslims beyond its borders and criticism of governments regarded as unjust or un-Islamic. For a ruling party committed to its own monopoly of political authority, the emergence of such a state presented a new kind of challenge. It was no longer possible to regard Khomeini only as an opponent of the shah. Baghdad had to consider the implications of revolutionary Iran for its own citizens and institutions.

It would nevertheless be a serious mistake to equate the Iraqi Shiʿi population with the political supporters of the Iranian leadership. Shiʿi religious affiliation encompassed people with different national identities, class interests, religious commitments and political preferences. Many Iraqi Shiʿis identified strongly with Iraq, and their relationship to the Iraqi government was shaped by local political circumstances. Likewise, religious scholars in Najaf did not simply accept Khomeini's political doctrine because he had taught there. The importance of transnational religious networks lay in the possibilities they created for communication, intellectual exchange and political solidarity. Those possibilities could be regarded by Baghdad as threatening, but the government's perception of a threat is not evidence that all members of a religious community supported a foreign revolutionary project.

Section 96.10

Saddam Hussein, Regime Security, and Iraqi Shiʿi Opposition

On July 16, 1979, Saddam Hussein succeeded Ahmad Hassan al-Bakr as president of Iraq. His accession occurred only a few months after the Iranian monarchy had collapsed. He had already accumulated considerable influence within the Baʿathist leadership, and his presidency further concentrated political authority. The institutions of the Revolutionary Command Council, party organization, security services and public administration supported a political system in which dissent was severely restricted. The political purges accompanying the leadership transition made the costs of challenging the new president especially apparent. Understanding this concentration of authority is important because decisions concerning Iran were made within a government that afforded limited room for open opposition to its central leadership.

Regime security was a major concern in Baghdad. The Iraqi state governed a population that was ethnically, religiously and politically diverse. The Baʿath Party's secular Arab nationalist program did not eliminate the importance of Shiʿi religious institutions or the demands of citizens for greater political representation. Independent political organization could be viewed by the party leadership as a threat to its control, whether the organizations were Kurdish nationalists, communists, religious activists or other political opponents. The government therefore used coercive measures against multiple forms of dissent. Its concerns about revolutionary Iran must be situated within this broader effort to prevent independent centers of political authority from acquiring institutional strength.

The Islamic Daʿwa Party emerged within the Iraqi Shiʿi religious-political environment before the Iranian Revolution. Its intellectual context included the work of Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr, a prominent jurist who wrote about Islamic economics, philosophy and political organization. His sister Amina al-Sadr, widely known as Bint al-Huda, was a writer and educator engaged in religious and social activity. The victory of the revolution in Iran increased the Iraqi government's suspicion of such networks. It also encouraged some Iraqi opponents of the Baʿath to see Islamic political mobilization as a more viable alternative. These developments did not mean that the Daʿwa Party and the Islamic Republic were institutionally identical. Iraqi religious opposition possessed its own history and political setting.

The crisis intensified in 1980. Iraqi authorities increased pressure on members and suspected supporters of Islamic opposition movements. In April, Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr and his sister were executed by the Iraqi regime. A scholarly account dates al-Sadr’s execution to April 8; the broader circumstances of the siblings’ deaths should be approached with care. [29] Their deaths became important symbols of repression for many Shiʿi activists and deepened hostility between the Iraqi government and revolutionary Iran. The detailed chronology and circumstances are treated differently in later accounts; the month and the responsibility of the Iraqi authorities are the relevant established points for this chapter. The event should be interpreted in relation to the regime's effort to suppress independent religious-political authority rather than as an isolated confrontation between individual religious figures and a single official.

Another element of the period was the expulsion of people whom Iraqi authorities classified as having Iranian nationality or origins, including members of communities with longstanding residence in Iraq. Official classifications did not always correspond to the ways those affected understood their own identities or citizenship. The expulsions separated families, disrupted livelihoods and intensified bilateral grievances. They also reveal how interstate rivalry could be transformed into restrictions on individuals who possessed no responsibility for their governments' policies. A historian must distinguish documented government actions from the general accusations of foreign loyalty that officials used to justify them. The Feyli Kurdish community was particularly affected by these expulsions and denationalization. [12]

The Iranian Revolution therefore entered Iraqi politics through multiple channels. It presented an ideological alternative to Baʿathist authority, intensified suspicions concerning domestic opposition and altered Baghdad's assessment of the strategic environment. Yet the Iraqi government had a choice among responses. It could have relied on diplomacy, sought additional guarantees against interference, or pursued other measures short of war. Its domestic repression was already a feature of its political system. The existence of opposition, even where supported by voices in a neighboring state, did not establish that an invasion of that state was necessary. Regime-security concerns help explain the context in which Iraq's leadership acted; they do not constitute legal authorization for the decision that followed.

Section 96.11

The Iranian Revolution and the Disruption of Military Institutions

The Iranian armed forces entered the revolutionary period as institutions that had been closely identified with the Pahlavi state. The shah had invested heavily in the development of the army, air force and navy. Their organizational structure depended on centralized command, a professional officer corps and extensive relationships with foreign suppliers and technical specialists. The collapse of the monarchy deprived those institutions of the political authority around which they had been organized. The new revolutionary leadership regarded elements of the armed forces as potential sources of resistance to the revolution, while many officers and enlisted personnel faced uncertainty concerning their future. The relationship between the military and the new state had to be reconstructed while the country was experiencing economic, administrative and political disruption.

The removal of senior officers was an important source of institutional change. Some were tried or executed; others left the country, retired or were removed from their posts. Reassignments and new appointments altered established chains of authority. The disruption extended beyond the loss of individual leaders, since a professional organization depends on accumulated experience, routines and relations of confidence among personnel. The challenge of reestablishing those arrangements was especially acute in a period when loyalty to the revolution had become a major criterion of political trust. It is important to distinguish the various ways personnel left service rather than presenting all departures as executions or attributing every organizational difficulty to a single policy.

The revolution also altered the international support relationships on which the military had relied. The departure of foreign advisers and specialists, the deterioration of relations with the United States, disputes over contracts and the restrictions associated with the hostage crisis complicated maintenance and procurement. Advanced military systems require more than the equipment itself. They depend on technical knowledge, trained personnel, maintenance schedules, spare parts and institutions capable of paying for and receiving supplies. An organization may retain substantial equipment while experiencing declining readiness. Conversely, interrupted supply relationships do not automatically render all existing equipment unusable. The extent of these problems varied across institutions and over time.

As Chapter 95 explains, the Islamic Republic simultaneously developed revolutionary security and military organizations. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps emerged in 1979 and received recognition under Article 150 of the new constitution. The regular armed forces continued to exist alongside it. Their coexistence reflected the government's need for professional national defense and its desire for an institution identified with revolutionary authority. The organizations possessed different origins, forms of recruitment and political relationships. Their duties were not always clearly separated in practice, and the competition for influence could complicate coordination. Nevertheless, the creation of the Guards was not equivalent to the replacement of the regular military. The later history of the war would demonstrate the importance of both institutions.

Iran's military difficulties were politically visible, but their actual effects were not easily assessed from outside the country. Officials in other governments could observe changes in leadership, disrupted international relations and public struggles over authority. From these they might infer a decline in Iran's ability to defend itself. Such an inference could be reasonable without being complete. A military institution retains capabilities that are less visible in political reporting: trained technical staff, professional knowledge, established units, organizational memory and personnel committed to the defense of their country irrespective of their attitude toward a former regime. Judging the effective capacity of the Iranian armed forces required attention to those continuities as well as to the disruptions. [13]

The political significance of this issue becomes clearer when it is separated into three questions. What had changed in Iran's military organization? What did the Iraqi leadership believe those changes meant? And how did Iran's institutions actually respond after the invasion? The first is a question about the revolution's effects, the second about Iraqi strategic assessment, and the third about the consequences of the decision to invade. The evidence of disruption supports the argument that Iraq perceived an opportunity; it does not support a claim that Iranian resistance was impossible. As the final division of this chapter demonstrates, the state under attack proved capable of organizing a defense despite its unresolved domestic political disputes. Chapter 97 will examine the much longer process through which wartime mobilization transformed the relations among these institutions.

Section 96.12

Revolutionary Foreign Policy and the Escalation of Bilateral Hostility

The Islamic Republic's foreign policy emerged from a revolution whose leaders rejected the international relationships of the Pahlavi monarchy. The principle commonly expressed as neither East nor West indicated opposition to dependence on either of the two Cold War superpowers. The 1979 constitution also combined commitments to national independence with a broader concern for the struggles of oppressed peoples. Article 154 is particularly instructive: it asserted support for such struggles while also affirming noninterference in the domestic affairs of other nations. The relationship between these commitments was not self-explanatory. Different political figures could understand revolutionary solidarity and the principle of sovereignty in different ways, and foreign governments could interpret Iranian declarations differently from the intentions claimed by their authors. [30]

Khomeini's public appeals were not confined to Iranian citizens. His denunciation of governments he regarded as unjust, including the Baʿathist state in Iraq, gave Iranian revolutionary politics an international dimension. These messages mattered because political authority in the Islamic Republic was associated with a religious and ideological movement rather than exclusively with the conventional institutions of diplomacy. The president, foreign ministry and other offices could speak in the language of interstate relations, while revolutionary leaders and organizations might address audiences abroad directly. As Chapters 93 and 94 show, the distribution of authority within Iran complicated the ability of officials to present a consistent foreign policy.

The Iraqi government interpreted the Iranian Revolution in part through its concerns about domestic opposition. Statements supporting Islamic revolution and condemning the Baʿathist leadership could be understood in Baghdad as evidence of hostile intentions. Iraqi representatives accused Iran of interference in Iraqi affairs and of contributing to unrest. Iranian representatives, in turn, accused Iraq of supporting separatist activity and violating the frontier settlement. There were real conflicts within both societies, but the existence of a government's accusation is not proof that every act by a domestic opposition movement was planned or controlled from abroad. The historian must examine the organizational relationships and documentary evidence underlying such claims. [11]

Ideological language sharpened these conflicts. Arab nationalism could be invoked by Iraqi leaders to portray a dispute with Iran as a wider conflict involving Arab political identity. Iranian revolutionary rhetoric could characterize the Iraqi government as hostile to Islam rather than simply as a rival state. These forms of political communication made compromise more difficult by connecting territorial disagreements to claims concerning religious or national legitimacy. They also risked extending blame from political institutions to populations whose beliefs and identities were diverse. Yet the governments' rhetoric did not exhaust the relationship between the two countries. Trade, kinship, pilgrimage and the activities of religious institutions continued to connect their societies even as state relations deteriorated.

A useful analytical concept is the security dilemma. When governments distrust one another, action undertaken by one government as a protective measure may be interpreted by the other as evidence of preparation for aggression. Measures adopted in response may then confirm the first government's suspicions. The concept helps explain why hostility intensified, especially where authoritative information about the other government's intentions was scarce. It has limits, however. Not all measures taken during this period were defensive, and the fact that two governments fear each other does not establish that their responsibilities for a subsequent war are symmetrical. It is therefore necessary to distinguish reciprocal suspicion from a deliberate decision to revise the interstate relationship through invasion.

The history of the bilateral relationship during 1979–1980 was also shaped by unfinished domestic political processes in Iran. The Islamic Republic was creating constitutional institutions, suppressing some of its opponents and attempting to establish authority over regional movements. Baghdad saw in these events both potential danger and potential opportunity. A government perceived as ideologically threatening might also appear temporarily unable to defend itself. These two assessments could coexist in the Iraqi leadership's calculations. They help explain why its concerns were not limited to requests for protection against foreign interference. The following division examines how Iraq's regional position and the responses of outside governments affected the perceived costs and benefits of a decision for war. Throughout that analysis, students should preserve the distinction between rhetoric, observable policy, strategic inference and the final use of force.

Section 96.13

The Persian Gulf States and the Revolutionary Balance of Power

The Iranian Revolution created uncertainty throughout the Persian Gulf. Until 1979 the shah had been an important participant in the region's security arrangements, supported by his relationship with the United States and the resources of the Iranian petroleum economy. His overthrow did not transfer that role automatically to Iraq or to any other state. Rather, it created a period in which neighboring governments had to reconsider the nature of regional stability. Some viewed the Islamic Republic as a challenge to established political authority, while others were particularly concerned about the economic consequences of instability. Their policies reflected local circumstances as well as their assessments of the changing balance between Iran and Iraq.

Saudi Arabia possessed substantial petroleum resources and an established relationship with the United States. Its ruling family was concerned about revolutionary appeals that challenged monarchical authority and about the effects of political upheaval in the oil-producing region. The relationship with revolutionary Iran was therefore increasingly difficult. Kuwait shared a border with Iraq and had major commercial and financial interests in the Gulf. Its leaders also had to account for earlier Iraqi claims and the vulnerability associated with living beside a much larger state. Concern about Iran did not eliminate concern about Iraq. Both governments had reasons to favor regional stability, and their eventual financial assistance to Iraq during the war must be understood as part of a developing policy rather than projected back as a prewar collective decision to invade Iran.

The smaller Gulf monarchies faced distinct combinations of economic and political pressures. Bahrain's population and political history made the relationship between religious identity and political authority especially sensitive. The United Arab Emirates had an unresolved territorial dispute with Iran concerning the islands discussed in Section 6, but this dispute did not establish an identical foreign policy among all of its emirates. Qatar was developing its own economic and diplomatic interests. Oman occupied an important position at the entrance to the Gulf and had experienced Iranian involvement in its domestic conflict during the shah's reign. Its government had reasons to maintain functioning relations with Iran as well as with Arab neighbors and Western partners. The diversity of these circumstances makes it inappropriate to treat the Gulf monarchies as a single decision-making body.

The wider Arab political environment was equally divided. Jordan cultivated a close relationship with Iraq, while Syria, despite also being governed by a Baʿathist party, was a rival of the Iraqi Baʿathist leadership. The competition between the Syrian and Iraqi governments demonstrates that similar ideological labels did not guarantee interstate cooperation. Syria's developing relationship with Iran became a particularly important exception to any claim that the war divided the region into an Arab camp and a Persian camp. Egypt's relations with Arab governments were also affected by its peace treaty with Israel in 1979. Regional alignments reflected several overlapping conflicts, not simply a single division concerning Iran. The governments of the Gulf had to make their own assessments within this complicated political landscape.

One chronological safeguard is essential. The Gulf Cooperation Council was formally established by the six member states at their Abu Dhabi meeting on May 25, 1981. It did not exist as an institution when Iraq invaded Iran in September 1980. Its creation reflected concerns that were already developing before the war, but those concerns cannot be used to attribute a collective policy to an organization that had not yet been founded. The GCC's own institutional history establishes the date. [14]

For the purposes of this chapter, three distinctions should guide interpretation of the regional environment. A government may regard another government as threatening without encouraging a war against it. It may offer political or financial assistance after war begins without having participated in the original decision. And it may cooperate with a rival on one issue while opposing it on another. The Iraqi leadership could hope that concern about revolutionary Iran would make certain neighbors receptive to Baghdad's policy, but an expectation of sympathetic reactions is not equivalent to a verified promise of support. These distinctions are particularly important because much of the later regional history of the war cannot be used as direct evidence of what governments intended in September 1980.

Section 96.14

The United States, the Soviet Union, and the International Strategic Environment

The transformation of American-Iranian relations was a major element of the international background to the war. Under the shah the United States had regarded Iran as a principal regional security partner. The revolution destroyed that arrangement and created considerable uncertainty about American policy in Southwest Asia. The hostage crisis, which began in November 1979 and continued when Iraq invaded, further restricted normal diplomatic relations and led to financial and economic measures against Iran. As Chapter 94 explains, American officials were attempting to obtain the release of the hostages while managing a broader crisis in their relationship with the Islamic Republic. The existence of this hostility did not automatically align American and Iraqi interests on all questions.

The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979 intensified American concern about the security of the Gulf. On January 23, 1980, President Jimmy Carter used his State of the Union address to announce that an attempt by an outside power to dominate the Persian Gulf would be treated as a threat to vital American interests. The declaration became known as the Carter Doctrine. Its immediate context was Soviet military intervention and concern about the international balance of power. The policy was not an authorization for one regional state to invade another. It nevertheless illustrates the extent to which the Iranian Revolution and developments in Afghanistan compelled American officials to reconsider the assumptions underlying their regional strategy. [15], [16]

The Soviet Union faced a different set of problems. Its relationship with Iraq was supported by a formal friendship treaty and extensive economic and military ties. Yet Moscow also had reasons to avoid an uncontrolled regional conflict that might increase American involvement in the Gulf or complicate its relations with revolutionary Iran. The Islamic Republic's opposition to both superpowers meant that its hostility to Washington could not be read simply as support for Moscow. Soviet intervention in Afghanistan was itself a source of tension in the region. Thus the relationship between Baghdad and the Soviet Union, although important to the development of Iraqi state capacity, does not establish that the Soviet government determined the decision to invade.

European governments had substantial commercial interests in the region. They depended to varying degrees on imported petroleum and maintained economic relationships with both countries. Their concern about the stability of energy markets was therefore accompanied by concerns about the safety of citizens, investments and commercial agreements. Different governments adopted different policies. The prospect of prolonged war in the Gulf was particularly problematic because it threatened an economic system that connected regional producers with distant consumers. These international relationships increased the consequences of the war's outbreak, but they do not establish that external powers shared a unified policy concerning the conflict.

A particularly contested historical question is whether the United States gave Saddam Hussein an explicit or implicit signal that an invasion would be acceptable. Hal Brands's 2011 article in Cold War History addresses this allegation through the problem of the available record. The controversy requires students to distinguish evidence of an actual assurance from evidence that Iraqi officials perceived an opportunity arising from American hostility to Iran. A diplomatic contact, a shared opponent or an expression of concern about the Islamic Republic is not sufficient in itself to demonstrate permission to initiate war. Establishing such a claim would require reliable evidence concerning the content, timing and reception of the communication in question. [17] The accessible bibliographic record identifies the article's research question; the full text was not available for collation here.

The chronology of subsequent policy provides another important safeguard. During later stages of the conflict the United States moved toward supporting Iraq in important ways, while also pursuing policies that reflected its own priorities and at times generated contradictions. Those developments belong principally to Chapter 97. A later shift cannot be treated as proof of an earlier authorization unless independent contemporary evidence establishes a connection. Likewise, the fact that the Soviet Union supplied Iraq before the war does not by itself demonstrate its approval of the initial invasion. International context matters because it shaped the Iraqi leadership's assessment of opportunity, constraints and likely consequences. It should not be used to erase the distinction between a state's own decision and the actions of the governments with which it maintained relationships.

Section 96.15

Saddam Hussein's Strategic Calculations and Iraqi War Aims

The decision to invade Iran was made within an Iraqi political system that concentrated authority in its leadership. The preceding sections have identified a number of circumstances that made a confrontation increasingly likely: the unresolved legacy of the frontier dispute, the changing regional balance, the Iranian Revolution, and Baghdad's fears concerning domestic opposition. The problem for the historian is to explain why these circumstances produced a full-scale invasion in September 1980 rather than a renewed diplomatic effort, a limited confrontation, or the continuation of the existing rivalry. An explanation of the decision requires more than a list of grievances. It requires attention to the political objectives that war was expected to serve and the judgments concerning its likely costs.

The most clearly documented declared objective was revision of the frontier settlement, especially the distribution of authority over the Shatt al-Arab. Saddam Hussein publicly repudiated the 1975 agreement and asserted Iraqi claims concerning the waterway and disputed territory. The declaration provides direct evidence of the terms in which Baghdad publicly presented its policy. Other evidence, however, indicates that the war had a wider political context. Iraqi leaders were concerned about the implications of the Islamic Republic for the stability of their government and about Iraq's position within the Gulf. A settlement that limited Iranian influence could therefore appear attractive for reasons extending beyond the border itself. [11], [18]

Historical accounts have also emphasized ambitions concerning regional leadership. The disappearance of the shah's government altered the political order in which Iraq had accepted the Algiers agreement. Baghdad had acquired substantial resources through oil income, state-led development and foreign relationships. The Iraqi leadership could perceive an opportunity to obtain concessions that had been unavailable when the Iranian monarchy was stronger. This interpretation does not require the assumption that Iraq possessed a single, precisely defined plan for dominating the entire region. Regional ambition is an analytical description of the possible benefits of an altered balance of power; its significance must be established through the political record rather than assumed from the outcome of the war.

Another important question concerns Khuzestan and the wider territorial or political ambitions attributed to Iraq. The economic importance of the region and the use of nationalist claims concerning its Arab population made it a prominent subject of the confrontation. Some interpretations emphasize an expectation that Iranian Arab communities might support Baghdad or that control of part of the province could be used in negotiations. Others emphasize the objective of weakening or destabilizing the Islamic Republic. These possibilities must not be collapsed into an unqualified assertion that Iraq had a formally settled program to annex the entire province or overthrow the Iranian government. Public demands, military conduct, internal documents and later recollections are different kinds of evidence. They may illuminate different dimensions of policy without proving that all the objectives identified by historians were equally important at the time of the decision.

The political condition of Iran contributed to the Iraqi government's perception of opportunity. The revolution had disrupted military command, created competition among institutions and altered the country's foreign relationships. These circumstances were visible to outside observers. It was possible to infer from them that the Islamic Republic would have difficulty responding effectively to a major external challenge. But such an inference was not a demonstration that Iranian society lacked the capacity to resist. The ability of a government to resolve a domestic political dispute and the willingness of a population to defend national territory are not the same thing. A state with serious institutional problems can still possess considerable resources and political capacity for national defense.

The concept of strategic miscalculation clarifies the difference between intended benefits and actual consequences. In this case, the invasion did not yield a rapid settlement favorable to Iraq. That outcome demonstrates that the expectations on which the decision rested were inadequate, but it does not give the historian direct access to every deliberation of the Iraqi leadership. Scholarship based on records of Iraqi decision-making is especially valuable for separating contemporary assessments from later efforts to justify them. [18], [19] The weight assigned to territorial objectives, regional ambition and regime security may differ among historians, but these explanations need not be mutually exclusive. The central point is that structural pressures became war through a political choice based on a particular assessment of power and opportunity. That choice, rather than an abstract inevitability of conflict, is the historical event requiring explanation.

Section 96.16

Border Escalation and the Failure of Diplomacy

Relations between Iran and Iraq deteriorated during 1979 and 1980 through a series of disputes involving the frontier, domestic opposition, diplomatic communications and competing claims of sovereignty. Each government attributed hostile intentions to the other. Baghdad accused Tehran of interference and violations of the 1975 settlement, while Tehran accused Baghdad of supporting political activities against the Islamic Republic and failing to respect its territorial rights. Reports of armed incidents multiplied. The precise chronology and responsibility for individual incidents are often contested because the governments reported them through communications intended to establish the legitimacy of their own positions. A historian should therefore treat such documents both as evidence of events requiring corroboration and as evidence of the ways governments presented the developing crisis.

One reason the dispute proved difficult to manage was the relationship between interstate diplomacy and the internal political structures of the two governments. In Iran authority remained divided among constitutional institutions and revolutionary organizations. Officials responsible for foreign relations did not necessarily control every political statement or activity that Iraq regarded as threatening. In Iraq authority was more concentrated, but this did not guarantee a greater willingness to negotiate. The institutional ability to make a decision is distinct from the political incentive to reach a compromise. A state may possess a functioning foreign ministry and established diplomatic channels while its leadership decides that negotiation is less attractive than coercion.

The 1975 instruments provided a possible framework for addressing disagreements. The governments could have examined alleged violations through joint institutions, negotiated questions concerning the frontier and sought assistance under the treaty's dispute-settlement arrangements. Article 6 was designed for disputes that direct negotiation could not resolve. The existence of these arrangements is important because it demonstrates that the historical alternatives were not limited to accepting the other government's demands or initiating full-scale war. Diplomacy required a willingness to recognize the other party as a participant in a legal process, however, and the political deterioration of 1979–1980 undermined that willingness. The governments' increasingly hostile public rhetoric further raised the domestic political costs of compromise.

Escalation can be analyzed as a process in which incidents and accusations alter the expectations of political leaders. An unresolved claim may be interpreted as evidence that an existing settlement is ineffective. A hostile public statement may increase fear of future interference. Responses to those developments may lead officials in the other state to conclude that further confrontation is likely. This sequence helps explain how governments can become trapped in a pattern of reciprocal hostility. It does not mean that every subsequent decision follows automatically from the preceding one. Political leaders can choose to limit, investigate or negotiate a dispute even when they believe that their opponent has acted unlawfully.

The Iraqi government increasingly presented the 1975 settlement as an arrangement that Iran had failed to implement. Iran did not accept this characterization. The dispute concerned both specific allegations about territory and the broader claim that an existing international agreement was no longer binding. Those questions must be separated. A disputed violation of a treaty is a subject for examination under applicable legal rules. It does not establish a general entitlement to revise borders by war. The later assessment of the United Nations Secretary-General explicitly recognized the difference between possible earlier Iranian encroachment and the legal responsibility entailed by Iraq's attack in September 1980. [20]

By September the Iraqi leadership chose to abandon the diplomatic framework it had accepted in 1975 and to pursue its claims through full-scale war. This was the decisive transformation of the crisis. Earlier border confrontations and Iranian revolutionary rhetoric are part of the causal history, and their omission would produce an incomplete account. Yet treating those circumstances as an automatic or equivalent explanation for the invasion would obscure the agency of the government that initiated it. The distinction between contributing conditions and initiating decisions is fundamental to historical reasoning. The causal diagram accompanying this section should be read as a model of relationships among conditions, not a prediction that war necessarily follows whenever the same conditions are present. Political alternatives remained conceivable, and their rejection is itself a subject of historical explanation.

Section 96.17

September 1980: Iraq's Repudiation of the Algiers Settlement

On September 17, 1980, Saddam Hussein announced to Iraq's National Assembly that the government no longer regarded the 1975 Algiers arrangement as valid. The announcement is the appropriate starting point for understanding the immediate political decision that preceded full-scale war. It changed the character of the dispute. Earlier complaints concerning compliance with the agreement had involved disagreement over obligations acknowledged at least in principle. The new declaration challenged the continued existence of the framework itself. Iraq maintained that Iran had violated its commitments and asserted that Iraqi authority over the Shatt al-Arab should be restored. The declaration expressed the government's public position; the validity of its legal claims requires separate examination. [11], [21]

The Iraqi Foreign Ministry communicated its position to the Iranian embassy and later presented its interpretation to the United Nations. The Iraqi communication dated September 24, 1980, recorded in the UN Digital Library, is an identifiable primary source for the government's assertion that the March 1975 agreement had terminated. The UN Yearbook for 1980 also summarizes the Iraqi diplomatic notes and subsequent exchanges. These materials show that the dispute extended beyond the river boundary to complaints concerning internal security, alleged interference and contested territorial obligations. A reader must distinguish the date of an official communication from the date of the events it describes. The September 24 letter followed the commencement of full-scale war and should not be described as the September 17 speech itself. [21], [22]

The Islamic Republic did not accept Iraq's assertion that the treaty had ceased to be binding. For Iran, recognition of the agreed frontier represented a central aspect of territorial sovereignty that could not be removed by the other state's unilateral declaration. The revolutionary government had inherited a territorial settlement negotiated by the monarchy it had overthrown. Although the two Iranian governments differed fundamentally in their political systems and foreign relationships, the Islamic Republic did not regard the change of regime as an automatic repudiation of the country's recognized frontier. This illustrates an important distinction between a change of government and the continuity of the state as a participant in international law.

The 1975 treaty contained provisions intended to make the settlement durable. Articles 1 and 2 confirmed the agreed land and river frontiers, and Article 3 addressed border security. Article 4 treated the arrangements as an integrated settlement and committed the parties to respect them. Article 5 separately affirmed the permanence and inviolability of the agreed boundaries. The treaty's peaceful dispute-resolution machinery provided an avenue for addressing disagreements concerning its interpretation or implementation. The political importance of these clauses is that Baghdad had previously accepted a procedure for resolving the types of controversy it subsequently cited in repudiating the agreement. Whether a particular alleged violation had occurred and whether it amounted to a legally relevant breach were questions that required evidence and legal analysis. Neither question was answered conclusively by the issuing of a political declaration. [3]

The broader principles of international law also matter. The stability of international boundaries is an important element of interstate relations. Treaty law provides rules concerning termination and suspension, but those rules cannot be read as general permission for one state to employ force against another. The United Nations Charter requires states to pursue peaceful means of settling international disputes and prohibits the threat or use of force against territorial integrity or political independence, subject to the Charter's applicable exceptions. [32] Even a claim that a treaty has been breached does not settle the distinct question of whether military action is lawful. It is therefore necessary to distinguish the treaty controversy from the subsequent invasion.

The political contrast with 1975 is striking. Saddam Hussein had publicly accepted the terms of the Algiers settlement when Baghdad faced different domestic and regional constraints. In 1980 his government publicly challenged those terms at a moment when Iran was experiencing revolutionary disruption. The shift is evidence of the relationship between assessments of political power and diplomatic policy. It does not demonstrate that the previous settlement was inherently incapable of working, nor does it establish that a full-scale invasion was the only available response. The decision announced on September 17 must be understood as a deliberate change of policy within an escalating dispute. Five days later, the Iraqi invasion transformed that dispute into a major interstate war.

Section 96.18

September 22, 1980: The Iraqi Invasion of Iran

The full-scale Iran–Iraq War began on September 22, 1980, when Iraqi forces invaded Iran. This date is distinguished from the earlier period of border clashes and political confrontation. The beginning of full-scale war represented a qualitative change in the relationship between the two countries: disputes previously conducted through a mixture of diplomatic pressure, limited armed incidents and political intervention had become sustained armed conflict between sovereign states. The Iraqi government described its actions in terms of security and territorial claims. The Iranian government regarded the invasion as aggression against its territorial integrity. The existence of competing public descriptions does not eliminate the need to establish the historical event itself or assess its legal significance. [11], [20]

The geographical scope of the crisis reflected the longstanding importance of the western Iranian frontier and the southwestern borderlands. Khuzestan, in particular, became central to the experience of the opening months of the war. Its cities, economic facilities and communities were connected to national petroleum production and to the commercial geography of the Shatt al-Arab. These places had figured in interstate disputes before 1980, but the invasion transformed their economic and political significance into a direct threat to the lives and livelihoods of their inhabitants. Students should understand this relationship between the earlier geography of rivalry and the human consequences of war without confusing the location of an affected community with the political allegiance of its residents.

The Iraqi leadership's public claims drew on a history of disagreements concerning the border. However, the scope of the new confrontation went far beyond the technical processes through which the 1975 settlement had been established. A river-boundary dispute could be addressed through legal interpretation and hydrographic work; a question about the implementation of land demarcation could be investigated through joint procedures. The invasion instead imposed on Iran a situation in which defense of national territory became an immediate priority. This altered the incentives confronting political actors in both states. Questions of diplomatic compromise were now inseparable from questions concerning the territory occupied during the conflict, the security of civilians and the prospect of further military action.

The political consequences for revolutionary Iran were especially significant. In the months before the invasion, the Islamic Republic had experienced intense disputes over the organization of its constitutional institutions and the authority of its revolutionary bodies. The attack introduced an external threat that demanded a national response. Officials who disagreed on the proper distribution of political authority nevertheless confronted the same fundamental problem of territorial defense. The relationship between revolutionary ideology and national identity became more complex: the state presented resistance as the defense of the revolution, while citizens could understand their participation as defense of their country, their communities or their families. These motives could overlap without being identical.

The civilian experience must also be integrated into the account of the war's beginning. For residents of affected Iranian and Iraqi communities, the escalation was not merely a change in government policy. It brought uncertainty about family members, interruption of commerce, loss of access to public services and the possibility of displacement. People whose livelihoods depended on regional trade were confronted by the breakdown of ordinary interstate relations. Communities that had maintained family or religious connections across the boundary were now divided by a major war. The early effects were uneven, and the later record of civilian losses and displacement cannot be assigned wholesale to the opening months. The wider social history of these developments belongs to Chapter 97.

The distinction between the outbreak of full-scale war and the entire eight-year conflict is essential to this chapter's chronology. By the end of 1980, neither a final territorial settlement nor a rapid political conclusion had been achieved. The governments' objectives and capabilities would continue to change during the years of fighting. The fact that Iraq initiated the full-scale invasion establishes responsibility for that initiating decision, but it does not answer every historical question concerning the later conduct of the war. Those questions require their own evidence and chronological analysis. Chapter 96 therefore focuses on the transformation of a deteriorating interstate relationship into sustained armed conflict; it leaves detailed campaigns and subsequent developments to Chapter 97.

Section 96.19

Iranian Resistance and the Failure of a Rapid Iraqi Victory

The opening months of the war demonstrated the inadequacy of the expectation that the Iranian Revolution had left the country incapable of effective resistance. Iran had experienced military disruption, political divisions and international isolation, as Section 11 explains. Those difficulties were real. They did not mean that the country lacked professional military institutions, functioning administrative organizations or citizens willing to defend their communities. The war introduced a new political circumstance in which the interests of diverse Iranian actors could converge around national defense, even where their views concerning the constitution and revolutionary leadership remained different. The ability to mobilize around a shared threat should be distinguished from the existence of political unanimity.

The regular armed forces retained important organizational capacities. Their personnel possessed training and experience that could not be erased by a change of regime, and the institutions continued to function despite changes in leadership and supply relationships. Revolutionary organizations, including the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, also participated in the defense of the country. Their relationship with the regular forces was neither perfectly coordinated nor simply antagonistic. The urgent demands of war required cooperation, while disputes concerning authority and organization persisted. The history of the opening months thus reinforces an earlier conclusion: the revolution weakened certain aspects of Iranian military capacity while simultaneously creating new institutions of mobilization. Evaluating the country solely through evidence of disruption underestimated these continuities and changes. [13], [23]

National resistance also had a social dimension. The affected regions were inhabited by citizens with varied religious, linguistic and political identities. Baghdad's nationalist rhetoric did not determine the response of Iranian Arabs or other residents of Khuzestan. Many regarded the invasion as an attack on their communities and their country. Individuals could participate in the defense of Iran without endorsing every policy of the Islamic Republic. This distinction is crucial in an account of wartime legitimacy: a government may benefit politically from national mobilization without being able to claim that every participant accepts its broader political program. The experiences of local residents, military personnel, volunteers and displaced families should be recognized as historical subjects in their own right.

Khorramshahr became a particularly important symbol of the opening period. The city suffered severe destruction and came under Iraqi occupation on October 24, 1980, according to the historical chronology in Encyclopaedia Iranica, after prolonged resistance. [23] Its history illustrates both the costs of the invasion and the limits of the expectation that the war would produce a quick Iranian capitulation. Abadan and other cities of southwestern Iran also faced severe disruption. The significance of these events in the present chapter is not the detailed sequence of military operations, but the political and social reality that a prolonged confrontation had emerged. Khorramshahr's subsequent liberation in May 1982 belongs to the fuller military and social history of Chapter 97. It should not be treated as an event of the initial months of the war.

The failure to secure an early settlement also exposed the difference between territorial occupation and political success. A state may acquire control of territory without obtaining the agreement of its opponent to a new boundary or political arrangement. Conversely, the inability to prevent the loss of territory does not necessarily demonstrate that a government's willingness to resist has collapsed. Political objectives require outcomes beyond temporary changes in territorial control. The opening phase of the Iran–Iraq War illustrates this distinction especially well. Iraq had initiated a conflict in the expectation that the prevailing conditions offered opportunities for advantageous change; by the end of 1980 it faced a continuing war whose costs and consequences extended well beyond the immediate disputes of September.

The effect of the invasion on domestic Iranian politics was equally important. National defense became a central subject of revolutionary mobilization, and the institutions responsible for the war acquired increased importance. Yet the conflicts between Bani-Sadr, the Islamic Republican Party and other political actors did not disappear, as Chapter 95 demonstrates. The wartime government had to confront both the demands of defense and the continuing struggle over political authority. Students should not infer that the invasion instantly resolved the institutional tensions of the Islamic Republic. Rather, it altered the environment in which those tensions were contested. By December 1980 the war had become a central condition of Iranian political and economic life, setting the stage for the transformations examined in the next chapter.

Section 96.20

International Responses, Responsibility for the War, and the Historical Transition

The outbreak of full-scale war brought the United Nations into an increasingly urgent diplomatic controversy. Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim appealed to both governments on September 22, 1980, and offered his good offices. The Security Council's president issued a statement on September 23 expressing concern about the deteriorating situation. On September 28 the Council unanimously adopted Resolution 479. The resolution called on Iran and Iraq to stop further use of force, pursue a peaceful settlement and accept appropriate mediation or other means of resolving their dispute. It also urged other states to exercise restraint so that the conflict would not widen. The text is an important contemporary record of the international response. [24]

The resolution did not expressly identify Iraq as the aggressor or require an immediate, specified withdrawal of Iraqi forces from occupied Iranian territory. This omission mattered to Tehran, which viewed a ceasefire that left its territory occupied as an inadequate basis for a settlement. The Iranian and Iraqi governments approached mediation from different political positions. For Iraq, a cessation of fighting could potentially preserve gains obtained after the invasion; for Iran, territorial restoration was a central concern. Negotiators thus confronted a recurring difficulty in conflict resolution: agreement to stop fighting does not necessarily establish agreement concerning the legal status of territory, the responsibility for initiating the war or the conditions of a permanent peace.

International diplomacy continued beyond the initial resolution. Waldheim's efforts included appointing the former Swedish prime minister Olof Palme as his special representative on November 11, 1980. Other regional and international actors also explored possibilities for mediation. The persistence of diplomatic initiatives shows that the outbreak of war did not eliminate the search for negotiated alternatives. Nevertheless, the governments' incompatible positions, the continuing conflict and the broader regional political environment prevented a rapid settlement. The international institutions involved could offer assistance and exert political pressure, but they could not simply impose a mutually acceptable outcome. The history of later efforts to end the war belongs principally to Chapters 97 and 98. [22], [25]

A distinct retrospective legal assessment came more than a decade later. Under paragraph 6 of Security Council Resolution 598 of 1987, the Secretary-General was asked to pursue an inquiry into responsibility for the conflict. In his report of December 9, 1991, document S/23273, Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar concluded that Iraq's September 22 attack on Iran was not justified under international law and entailed responsibility for the conflict. The report further explained that possible earlier Iranian territorial encroachment would not justify Iraq's aggression. This is a specific documented conclusion by the Secretary-General, not a ruling of the International Court of Justice or the wording of Resolution 479. The different dates, authors and purposes of these documents are fundamental to their interpretation. [20], [26]

The distinction between historical causation and legal responsibility is the central lesson of the chapter. The historian must examine the territorial disputes, economic geography, political rivalries, revolutionary transformations and diplomatic failures that contributed to the emergence of war. Recognizing those factors does not distribute responsibility for initiating full-scale war evenly across all of the governments or communities involved. Likewise, the legal judgment concerning the initiating use of force does not require the historian to deny the existence of earlier disputes or the importance of Iranian revolutionary foreign policy. Explanation is not justification. A mature historical analysis can recognize complex causation while identifying the consequential choice that transformed a dispute into a war.

The Iran–Iraq War therefore began through the interaction of long-term conditions and immediate political decisions. The Algiers settlement of 1975 shows that alternatives to full-scale war had been available; the transformation of Iran after 1979 altered the circumstances in which the Iraqi leadership assessed those alternatives. The ensuing invasion failed to produce a rapid resolution and imposed a new set of challenges on both societies. For the Islamic Republic, national defense, revolutionary mobilization, the allocation of economic resources and the relationships among military and political institutions would become inseparable from the war. Chapter 97 turns from the origins of the conflict to these consequences: how the war was conducted, how Iranian society experienced it, and how its memory became part of the political and cultural history of modern Iran.

Chapter Summary

The modern Iranian-Iraqi frontier emerged through centuries of imperial negotiation and twentieth-century state formation. The Treaty of Zuhab provided an early framework, while the Erzurum treaties and the 1913–1914 boundary work progressively addressed territorial questions. The establishment of independent Iraq introduced a new interstate setting for disagreements inherited from the Ottoman period.

The Shatt al-Arab–Arvand Rud was both a commercial waterway and a disputed frontier. Jurisdiction over its lower shared reach, navigation rights and the regulation of channel changes were distinct questions. The 1937 treaty generally placed the boundary near the Iranian bank with exceptions. The 1975 river protocol instead adopted the thalweg along the shared stretch.

The economic significance of Khuzestan and southern Iraq increased with the development of petroleum production, refining and maritime trade. Cross-border communities retained social, linguistic and religious connections, but those relationships did not establish a single political allegiance. Economic geography supplied reasons both to compete and to cooperate.

The Pahlavi and Baʿathist governments pursued different regional ambitions and international relationships. The 1969 Iranian denunciation of the 1937 treaty, the development of Gulf rivalry and the Kurdish conflict in Iraq increased tensions. Kurdish organizations sought political recognition and autonomy, while outside governments offered assistance for purposes that did not necessarily coincide with Kurdish objectives.

The Algiers communiqué of March 6, 1975, linked the two governments’ priorities. The June treaty and associated protocols established the legal framework, and the exchange of ratifications in 1976 brought it into force. Iraq accepted a revised river frontier; Iran ended its assistance to the Iraqi Kurdish insurgency. The settlement included procedures for peaceful dispute resolution.

The Iranian Revolution transformed the political setting. Khomeini’s rise to leadership made transnational religious networks and revolutionary appeals a concern for Baghdad. Saddam Hussein’s assumption of the Iraqi presidency further concentrated governmental authority. The repression of Iraqi religious opposition and the expulsion of people classified as of Iranian origin intensified hostility.

Iran’s military institutions experienced purges, personnel changes and disrupted international support after the revolution. Yet the regular forces continued to function alongside new revolutionary organizations. Baghdad’s perception that Iran was vulnerable must be distinguished from the country’s actual capacity for resistance.

The international environment included the American hostage crisis, Soviet intervention in Afghanistan, the Carter Doctrine and divergent regional interests. The later development of foreign support for Iraq cannot prove earlier authorization of the invasion. The GCC did not yet exist in September 1980. Evidence of outside contacts or shared concerns must not be substituted for evidence of an explicit decision to encourage war.

On September 17, 1980, Iraq repudiated the 1975 settlement. On September 22 it initiated full-scale invasion. Iranian resistance and the continued operation of state institutions prevented a rapid political settlement, even as the conflict brought destruction and displacement to affected communities. By the end of 1980 the war had become a central condition of Iranian political life.

Resolution 479 sought a peaceful settlement without expressly designating an aggressor. The Secretary-General’s 1991 report, submitted in connection with Resolution 598, concluded that Iraq’s initiating attack was unjustified and entailed responsibility for the conflict. The distinction between complex historical causes and responsibility for a specific political decision prepares the reader for Chapter 97’s account of wartime conduct and social change.

Chapter Reflection

The origins of the Iran–Iraq War demonstrate that conflict is rarely explained adequately by a single historical grievance. Geographical and economic conditions created enduring interests, but their political importance changed as states acquired new capacities, alliances and institutions. The agreement of 1975 shows that rivalry could be managed through diplomacy when leaders regarded cooperation as advantageous.

The years following the Iranian Revolution reveal the importance of perceptions. Baghdad confronted a state undergoing political and military transformation. An assessment of vulnerability could influence policy even when the actual capacity of the opposing society was greater than anticipated. Historical outcomes therefore require attention to both the material circumstances of a decision and the limits of the information or assumptions underlying it.

Finally, the outbreak of war demonstrates why explanation and responsibility should not be confused. Students can recognize contested historical grievances, failures of diplomacy and competing ideological claims while distinguishing the Iraqi decision to initiate full-scale war from the wider conditions in which it occurred. That distinction provides an essential foundation for the study of the prolonged conflict, its human costs and its place in Iranian historical memory.
