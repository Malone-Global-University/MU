The Young Shah, the Azerbaijan Crisis, and Postwar Political Competition

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between 1945 and 1947 Iran faced a question that its constitutional order had never been forced to answer so starkly: who has the authority to govern Iranian territory, and in whose name? The answer emerged from a crisis in the northwest, a diplomatic contest in Moscow and New York, a military advance in December 1946, and a parliamentary vote in October 1947. It left the territory restored and the larger constitutional question open.

In the winter of 1945–1946, two provincial centers in northwestern Iran, Tabriz and Mahabad, were governed by authorities that did not take orders from Tehran. In Tabriz, a self-styled National Government led by Ja'far Pishevari ruled in the name of Iranian Azerbaijan. In Mahabad, Qazi Muhammad presided over a Kurdish administration whose supporters called it a republic. Both existed inside the Soviet occupation zone, and both were shielded, in different ways and to different degrees, by the presence of Soviet troops. Neither can be understood as simply a Soviet invention, and neither can be understood without the Soviet Union.

Chapter 84 showed how the Anglo-Soviet invasion of August 1941 ended the personal rule of Reza Shah, left his son on the throne, and reopened a political arena that had been closed for years. This chapter follows what happened when that arena, still under foreign occupation, met the end of the Second World War. The occupying powers had promised in writing to leave. The Soviet Union did not leave on schedule, and its delay coincided with the proclamation of autonomous governments in the provinces it held. The crisis that followed was at once a regional conflict, an Iranian constitutional struggle, and one of the first major confrontations of the Cold War.

The chapter's governing question is how Iran restored its territorial sovereignty after World War II while struggling to establish stable constitutional government and determine who would exercise political power. The first half of that question has a relatively clear answer. Through the diplomacy of Prime Minister Ahmad Qavam, the appeal to the United Nations, American and British pressure, Soviet withdrawal in May 1946, and a military advance in December 1946, the central government regained control of Azerbaijan and Mahabad. The second half has no comparably clear answer. The Shah, the Majles, cabinets, the army, mass parties, and landed and commercial elites all contended over power, and the settlement of the northern crisis strengthened some of them more than others.

The chapter keeps three analytical distinctions in view. The first is between genuine regional grievances and the use made of them by an outside power. The second is between the Soviet withdrawal, which came in spring 1946, and the Iranian government's restoration of control, which came in December of that year; historians who merge the two misread the sequence and the role of the Iranian state. The third is between territorial restoration and political settlement. Control of Tabriz did not resolve who would control the Majles, the court, the army, or the nation's petroleum.

The evidence is uneven and often partisan. Soviet and Azerbaijani archival documents, published since the end of the Soviet Union, have clarified Soviet involvement, although access and selection remain limits. Iranian newspapers, parliamentary records, memoirs of ministers and officers, British and American diplomatic reports, United Nations records, and the publications of the autonomous governments themselves all survive, and each reflects an interest. Where accounts conflict, especially on casualty figures and on the motives of Stalin, Qavam, and the Shah, this chapter says so.

Chapter 85 occupies a hinge in the book. It follows the occupation and abdication of Chapter 84 and precedes the oil nationalization and the premiership of Mohammad Mosaddegh in Chapter 86. It should not be read as a prelude that made Chapter 86 or the 1953 coup in Chapter 87 inevitable. The chapter ends in 1949–1950, with a Shah who had survived an assassination attempt and acquired new constitutional powers, a Tudeh Party driven underground, and a new National Front pressing for honest elections. Each of these positions was contingent and contested.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the constitutional and practical limits on Mohammad Reza Shah's authority after 1941 and how they shaped his relations with the army, the court, and prime ministers.
  2. Describe the postwar party system, including the Tudeh Party, conservative and nationalist groups, trade unions, and the press.
  3. Trace the withdrawal commitments of the wartime allies and explain why the Soviet failure to meet the March 1946 deadline became an international crisis.
  4. Analyze the Azerbaijan Democratic Party and the Azerbaijan People's Government, distinguishing local grievances from Soviet sponsorship.
  5. Compare the Azerbaijani and Kurdish movements, including their social bases, leaders, relations with the Soviet Union, and relations with each other.
  6. Evaluate the roles of the United Nations appeal, Ahmad Qavam's diplomacy, American and British pressure, and Soviet decision-making in ending the occupation.
  7. Distinguish the Qavam–Sadchikov letters of April 1946 from an existing oil concession, and explain the parliamentary rejection of October 1947.
  8. Assess the labor movement and the July 1946 Khuzestan oil strike as expressions of postwar social conflict and of British economic interests.
  9. Interpret the 1949 assassination attempt, the banning of the Tudeh Party, and the constitutional amendments as steps in the reassertion of royal authority.
  10. Explain the emergence of the National Front and why electoral integrity and parliamentary authority became its central demands.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the collapse of the autonomous governments and of the character of the 1941–1949 political opening.

Historical Context

Students should enter this chapter with the outcomes of Chapters 81 through 84 in mind. Reza Shah (Chapter 81) built a centralized monarchy on the army, a growing bureaucracy, and oil revenue. His state (Chapters 82 and 83) imposed legal, educational, and dress reforms, disarmed and resettled tribal populations, standardized Persian as the language of public life, and built infrastructure including the Trans-Iranian Railway. These policies produced real institutions and real resentments. They were enforced by a ruler who tolerated almost no organized political competition. When the Anglo-Soviet invasion of August 1941 ended his reign (Chapter 84), his son inherited the throne but not his authority.

Iran was neutral when Britain and the Soviet Union invaded in August 1941, but declared war on Germany on 9 September 1943 and then entered the Allied diplomatic order while remaining under foreign occupation. Soviet forces held the north, British forces the south and the oil region, and American logistical personnel later operated in the Persian Gulf Command that moved supplies to the Soviet Union. Occupation strained the economy through inflation, shortages, and the requisitioning of transport. It also loosened the central state's grip. Political prisoners were released, newspapers reappeared, and parties formed. Those freedoms existed within a country whose sovereignty was constrained and whose provinces were under different foreign armies.

Two long-standing structural features framed the crisis of 1945–1946. First, the northwestern provinces had a history of distinct political life. Tabriz had been a leading commercial city, the seat of the crown prince under the Qajars (Chapters 70 and 73), and a center of the Constitutional Revolution (Chapters 78 and 79). Its population included a Turkic-speaking majority, communities of Kurds, Armenians, and Assyrians, and trade connections across the Caucasus border. Second, the northern frontier had long been a zone of Russian and later Soviet interest. Russian forces had occupied parts of the north during the Qajar period and again during World War I (Chapters 70, 71, and 80), and a Soviet-sponsored republic had briefly existed in Gilan in 1920–1921.

The international background mattered as much as the domestic one. The wartime coalition between the Soviet Union and the Western allies was dissolving into rivalry over eastern Europe, Turkey, Greece, and the Middle East. Iran's northern provinces became one of the places where each side tested the other. The creation of the United Nations in 1945 gave Iran a forum that no earlier Iranian government had possessed, and the decision to use it shaped both the crisis and the early history of the Security Council.

Section 85.1

Mohammad Reza Shah and Constitutional Monarchy after 1941

Mohammad Reza Pahlavi was twenty-one when he succeeded his father following Reza Shah’s abdication on 16 September 1941; the constitutional oath before the Majles is usually dated 17 September. Born in 1919, he had been named crown prince in 1925, educated in Switzerland, and trained at the military college in Tehran. In 1939 he had married Princess Fawzia of Egypt, a union intended to bind the new dynasty to an established Muslim royal house. Nothing in his preparation had given him experience of governing, and the circumstances of his accession offered little chance to acquire it quickly. The throne passed to him because the Allied powers, who held the capital's approaches and the country's railways, preferred a compliant heir to a republic or an uncertain successor.

The formal framework he inherited was the constitutional order of 1906–1907 (Chapters 78 and 79), consisting of the Fundamental Law and the Supplementary Fundamental Law. On paper it made ministers answerable to the Majles and bound the monarch to the constitution. In practice, Reza Shah had governed through the army, the court, and a compliant parliament, and the constitutional text had become a facade. After 1941 the facade acquired content. Deputies bargained, cabinets rose and fell on parliamentary votes, and the new Shah had to compete for influence rather than assume it. The Senate that the 1907 law envisioned had never been convened, so parliament consisted of a single chamber.

The young Shah's initial resources were limited but not negligible. He had the dynastic title and the symbolic position of head of state. He had access to the court, which controlled patronage and communication with ministers. And he had a claim on the army, the one national institution built under his father. Foreign powers also mattered: for several years his political survival rested on British, American, and Soviet acceptance. In 1941–1942 he agreed to transfer a substantial portion of his father's very large landed estates to the state, a gesture that responded to public resentment of Reza Shah's acquisitions. The terms of those transfers, and the later fate of the lands, are matters on which accounts differ.

The army was the arena in which the Shah worked hardest. Many senior officers owed their careers to his father, and the force remained the Shah's most important institutional asset. The army had been reduced and disorganized by the invasion of 1941, and its supply, pay, and morale were uncertain throughout the occupation. Even so, the Shah cultivated its leadership, took a close interest in promotions and appointments, and presented himself as its commander. Contests over the budget and the war minister's authority recurred between the court and parliament. The army's performance in December 1946, discussed in Section 85.12, increased its standing and the Shah's claim to its loyalty.

The court itself was a political institution. Court ministers and advisers such as Hossein Ala and, later, Abdolhossein Hajir managed access to the Shah, relayed his preferences to deputies and officers, and sometimes served as prime ministers. Their role fed a persistent suspicion in parliament and the press that unelected royal counselors shaped decisions that the constitution assigned to ministers. That suspicion would shape the constitutional arguments of 1949.

Competition with prime ministers defined the period's executive politics. Cabinet after cabinet formed through negotiation among deputies, the court, and the embassies. Prime ministers who held real support in parliament or the provinces, above all Ahmad Qavam, were reluctant to defer to the palace. The Shah, for his part, could remove or weaken prime ministers by withholding support, working through the officer corps, or encouraging parliamentary factions. The result was an unstable equilibrium: a monarch strong enough to obstruct, but not yet strong enough to dominate.

Historians disagree about how to describe this equilibrium. Some emphasize the Shah's weakness and dependence in the early 1940s and treat his later authority as the product of events he did not fully control. Others stress his persistent aim of restoring monarchical primacy and read his actions in the 1940s as a deliberate campaign. The evidence supports elements of both views. The Shah was constrained, but he also consistently acted to enlarge his room for maneuver, and the northern crisis gave him unusual opportunities to do so.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional monarchy (mashruteh)

Definition: A system in which a monarch reigns under a written constitution and an elected or representative parliament holds legislative power. In Persian political language the constitutional order born in 1906 is commonly called mashruteh, a term rooted in "conditional" or "bound by conditions" rule.

Historical context: The text of the 1906–1907 constitution did not change in 1941, but the practice did. Under Reza Shah, constitutional form coexisted with personal rule. After 1941 the text mattered more because several actors could invoke it against one another: deputies against the court, the court against cabinets, and opposition leaders against both.

Why it matters: The central conflict of this chapter turns on how to interpret the monarch's powers within mashruteh: as ceremonial, as a balancing authority, or as an executive center.

Potential misunderstanding: "Constitutional monarchy" in Iran did not mean the same thing as in Britain, whose model many reformers admired. The Iranian monarch held real levers, notably control of the army and patronage, that British constitutional practice had largely removed from the crown.

Section 85.2

Political Parties, Parliament, and the Press

The reopening of public life after 1941 began with prisoners. Reza Shah's government had jailed Marxists, dissident clerics, and political critics, and the release of many in 1941 returned experienced organizers to the capital. Newspapers multiplied, some serious and some partisan or short-lived. Unions, women's associations, youth groups, and professional societies appeared. The legal and administrative apparatus of control survived in part, and martial law at times restricted the press in Tehran. The change nevertheless marked the largest widening of political space since the early 1920s.

The most important new party was the Tudeh (the "masses") Party, founded in 1941. Its core came from the Marxist circle imprisoned in the late 1930s, including followers of the physicist Taqi Arani, who had died in prison in 1940. Its titular leader was an older figure, Soleiman Mirza Eskandari, while younger intellectuals ran its organization and press. The party's program stressed constitutional rights, labor legislation, education, and social reform rather than immediate revolution, and it built a wide following among teachers, writers, clerks, and industrial workers. It also had a close relationship with the Soviet Union, which its opponents emphasized. Estimates of its membership and following in the mid-1940s vary considerably, and party and hostile sources both exaggerated.

Tudeh pioneered mass organization in Iran. It built newspapers, a youth wing, a women's organization, and, with its allies, a federation of trade unions that grew rapidly in the textile mills of Isfahan, the factories of the north, and the oil fields of Khuzestan. Section 85.13 examines the labor movement in detail. For now it is sufficient to note that Tudeh gave organized political form to groups that earlier Iranian politics had mostly left outside parliament.

Other currents were more fragmented. Conservative landowners and notables dominated parliament and generally relied on patronage, local influence, and family networks rather than disciplined parties. Seyyed Zia Tabataba'i, a figure of the 1921 coup (Chapter 80) who returned from exile in 1943, formed a pro-British conservative organization. The Iran Party attracted Western-educated engineers, lawyers, and civil servants committed to constitutional reform and national autonomy. The clergy were not a single bloc. Ayatollah Abol-Qasem Kashani, interned by the British during the war, became a prominent political cleric after his release. Militant religious groups such as the Fada'iyan-e Islam, founded in 1945, attacked figures they regarded as enemies of Islam, among them the writer Ahmad Kasravi, assassinated in March 1946. Mohammad Mosaddegh, an independent deputy rather than a party leader at this stage, built a reputation as a defender of constitutional procedure.

The Fourteenth Majles was elected in 1943–1944 under the electoral law inherited from 1909, which used a two-stage system and a limited electorate that excluded women. Elections in the provinces were shaped by local magnates, gendarmerie and army officers, and, in the occupation zones, by the presence of foreign forces. Tudeh won about eight seats, a small but notable beginning. Mosaddegh won election from Tehran. Ja'far Pishevari was elected from Tabriz but was denied his seat when the Majles rejected his credentials, an episode his supporters remembered as proof that the Tehran establishment would not tolerate Azerbaijani leftist representation. It was not the only seat disputed, and the rejection reflected both ideological hostility and ordinary parliamentary practice, but it became a grievance of long duration.

The press was both a mirror and an instrument of this politics. Party newspapers, independent dailies, and foreign-sponsored publications fought over wages, bread, oil, foreign influence, and the monarchy. The British, Soviet, and American embassies each cultivated sympathetic papers and organizations through cultural and information offices. Researchers must therefore read the Iranian press of the 1940s with attention to ownership and patronage, and with awareness that circulation figures and party claims are rarely trustworthy.

The Fourteenth Majles also became the arena for the first sustained parliamentary assertion of Iranian control over natural resources, a theme that returns in Section 85.15. The occupation had, in short, produced a political class and a public sphere. It had not produced a consensus about whether power belonged to the court, to cabinets answerable to parliament, to organized parties, or to some combination. The northern crisis forced the question.

Section 85.3

The End of World War II and Allied Withdrawal

The legal framework for the Allied presence was the Tripartite Treaty of Alliance, signed on 29 January 1942 by Iran, Britain, and the Soviet Union. In it, Britain and the Soviet Union pledged to respect Iran's territorial integrity, sovereignty, and political independence, and committed to withdraw their forces from Iranian territory no later than six months after hostilities with Germany and its associates ended through an armistice or peace. Iran undertook to facilitate Allied use of its communications. The treaty was the basis on which Iranian leaders could later demand withdrawal. It also left room for dispute about when hostilities had ended.

The great powers reaffirmed Iranian independence at the Tehran Conference. The Declaration of 1 December 1943, issued by Roosevelt, Stalin, and Churchill after meeting in the Iranian capital, recognized Iran's contribution to the war effort, promised economic assistance, and stated the allies' commitment to Iran's independence, sovereignty, and territorial integrity. Iran declared war on Germany in September 1943, which helped secure its place among the founding members of the United Nations in 1945. These statements were not guarantees of Iranian security, but Iranian diplomats would cite them repeatedly in 1946.

Oil sharpened the stakes. In 1944, British, American, and Soviet interests sought new petroleum concessions in Iran. In the autumn a Soviet delegation led by Sergei Kaftaradze arrived in Tehran to seek rights in the north. Prime Minister Mohammad Sa'ed, anticipating pressure from all sides, had already announced that Iran would defer all concession negotiations until after the war. The Soviets protested, publicly and through their press and the Tudeh Party, and Sa'ed resigned in November 1944. The episode demonstrated that Soviet demands for northern oil were tied to the Soviet military presence, and that Iranian politicians could not easily refuse without paying domestic political costs. The Majles responded with legislation on 2 December 1944, discussed in Section 85.15.

The end of the war set the clock. Japan's surrender on 2 September 1945 marked the end of the war for the purposes most governments adopted, which placed the treaty deadline on 2 March 1946. The Americans, whose forces were not covered by the Tripartite Treaty but who had agreed to leave, completed their withdrawal by the beginning of 1946. British forces withdrew in stages and, by the March deadline, were gone from the country. The Soviet Union announced on or near the deadline that it would withdraw from some northern areas while retaining forces elsewhere, particularly in Azerbaijan, citing security concerns. Soviet representatives also pointed to the 1921 Soviet–Iranian treaty, which had allowed intervention if third parties used Iranian territory to threaten Soviet security, a provision Moscow had invoked in 1941.

The refusal to leave was therefore a breach of a treaty obligation, and Iranian officials treated it as such. Soviet leaders and their defenders have argued variously that conditions in Iran justified a longer stay, that the treaty text left room for interpretation, that the security of Baku's oil region required a friendly border, and that Soviet forces were protecting local democratic movements. The Soviet archival record published since the 1990s shows that Soviet policy was neither fully coordinated nor merely defensive. Moscow combined demands for oil, support for the autonomy movements, and leverage against Iran and Turkey, and its goals shifted as international and Iranian conditions changed.

This was the situation in March 1946: a Soviet army remaining in violation of its treaty commitments, two autonomous administrations existing under its protection, an Iranian army confined by that protection to areas south of an informal line near Qazvin, an Iranian government weakened by short-lived cabinets, and a former wartime ally increasingly alarmed by Soviet conduct in the Middle East. The Iranian government's options were limited. It could submit; it could appeal to the powers; or it could negotiate. Qavam, who became prime minister in January 1946, chose to do the second and third at once.

Section 85.4

Azerbaijan: Regional Grievances and National Identity

Iranian Azerbaijan, introduced geographically in Chapter 1, lies in the northwest, around Tabriz and Lake Urmia, along routes toward the Caucasus and Anatolia. Its population in the 1940s was mostly Azerbaijani Turkic in language, with Kurds, Armenians, Assyrians, and Persian speakers living alongside them. Its provincial capital, Tabriz, was among the largest cities in the country and one of its main commercial centers, tied by long habit to trade through the Caucasus and Anatolia.

Identity in this region was layered rather than singular. Azerbaijanis spoke a Turkic language, practiced mostly Twelver Shi'ism, and participated in the Iranian state, bureaucracy, bazaar, and intelligentsia. Tabriz had been a stronghold of the Constitutional Revolution (Chapters 78 and 79). Many leading figures of modern Iranian political and intellectual life were of Azerbaijani background, and some, such as the historian and critic Ahmad Kasravi, argued that the older language of the region had been Iranian and that the Turkic speech was a later layer. The existence of this debate shows that the issue was not a simple conflict between "Persian" and "Azerbaijani" identity. Many Azerbaijanis were convinced Iranian nationalists. Others were increasingly attentive to the Turkic cultural world across the border, in Soviet Azerbaijan, where an Azerbaijani national culture was officially promoted in schools, press, and publishing.

Grievances developed under Reza Shah's centralizing state (Chapter 82). Persian became the exclusive language of schooling and government. Publication in Turkic languages was restricted, and local administration was brought under officials appointed from Tehran. Azerbaijani cultural activists later remembered this policy as cultural repression. Economically, Tabriz had lost the trade advantages it had enjoyed before 1917, when the Russian Revolution and the tightening of the Soviet border severed old commercial routes. The Trans-Iranian Railway and the investments of Reza Shah's regime favored other regions. Landholding in the countryside was concentrated among large proprietors, many of whom lived in towns, and peasants experienced rents, obligations, and insecurity that Tudeh and later the Azerbaijan Democratic Party addressed in their appeals.

The Soviet occupation altered this landscape. Soviet officials in Tabriz, Urmia, and other towns cultivated local sympathizers through consulates, cultural societies, and the press, while Soviet authorities in Baku, notably the party leader of the Azerbaijan Soviet Socialist Republic, Mir Ja'far Baghirov, took an active role in guiding political developments across the border. Soviet forces also controlled movements: they restricted the Iranian army and gendarmerie in the zone, and they facilitated the organization of local political groups that favored Soviet aims. Tudeh organized in the province from 1941 and became the framework through which many local activists entered politics.

Political mobilization in 1945 therefore drew on three streams. One was an older tradition of Azerbaijani constitutionalist and leftist politics. A second was resentment of centralization, linguistic restriction, and neglect, expressed in demands for the use of Azerbaijani Turkish in schools and administration and for local control of local revenues. A third was Soviet policy, which supplied protection, money, weapons, and organizational direction. Historians differ over the relative weight of these streams, and the next three sections show how the evidence bears on that question. A defensible reading is that the movement had a real social base but would not have acquired governmental authority without Soviet protection.

Multiple Perspectives

Azerbaijan in 1945: Who Saw What?

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions drawn from patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not possess equal power or equal freedom of action.

Azerbaijani cultural and political activists: Many saw an opportunity to secure schooling and administration in their own language, to escape Tehran's appointed officials, and to address poverty and the concentration of land. Most presented their cause as autonomy within Iran, not secession, though some were drawn toward closer ties with Soviet Azerbaijan.
Landowners and established notables: For large proprietors, the movement threatened land, local influence, and personal safety. Many left for Tehran as the movement gained control, taking political grievances with them, and they supported the use of force to end it.
Tehran officials and Persian-language nationalists: They saw a violation of sovereignty carried out by a foreign army, and they often treated Azerbaijani demands as Soviet fabrications. This view captured the real foreign role but underestimated local grievances.
Kurdish, Armenian, and Assyrian communities in the province: Minorities experienced the movement unevenly. Some Kurds had their own political hopes, as Section 85.8 shows, and Kurdish and Azerbaijani leaders disputed territory around Urmia. Armenians and Assyrians faced the insecurity common to minorities when local armed groups and foreign armies shifted power.
Soviet officials: They viewed Azerbaijan as a security buffer, a source of leverage over Tehran, and a base for an oil concession, and they justified support by appeals to democratic self-government. Soviet motives were not identical to local ones.

Section 85.5

Pishevari, the Azerbaijan Democratic Party, and Soviet Intervention

Ja'far Pishevari was born in 1893 in the Khalkhal area of Azerbaijan and spent much of his early adult life in the Caucasus, where he worked in Baku and became involved in revolutionary politics. He was active in the Soviet-backed Gilan republic of 1920–1921 and was later arrested in Iran under the 1931 law against collectivist ideologies, serving years in prison until the amnesty of 1941. After his release he edited a newspaper in Tehran and turned toward Tudeh politics without becoming a settled member of its leadership. His election from Tabriz in 1944 and the Majles's refusal to seat him left him embittered toward the Tehran establishment and gave him a platform and a grievance in Azerbaijan.

The Azerbaijan Democratic Party (ADP) was announced in Tabriz in early September 1945, with Pishevari as leader. It absorbed the Azerbaijan branch of Tudeh and drew in former Tudeh cadres, teachers, journalists, and some bazaar merchants. Its program called for democratic reforms and for autonomy for Azerbaijan within Iran, with Azerbaijani Turkish recognized in local schools and administration, an elected provincial authority, local control of local resources, land reform, and labor protections. The party's formation did not occur in a vacuum. Documents from Soviet and Azerbaijani archives, published since the early 1990s and analyzed most thoroughly by the historian Jamil Hasanli, show that Soviet authorities, including Baghirov in Baku, helped to organize the party, supplied weapons and funds, and gave detailed direction. A Soviet decision in the summer of 1945 to promote autonomy movements in northern Iran, and to seek an oil concession, preceded the ADP's emergence.

The movement acquired armed force in the autumn. Volunteer units, known as fedayi, formed under party leadership and, supported by Soviet advisers and equipment, attacked or pressured gendarmerie posts and small army garrisons through the province. Soviet troops obstructed the Iranian government's attempts to reinforce Azerbaijan. In November 1945 an Iranian column heading toward the northwest was stopped by Soviet forces near Qazvin. Government forces in Tabriz and other towns were increasingly isolated, and by the second week of December Tabriz was in the movement's hands. Soviet protection was therefore decisive in establishing control, which is a point of agreement among historians even where they differ on motives.

Historians have framed the movement in several ways. One interpretation treats it chiefly as a Soviet creation, a tool for pressure on Tehran and a bargaining chip for an oil concession. Another stresses the autonomy movement's local roots, in language rights, land, and resentment of centralization, and sees Soviet support as an opportunity the movement seized. A third interpretation, increasingly supported by the archival record, treats the movement as a hybrid: local grievances and activists mattered, but Soviet decisions determined its timing, scale, weapons, and survival. The evidence suggests that Soviet leaders also hedged, preparing for either an autonomous Azerbaijan within Iran or a bargaining arrangement in which autonomy could be traded for an oil agreement.

Soviet motives were not simple. The security of the Baku oil region, ambitions in northern Iranian oil, rivalry with Britain, concern about American entry into Middle Eastern politics, pressure on Turkey, and ideological sympathy with the Azerbaijani left all appear in the sources. How Stalin ranked them is uncertain. The documentary record shows that Soviet policy responded to Western pressure and to Qavam's diplomacy in early 1946, which is one reason historians treat later Soviet decisions as the product of changing circumstances rather than a fixed plan.

Section 85.6

Establishment of the Azerbaijan People's Government

The ADP's move from party to government took only a few weeks. A National Congress of Azerbaijan, with several hundred delegates, met in Tabriz in November 1945, and, according to contemporary accounts and the party's own press, called for autonomy and elections to a provincial body. A rapid vote followed, conducted amid armed control and without competing parties, and produced a National Assembly (Majles-e Melli). The assembly convened on 12 December 1945 and named Pishevari prime minister, forming a cabinet whose ministries included interior, education, finance, and defense. A national army and gendarmerie were organized from the party's armed units and defecting local personnel, under officers with military backgrounds.

What the new authority claimed for itself is an important question. Its leaders insisted that they sought autonomy within the Iranian state and not independence. They addressed Tehran with demands for reform and recognition, rejected the label of separatism, and presented the movement as a democratic force that would benefit all of Iran. Opponents answered that a regime protected by foreign troops and shaped by a foreign government could not be classed as an expression of democratic autonomy, and many Iranians, including some sympathetic to the underlying demands, rejected the movement for this reason. The government adopted its own flag, institutions, and symbols, which supporters regarded as natural features of regional self-government and opponents as signs of separation.

The relationship to Tehran changed with events. Under Prime Minister Hakimi and others in late 1945 the central government had little capacity to react. The appointment of Qavam in January 1946 shifted the pattern. Qavam recognized that force was not available, that a Soviet refusal to leave was the larger problem, and that negotiation with Pishevari could help isolate him from Moscow. He therefore treated the northern leaders as Iranian political actors with whom a bargain could be struck, while using diplomacy abroad to remove their protection. Pishevari and his colleagues, for their part, repeatedly invited Tehran to open negotiations on terms that recognized the autonomy of Azerbaijan, a stance that evolved over the months.

The National Congress was therefore not the equivalent of a freely contested constitutional election. The movement had succeeded in displacing much of the authority that Tehran normally exercised through governors, police, and local commanders. In the occupied zone, Soviet forces prevented the Iranian army from restoring the old administrative chain, while the ADP's armed supporters could influence who took part in the provincial vote and who dared to oppose it. Yet the presence of intimidation does not establish that every delegate was an unwilling participant. Party activists drew on a real infrastructure of teachers, journalists, workers, small merchants, and veterans of earlier constitutional and left-wing campaigns. They possessed local knowledge and networks that an administration wholly imported from abroad would have lacked. Understanding the government requires holding these facts together: it recruited Iranian Azerbaijani supporters and articulated local complaints, but its accession to power was made possible by foreign military protection and coercive exclusion of opponents.

The institutions created in Tabriz were more than decorative. Ministers attempted to collect revenue, supervise schools, administer justice, and supply an armed force; the assembly issued decisions that were communicated through provincial offices and newspapers. Police powers and control over transport were especially important because a government unable to protect or provision its officers cannot operate beyond its capital. Implementation, however, was uneven. District officials differed in their commitment to the party, established landowners could resist or accommodate, and areas with mixed-language populations confronted competing claims of representation. The central government's absence did not erase older hierarchies of property, patronage, and village authority. Nor did a ministerial title establish effective oversight of every town and road. For a historian, government proclamations reveal ambitions, while district reports, complaints, and recollections are needed to measure their actual reach.

This distinction also matters when considering legitimacy. The autonomous government invoked the constitutional principle of provincial councils, an institution promised in Iran's earlier constitutional settlement but not consistently implemented. Tehran could reasonably answer that an armed assembly formed under occupation lacked the authorization of national law. The resulting argument was not simply over whether Azerbaijan deserved schools or local budgetary control; it was over who could define and enforce the legal route to such reforms. The leadership's insistence on remaining within Iran stood alongside symbols and military arrangements that looked to nationalists elsewhere like a rival state. Later negotiations in 1946 would attempt to convert these contested institutions into ordinary provincial offices, revealing just how much of their authority depended on being exceptional.

Historians disagree about whether the autonomous government was ever more than a transitional arrangement in Soviet strategy. Documents show Soviet officials discussing its status in terms of the larger bargaining position, and some indicate that Moscow prepared for the possibility that Azerbaijan might have to be traded for an oil agreement. Pishevari's own papers and speeches show a leader who believed he was leading a lasting political movement, and many of his followers shared that belief. Both can be true.

Section 85.7

Language, Land, Education, and Social Reform in Azerbaijan

The People's Government's most visible policy concerned language. Azerbaijani Turkish was declared the official language of administration and was introduced into schools, publications, radio broadcasts, and the courts. Textbooks were prepared, newspapers appeared in Turkish, and a theater and cultural institutions were promoted. A university, known later as Tabriz University, was established in 1946. These measures answered grievances rooted in the Reza Shah period, and they had popular appeal among teachers, writers, and students who had long wanted instruction in their mother tongue. Accounts differ on how far Persian remained part of the curriculum, and on how widely the new Turkish textbooks reached rural schools. Language policy also had a political edge: the government's opponents saw it as evidence of separation, and its supporters saw it as the correction of a deliberate cultural exclusion.

Land policy was the government's principal social measure. Authorities redistributed some state-owned land, known as khalesseh land, and land belonging to proprietors who had fled, and promised broader reform. Peasants in some districts received land or reduced obligations, while the government also set prices and attempted to manage supplies in a period of shortage. The record on the extent of redistribution is incomplete, because the government lasted only one year and because both supporters and opponents shaped later accounts. Landowners who remained in the province, or returned afterward, often recovered their holdings after December 1946, which limited the permanent effect of the reforms.

In other areas the government set out reform programs on education, health, public works, and the status of women. Reports describe the opening of schools, the expansion of local administration, and the involvement of women in political organizations and, according to some sources, in voting for the provincial assembly. These claims should be read with caution, because evidence comes largely from party publications and later memoirs. They indicate the government's intent to present itself as modernizing and democratic rather than the full extent of change on the ground.

The regime's methods were also coercive. The People's Government suppressed opposition parties and newspapers, arrested political opponents and landlords, and relied on armed units, including the fedayi, to enforce its authority. Some opponents were executed, and many officials, landowners, and merchants left for Tehran. Soviet forces and advisers remained influential. These facts do not erase the reforms, but they limit how far the government can be described as a freely elected democratic administration. A balanced judgment places it between a genuine autonomy movement and a Soviet-protected regime that enforced its rule through political control.

Relations with minorities within Azerbaijan were uneven. Kurdish leaders in the Urmia and Mahabad areas pursued their own project, and conflict arose over territory and loyalty. Armenian and Assyrian communities in the region, whose members had suffered displacement and violence during and after World War I, had varied responses. Some local Kurdish tribal leaders opposed both movements. The existence of these tensions shows that the Azerbaijani government's claim to speak for the whole province was contested.

A language policy could change public life quickly, but it could not be measured only in the number of proclamations issued. A teacher assigned to instruct children in Azerbaijani Turkish needed usable textbooks, training, paper, and the consent or cooperation of families. For parents excluded from Persian-language schooling, vernacular instruction promised access to literacy and public employment. For families who hoped children would attend universities or obtain careers in Tehran, continued competence in Persian could be equally important. Multilingual administration therefore involved practical choices about examinations, legal documents, and the language in which officials corresponded with the central state. These choices were especially delicate in towns where Kurdish, Armenian, Assyrian, Azerbaijani, and Persian speakers interacted. Recognition of one regional language was a major departure from the centralizing policies of the preceding reign, but it did not automatically solve the claims of other language communities.

Land reform likewise had to pass through local conditions. A policy promising peasants a better share of income could encounter powerful landlords, disputes over tenancy, and records that did not match customary rights. Some rural households valued relief from dues and greater security, while others feared that sudden intervention would disrupt cultivation, irrigation, access to credit, or village relationships on which survival depended. The government faced the additional difficulty of funding schools and administration while its international position was uncertain and commerce was disturbed by road controls. Public works, cultural programs, and symbolic measures could be announced more quickly than sustainable taxation or redistribution. This is why the movement's social program should be evaluated both as an expression of demands neglected by Tehran and as a short experiment whose duration and dependence on Soviet-controlled transport restricted what can be demonstrated about its longer-term results.

The government's reach was also limited by its finances and its dependence. It sought revenue from local taxes, from the seizure of state resources, and from Soviet assistance, and it faced shortages of trained administrators. Its army and gendarmerie were new and untested, a weakness that would matter when the Soviet protection ended. The evidence for ordinary people's reactions is thin, though memoirs, newspapers, and foreign consular reports suggest a mixture of enthusiasm, calculation, fear, and indifference that varied by class, district, and language.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under a Provincial Government

For a teacher in Tabriz, the change might mean teaching in Turkish for the first time and facing the question of which textbooks to use. For a peasant in a village near Maragheh, it could mean a change in what was owed to a landlord, or the arrival of fedayi and officials with new demands. For a bazaar merchant, it meant new regulations, new taxes, and uncertainty about the border trade with Soviet Azerbaijan. For a landowner, it meant danger and flight. For a young man of military age, it could mean enrollment in the new forces. For women in the city, it could mean participation in new associations and, in some accounts, in elections, though evidence for this comes largely from partisan sources.

Historians reconstruct this everyday history from newspapers published by the government, from memoirs written after 1946 by participants and opponents, from British and American consular reports, and from Soviet documents. Each source type has a limit: official newspapers promote the government, memoirs often serve later political purposes, and foreign reports reflect outsiders' limited access. The least documented groups are the peasants and the women whose experience the reforms claimed to improve.

Section 85.8

Kurdish Nationalism and the Republic of Mahabad

The Kurds of northwestern Iran had a distinct history from the Azerbaijanis. Kurdish speakers live across several states, and in Iran they are concentrated in the western highlands from the Urmia region to Kermanshah. In the area around Mahabad, a small town south of Lake Urmia, the population was mostly Sunni Kurdish and organized by tribal confederations such as the Shikak and by townsmen, landowners, and religious families. Reza Shah's tribal disarmament and resettlement policies (Chapter 82) had weakened some chiefs and alienated others. The wartime collapse of central authority gave Kurdish tribal leaders and activists new freedom.

Kurdish political organization took shape in these years. In 1942 a group of young activists in Mahabad founded a secret society, Komala, which promoted Kurdish cultural and political rights and cultivated contacts across the frontiers with Iraqi and Turkish Kurds. In 1945, after Soviet officials had shown interest in Kurdish politics, Komala transformed into the Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran, with Qazi Muhammad as leader. Qazi Muhammad, a judge and a member of an established landed and religious family of Mahabad, had the social standing to bring together townsmen and tribal leaders. In the autumn of 1945 he and other Kurdish leaders visited Baku, where Baghirov advised them, and Soviet support for the Kurdish movement, though real, was smaller and less continuous than for the Azerbaijanis.

On 22 January 1946 Qazi Muhammad proclaimed the Kurdish republic in Mahabad, in a public ceremony in the town's central square. The new administration had a president, a council of ministers, a flag, a newspaper in Kurdish, Kurdish-language schools, and an army that included both local forces and, importantly, fighters led by Mustafa Barzani. Barzani, a Kurdish leader from Iraq, had crossed into Iran with his followers after the failure of a rebellion in Iraq in 1945. His forces gave the republic its principal military strength, though relations between Barzani's men and some local tribal leaders were sometimes difficult.

The Kurdish republic had a very small territorial base, centered on Mahabad and a few surrounding districts such as Naqadeh, Bukan, and Piranshahr. It did not extend to the large Kurdish population further south, most notably the Shi'i Kurds around Kermanshah, and it lacked the economic base of the Azerbaijani region. It had limited revenues, depended on tribal alliances that did not always hold, and relied on Soviet supplies, which were modest by comparison with the aid to Tabriz. Kurdish and Azerbaijani leaders signed an agreement of friendship in April 1946, though relations between the two movements remained difficult over territory and over the status of mixed districts.

The differences between the movements are important. The Azerbaijani movement was urban, part of an organized party, and tied to an existing left-wing tradition within Iran, while the Kurdish movement was smaller, more tribal in structure, and more explicitly national in its aims. Azerbaijanis spoke of autonomy within Iran. Kurdish leaders also demanded autonomy within Iran in their official statements, but the movement drew on a pan-Kurdish national consciousness that extended beyond the Iranian frontier. The Soviet connection existed in both cases, but Soviet sponsorship was less decisive for Mahabad's identity than for Tabriz's.

Mahabad's reach was narrower than the title "republic" might imply. Its authority was strongest in the town and its immediate surroundings; elsewhere, Kurdish communities followed tribal leaders, religious figures, and local political networks whose priorities did not necessarily match those of Qazi Muhammad. Some sought recognition of Kurdish language and representation, others protection from neighboring rivals, and still others a favorable relationship with Tehran. Armed allegiance could be negotiated and withdrawn. The Barzani fighters who had crossed from Iraq brought experienced military manpower, but they also carried a political history distinct from that of Iranian Kurdish townspeople. Their presence gave the republic a more formidable defensive element while complicating relations with local commanders and with the Iranian state. A map showing one uninterrupted autonomous Kurdish territory would exaggerate the practical jurisdiction of Mahabad's institutions.

The Kurdish case also illustrates the limits of treating Soviet help as an undifferentiated endorsement of national self-determination. Moscow's support was conditioned by its military position and by its dealings with Tehran over Azerbaijan and oil. Kurdish nationalists, by contrast, could imagine institutions and cultural protections extending beyond the immediate diplomatic bargain. Their expectations were not necessarily shared by all tribal notables, and the republic had insufficient time to reconcile such differences through dependable taxation, representative structures, and control over the countryside. When the Soviet presence disappeared, Qazi Muhammad had to confront both Iranian military pressure and the reluctance of some local allies to wage a costly war. The eventual defeat was therefore neither proof that Kurdish national aspirations lacked a social base nor evidence that the republic had commanded universal Kurdish allegiance.

The republic's cultural policy gave Kurdish language and literature a place in public life. Schools used Kurdish, a printing press produced newspapers and journals, and poets such as Hemin and Hejar, associated with the movement, contributed to a Kurdish nationalist literary culture. These achievements outlasted the republic in memory, and later Kurdish movements in Iran, Iraq, and Turkey would invoke Mahabad as a precedent. Their durability should not obscure the republic's fragility: it controlled little territory, faced strained relations with tribal elites, and depended on protectors who could withdraw. Chapter 85 treats the Kurdish republic as a movement in its own right and not as an appendix to the Azerbaijan crisis, while recognizing that the two were linked in time, geography, and Soviet strategy.

Section 85.9

Iran Appeals to the United Nations

On 19 January 1946, during the first session of the United Nations Security Council in London, the Iranian delegation under Hossein Ala lodged a complaint against Soviet interference in Iran's internal affairs. The complaint alleged that Soviet officials and forces had supported political movements in the north and obstructed the Iranian government's exercise of its authority. It was among the first matters the new Council addressed. For Iran it was both a defensive measure and a bid for diplomatic leverage, since the occupation could not be reversed by Iran's army.

The Soviet delegation denied the charge, asserting that the situation was a domestic matter and that bilateral negotiations were the proper way forward. The Council, divided by the veto and by the emerging East–West divide, adopted a resolution on 30 January that took note of the parties' statements and asked them to seek a settlement by direct negotiation and report the results, while keeping the matter within its competence. The resolution did not condemn the Soviet Union, but it kept the issue on the international agenda and gave Iran a legal standing it would have lacked in purely bilateral talks. This was Security Council Resolution 2 (1946), adopted on 30 January; subsequent resolutions were No. 3 (4 April) and No. 5 (8 May).

Events outside the chamber made the matter more serious. In February 1946 the American diplomat George Kennan sent his long telegram from Moscow arguing that the Soviet leadership would resist cooperation with the West. In early March, Winston Churchill gave his speech at Fulton, Missouri, describing an iron curtain across Europe. The United States protested the Soviet failure to withdraw by the 2 March deadline, and reports circulated of Soviet troop movements in northern Iran and toward Turkey. The American ambassador in Tehran, George Allen, reported on Iranian developments and encouraged Qavam's government in its negotiating position. The British government supported Iran as well, though Britain had its own interests in southern oil, which Iranian leaders and the Soviets did not forget.

A further episode in this period has become a legend. According to a well-known account, President Truman sent an ultimatum to Stalin demanding that Soviet forces leave Iran. Historians have scrutinized this story and found it poorly documented. The contemporary record shows that Washington sent diplomatic protests, that Secretary of State James Byrnes took a firm line, and that American public opinion and officials were increasingly alarmed. It does not show a formal ultimatum. This matter is considered in the Historical Debate in Section 85.12.

The Security Council debate continued into the spring. In late March the Soviet representative, Andrei Gromyko, argued that the issue should be removed from the agenda because negotiations were in progress between Tehran and Moscow, and he withdrew from the Council when it declined. On 4 April, after the Qavam–Sadchikov agreement described in Section 85.10, the Council voted to defer its proceedings until 6 May, requiring the parties to report on whether Soviet forces had in fact left. Ala, on instructions or his own judgment, insisted on keeping the case before the Council even when Qavam, now in a bargain with Moscow, was reported to favor withdrawing it. A widely repeated account describes Ala's persistence as a significant factor in maintaining pressure on Moscow, though the exact sequence of instructions remains disputed.

The sequence of Council decisions helps separate legal process from diplomatic theater. Resolution 2, adopted on 30 January 1946, encouraged negotiations and asked the parties to report without pronouncing on every allegation. After Soviet forces missed the withdrawal deadline, the dispute returned to the Council amid sharp disagreement about whether a bilateral understanding should remove it from consideration. Resolution 3, adopted on 4 April, deferred proceedings until 6 May while awaiting information about evacuation; the Soviet position presented troop withdrawal as independent of its continuing petroleum negotiations. Resolution 5, adopted on 8 May, again postponed further proceedings because Iran had not yet completed its own verification of the withdrawal. The successive decisions did not authorize an international force or compel compliance. They preserved a public forum in which the stated grounds for postponement could be compared with events on the ground.

The diplomatic archives reveal another constraint: Iran's representatives did not always receive, interpret, or follow instructions in the same way as the prime minister, who was negotiating directly with the Soviet ambassador. An envoy trying to keep the case open might believe that withdrawal required outside scrutiny, while a premier negotiating face to face might believe that an apparent lack of trust could destroy an emerging bargain. Neither choice was purely procedural. Each could alter what Moscow thought Iran could credibly promise and what domestic politicians would later accuse Qavam of having conceded. The new United Nations mattered partly because its procedures created an arena outside Tehran's cabinet and the great powers' closed negotiations.

The UN episode had consequences beyond Iran. It demonstrated that a smaller state could bring a great power before the Council and that the Council could keep a dispute alive despite the objections of a permanent member. It also anchored the Iranian case in the language of sovereignty and territorial integrity, which Iranian governments would use afterward. It did not by itself force the Soviet withdrawal. The effect of the appeal combined with American and British pressure, Qavam's negotiations, and the Soviet calculation of its interests.

Section 85.10

Ahmad Qavam, Stalin, and the April 1946 Agreement

Ahmad Qavam, known as Qavam al-Saltaneh, was born in 1876 into a family of high officials, served in Qajar and early Pahlavi governments, and had been prime minister in the early 1920s and again briefly in 1942–1943. He was widely regarded as shrewd, aloof, and ambitious. He became prime minister again in late January 1946 after the Majles, by a narrow margin, preferred him to the alternatives. The Shah distrusted him, since Qavam's authority came from his political skill and parliamentary position rather than from the court, but the circumstances left the Shah little choice.

Qavam's strategy combined several elements. He sought to remove the Soviet presence by negotiation while keeping the Iranian case before the United Nations. He conceded an informal opening to the left, releasing prisoners and allowing Tudeh and other parties greater scope, in part to reduce the left's reasons to side with the northern regimes against him. And he held out the prospect of Soviet economic participation in Iranian oil as a possible price for withdrawal, a card he would play carefully because the Majles controlled concessions. In mid-February 1946 he traveled to Moscow, where he spent several weeks, meeting Stalin and Foreign Minister Molotov.

The negotiations were difficult. The Soviet side demanded recognition of Azerbaijan's autonomy, a Soviet oil concession in the north, and a delay in withdrawal. Qavam resisted, arguing that Iran could not accept constraints on its sovereignty and that the Majles had to approve any concession. He left Moscow without a final agreement in early March, at a time when Soviet forces had passed the treaty deadline and the Security Council debate was beginning. Over the following weeks, in Tehran, he negotiated with the Soviet ambassador, Ivan Sadchikov. On 4 April the two exchanged letters, and the Soviet Union announced that its forces would leave Iran within about six weeks of 24 March.

The agreement had three main parts. The Soviet Union would evacuate its troops. Iran accepted fundamental terms for a proposed joint Soviet–Iranian oil company in designated northern territories—51 percent Soviet and 49 percent Iranian ownership for twenty-five years, then equal ownership for another twenty-five years—with a future implementing agreement subject to presentation to the Majles, with the proposal to be placed before the new parliament within seven months of 24 March. And the Azerbaijan problem would be treated as an internal Iranian matter to be settled peacefully through reform and negotiation, without Soviet involvement. These terms appeared to give Moscow much of what it sought, and critics at the time and since have denounced them as a surrender. Qavam's defenders respond that the letters promised only a proposal, that the existing legal framework required parliamentary consent, and that the arrangement bought the withdrawal of the Soviet army.

Whether Qavam intended to honor the oil provision is one of the unresolved questions of the period. The 1944 law prohibited specified officials from negotiating or concluding foreign oil concessions, while distinguishing limited discussions of petroleum sales and Iranian exploitation, and the Majles then in office was near the end of its term. The Fifteenth Majles, not yet elected, would decide. Qavam may have judged that a nationalist parliament would reject the proposal, which would allow him to claim that he had fulfilled his promise while the Majles bore responsibility for the refusal. Other historians argue that he was willing to accept some concession if circumstances required, and that the later rejection was a consequence of parliamentary independence and changed international conditions rather than a plan. The evidence supports the claim that he took a significant risk.

The petroleum correspondence of 4 April was detailed enough to make the proposed concession politically consequential. In a translated exchange reproduced in the United States diplomatic record, Qavam accepted fundamental terms for a joint company that would explore and exploit designated northern Iranian territory. Iran would hold 49 percent of its shares and the Soviet Union 51 percent for the first twenty-five years; the proportions would become equal for the second twenty-five years. Profits would follow share ownership. The projected arrangement thus contemplated a fifty-year enterprise, not a limited technical study. The correspondence also identified the proposed exploration area with qualifications, including the exclusion of part of western Azerbaijan, and assigned protection of installations to Iranian security forces. A later agreement embodying the terms was to be submitted to the new Majles within seven months of 24 March. These provisions made the letters more binding as diplomatic commitments than a casual suggestion, but they still did not bring an oil company into legal existence or authorize drilling without the required Iranian processes.

The legal distinction was central to Qavam's room for maneuver. The statute of 2 December 1944 constrained ministers and other officials from negotiating or concluding foreign oil concessions, while treating discussions about the sale of petroleum and its exploitation by Iran differently. A prime minister who made commitments about a joint concession risked the charge that he had violated this law even if he intended later parliamentary ratification. Conversely, Moscow had reason to believe that written governmental promises carried more weight than an abstract expression of interest. The April exchange can be read as a tactical compromise that exploited a gap between executive diplomacy and legislative power. It cannot be reduced without loss either to a fully executed surrender of northern oil or to a promise so vague as to impose no political obligation.

The agreement also shaped relations between Qavam and the Soviets inside Iran. By conceding the oil proposal, he secured the Soviet army's departure, and he bargained with Azerbaijan's leaders from a stronger position. The agreement also split opinion. Some nationalists saw Qavam as having endangered Iran's resources, while others saw a clever resolution to an occupation that could not be ended by force. The ambiguity remained until the Majles voted in October 1947.

Section 85.11

Soviet Withdrawal and Negotiations with Azerbaijan

Soviet forces left Iran in the weeks after the April agreement, and Soviet announcements dated completion to 9 May 1946, while Iranian and foreign observers continued checking the withdrawal into mid-May. The evacuation was the decisive change in the northern crisis, and it came months before the Iranian army moved. This sequence is essential to the chapter's argument. Without Soviet troops, the autonomous governments lost their protector and the Iranian government regained room to maneuver, but the two movements still held their territory, their armed units, and their administrations.

Qavam now negotiated directly with the Azerbaijani leadership. A delegation led by Pishevari traveled to Tehran, and the talks continued through the spring and early summer. The two sides signed a fifteen-point agreement at Tabriz on 13 June 1946. According to the commonly cited summary, it recognized Azerbaijan's provincial status with an elected provincial council, allowed the Azerbaijani language in local education alongside Persian, provided for the integration of the autonomous government's armed units with the national army, and required Tehran to choose the governor-general from a list nominated by the provincial council and allocated 75 percent of locally collected government revenue to provincial expenditure. The People's Government agreed to give up its claims to separate national institutions in return for provincial self-administration.

The agreement failed to produce a durable settlement. The two sides differed over its interpretation, over the status of the armed units, and over control of administration. Pishevari's movement was reluctant to dissolve its institutions without firm guarantees, and Tehran was reluctant to concede lasting regional power. Qavam's government, meanwhile, was reconfiguring its domestic position. In July the Khuzestan strike and Qavam's decision to bring Tudeh ministers into his cabinet (Sections 85.13 and 85.14) complicated relations with the Shah, the conservatives, and the Western embassies. In the autumn the southern tribal rebellion and the dismissal of the Tudeh ministers marked a shift to the right.

The change in Tehran's posture also reflected international conditions. With the Soviet army gone and the Security Council engaged, the United States and Britain increased their support for the Iranian government, and American officials encouraged Qavam to assert central authority. The Shah and army leaders pressed for a military solution. Qavam seems to have concluded that the Azerbaijan question could not be settled by negotiation alone and that its resolution would strengthen his claims for election of a parliament favorable to him. In late autumn he announced that the army would enter Azerbaijan to ensure security for the coming parliamentary elections, a formula that made the move appear a routine exercise of state authority rather than an attack on the autonomous government.

Soviet conduct in these months was cautious. Moscow did not send troops back, and Soviet officials in Baku and Tabriz seem to have offered no commitments to defend the Azerbaijani regime. The reasons are debated. Possible explanations include the expectation of an oil agreement, anxiety about renewed Western pressure, preoccupation with other priorities in Europe and Turkey, and doubt about the Azerbaijani leaders' prospects. Historians cannot be sure which weighed most heavily, and the Historical Debate in Section 85.12 treats the problem.

The precise chronology of evacuation shows why the distinction between Soviet assurances and Iranian verification mattered. Soviet military announcements gave 9 May 1946 as the completion date, and American diplomatic reporting suggested that some units had remained in northern Iran until approximately 9–10 May. The Security Council's 8 May postponement occurred before Iran had been able to certify the complete departure of foreign troops. By the time Tehran could begin to exercise freer administrative pressure on Azerbaijan, the international negotiation had already shifted from military occupation to the legal status of local institutions. The Soviet Union still had agents, contacts, press influence, and interests across its border, but the absence of its army fundamentally changed the costs and risks of resistance in Tabriz and Mahabad.

The fifteen-point agreement signed at Tabriz on 13 June 1946 attempted to translate the revolutionary language of autonomy into the constitutional vocabulary of provincial government. Azerbaijan's National Assembly would be recognized as a provincial council rather than as the legislature of a separate government. Tehran would select the governor-general from candidates proposed by that council, a shared appointment process substantially different from unilateral appointment by the capital. Regular and volunteer forces were to be incorporated into the Iranian army and gendarmerie under arrangements that required further agreement. The central government accepted that 75 percent of government receipts collected in Azerbaijan would be used for provincial expenditures. Other provisions addressed educational and minority questions, including the situation of Kurdish, Armenian, and Assyrian residents. These were concrete concessions in fiscal administration and representation, not merely promises of goodwill.

Yet the same agreement exposed the danger of postponing decisions about command. An army unit's nominal integration could mean very different things if its officers retained local loyalties, if arms remained in provincial depots, or if Tehran controlled appointments and deployment. A provincial council might regard revenue allocation as the basis of meaningful self-government; officials in the capital might treat the figure as a temporary arrangement pending the return of ordinary treasury supervision. The agreement was announced as a settlement while several critical implementing questions remained open. Its failure was not proof that compromise had never been attempted. It showed how difficult it was to turn a power-sharing formula into enforceable practice after months of armed confrontation and mutual distrust.

For the autonomous governments, the months between May and December 1946 were a period of mounting isolation. They lacked foreign troops, revenues were limited, and the army of the central government stood at the borders of the region. Reports record local shortages, desertions, and growing political uncertainty. At the same time the movement's leaders had no clear alternative. A compromise would erase their institutions, resistance seemed to invite attack, and the Soviet Union appeared unwilling to protect them. The leaders' options narrowed as the Iranian state's options widened.

Section 85.12

The Iranian Army's Return and the Collapse of the Autonomous Governments

In early December 1946 Iranian forces advanced toward Azerbaijan. They entered Zanjan, the town on the road to Tabriz, in the first week of the month with little resistance. Qavam publicly framed the advance as security for elections, while the army and the Shah regarded it as the long-awaited reassertion of central power. The People's Government's armed units made some stand at certain points, but its leadership could not mount a sustained defense. By 12 December the army was in Tabriz. Pishevari and many of his colleagues fled across the frontier to the Soviet Union, where Pishevari died in 1947 in a car accident whose circumstances have been questioned.

The collapse was quick and was followed by violence. Troops, gendarmes, local irregulars, and returning landowners targeted supporters of the autonomous government. Fedayi were killed or arrested, and local residents suspected of collaboration were attacked, beaten, imprisoned, or killed. Accounts of the scale differ widely. Reports from the time and later estimates vary from hundreds to considerably more, and no figure is secure. Reprisals included looting and the suppression of Azerbaijani-language publications and institutions. Foreign observers who visited afterward described a climate of fear. Some Azerbaijanis who had not belonged to the movement also suffered.

The Kurdish republic fell almost simultaneously. Qazi Muhammad, facing an army advance and lacking a reliable tribal alliance, avoided battle and met the army's commanders in mid-December, and the army entered Mahabad on or about 17 December. Kurdish-language schools were closed, printing equipment was seized, and Kurdish books were destroyed or confiscated. Qazi Muhammad and two relatives were tried by a military court and hanged in Mahabad in March 1947, after months in custody. Mustafa Barzani and his followers fought and withdrew, eventually making a long march across Iran and into the Soviet Union in mid-1947, a feat that entered Kurdish national memory. The execution of Qazi Muhammad also entered it, as a martyrdom that shaped later Kurdish nationalism.

The restoration of government control was therefore a distinct act, separate from Soviet withdrawal and months later. This matters for historical judgment. Accounts that present the Soviet withdrawal as the end of the crisis omit the December operation, and accounts that credit the army alone omit the diplomacy that made it possible. The Soviet army's departure in May and the Iranian army's advance in December were different events with different causes, and the autonomous governments remained in place for seven months between them.

The December operation unfolded through changes inside the province as well as movements of regular troops. The army's advance toward Zanjan and then Tabriz occurred after the Soviet withdrawal had left the autonomous government without an external military shield. Local opponents of the ADP became more active, and the party faced deteriorating morale and disagreements over whether to resist. In the days before the arrival of central forces, the balance of power within Tabriz itself shifted; opponents of the autonomous administration attacked or displaced some of its personnel. A description that makes the army the sole actor in restoring central authority misses this internal reversal. Conversely, describing the collapse as an entirely spontaneous popular uprising ignores the pressure created by an advancing national army and the earlier withdrawal of Soviet protection. State restoration took place through the interaction of military force, local political hostility, and retreating elite support.

The experience of those who had worked for the People's Government was not uniform. Some leaders and activists fled across the frontier into Soviet Azerbaijan, where their subsequent lives depended on authorities whose priorities did not necessarily match their own. Others remained and faced arrest, violence, property loss, or exclusion from public office. Contemporary reports and later memories disagree over the scale of killings, the proportion attributable to regular troops as opposed to local reprisals, and the extent to which reported numbers represent politically motivated estimates. A responsible history identifies repression and the destruction of institutions without inventing a comprehensive casualty figure. Educational materials printed under the autonomous government, including Azerbaijani-language books, became symbols in later accounts of the restoration of central authority, but particular claims about their destruction require close scrutiny of their sources.

Mahabad followed a related but distinct course. The town's leaders had less capacity to sustain a prolonged defense, and the choices of surrounding tribal forces constrained any decision to fight. Qazi Muhammad surrendered; the Iranian army entered Mahabad in December, and he and other leaders were executed in March 1947 after proceedings that raise serious questions about political justice. Mustafa Barzani and his fighters did not share Qazi's exact fate; their withdrawal and later movements belong to the wider history of Kurdish political mobilization. These divergent outcomes warn against imposing a single narrative of defeat on every participant. For Iranian officials the campaign symbolized the restoration of sovereignty. For many Azerbaijani and Kurdish activists it also became a memory of promises of cultural recognition followed by coercive recentralization.

The consequences extended beyond the northwest. The army and the Shah gained prestige as the guarantors of national unity, and the Shah took credit for the recovery. Qavam gained a major success but also gave the army and the Shah increased standing at the expense of his own coalition. The Tudeh Party, which had been associated in the public mind with Soviet policy, lost support among nationalists. Azerbaijani and Kurdish demands for linguistic and cultural recognition did not disappear, but they were repressed for decades. The memory of the Azerbaijan crisis and the Mahabad republic would shape how Iranian governments, Azerbaijani and Kurdish activists, and the Soviet Union and its successors interpreted regional politics long afterward.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Soviet Withdrawal and the Autonomous Governments' Collapse Occur?

The question: The Soviet army left in spring 1946, and the autonomous governments fell in December. Historians ask what drove the Soviet decision to withdraw, why it did not defend the movements, and which factors best explain the collapse.

Interpretation A — International pressure: Some accounts emphasize the Security Council appeal and American and British pressure, including diplomatic protests and public alarm, as the primary reason Stalin withdrew. The "Truman ultimatum" version of this argument is poorly documented, but a broader claim that Western pressure raised the costs of staying is widely accepted.
Interpretation B — Qavam's diplomacy and the oil bargain: Others stress that Soviet leaders accepted withdrawal in return for the April agreement and the expectation of an oil concession, and that Qavam's mix of negotiation, concession, and delay shaped the outcome. On this account, the later Majles rejection of the oil proposal was the Soviet Union's real defeat.
Interpretation C — Internal weakness of the autonomous regimes: A further argument holds that the governments lacked deep roots, depended on repression and Soviet backing, faced hostility from landowners and some minorities, and could not survive once the Soviet shield was withdrawn.

Evidence: Soviet archival documents show that Moscow's policy shifted in response to Western pressure and Iranian diplomacy, and Iranian, American, and British diplomatic records show the pressures applied. The sources on local support are less reliable, since both partisans and opponents wrote with purpose, and accounts of casualties in December vary.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and weight more than facts: whether external or domestic factors were primary, and whether the Soviet Union traded the movements for oil or simply miscalculated.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians judge Qavam, who has been seen both as a skilled diplomat and as a calculating opportunist, and how they assess the Azerbaijani and Kurdish movements, whether as authentic national movements, Soviet instruments, or something between.

Section 85.13

The Tudeh Party, Labor Movements, and the 1946 Oil Strike

The industrial working class that mobilized in the 1940s was small relative to the rural population but concentrated in strategic places. Textile mills in Isfahan, factories in Tehran and the northern provinces, the railway, the ports, and above all the oil installations of Khuzestan employed tens of thousands of wage workers. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's operations around Abadan, which included one of the largest refineries in the world, formed the most important single industrial site in the country (Chapter 83). They employed Iranian workers at very low wages relative to British staff, under a hierarchy that segregated housing, pay, and facilities.

Tudeh and its allied unions, organized in a federation that grew rapidly after 1943, drew on this discontent. They campaigned for higher wages, an eight-hour day, paid holidays, safe conditions, and recognition of unions. Strikes in Tehran, Isfahan, and elsewhere, as well as in the oil fields, tested both employers and the state. The wartime inflation that cut real wages, the shortages of food and housing, and the visible contrast between workers' conditions and company profits gave these demands force. By 1946 the labor movement was among the strongest in the Middle East, though estimates of union membership vary, and Tudeh claims should be treated with caution.

Khuzestan was the point of greatest tension. Workers in the oil fields and at Abadan organized around grievances over wages, housing in the shanty districts around the refinery, and the treatment of Iranian employees. The AIOC resisted unions and sought support from British officials and from Iranian authorities. In the spring and early summer of 1946, tension grew. After a May Day demonstration, union leaders pressed demands, and a general strike in the oil region began on 14 July. Clashes followed between strikers and groups opposed to them, including Arab tribal elements, with British and Iranian officials and company personnel taking part in different ways. Violence in the following days killed a number of people. Estimates of the dead vary by source, from several dozen to figures considerably higher, and the extent of company or British involvement in organizing counter-demonstrations is disputed.

The strike that began on 14 July 1946 was rooted in the special geography of the southern oil industry. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company had built industrial facilities, pipelines, ports, and a hierarchy of residential and employment arrangements in Khuzestan. Iranian workers often compared pay, housing, medical provision, and advancement with the conditions available to foreign technical staff. Unions used these disparities to link everyday grievances to wider questions of national economic power. They also operated in an arena where a British-controlled enterprise and Iranian authorities claimed different forms of responsibility for industrial order. The Iranian labor law adopted in 1946 raised expectations for regulated employment and rest, but workers still faced uncertainty over enforcement. Payment for Friday, a day of rest, was a central immediate demand, not a trivial technicality: regular payment could determine whether a household met its weekly expenses.

The Tudeh-associated labor federation could mobilize large gatherings, but the workers were not one homogeneous political bloc. Skilled and unskilled employees, migrants, local Arab communities, and staff in different installations had different interests and levels of security. In Abadan, rivalry between trade-union activists and local political organizations, including Arab tribal groups opposed to the left, contributed to violent confrontation. British officials portrayed the disturbance through concerns about Communist influence and the security of oil supplies; labor organizers emphasized rights and discriminatory treatment; Tehran confronted both its obligation to maintain order and the danger of appearing to act as the company's police. A statement to the British House of Commons on 17 July referred to approximately 50,000 affected workers and reported 17 deaths and 150 wounded. Those figures were provisional contemporary reports, not a settled final toll.

The strike demonstrated both the leverage and the vulnerability of concentrated industrial labor. Stopping work at a refinery affected export earnings and international interests much more quickly than a dispersed rural protest could. It also invited a powerful response from a company, a government, and local forces worried about disruption. The strike's settlement and subsequent repression did not eliminate the underlying dispute over whose law governed company employment or how much of the national economy should depend on British-managed oil. The experience fed later nationalist critiques, but workers' demands should not be read backward merely as the opening chapter of a future oil-nationalization campaign. In 1946 their most urgent concerns included wages, safety, rest, recognition, and protection from violence.

The Iranian government responded in several ways. It dispatched troops and officials to restore order, and the strike ended after the intervention. At the same time, Qavam used the episode to bargain with Tudeh, whose cooperation he wanted for his political plans, and the government adopted labor legislation in 1946 that recognized some worker rights. The strike and its aftermath showed that the labor movement could affect Iranian politics, and that British economic interests were still heavily protected by their influence in the south.

Cause and Effect

The July 1946 Khuzestan Oil Strike

Long-term causes

The foreign-owned concession and its terms (Chapter 83), wage and housing inequality between Iranian and British employees, and the absence of legal protection for unions under the earlier regime.

Short-term causes

Wartime inflation, shortages, the growth of Tudeh-aligned unions after 1943, and the weakening of state controls after 1941.

Immediate trigger

Disputes over union recognition and demands in the spring and early summer of 1946, followed by the call for a general strike on 14 July.

Effects

Violence and deaths, government intervention, new labor legislation, Tudeh's inclusion in Qavam's cabinet in August, and a hardening of conservative and British attitudes toward the left.

Historical complexity: The strike did not result from any single factor. It combined labor grievance, political organization, foreign economic interests, and Iranian government calculations, and its meaning was contested at the time. Its legacy fed later disputes over the AIOC that lead to Chapter 86.

Section 85.14

Qavam's Political Coalition and the Democratic Party

Qavam did not rely only on diplomacy. Throughout 1946 he constructed a political base that could give him independence from both the court and the established parliamentary factions. In the early summer he founded the Democratic Party of Iran, which combined reformist officials, intellectuals, some landowners, and professional and commercial groups under a program of modernization, social measures, and cooperation across political lines. The party's character is easy to caricature as a mere vehicle for Qavam's ambition, and he did intend it to elect a favorable parliament, but it also drew committed reformers.

The coalition strategy extended to the left. In August 1946 Qavam brought three Tudeh figures into his cabinet, including Iraj Eskandari as a minister, giving the party its first formal role in government. The decision was meant to secure Tudeh's cooperation in a moment of labor unrest and to demonstrate to Moscow that Iran's government was not hostile to the left. It also alarmed the Shah, the army, conservative landowners, and the British and American embassies, who feared Tudeh influence in the state. Qavam balanced this by keeping control of key ministries and relying on his own authority.

The coalition did not hold. In September a rebellion in the south, led by tribal leaders of the Qashqai confederation and associated with other southern leaders, demanded the dismissal of the Tudeh ministers and greater provincial representation. The rebels' movement drew on tribal and regional interests and the resentments of the Reza Shah period, and some British officials were believed to sympathize with it, though the extent of British involvement is debated. Qavam responded by negotiating with the rebels and, in October, reshuffling his cabinet and removing the Tudeh ministers. The episode was a reminder of how far provincial elites and tribal confederations retained political weight after the collapse of Reza Shah's centralized order (Chapter 82).

The change in the cabinet also reflected the new relationship with the Shah and the army. Qavam now turned toward a security-oriented policy, in line with American encouragement, and soon authorized the army's advance into Azerbaijan. In the elections for the Fifteenth Majles, held from December 1946 into early 1947 under the army's supervision in many areas, his party and allies won a substantial presence, but not a loyal majority. Deputies shifted according to local and factional interests, and the Shah's influence was strong among them.

Qavam's domestic balancing effort also illustrates why a coalition assembled for emergency diplomacy might not survive ordinary political bargaining. The Democratic Party offered a vehicle for government supporters to contest elections and claimed a program of reform and national recovery. Its appeal, however, relied heavily on Qavam's access to office and on local notables whose loyalty was often contingent. A party controlled through appointments and patronage had difficulty developing the durable branch organization, accountable leadership, and common social program that might have survived the fall of its founder. Qavam's temporary cooperation with Tudeh figures was particularly costly. It helped him demonstrate that he could negotiate with the left and might reduce the appeal of Soviet-backed opposition, but it alarmed conservative deputies, military commanders, religious leaders, and the British government. When diplomatic success no longer supplied a unifying purpose, his coalition began to pull apart.

The royal court was not passive in this process. The Shah's close ties to officers and his increasing identification with the restoration of control in Azerbaijan made it harder for Qavam to claim exclusive credit for the outcome. Deputies could invoke the October 1947 oil question to assert parliamentary independence while also distancing themselves from a premier suspected of excessive accommodation with Moscow. The outcome was not simply a victory of one institution over another. Parliament could defeat a foreign petroleum arrangement while leaving intact political practices that concentrated security power in the court and disadvantaged organized opponents. That dual character would mark the subsequent political settlement.

The consequence was that Qavam's position proved fragile. His successes in diplomacy and in ending the autonomous governments did not translate into a durable parliamentary base. He had been the Shah's rival and was now the Shah's beneficiary; he was disliked by the left, resented by nationalists for the oil letters, and mistrusted by conservatives for his courting of Tudeh. When the Majles turned against him in 1947, he had few reliable allies. The Shah, who had strengthened his position through the army's role, did not rescue him.

Section 85.15

The Fifteenth Majles and the Rejection of Soviet Petroleum Demands

The legal foundation of the 1947 decision was set earlier. On 2 December 1944, during the dispute over requests for foreign oil rights, the Fourteenth Majles passed a statute prohibiting specified Iranian officials from entering discussions or agreements about foreign oil concessions. It did not merely allow negotiations provided parliament later consented. The statute distinguished negotiations for petroleum sales and for the exploitation of resources by the Iranian state, which carried reporting requirements, from the prohibited concession discussions. It also provided criminal penalties for contravention. These distinctions mattered to the legality of Qavam’s April 1946 commitments and to the Majles’s authority to repudiate them.

That law shaped the handling of the April 1946 letters. The agreement committed Qavam to submit a proposal to the new Majles within seven months of the start of the evacuation period. The Fifteenth Majles, elected in the winter of 1946–1947, did not convene until mid-1947, and Qavam placed the matter before it in the autumn. The debate took place in a changed international climate. Soviet forces had been gone for months, the Truman Doctrine of March 1947 had announced American support for Greece and Turkey, and American aid and military assistance to Iran were increasing. The Soviet Union had little means to compel acceptance.

On 22 October 1947 the Majles rejected the proposal by an overwhelming vote, commonly reported as 102 to 2. The resolution also instructed the government to review Iran's rights in its natural resources, directed it to negotiate with Britain over the AIOC concession, and barred the granting of concessions to foreign powers in the north. The Soviet Union protested that the rejection violated the 1946 arrangement. Iran answered that the letters had promised only a submission to parliament and had always depended on parliamentary consent. The question of whether Qavam acted in bad faith, and whether the Soviet Union had been deceived or had understood the risk, remains debated. Contemporary American diplomatic reporting specifies that the two counted against the motion comprised one blank ballot and one abstention, not two affirmative pro-Soviet votes.

The vote mattered in three ways. It affirmed that the Majles held authority over Iranian petroleum, a principle that would underlie the oil nationalization of 1951 (Chapter 86). It ended Soviet hopes of a northern oil concession. And it turned the focus of Iranian oil politics to the south and the British company, whose concession many deputies now wanted to revise. In this sense the rejection of the Soviet proposal was not only a defeat for Moscow but the opening of a new phase of oil politics, in which Iranian nationalists asked why the standards applied to the Soviet Union should not apply equally to Britain.

The vote also exposed Qavam. Many deputies treated the April letters as evidence that he had been willing to compromise Iran's resources, and the debate was used by his opponents. In December 1947 the Majles voted no confidence in his cabinet, and he left office. His fall was not only a personal one. It indicated that the parliament, shaped by landowners, nationalists, and royal influence, could make and unmake prime ministers, and that the Shah's influence among deputies was considerable. The political winners of the oil vote were the nationalists, the court, and, in a different way, the United States, whose influence in Tehran grew.

Section 85.16

Political Fragmentation and the Limits of Liberalization

After Qavam's fall, a succession of prime ministers governed with narrow and unstable majorities. Ebrahim Hakimi formed a government in December 1947, followed by Abdolhossein Hajir in 1948 and Mohammad Sa'ed in late 1948, among others. Each depended on the Majles, the court, and foreign embassies in varying proportions. The turnover reflected the absence of disciplined parties in parliament and a persistent struggle between the Shah and cabinets over executive authority.

The party system also fragmented. The Tudeh Party, weakened by the loss of Azerbaijan and by the stigma of Soviet association, split in early 1948 when a group led by Khalil Maleki broke away to form a socialist current critical of the party's subordination to Moscow. Nationalist and religious groups expanded. Kashani's influence grew among the clergy and the bazaar, and the Fada'iyan-e Islam continued their campaign of assassinations. Political violence became a regular feature of the period, ranging from attacks by militant groups to street clashes between rival parties, and the killing of Kasravi in 1946 was an early example.

The state also narrowed political space. Governments used martial law, press restrictions, arrests, and bans on organizations. After 1946 the left faced increasing pressure: unions were limited, newspapers were closed, and Tudeh leaders and activists were harassed or imprisoned. These measures were framed as responses to subversion and to the Soviet connection, and many nationalists and conservatives supported them. They also reflected the Shah's and army's interest in a political order less open to mass mobilization.

The limited opening after 1941 created several different forms of political participation, not a single democratic system. Newspaper editors could attack a premier but remain vulnerable to suspension or prosecution. Parties might recruit members in cities and workplaces while rural districts continued to depend on landlords, village intermediaries, and electoral influence exercised through officials. Deputies could block budgets or ministries without commanding a stable legislative majority. When cabinets changed frequently, civil servants and security commanders often outlasted elected political arrangements. These discrepancies help explain why parliamentary vigor did not automatically yield dependable government or equal political rights. They also limit descriptions of the era as either a functioning parliamentary democracy or an empty façade.

Religious networks, bazaar associations, professionals, students, and ethnic or regional movements were not interchangeable with the principal parliamentary factions. A cleric's constituency could be organized through mosques and personal authority; a labor organizer relied on factory committees; a regional reformer might seek provincial control of schools or revenues. Political coalitions were therefore built across institutions that rarely shared a permanent program. The State's response to organized opposition could include cooperation, surveillance, censorship, arrest, and negotiation, sometimes in rapid succession. By the late 1940s the contest was not merely who should become the next prime minister but whether independent associations could operate without being cast as threats to public order or foreign security.

International alignment shifted accordingly. The United States, which had played a significant part in the northern crisis, expanded its interest in Iran as part of its containment policy, providing military and economic assistance and cultivating ties with the army and the Shah. Britain remained powerful through the AIOC. The Soviet Union, having lost its northern position, shifted to political and propaganda pressure. The competition made foreign influence a continuing issue in Iranian politics, and nationalists began to speak of the need to resist all foreign interference.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iranian Politics from 1941 to 1949

What Changed

  • Mass-membership parties, unions, and a lively press entered Iranian politics
  • The Majles acted as a real arena, passing the 1944 oil law and rejecting the 1947 Soviet proposal
  • The central government recovered authority over Azerbaijan and Kurdistan
  • The Shah moved from a weak successor to a monarch with an expanded constitutional position by 1949
  • The United States became a major actor beside Britain and the Soviet Union

What Stayed the Same

  • Large landowners and notables retained strong representation in the Majles and in rural society
  • A narrow, indirect electoral system excluded women and many men
  • The army and court remained central to executive authority
  • The foreign-owned oil concession in the south remained unchanged
  • Centralizing policies on language and administration were maintained after 1946

Who experienced change most directly: Urban workers, intellectuals, and minority activists felt the widest change, both in opportunity and in subsequent repression. Peasants and rural communities experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: The decade shows that political opening and institutional consolidation were separate processes, and that a recovered state could narrow space opened during its absence.

Division V Royal Reassertion and the Rise of the National Front
Section 85.17

The Attempted Assassination of Mohammad Reza Shah, February 1949

On 4 February 1949 the Shah attended a ceremony at Tehran University commemorating the founding of the university. As he moved through the gathering, a man named Nasser Fakhrarai drew a pistol and fired several shots at close range. The Shah was wounded in the face but survived. Guards and officers shot and killed the assailant on the spot, so the attacker could not be questioned. The event was reported at once as an attempt on the monarch's life and became a turning point.

The identity and motives of the gunman have been debated. He carried press credentials associated with a religious publication, and some evidence pointed to religious or nationalist motives. Earlier associations with leftist circles were also alleged. The government and the court, however, quickly attributed the attack to the Tudeh Party, and the Shah's supporters used the event to move against it. Tudeh leaders denied involvement, and historians have generally found little reliable evidence that the party planned the shooting. The attack's real origin remains uncertain, and this chapter does not claim to settle it.

The response was swift. Martial law was declared in Tehran and extended, the Tudeh Party was banned, its offices closed and its newspapers suppressed, and many members were arrested. Ayatollah Kashani, who had no known part in the plot, was arrested and sent into exile in Lebanon, indicating that the crackdown extended beyond the left to political figures of whom the government and court disapproved. The press was restricted. The measures were popular with conservatives, with much of the army, and with some nationalists who distrusted Tudeh's Soviet ties.

The attack changed the Shah's public position. Presented as a survivor and as a figure above partisan politics, he gained sympathy and an opening to argue for stronger constitutional powers. Supporters described his survival as evidence of providential protection, a theme the monarchy would use in later years. For the first time since 1941 the Shah acted from a position of evident strength. The assassination attempt thus did not create royal ambition, but it provided an occasion that made it politically possible to enlarge royal authority.

The attack altered the terms of constitutional discussion even before anyone had established an authoritative account of the shooter's political ties. A government confronting an attempted assassination could plausibly demand immediate protection for public officials and greater scrutiny of weapons and conspiratorial activity. Yet the breadth of subsequent restrictions raised a separate question: whether emergency powers were being used to punish an organization as a whole without demonstrating that its leadership had ordered the crime. The Tudeh Party's Soviet connections made it vulnerable in an increasingly polarized international setting; they did not by themselves prove responsibility for Fakhrarai's act. Contemporary allegations, the attacker's reported personal associations, and later retrospective claims must be kept apart. Historians can recognize the seriousness of the violence while evaluating the legal and political proportionality of the government's response.

The ban on Tudeh also pushed the party underground and radicalized some of its members. Its leaders organized clandestine networks and, in later years, engaged in sporadic efforts to rebuild. The ban removed the largest organized left from legal politics at a time when the Cold War made the party's Soviet associations a major liability. How far its suppression helped or hindered the Shah and the Western powers in the longer run is considered in later chapters.

Section 85.18

The Constitutional Amendments of 1949

In the weeks after the attack, the Shah and his supporters moved to alter the constitution. The Majles authorized the convening of a constituent assembly, which met in April 1949. Its members were chosen under conditions of martial law and in an atmosphere that favored the court. Critics, including Mosaddegh and other constitutionalists, objected that the assembly was assembled in a manner and a climate that undermined its legitimacy.

The constituent assembly revised Article 48 of the original 1906 Fundamental Law, granting the Shah power to dissolve the Majles and the Senate under conditions requiring reasons and new elections. It also approved a separate article prescribing a procedure for future constitutional amendments. These changes must be distinguished from the Senate itself: the original constitutional framework had already provided for a sixty-member upper house, half appointed by the monarch; an enabling statute passed in 1948 and the chamber first met in 1950. The 1949 amendments increased royal leverage over parliament, but they did not newly establish that second chamber.

The meaning of these changes was contested then and is contested now. Supporters argued that a Senate would complete the constitutional design intended in 1906–1907, balance the lower house, and provide continuity and stability against a parliament they saw as fractious. They argued that a power of dissolution was a normal feature of constitutional monarchies and a remedy for deadlock. Opponents argued that the amendments tilted the balance of the constitution decisively toward the crown, since the power to dissolve and the partial power to appoint senators reduced the Majles's independence at a time when the Shah already influenced the army and the court. The long-term significance depends on how the powers were used, a subject of Chapters 86 through 91.

The amendment process had an institutional history longer than the few weeks after the attack. The Shah had discussed broader dissolution powers with foreign diplomats years earlier, and proposals to convene the constitutionally envisaged Senate were already under debate before 1949. The Majles enacted enabling legislation for a Senate in May 1948; the chamber was still not meeting when a constituent assembly was elected under martial law in 1949. The original constitution of 1906 had already provided for a sixty-member Senate with a royal appointment component, so the later assembly did not create that chamber from nothing. This chronology matters because it separates the older bicameral design, the law enabling its practical organization, and the new royal powers adopted in response to a specific political opportunity.

The principal change on 8 May 1949 replaced Article 48 of the 1906 Fundamental Law. It empowered the monarch to dissolve the Majles and Senate either separately or together, but required a stated reason and arrangements for new elections. A separate additional constitutional article prescribed a future revision procedure involving supermajority resolutions in both houses, royal approval, and a constituent assembly. These measures shifted the balance of constitutional instruments toward the crown without formally removing elections, parliamentary lawmaking, or ministerial responsibility. Their consequences depended on how the palace, cabinets, courts, deputies, and security forces interpreted and used them. A rule that permits dissolution with procedural conditions is different from an unlimited power to govern by decree; it may nonetheless increase royal bargaining leverage if electoral administration and the choice of ministers are already heavily influenced by the court.

The Senate first convened in 1950, bringing into operation an element of the constitutional design that had existed on paper for decades. Royal appointees in the upper chamber could now contribute to legislative politics, but the chamber's appearance should not be confused with the legal amendment to Article 48. Critics feared that combining a dissolution power with influence over a chamber and the security apparatus would permit the Shah to discipline uncooperative deputies. Supporters argued that unstable cabinets and parliamentary obstruction required an institution capable of restoring order. Both positions need to be situated in the experience of the 1940s, when disorder had been real but so had efforts to narrow independent political activity.

The amendments also illustrate a point about constitutional change. The 1949 revision followed legal procedure but occurred under martial law and amid the repression of the Tudeh, a situation that allowed the monarchy to enlarge its role through lawful forms. Historians therefore distinguish between formal legality and political legitimacy, and ask whether the amendments reflected a settled consensus or an advantage seized during a crisis.

Section 85.19

Mohammad Mosaddegh and the Formation of the National Front

Mohammad Mosaddegh was born in the early 1880s into a family of high officials with ties to the Qajar court. He studied in France and Switzerland, took a doctorate in law at Neuchâtel, and returned to hold ministerial and provincial posts in the 1910s and 1920s. He opposed the transfer of the throne to Reza Pahlavi in 1925, withdrew from public life, and was imprisoned in 1940. After 1941 he re-entered politics as a deputy, gaining a reputation for legal rigor, personal austerity, and insistence on constitutional procedure. His role in the 1944 oil law and his opposition to Soviet and British concessions made him a prominent nationalist.

The elections to the Sixteenth Majles in 1949 became the occasion for organized opposition. Held under Prime Minister Sa'ed, with martial law only recently lifted, they were widely accused of manipulation, especially in Tehran, where officials and the court were thought to have favored their own candidates. In October 1949 Mosaddegh led a delegation of supporters to the Shah's palace to protest, and a sit-in followed. The Shah and officials promised an inquiry. The Tehran election was later annulled and a new vote held in early 1950, in which Mosaddegh and several allies won seats.

The protest group formed the nucleus of the National Front, established in October 1949 as a coalition of nationalist politicians, professionals, and members of the Iran Party and other groups. Its demands centered on free and honest elections, freedom of the press and assembly, respect for the constitution, and independence from foreign influence. The Front was not a disciplined party. It gathered secular nationalists, modernizing professionals, and others who disagreed on many matters, and it remained at this stage an alliance around a shared grievance with electoral practice. Its relationship with Kashani and religious politicians was partial and shifting.

The Front's emergence indicates the way the 1949 political settlement produced opposition. By banning the Tudeh Party and expanding the monarch's constitutional powers, the Shah and his allies narrowed some forms of political participation, and the new opposition mobilized around constitutional principles. Its argument was that the constitution's promise of representative government had been weakened by fraud and royal influence. The demand for national control of resources, which the Front would take up, linked this constitutional claim to the oil question.

The National Front took shape around a procedural dispute because elections offered a tangible test of constitutional claims. The October 1949 protest at the palace was not merely a demand for one politician's seat; it raised the issue of whether a representative assembly could be regarded as legitimate when the court and authorities were accused of steering candidate selection and vote counting. Bringing together politicians, journalists, lawyers, merchants, and activists from several organizations made the Front potentially wider than a personal following, but the same diversity constrained the specificity of its program. Participants could agree on clean elections and national independence while differing about the monarchy, labor, religion, and the distribution of property. That coalition character helps explain why later achievements depended on continued negotiation among allies rather than an automatic expansion of a unified party apparatus.

Mosaddegh's position was distinctive because he linked legal forms to practical sovereignty. A constitution could promise ministerial responsibility, but that principle would be hollow if ministers controlled elections or if unelected advisers directed policy. Parliamentary control of concessions similarly meant little if executive agreements created commercial obligations in advance of legislative scrutiny. These concerns connected the disputes of 1944 and 1947 with the political opposition of 1949. Nevertheless, the formation of a coalition did not settle how it would relate to religious leaders, the left, provincial movements, or the growing monarchy. At the close of this chapter the National Front was an emerging constitutional force, not yet the governing movement whose achievements and divisions are the subject of the next chapter.

The Front's rise was contingent. It depended on the Tehran election dispute, on Mosaddegh's prestige, on the weakness of rival parties, and on the Shah's decision to yield on the Tehran vote. Nothing in 1949 made the oil nationalization or Mosaddegh's premiership inevitable. Those developments belong to Chapter 86 and depended on events after 1949, including the Anglo-Iranian dispute and the political crises of 1950–1951.

Section 85.20

Historical Synthesis: National Sovereignty and Constitutional Conflict

By 1950, Iran had restored its territorial authority in the north. The Soviet army had left, the Azerbaijan People's Government and the Republic of Mahabad had been destroyed, and the Majles had rejected the Soviet oil proposal. These outcomes resulted from the interaction of Iranian diplomacy, international pressure, Soviet decisions, and the Iranian army's action. They also left costs: repression in Azerbaijan and Kurdistan, the death of Qazi Muhammad, and a deepening of the center's suspicion of regional demands for cultural and political recognition.

The political settlement was less complete. The crisis had strengthened the army, the court, and the Shah, whose role in December 1946 and in the aftermath of the 1949 attack increased his standing. It had weakened Qavam and the left. At the same time, it had also created a parliament that insisted on authority over oil and a nationalist opposition that demanded honest elections. The question of who should exercise sovereign power in Iran, the Shah, parliament, ministers, or organized political movements, was not resolved but sharpened.

The economic questions remained unsettled as well. In early 1949 the government launched the First Seven-Year Development Plan and set up a Plan Organization to direct investment in agriculture, transport, and industry, expecting to finance much of it from oil revenues. The dependence on revenue from the AIOC made the terms of the British concession an increasingly political matter. In July 1949 the government signed a supplemental agreement with the company that would have modified the terms, but it faced strong criticism in the Majles and was not ratified. The disputes it provoked lead directly into Chapter 86.

These developments should not be read as a march toward 1951 or 1953. Different outcomes were possible. A Majles less committed to oil nationalism, a Shah less successful in using the 1949 crisis, a Soviet Union more willing to fight for Azerbaijan, or a Qavam more skillful in building a durable coalition could each have changed the path. Historians of the period, including Abbas Amanat, Ervand Abrahamian, Fakhreddin Azimi, and Homa Katouzian, differ in how they weigh these contingencies and in how they interpret the decade as a whole.

Historical Debate

Was 1941–1953 a Lost Democratic Opportunity?

The question: Historians disagree about how to characterize the political opening that followed 1941 and how to explain why it narrowed.

Interpretation A — A democratic opportunity cut short: Some scholars, including Fakhreddin Azimi in his account of a crisis of democracy, emphasize the genuine pluralism of the period, the strength of parliament, and the role of the Shah, the army, and foreign powers in closing it down.
Interpretation B — Weak institutions and recurring patterns: Others stress structural weaknesses, including narrow electoral rules, landowner dominance, unstable cabinets, and political violence, and see a recurring pattern in Iranian politics in which periods of arbitrary rule alternate with fragmentation, an argument associated with Homa Katouzian.
Interpretation C — Social conflict and foreign intervention: A third emphasis, found in the work of Ervand Abrahamian and others, focuses on class and social forces, including labor, landowners, and the middle classes, and on the pressures of oil and the Cold War.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on parliamentary records, newspapers, memoirs, foreign diplomatic reports, and statistical and social evidence. The same events, such as the 1946 coalition or the 1949 amendments, are read differently.

Points of disagreement: Scholars disagree about how democratic the system was, how decisive foreign intervention was, and how much weight to give structural factors compared with contingent decisions. These interpretations are not strictly exclusive.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians judge the monarchy, the left, nationalism, and the foreign powers, and how they interpret the road to 1951–1953.

Student task: Select one event from this chapter and explain how each interpretation would read it. Identify evidence that could favor one reading over another.

The record of the northern crisis is particularly revealing because the meanings of sovereignty diverged. The Iranian government sought the right to control territory and command armed forces without interference from a foreign army. Azerbaijani and Kurdish activists also used the language of political rights, but often understood legitimate government to include provincial voice, language protections, and locally accountable administration. Soviet officials spoke of regional peoples' grievances while pursuing strategic and petroleum objectives. Western diplomats defended the principle of withdrawal yet viewed Iran through the security of oil and their wider struggle with Moscow. These were not necessarily equally legitimate claims, but a history confined to one definition of sovereignty cannot explain the conflict's origins or its bitter aftermath.

One additional distinction is between surviving institutions and the political constituencies they were supposed to represent. The fall of an autonomous government ended its ministries, military organization, and public symbols. It did not erase the social experience of cultural restriction, the aspirations of Kurdish nationalists, or disagreements about central authority. Equally, an elected Majles that resisted a foreign oil arrangement did not automatically guarantee unrestricted newspapers or fair elections. By placing regional administration, organized labor, the royal court, parliament, and international diplomacy in the same narrative, the chapter reveals a persistent tension: Iran could recover independence from foreign soldiers while continuing to dispute the domestic distribution of power. That conflict made the approaching petroleum controversy consequential far beyond the question of company revenue.

The central conclusion is this: Iran emerged from the Azerbaijan crisis having restored its territorial authority, but the question of who should exercise sovereign political power within Iran, the Shah, parliament, ministers, or organized political movements, remained unresolved. Chapter 86 takes up that question through the struggle over oil and the premiership of Mohammad Mosaddegh.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 85 followed the Pahlavi state through its most dangerous postwar test. The 1941 occupation had left Mohammad Reza Shah on the throne but with limited authority, and it had reopened a political arena in which parties, unions, a lively press, and a revived Majles competed for influence. The chapter's governing question was how Iran restored its territorial sovereignty after World War II while struggling to determine who would exercise political power.

The Shah's position in the 1940s was constrained by the constitution, foreign powers, and rival politicians, yet he worked steadily to secure the army and enlarge the role of the court. Cabinets depended on parliamentary bargaining, and prime ministers such as Qavam resisted royal tutelage. Historians differ on whether to emphasize his weakness or his persistent ambition, and the evidence supports both.

The Tripartite Treaty of 1942 promised withdrawal of Allied forces within six months of the end of hostilities, and after Japan's surrender that put the deadline at 2 March 1946. British and American forces left, but Soviet forces remained in the north. The refusal to withdraw, tied to Soviet oil ambitions, security concerns, and leverage over Tehran, turned an occupation into an international crisis.

In Azerbaijan, grievances over language, centralization, land, and neglect combined with Soviet organization and protection. The Azerbaijan Democratic Party under Ja'far Pishevari took shape in September 1945, and the People's Government was established in December. Historians generally agree that the movement had real local roots but could not have governed without Soviet protection, though they disagree on the weight of each.

The Azerbaijani government introduced Turkish in administration and schools, redistributed some land, and promoted cultural institutions, while also suppressing opponents and depending on armed units and Soviet backing. At Mahabad, Qazi Muhammad proclaimed a Kurdish republic in January 1946. It was smaller, more tribal, and less dependent on Soviet support, and it deserves study as a movement in its own right.

Iran's appeal to the United Nations in January 1946, together with American and British pressure and Qavam's negotiations in Moscow and Tehran, produced the 4 April letters. These linked Soviet withdrawal to a proposed joint oil company that required parliamentary approval but did not create a concession. Soviet forces left by May 1946.

The Soviet withdrawal and the Iranian army's advance were separate events seven months apart. Talks with Pishevari in June produced a compromise that failed. In December 1946 the army entered Tabriz and Mahabad, the autonomous governments collapsed, reprisals followed, and Qazi Muhammad was hanged in March 1947.

Domestic politics reflected the same tensions. Labor mobilization, culminating in the July 1946 Khuzestan strike, brought Tudeh ministers into Qavam's cabinet, provoked a southern tribal revolt, and ended with their dismissal. The Majles, building on its 1944 law, rejected the Soviet oil proposal on 22 October 1947, affirmed its authority over petroleum, and contributed to Qavam's fall.

Political fragmentation followed. After the attempt on the Shah's life in February 1949, the Tudeh Party was banned and a constituent assembly expanded the monarch's powers, including dissolution and a partly appointed Senate. In response, Mosaddegh and allies formed the National Front in October 1949 around honest elections and constitutional government. The chapter's conclusion is that territorial authority was restored, but the question of who should exercise sovereign power remained open.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a crisis over territory became a crisis over authority. The Soviet occupation of northern Iran and the autonomous governments that formed under its protection tested the principle of sovereignty in a divided world. Iran's response, combining diplomacy, international appeal, negotiation, and eventually force, restored control of the north. The restoration was real, and so were its costs.

The chapter also shows how that restoration changed domestic politics. The army and the Shah emerged stronger, the left weaker, and the parliament more assertive about resources and more vulnerable to royal pressure. The 1949 amendments and the formation of the National Front show that the postwar settlement created both new royal powers and a new constitutional opposition.

Many of these developments left long shadows. Debates over language rights, regional autonomy, and the treatment of Kurdish and Azerbaijani demands continued for decades. The principle that parliament controlled petroleum underlay the politics of Chapter 86. The tension between monarchy and elected institutions ran through the Pahlavi period and into the revolutionary crisis of Chapter 91.

Students should resist reading the decade as a straight line to 1953. The period contained real alternatives, and its actors, from Qavam to Mosaddegh to the Shah, made choices under pressure and uncertainty. Understanding those choices, and the evidence on which historians reconstruct them, is the purpose of this chapter.
