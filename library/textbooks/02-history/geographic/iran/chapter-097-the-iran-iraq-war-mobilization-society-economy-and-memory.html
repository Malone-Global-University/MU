The Iran–Iraq War: Mobilization, Society, Economy, and Memory

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic · Principal chronological scope: 1980–1988

Chapter Opening

On 24 May 1982 Iranian forces recovered Khorramshahr, the Khuzestan port city that Iraq had held since the first weeks of the war. It was the most celebrated Iranian victory of the conflict, and it did not end the fighting. The war lasted six more years, and in those years it reshaped the Islamic Republic more deeply than the victory had suggested it would.

Khorramshahr stands at the confluence of the Karun and Arvand (Shatt al-Arab) waterways, close to Abadan and the head of the Persian Gulf. Before September 1980 it was one of Iran's principal commercial ports, with a mixed population in which Persian-speaking, Arab, and other communities lived and worked. Iraqi forces attacked the city in the opening weeks of the invasion launched on 22 September 1980, and after a defense of roughly a month by a mixture of regular army units, Revolutionary Guards, police, and local volunteers, they took it on 24 October 1980. Much of the civilian population had already fled. The city that Iranian troops re-entered in May 1982 was badly damaged and largely empty, and Iranian commemorative culture has long remembered it under a name that joins the words for city and blood. Historians treat that name as part of the memory of the war as well as a description of it, and this chapter will return to the distinction repeatedly.

The liberation of Khorramshahr is the right starting point for a chapter on the war as a whole because it exposes the central contradiction of the conflict. In June 1982 Iraq announced withdrawal from most of the Iranian territory it held. On 12 July the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 514, calling for a ceasefire and withdrawal to internationally recognized boundaries. Iran had, in the narrow sense of a defensive war, largely achieved what it set out to do. Yet its leadership chose to carry the war into Iraq, and for six more years the two states fought a conflict of attrition that cost an enormous number of lives, drained both economies, and brought civilians in cities, towns, and shipping lanes into the line of fire. The question that organizes this chapter is not only how the war was fought but what eight years of warfare did to Iranian institutions, households, and memory.

The chapter therefore treats the war as a national experience and not only a military one. Its first division traces the campaigns and the political decision to continue after 1982. Its second examines the institutions and the ideas through which the Islamic Republic mobilized soldiers, volunteers, and civilians: the regular armed forces (the Artesh), the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the Basij, the language of martyrdom, and the wartime state. The third division asks how a government dependent on petroleum exports paid for a long war, kept essential goods flowing, and adapted its industry and agriculture. The fourth turns to people: displaced families, women, children, victims of missile and chemical attack, prisoners, and the wounded. The fifth follows the internationalization of the conflict, tallies its human costs, and examines how it has been represented and remembered.

The evidence is uneven, and the chapter says so as it goes. Contemporary sources include United Nations reports and Security Council resolutions, humanitarian records of the International Committee of the Red Cross, Iranian and Iraqi government publications, newspapers, radio and television output, and economic statistics compiled by the Central Bank of Iran, the Statistical Center of Iran, and international institutions. Later sources include memoirs, oral histories, commemorative literature, films, and scholarship produced in Iran and abroad. Wartime statistics were compiled by belligerent governments with strong reasons to emphasize particular facts, and postwar commemoration was organized by institutions with their own interests. Figures in this chapter are given as documented observations, attributed ranges, or explicitly bounded estimates, with their sources and limits stated, and where no reliable comparable figure exists the chapter says that rather than supply one.

The chapter's boundaries follow the division of labor in the textbook outline. Chapter 95 examined revolutionary consolidation and political repression, and Chapter 96 explained the origins and outbreak of the war, so this chapter does not retell the border disputes, the diplomacy of 1979 and 1980, or Iraq's decision to invade. Chapter 98 will take up the negotiations that led to the ceasefire, Ayatollah Khomeini's decision to accept Security Council Resolution 598, the political prisoner executions of 1988, and the succession of 1989. Chapter 97 reaches the military reversals of 1988 and the diplomatic bridge to those events, but it stops short of them. Later chapters will also take up postwar reconstruction (Chapter 99), the long-term commercial role of the Revolutionary Guards (Chapter 105), and contemporary social life (Chapter 106).

Three cautions shape the narrative. First, Iran was not a single, uniformly mobilized society. Regular soldiers, conscripts, volunteers, clerics, bureaucrats, women, minorities, villagers, and urban middle-class families entered the war on different terms, and some resisted or avoided it. Second, the Islamic Republic did strengthen its institutions through the war, but institutional survival is not the same as military victory, and neither is the same as the fulfillment of declared political goals. Third, suffering was not confined to one side. Iraqi soldiers and civilians died, were imprisoned, and were displaced, and Iraq's Kurdish population endured separate and devastating violence at the hands of its own government. A responsible account keeps these distinctions visible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the principal phases of the Iran–Iraq War from the 1980 invasion to the military circumstances of mid-1988.
  2. Explain why Iran continued the war inside Iraq after recovering most of its occupied territory in 1982, and why historians disagree about that decision.
  3. Compare the institutional origins, roles, and relationships of the Artesh, the Revolutionary Guards, and the Basij.
  4. Distinguish conscripts, volunteers, and professional personnel, and assess the evidence on motivation and on the recruitment of minors.
  5. Analyze how religious symbolism, nationalism, and political authority were combined in the language of martyrdom and the Sacred Defense.
  6. Evaluate how the Islamic Republic financed the war, separating the effects of revolution, war, sanctions, and the 1986 oil-price collapse.
  7. Describe the wartime experience of industry, agriculture, rationing, and household consumption, recognizing sectoral variation.
  8. Assess the social consequences of displacement, urban attack, and bereavement for borderland communities, women, children, and veterans.
  9. Explain the documentation of chemical warfare, the role of international law, and the difference between Sardasht and Halabja.
  10. Analyze the Tanker War and international involvement without reducing the war's length to foreign assistance alone.
  11. Interpret casualty and humanitarian statistics with attention to definitions, sources, and limitations.
  12. Evaluate official commemorative narratives against personal and alternative memories, and judge how the war transformed the Islamic Republic.

Historical Context

When Iraqi forces crossed the frontier on 22 September 1980, the Islamic Republic was less than two years old. The monarchy had fallen in February 1979 (Chapters 91 and 92), a referendum and a constitution had established a republic with a clerical supreme leader (Chapter 93), the seizure of the American embassy had hardened the revolution's divisions and its isolation (Chapter 94), and the new authorities were fighting for control against Islamist rivals, leftists, nationalists, and regional movements, notably in Kurdistan (Chapter 95). Chapter 96 explained how this domestic turmoil, a long-running dispute over the Shatt al-Arab waterway and other border questions, the ambitions of Saddam Hussein's government, and the collapse of the Algiers Agreement of 1975 combined to produce Iraq's decision to invade.

Iran's armed forces entered the war weakened. The imperial army, air force, and navy had been built up in the 1970s with large purchases of Western equipment, above all from the United States, and their officer corps had been purged, executed, imprisoned, or driven into exile in the revolution's aftermath. Spare parts and technical support from American suppliers ended with the rupture of relations. The Revolutionary Guards, founded in 1979 as a revolutionary force loyal to the new order, had grown quickly but had limited conventional military experience. Khomeini called for mass mobilization in November 1979; the Basij was legally organized in July 1980 and incorporated into the Revolutionary Guards in January 1981. Command was divided, and political rivalry between the president, Abolhassan Bani-Sadr, who sought to lead the war through the regular army, and elements of the clerical leadership and the Islamic Republican Party complicated operations until Bani-Sadr was removed in June 1981.

The economy was also in transition. The revolution had been followed by capital flight, the nationalization of banks, insurance companies, and many large industrial firms, and a sharp fall in investment. Petroleum production, which had reached several million barrels a day in the 1970s, collapsed during the revolutionary strikes and recovered only partially. Oil nevertheless remained the main source of government revenue and of foreign exchange. A war that struck at oil facilities, ports, and industrial centers in Khuzestan would test an economy that was already adjusting to revolutionary change.

Demographically, Iran was a young and fast-growing society. Census figures record a population of roughly 33.7 million in 1976 and 49.4 million in 1986, a rate of increase among the highest in the world during those years. A large cohort of teenagers and young men therefore reached military age during the war, a fact that shaped both conscription and volunteer recruitment. The country's military geography also mattered. The war's southern theater ran through the lowlands, marshes, rivers, and canals of Khuzestan and southern Iraq, while the central and northern theaters crossed the Zagros, the terrain discussed in Chapter 1 as both barrier and corridor. Iraq, with a much smaller population but a government able to concentrate resources, held the advantage of a unified command, a well-equipped army, and, soon, large loans from neighboring Gulf states and wide access to arms suppliers.

Finally, the war arrived in a Cold War and Gulf setting. Neither the United States nor the Soviet Union wished to see either belligerent decisively defeat the other in a way that would upset the regional balance, but both pursued shifting policies, and neighboring Arab governments, alarmed by revolutionary Iran's rhetoric, backed Iraq financially. Those external relationships, treated in Section 97.17, were never the whole explanation of the war's course, but they conditioned the resources each side could draw upon.

Division I · Sections 97.1–97.4

Military Campaigns and the Transformation of the War

Why a war of territorial defense became a war of attrition.

Section 97.1

The Iraqi Invasion, Iranian Resistance, and the First Year of War

Iraq's offensive of September 1980 opened along a broad front, from the Kurdistan borderlands in the north to the Shatt al-Arab and Khuzestan in the south. Its principal thrusts aimed at the oil-bearing lowlands and at the cities of Khorramshahr, Abadan, Ahvaz, Susangerd, and Dezful. Planners in Baghdad seem to have expected that a state in revolutionary disarray would crack quickly. That expectation was wrong, though it was not absurd. Iran's air and ground forces had been disrupted, and Iraqi columns did seize substantial ground in Khuzestan and in the western provinces of Ilam and Kermanshah. By the end of 1980 Iraq held a sizable belt of Iranian territory, together with the ruins of Khorramshahr; published estimates of its area vary.

The defense of Khorramshahr illustrates how improvised resistance worked. Regular army units, naval personnel, police and gendarmerie, Revolutionary Guards, armed civilians, and members of local communities, including Arabic-speaking residents of the province, fought block by block in a month-long struggle that slowed the invasion and cost Iraq heavy casualties. The city fell, but the delay gave Iran time to organize the defense of Abadan, which was encircled but never captured, and of the routes toward Ahvaz and Dezful. Abadan's refinery, among the largest in the world before the war, was shelled and largely shut down. Iranian civilians fled in great numbers from the battle zones, and the provincial economy of Khuzestan, which produced a large share of national oil output, was thrown into confusion.

Equally important was what failed to happen. The Iraqi advance stalled in late 1980, partly because of Iraqi logistical and doctrinal limits, partly because of Iranian resistance, and partly because Iran's armed forces, despite the purges, retained pilots, armored crews, and staff officers able to mount effective local operations. The Iranian air force launched strikes deep into Iraq in the war's first days, and the army's armored units fought the winter battles around Susangerd and Dezful. A rivalry between Bani-Sadr, who wished to make the Artesh the main instrument of the war, and the Guards and their political patrons produced friction, and the unsuccessful January 1981 Iranian counteroffensive around Hoveyzeh (Operation Nasr) became a subject of debate about command and military effectiveness. Yet the two kinds of force also learned to cooperate, in the field if not always at the top.

The year 1981 stabilized the front. Mostafa Chamran's irregular-warfare headquarters, active around Ahvaz and Susangerd, represented the revolutionary effort to fight an unconventional war, and his death in June 1981 made him one of the war's most commemorated figures. That same month the Majles voted to remove Bani-Sadr, who left the country, and the political command of the war passed decisively to the clerical leadership and its allies. In the following months Ali Khamenei became president, Mir-Hossein Mousavi became prime minister, and a more integrated pattern of war management began to emerge, with the Supreme Defense Council and the leaders of the Artesh and the Guards negotiating strategy. The political price of this consolidation, measured in repression of opposition groups, belongs to Chapter 95.

For ordinary Iranians the first year meant sudden displacement, anxiety about the future, and rapid militarization of public life. Tens of thousands of volunteers, many of them untrained, went to the front, and hospitals, mosques, and schools in nearby cities became centers of relief. Aircraft raids and, from late 1980, missile and shell attacks on frontier towns made the war a civilian reality as well as a military one. The war's first year thus established patterns that persisted: a state that could not rely on a single institution to defend it, a population mobilized unevenly through formal and informal channels, and a conflict in which territory, politics, and the legitimacy of the revolution were bound together.

For the inhabitants of Khuzestan, the transition from peacetime to invasion was also a transition from a functioning provincial economy to an emergency society. The province had been a major center of organized labor under the monarchy. Port workers, refinery employees, civil servants, transport workers, and their families possessed skills and connections that had survived the upheavals of the revolution. Once their workplaces closed or became dangerous, those capabilities did not disappear; some were transferred to other parts of the country, others were used in emergency relief and repair, and others were lost for long periods through displacement. Thus the resistance of 1980 should be understood not solely through the institutions that fought, but through the civilian economy that sustained them. The defense of the country depended on continuity in occupations that did not ordinarily belong to the armed forces.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Revolutionary Instability to Organized National Defense, 1980–1981

What changed?

  • The command of the war moved from a contested presidency and parallel organizations toward more coordinated councils and commanders.
  • The Guards and the Basij grew from revolutionary auxiliaries into large field forces.
  • Improvised civilian defense gave way to organized recruitment and logistics.

What remained continuous?

  • The regular armed forces and their officers remained indispensable to defense.
  • Competition between institutions persisted, though in changed forms.
  • Dependence on oil exports and on border provinces' economies continued.

Why the pattern matters: The change was uneven and gradual rather than abrupt, and it was experienced most directly by residents of Khuzestan and by those who joined the front. Treating 1981 as a simple shift from chaos to order would hide the continuing disagreements within the leadership.

Section 97.2

Iranian Counteroffensives and the Liberation of Khorramshahr, 1981–1982

The recovery of Iranian territory proceeded through a series of operations that grew in scale and sophistication. In late September 1981 an offensive known as Samen-ol-A'emeh broke the siege of Abadan, a victory achieved by combined army and Guard forces that cleared Iraqi positions near the city and restored land communication with it. In November and December 1981 operations around Bostan extended the recovery in the Susangerd area. These were important not only for the ground regained but because they showed that Iranian commanders could plan combined operations, including night attacks, infiltration, and the use of armor, infantry, artillery, and helicopters in coordination.

The offensive of March 1982, Fath ol-Mobin (conventionally rendered "Undeniable Victory"), was larger. Conducted in the area between Dezful and Shush, it destroyed or captured a considerable portion of the Iraqi forces opposed to it and recovered a large section of northern Khuzestan. It was followed by a still more ambitious operation, Beit ol-Moqaddas, launched in late April 1982, which aimed at the Karun River line and ultimately at Khorramshahr. The operation involved a crossing of the Karun and a drive toward the city that drew on Guards, Basij, and Artesh units, with extensive engineering and logistical support from Reconstruction Jihad and other revolutionary organizations.

On 24 May 1982 the city was recovered. Thousands of Iraqi soldiers were taken prisoner in the operation as a whole; published totals vary by source and are best treated as approximate. The victory was a military event, but it was also a political and psychological one. It ended the sense that Iran was condemned to retreat, validated the institutions that had organized the offensive, and strengthened the leadership's claims about the capacity of revolutionary mobilization. Commemoration of the liberation later became a fixed element of the annual calendar, and Khorramshahr entered the national vocabulary as a name for resistance and recovery.

Historians nevertheless caution against reading the victory as simply the product of faith and numbers. Iran's success depended on a series of practical factors: Iraqi overextension, weaknesses in Iraqi defensive doctrine, the willingness of Iranian commanders to learn from earlier failures, effective use of reconnaissance and local knowledge, and the combination of regular and irregular forces. It was also paid for in lives. Iranian casualties in these operations were substantial, and the offensive tactics that proved effective against exposed Iraqi positions in Khuzestan would prove far less effective against prepared defenses elsewhere.

The Iraqi government responded politically. In June 1982 Saddam Hussein announced a withdrawal from Iranian territory, and Iraq presented itself as willing to accept a ceasefire. Whether that offer was sincere, whether its terms were acceptable, and whether Iran should have accepted it are questions taken up in the next section. Here it is enough to note that the liberation of Khorramshahr created the conditions in which the Iranian leadership faced a decision, and that the decision it took defined the remaining six years of the war.

Territorial recovery also had a civilian dimension. The reopening of access to Abadan and the return of Khorramshahr to Iranian control did not restore the economic and social conditions that had prevailed there before the war. A place could be liberated while its inhabitants remained displaced, its commercial premises unusable, and its ordinary municipal services unable to function. For government planners, this created a distinction between the military achievement of ending occupation and the administrative task of making a damaged region habitable. It was possible to recover territory faster than to restore its housing, employment, or public utilities. This helps explain why the significance of the 1982 victory was much greater in the political and commemorative spheres than its immediate contribution to provincial production.

The changing relationship between the armed institutions was another important feature of the recovery. The army continued to contribute experienced personnel and established technical services; the Guards expanded their organizational competence; and volunteers, public employees, and relief workers supplied labor that could not be measured by conventional troop-strength figures. Historical accounts written by particular institutions may emphasize the role of their own members. Annie Tracy Samuel's study of the Guards' historiography, for example, gives students a reason to examine institutional accounts as records of how the war was understood and contested, rather than to accept the claim of any one organization to exclusive credit. The military successes of 1981 and 1982 were achieved within a changing system of relationships, and their commemoration later became a means of asserting institutional legitimacy. The liberation of Khorramshahr on 24 May is well established; explanations of how credit should be distributed among the participants require a different kind of evidence.

Section 97.3

The Decision to Continue the War Inside Iraq

By June 1982 the strategic position had reversed. Iraqi forces had been pushed from most Iranian soil, Iraq's economy was under strain, and Syria's closure of the Iraqi oil pipeline to the Mediterranean in April 1982 had cut an important export route. The Security Council, in Resolution 514 of July 1982, called for a ceasefire and a withdrawal to internationally recognized boundaries. For Iran's leaders the question was whether to accept an end to hostilities or to continue the war by advancing into Iraq.

The decision to continue rested on several arguments, which should be separated because different leaders held different combinations of them. One was a demand for reparations for the destruction of the invasion. Another was the insistence that Iraq be formally identified as the aggressor, a demand rooted in the belief that the Security Council had been slow to condemn the invasion in 1980. A third was a concern for security: Iranian leaders doubted that Saddam Hussein would honor an agreement and feared that Iraq could rebuild and attack again. A fourth was ideological, the call for the removal of the Baathist government, which the revolution's leaders regarded as an enemy, and which some saw as a stage toward wider revolutionary aims, including the liberation of Iraq's Shi'i holy cities. Finally there was momentum: the victories of 1981–1982 had created confidence, a mobilized military machine, and public expectations that were difficult to reverse.

Not every leader or observer agreed. Some Iranian political figures, including critics outside the ruling coalition, argued that the original aims had been achieved and that further fighting would cost lives without securing a better settlement. Others argued that the Iraqi proposals did not offer adequate guarantees. Within the leadership there were also differences in emphasis about timing and objectives, and the available evidence, much of it memoir literature and later interviews, must be read with the awareness that participants often shaped their accounts to defend or distance themselves from the decision. What can be said is that the final authority rested with Ayatollah Khomeini, who supported continuing the war until the Iraqi government was punished or removed.

In July 1982 Iranian forces launched Operation Ramadan, an offensive toward Basra. It failed to achieve its objectives against prepared Iraqi defenses, and subsequent operations in the south and in the central and northern sectors in 1982 and 1983 likewise produced limited gains at heavy cost. The attack on Iraqi territory changed the diplomatic position of Iran. Whereas Iraq had been the aggressor in 1980, Iran in 1982 was widely perceived abroad as the party prolonging the war, and Gulf Arab governments, which had earlier been ambivalent, increased their financial support for Iraq. This shift is crucial to understanding the war's later internationalization.

A responsible account must therefore distinguish two things that wartime and postwar rhetoric often merged. The defense of Iranian territory was widely supported and widely accepted as legitimate. The decision to carry the war into Iraq was a political choice, made by identifiable leaders, with identifiable aims, and open to evaluation on its own terms. It cannot be assumed that peace was guaranteed by any particular Iraqi proposal, nor that Iran's leaders were united in their reasoning or confident in their prospects.

A close reading of Security Council Resolution 514 clarifies the limits of the diplomatic opening. The resolution called for an immediate ceasefire and an end to military operations, together with withdrawal to internationally recognized boundaries; it envisaged United Nations observers and continuing mediation. It did not identify Iraq as the aggressor, fix compensation, or provide a comprehensive settlement of all of Tehran's claims. The timing also mattered. It was adopted on 12 July 1982, after the liberation of Khorramshahr and Iraq's June withdrawal announcement, rather than during the immediate days after the city's recovery. In assessing its significance, historians must distinguish the text actually adopted from the larger package of conditions Iranian leaders wished to obtain. Another grievance was the treatment of people expelled from Iraq, many classified by Baghdad as being of Iranian origin. Their status formed part of the wider dispute between the two governments and could not be settled simply by the movement of military forces away from the frontier.

There were genuine alternatives to continuing the war, but none can be reconstructed as a guaranteed outcome. One alternative was to accept a ceasefire and press unresolved claims through international institutions. Another was to insist on more explicit guarantees before ending hostilities. The leadership instead continued the conflict in pursuit of objectives beyond immediate territorial recovery. Some arguments for this choice were grounded in security concerns; others depended on an expectation that additional military pressure would produce political concessions or the fall of the Iraqi government. The latter expectation proved unsuccessful. Later recollections are particularly difficult evidence because leaders who defended the decision in 1982 might later emphasize their doubts or assign responsibility to others. The distinction between a contemporary official statement and a retrospective interview is therefore essential. The historian can identify the decision and its consequences without claiming certainty about every private discussion or assuming that a hypothetical peace agreement would necessarily have endured.

Historical Debate

Should Iran Have Ended the War in 1982?

The question: After the recovery of Khorramshahr and Iraq's withdrawal announcement, was it a strategic error, or a defensible choice, for the Iranian leadership to continue the war inside Iraq?

Interpretation A — The war should have ended: Critics argue that the principal legitimate aim, expelling the invader, had been achieved, that ceasefire terms could have been negotiated, and that six more years of war brought immense human and economic costs without securing reparations, Saddam Hussein's fall, or a lasting settlement.
Interpretation B — Continuation was understandable or justified: Defenders of the decision argue that Iraq had not accepted responsibility, that a ceasefire without guarantees risked a renewed attack, that Iranian public opinion and the military situation supported pressing the advantage, and that Iraq's own conduct, including later chemical warfare, vindicated mistrust.

Evidence: Both views draw on the text of Security Council resolutions, the diplomatic record of 1982, memoirs and interviews with Iranian and Iraqi officials, and the outcome of the later campaigns. Because the counterfactual question, what would have followed a ceasefire in 1982, cannot be tested, the debate also turns on judgments about intentions.

Points of disagreement: Historians disagree about the sincerity and terms of Iraq's offers, about the unity of the Iranian leadership, about the role of ideology relative to security calculation, and about whether a better settlement was realistically available later.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the war's costs are attributed: to an unavoidable defense, to a miscalculation, or to a mix of both.

Section 97.4

Prolonged Warfare, Military Attrition, and the Reversals of 1988

From 1983 to 1987 the war settled into a pattern of repeated Iranian offensives that gained limited ground, Iraqi defensive successes, and growing reliance on air and missile attack and on chemical weapons by Iraq. The southern marshes and the Majnoon Islands, the approaches to Basra, the Zagros foothills in the north, and the Kurdish borderlands were the principal arenas. In Operation Khaybar in February–March 1984 Iranian forces seized part of the Majnoon Islands, an area believed to contain major oil reserves, but the gains could not be exploited. Offensives in 1985 produced little change. Iraq, supported by foreign loans, large-scale purchases of foreign arms, and growing engineering resources, built extensive fortifications and water obstacles.

In February 1986 Iran launched Operation Valfajr-8 and captured the al-Faw Peninsula at the head of the Persian Gulf, a notable achievement, since it threatened Iraq's narrow outlet to the sea and brought Iranian forces near Kuwait. Al-Faw alarmed neighboring Gulf states and contributed to their willingness to support Iraq more openly. In early 1987 the large Karbala-5 offensive toward Basra, fought on a front east of the city, came within sight of the objective but failed at very high cost. Further operations in 1987 did not break the stalemate. Iranian offensive power was exhausted less by single defeats than by the cumulative effect of casualties, shortages, and diminishing returns on mass assault.

In 1988 the military balance shifted. Iraq, having reorganized its forces and improved its coordination of air, artillery, armor, and chemical weapons, launched a series of counteroffensives. In mid-April it recaptured al-Faw, and in the following weeks and months it retook ground near Basra and in other sectors. Iran suffered large losses of equipment, and recruitment of volunteers had declined. Meanwhile attacks on cities and the Tanker War, treated below, added pressure. By summer 1988 Iranian leaders confronted a deteriorating position, economic exhaustion, and diplomatic isolation. How they responded, and the role of Resolution 598, belong to Chapter 98.

The prolonged character of the war has encouraged comparison with attritional conflicts elsewhere. Neither government could translate battlefield effort into its principal political objective. Iraq did not overthrow the revolution, extract a favorable territorial settlement, or establish dominance in the Gulf. Iran did not remove Saddam Hussein, obtain reparations, or secure a formal condemnation of Iraq's aggression at the war's end. Both societies absorbed losses that far exceeded what either government had foreseen in 1980. The central military lesson is thus a limited one. Mass mobilization and endurance could preserve a state and recover its territory, but they could not by themselves force a decisive political result against a well-armed and externally supported adversary.

The pattern of the middle years is best understood by comparing political results, rather than simply measuring the territory gained or lost in each campaign. The Iranian leadership had expected that military pressure would improve its negotiating position. Iraq, in turn, sought to preserve the regime, restore its territorial position, and weaken Iran's capacity to pursue the war. During the middle years of the conflict, neither government achieved the decisive political settlement it wanted. The capture of al-Faw in 1986 was a significant Iranian success, yet it did not produce the fall of Baghdad or compel the Iraqi government to accept Tehran's conditions. The campaigns of 1987 likewise demonstrated the ability to continue fighting without establishing the political result for which the fighting was undertaken. Iraqi forces recovered the peninsula in April 1988, and their broader reversals of earlier Iranian gains helped change the balance of pressure on Tehran. These episodes are useful precisely because military success and political success did not consistently coincide.

Attrition must also be understood as the progressive consumption of institutional capacity. Losses among experienced officers, medical workers, transport personnel, and industrial specialists could be harder to replace than a numerical count of newly enrolled people suggested. A country could continue to call up large numbers of men while becoming less able to maintain essential services or replace lost expertise. Repeated periods of emergency made it more difficult to preserve training programs, plan industrial investment, or stabilize family life. Both governments faced these problems, but their access to credit, trade, and external assistance differed. The military balance in 1988 therefore reflected an accumulation of conditions rather than the consequence of one battlefield defeat.

Definition: A strategy or condition of war in which each side seeks to wear down the other's personnel, equipment, and economy through sustained losses.

Historical context: In the Iran–Iraq War the term describes the long phase from 1983 to 1988, when frontal offensives and defensive fortifications exchanged heavy losses without decisive territorial change.

Military Institutions and National Mobilization

How the Islamic Republic organized people, institutions, and ideas for an eight-year war.

Section 97.5

The Regular Armed Forces and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps

The Islamic Republic fought the war with two principal military establishments of different origins. The Artesh was the army, navy, and air force inherited from the monarchy. Its officers were professionally trained, much of its equipment was American, and its institutional culture was formed under the Pahlavi state. After the revolution it was purged, reduced, and placed under political suspicion, yet it remained the repository of most technical military expertise and, in 1980, of most of the country's heavy weapons, aircraft, and armor. The Revolutionary Guards, by contrast, were created in 1979 by revolutionary leaders who distrusted the old army and wanted a force committed to the revolution. They absorbed revolutionary militias and volunteers, and during the war they developed their own ground, naval, and air components and large logistical and engineering structures.

The relationship between the two was neither simple rivalry nor seamless cooperation. In 1980 and 1981 the divide was acute, intensified by the quarrel between Bani-Sadr and his opponents. As the war went on, joint commands, shared planning, and combined operations became normal, particularly for the large offensives of 1982 onward. Artesh officers such as Ali Sayad Shirazi acquired senior command responsibilities, while Guard commanders, among them Mohsen Rezaei, gained influence over strategy. The Guards' wartime growth was reflected in institutional change, including the creation of a separate ministry for the Guards in 1982 and, later, the establishment of Guard air and naval branches. Scholars who study the Guards' own accounts of the war, such as Annie Tracy Samuel, point out that the institution later emphasized its centrality in the war, so Guard memoirs and publications must be read as both sources and constructions.

Individual commanders illustrate the variety of paths. Mostafa Chamran, a former defense minister, had organized irregular forces before his death in 1981. Mohammad Jahanara, associated with the defense of Khorramshahr, became a founding figure in the Guards' remembrance of the first months of the war. Sayad Shirazi represented the regular officer corps that remained loyal to the Republic and was recruited into its senior ranks. Their differing backgrounds correspond to a real diversity of military cultures, and the war's story cannot be told through any one of them.

A common error is to portray the Guards as the sole defender of Iran or the Artesh as irrelevant. Regular air, armor, artillery, naval, and engineering units were involved in nearly all the major operations and bore a large share of the burden of defending the frontier, especially in the early stage. Equally, the Guards' capacity for rapid recruitment and political mobilization was indispensable to the offensives of 1982–1987. The institutional consequences of this partnership were lasting. The Guards emerged from the war as a larger, better-resourced, and politically more significant institution, a development whose economic and political aspects are examined in Chapter 105. Command structures that placed political leaders in decisive roles also set precedents for the relationship between the military and the clerical leadership.

One consequence of the war was the creation of parallel institutional systems with overlapping responsibilities. The army possessed established personnel administration, technical schools, and a national organizational identity inherited from the pre-revolutionary period. The Guards developed their own personnel networks, systems of political education, administrative arrangements, and institutional records. Their establishment as a distinct organization was recognized by Article 150 of the Constitution, but this provision did not by itself define the practical relationship between the two military establishments. The 1982 development of the Guards' organizational and ministerial arrangements marked a further stage in their institutionalization. In 1985 the establishment of separate Guard ground, naval, and air components gave the organization a broader permanent structure. Neither the legal establishment of the organization nor the creation of a branch should be confused with the date at which all of its functions became operational.

These institutional changes were important to the postwar political system because organizations accumulate expertise and relationships as well as personnel. A wartime commander may return to civilian employment, become a public official, or continue in military service; the relationships among such people can influence later appointments and policy debates. The war also generated written records, commemorative institutions, and systems of welfare for former members. A military organization consequently became capable of influencing how society understood the war as well as how it was fought. Samuel's research on the Guards' internal histories is especially relevant to this point. It demonstrates why an institutional history must ask who produced the records and what disputes they were intended to resolve. The Artesh, too, retained its own history and professional identity. The lasting Iranian defense establishment emerged from these overlapping institutional traditions, not from the simple replacement of one by the other.

Section 97.6

Conscription, the Basij, and Volunteer Mobilization

The Iranian war effort drew on several distinct streams of personnel, and it is misleading to describe the whole as volunteer. Professional soldiers and officers formed the core of the Artesh. Conscripts, young men subject to compulsory service, filled the regular forces and later the Guards, and the formal obligation was enforced through registration and administrative pressure. Guard members were a mixture of career personnel and volunteers. The Basij, envisioned in November 1979, legally established on 10 July 1980 and incorporated into the Guards in January 1981, recruited volunteers from towns and villages, often through mosques, schools, workplaces, and neighborhood committees, and sent them to the front for fixed periods, commonly of a few months, before rotating them out.

The proportions among these groups changed over time and varied by region, and no fully reliable aggregate figures exist. Iranian officials and scholars have cited very large totals of Basij participants over the war's duration, but such numbers count different things: enlistments, rotations, registrations, and actual deployments. Students should treat them as indications of scale rather than precise counts. Participation was heaviest in the first years, when enthusiasm for defense was high, and declined as casualties mounted and as the war's character changed after 1982. By the late 1980s, authorities had to rely more on conscription, organized recruitment campaigns, and social pressure.

Motivations overlapped. Many Iranians, including those with little sympathy for the clerical leadership, fought for the defense of the homeland. Some were moved by religious commitment, and for them the language of martyrdom and the example of Karbala was central. Others served because they were required to, because the community expected it, because family members were already at the front, or because the Basij offered social status, material support, or educational opportunities. Social pressure operated through the neighborhood, the school, and the workplace, and in some cases non-participation carried a cost. These categories were not mutually exclusive, and the same person's reasons could change over time.

The participation of minors is a documented but difficult subject. There is evidence from Iranian sources, journalists, and international observers that boys and teenagers under eighteen, and in some cases under sixteen, took part in Basij deployments and fighting, and Iranian wartime propaganda sometimes celebrated young volunteers. The extent is not reliably known. Regulations and practice about minimum age appear to have varied and to have been unevenly enforced. Popular accounts of mass use of children, and in particular the often-repeated story that boys were given plastic keys to paradise, are poorly documented and should not be treated as established. A careful historian evaluates the matter against the legal standards of the time. The 1989 Convention on the Rights of the Child came after the war, but the 1977 Additional Protocol I articulated protections for children under fifteen, and international criticism of the practice was already present in the 1980s.

Experience of mobilization differed by region and class. Residents of border provinces and of provincial towns with strong religious networks contributed disproportionately in some periods, while educated urban families sometimes found ways to defer or avoid service. Ethnic and religious minorities participated, though their role is often neglected in official narratives. In Kurdistan, where local insurgency complicated the situation, mobilization took different forms. The effect of loss on communities was significant: some villages and neighborhoods lost many young men, and the loss reshaped local life for decades.

The legal history of children's participation deserves careful treatment. Article 77 of Additional Protocol I to the 1949 Geneva Conventions, adopted in 1977, articulated special protections for children and a standard concerning the direct participation and recruitment of those under fifteen. The ICRC's treaty-status database records that Iraq did not accede to the Protocol until 2010; Iran signed it in 1977 but has not ratified it. The specific treaty obligation therefore cannot simply be projected backward onto both states during the 1980s. The customary rules and the protective norms reflected in the Protocol, as well as the treatment of captured and wounded children under other applicable humanitarian rules, are separate questions. The Convention on the Rights of the Child was adopted in 1989, after the fighting ended. Distinguishing the dates does not diminish the humanitarian concern about child recruitment; it makes the historical and legal assessment more accurate.

Section 97.7

Martyrdom, Karbala, Patriotism, and Revolutionary Ideology

Mobilization was sustained by a language of meaning as well as by organization. For Twelver Shi'is, the martyrdom of Imam Husayn at Karbala in 680 is the central narrative of sacrifice in the face of injustice, commemorated annually in the month of Muharram through sermons, processions, and passion plays. Long before 1980 this tradition had served as a source of political and moral imagery, including in the revolution of 1978–1979 (Chapter 91). During the war, leaders, preachers, and propagandists drew the parallel directly: Iran's soldiers were represented as followers of Husayn, the enemy as an unjust tyrant, and death in battle as martyrdom (shahadat) with spiritual reward. Funerals, memorial gatherings, and the annual calendar of commemorations drew on this imagery, and the families of the dead were honored by the state and community.

The phrase Sacred Defense (Defa'-e Moqaddas) came to designate the war in official Iranian usage. It joins two ideas, the defense of the homeland and the religious significance of sacrifice, and its emphasis on defense reflects the legitimacy that the response to Iraq's invasion carried at home and abroad. The term is not neutral. It presents the whole eight years, including the offensives inside Iraq after 1982, under the heading of defense, and it carries the weight of a political and religious interpretation. This chapter uses it as the name of a historical concept and a commemorative category, not as a description that settles contested questions about the war's later phase.

It would be a mistake to treat the ideology as the whole motivation. The war also called upon Iranian nationalism, an older sense of belonging to a historical homeland that predated the revolution and that was invoked by monarchists, leftists, and liberals as well as by Islamists. Many who disliked the new government fought or supported the war because the invader was a foreign power. Alongside the Shi'i majority, members of other communities took part. Iranian Armenians, Assyrians, Zoroastrians, Jews, and Sunni Muslims served and died, and recent scholarship has examined how their sacrifices were commemorated, and sometimes marginalized, in the dominant narrative. The Constitution had given recognized religious minorities representation, and the war was one arena in which their civic belonging was asserted and tested.

Official rhetoric also served to legitimize the political order. By presenting war service as devotion to the revolution, the leadership linked the revolution's survival to the defense of the nation and gave moral weight to the demand for unity. Critics of the government were sometimes portrayed as obstructing the war effort, a framing that raised the cost of dissent. At the same time, ordinary participants did not always accept official interpretations; letters, memoirs, and later oral histories record doubt, weariness, and anger alongside conviction. The ideology of martyrdom was therefore both a real source of motivation and a tool of state power, and these two aspects cannot be separated cleanly.

The contrast between the defensive phase of 1980–1982 and the subsequent years helps illuminate the changing political use of the term Sacred Defense. In the first phase, the defense of territory against foreign invasion was a readily identifiable objective shared by people of different political orientations. After the decision to continue the war inside Iraq, the application of the same commemorative language to the whole conflict made a more expansive claim about the meaning of defense. It could present military pressure beyond the border as necessary for the security of the revolution and the country. Historians should not assume that the original defensive consensus automatically extended to the continuation of the war. Nor should they assume that all forms of religious mobilization were insincere. The published work of Annie Tracy Samuel and the collection edited by Narges Bajoghli and Amir Moosavi offer distinct entry points into the study of these enduring debates. The first concerns the institutional writing of war history; the second brings together inquiries into how the war is discussed and remembered in contemporary Iran. The distinction between personal conviction and public representation is fundamental to both subjects.

Section 97.8

Wartime Governance, Political Leadership, and Revolutionary Institutions

The war reshaped the Islamic Republic's government. Ayatollah Khomeini, as supreme leader and commander of the armed forces under the constitution, held final authority on strategic questions, including whether to continue or end the war, and his speeches set the moral tone for the national effort. Day-to-day management involved a circle of leaders. Ali Khamenei, as president from October 1981, gave public and administrative leadership and had a role in the war councils. Mir-Hossein Mousavi, as prime minister, directed the government's economic and administrative response, including the allocation of scarce resources. Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, as Speaker of the Majles and an influential figure on the Supreme Defense Council, became a central coordinator between the political leadership and the military commanders, and his authority grew as the war continued.

Revolutionary organizations multiplied tasks. Reconstruction Jihad, created in 1979 to develop villages, became an extensive wartime organization that built roads, bridges, and fortifications, supplied the front, and later contributed to the reconstruction of damaged towns. The Martyrs Foundation cared for families of the dead, providing pensions, housing assistance, and social status, and served as a commemorative institution. Other bodies, including relief committees and agencies for disabled veterans, handled welfare. These organizations blurred the line between government and revolutionary movement, and their wartime growth helped create a parallel structure of patronage and loyalty that outlasted the war.

The relationship among government, Majles, and military organizations was not harmonious. Debates over the budget, the priority of the front, and the treatment of the economy recurred. The Majles contained factions with different views about state intervention in the economy, land reform, and trade, and Mousavi's government, which favored a strong state role in distributing goods, faced criticism from more market-oriented conservatives. The Ministry of the Revolutionary Guards and the Ministry of Defense competed for resources and, at times, for influence over strategy. These disagreements show that the war did not abolish politics; it changed the terms on which political conflict took place.

The tension between unity and repression was a defining feature. The war was invoked to justify restrictions on opposition, and the political violence of the early 1980s, treated in Chapter 95, ran alongside the war effort. The leadership sought national unity while excluding those regarded as enemies of the revolution, and the line between criticizing a policy and obstructing the war was drawn by those in power. Yet it would be too simple to say that wartime governance was merely repressive. The state also delivered services, organized relief, and kept an ordinary administration functioning under severe strain, and for many citizens the war era state was a source of assistance as well as control.

The constitutional framework matters because the government of the war was not identical to its military command. The original Constitution vested important military powers in the Leader and provided for a Supreme Defense Council. The presidency, prime minister's office, ministries, and parliament nevertheless possessed different responsibilities for executive administration, public expenditure, and legislation. The political leadership could approve a broad policy while the armed institutions and civilian departments had to implement it. A national decision to sustain the war thus became a series of separate administrative obligations: maintaining employment records, approving appropriations, procuring essential imports, arranging medical provision, and supporting affected communities. The increasing role of Rafsanjani in coordination reflected the difficulty of integrating these responsibilities. In the final period of the war he was appointed deputy commander-in-chief, a development that illustrates the increasing effort to coordinate political and military authority; its relationship to the ceasefire decision belongs to the next chapter.

Welfare organizations were also part of the administrative history of the conflict. The revolutionary foundations discussed in Chapter 95 were not simply government ministries under another name. They possessed distinct administrative arrangements and resources, and their operations were not always visible in conventional central-government budget figures. The recognition of a family as eligible for wartime assistance involved records, institutional judgments, and the application of benefit rules. This means that the delivery of assistance was also a process through which the state classified citizens and established enduring relationships with them. Reconstruction Jihad, likewise, combined a revolutionary origin with increasingly permanent responsibilities. Its wartime activities linked rural development to the requirements of national defense, and its organization as a ministry in 1983 illustrated the broader movement from voluntary revolutionary bodies toward more formal administrative structures. These developments did not eliminate bureaucratic overlap. They show how the Iranian state acquired new capabilities while preserving a complex set of parallel institutions, some accountable through different channels. The Wartime Economy and National Administration

How a petroleum-dependent revolutionary state paid for, supplied, and adapted to prolonged war.

Section 97.9

Financing the War: Government Revenue, Expenditure, and Oil Dependence

Petroleum income was indispensable to the Islamic Republic’s ability to finance the war, particularly its imports, alongside domestic taxation and financing. Oil export revenue was the principal source of foreign exchange and a very large share of government income, so the state's capacity to buy weapons, import food and industrial inputs, pay salaries, and subsidize consumption depended on how much oil Iran could produce, export, and sell, and at what price. Oil revenues partly recovered after the initial disruption, but financing remained difficult, especially after the 1986 oil-price shock. The world oil price, which had been at historically high levels around 1980, fell steeply through the mid-1980s and collapsed in 1986, cutting Iran's export revenue sharply even where export volumes held up.

Expenditure rose on several fronts. Military spending covered personnel, ammunition, vehicles, aircraft, and spare parts. Civilian expenditure included the wages of a swollen public sector, subsidies on basic goods, relief for the displaced, welfare for the families of the dead and wounded, and reconstruction. Governments rarely separated these streams cleanly, and published budgets understate off-budget spending by revolutionary foundations and by military bodies. Deficits were met by drawing down foreign-exchange reserves, by borrowing from the domestic banking system, and by monetary financing, which fed inflation. Iran also had limited access to foreign credit, partly because of political isolation and partly because of its own policy preference for self-reliance and avoidance of foreign debt.

Distinguishing causes is essential. The economy that entered the war had already been disrupted by the revolution: capital flight, the departure of managers and technicians, the nationalization of major enterprises, labor unrest, and the interruption of oil production. Wartime damage, the diversion of resources to the military, and attacks on infrastructure then compounded these problems. International restrictions on arms and trade, notably American measures, complicated procurement and finance. The oil-price collapse of 1986 was a separate shock, not attributable to the war itself, though the war's effect on Gulf oil markets and on Iran's own exports interacted with it. Economists who study the period disagree about how much of the decline in living standards and growth should be attributed to each factor, and the evidence does not permit a clean decomposition. Students should resist the shortcut of attributing every wartime difficulty to the war alone.

Government policy responses combined state control with improvisation. Under the Mousavi government, the state expanded its role in trade and distribution, maintained multiple exchange rates, and controlled many prices. These measures protected the purchasing power of poorer households and ensured the supply of staples, but they also created distortions, black markets, and incentives for smuggling and rent-seeking. The balance between equity and efficiency became a recurring political controversy, with some leaders favoring greater state intervention and others favoring private commerce. This debate carried into the postwar reconstruction period discussed in Chapter 99.

Adnan Mazarei's account of Iranian fiscal history in Encyclopaedia Iranica supplies a consistent set of central-government indicators. The deficit reached approximately fifteen percent of gross domestic product in the Iranian fiscal year beginning in 1980. It narrowed during the following years, then rose to around nine percent in 1986 as oil prices fell. The average deficit for the 1979–1988 period was eight percent of GDP, against two percent for 1973–1978. Average government revenue also contracted sharply relative to national output, from thirty-eight percent of GDP in the earlier period to twenty percent in the later one. These comparisons describe broad fiscal pressures, not an estimate of military spending alone. The earlier period was characterized by exceptionally favorable oil revenues, while the later period includes the revolution as well as the war. The underlying accounts are central-government accounts and do not encompass the whole economy of revolutionary foundations or every extra-budgetary activity.

To understand the practical implications, consider the different forms of scarcity confronting the government. A budget can be financed in domestic currency, but a foreign supplier generally requires payment in an accepted international currency. Additional rial balances therefore cannot directly resolve a shortage of foreign exchange. When export earnings declined, ministries competed for access to foreign currency, and the government had to allocate imports among military, industrial, and household uses. A favorable official conversion rate could make an imported good appear less expensive in the budget without increasing the amount of foreign currency available to import it. Multiple rates also meant that the same dollar receipt or liability could have different recorded rial values depending on the administrative category involved. Comparing a nominal dollar export series with nominal rial expenditures without accounting for these arrangements would produce misleading results.

Another response was to reduce or postpone capital expenditure. Maintaining a payroll or providing food relief addressed an immediate need; constructing a new factory, renewing infrastructure, or expanding a public facility could often be delayed. But the resources saved in the short term had a later cost in deteriorating capital stock and deferred services. A wartime economy can therefore display institutional resilience in the present while accumulating reconstruction requirements for the future. A reconstruction program after 1988 had to address both direct physical destruction and investment that had not occurred during the conflict. This distinction provides the economic bridge from the war to Chapter 99. The fiscal indicators in Feature 9 should be interpreted alongside the oil production figures in Feature 10 rather than as a complete account of the wartime economy.

The World Bank consumer-price series reproduced in Feature 9 records a particularly sharp rise in the rate of inflation after 1985, reaching about 28.6 percent in 1987 and 28.7 percent in 1988. A lower rate in 1985 did not mean prices had returned to their earlier levels: a positive annual rate indicates that the measured price level continued to increase. The effect on a particular household also depended on wage changes, access to controlled-price goods, and the composition of its purchases. The series therefore permits a comparison across years but should not be equated with an individual family’s experience of shortages.

War economy

Definition: An economy whose allocation of money, labor, materials, and foreign exchange is shaped by military requirements and by the scarcity those requirements create.

Section 97.10

Petroleum, Industrial Production, and Wartime Disruption

Petroleum was at once Iran's lifeline and its greatest vulnerability. Production, refining, and export depended on facilities concentrated in Khuzestan and on the Gulf coast, within reach of Iraqi forces. The Abadan refinery, one of the largest in the world, was shut by the early fighting and badly damaged, and the Iranian oil system had to adapt by relying on other refineries, importing refined products, and rebuilding capacity. Petrochemical complexes and other industrial plants in the south, including the large complex at Bandar Imam Khomeini, were struck or disrupted. Kharg Island, the principal offshore crude export terminal, was attacked repeatedly by Iraqi aircraft from the mid-1980s, and Iran developed alternative loading arrangements, including shuttle shipping and the use of Lavan and Sirri Island terminals, to maintain exports.

Quantitative evidence illustrates both the disruption and the recovery. Production fell from several million barrels a day in the late 1970s to well under two million in 1980 and 1981, then recovered to around two to two and a half million barrels a day for most of the rest of the war. That recovery, achieved despite attacks, reflects substantial repair and adaptation. It also shows why distinguishing production, exports, export revenue, and refinery capacity matters: a country can pump oil yet be unable to refine it for domestic use or to sell it at a good price.

Beyond oil, the war disrupted industry through damage, the loss of imported machinery and spare parts, shortages of foreign exchange, labor shortages caused by mobilization and displacement, and interruptions of electricity and transport. Many large industrial firms had been nationalized after the revolution and were run by state-appointed managers, a condition that compounded disruption. Industrial output generally stagnated or fell relative to the prerevolutionary trend, though production for the war and essential goods received priority. At the same time, the war encouraged domestic adaptation. Iranian engineers and workshops learned to repair, copy, and substitute for imported parts, and military industries expanded, a pattern that would have long-term consequences for the country's industrial structure.

Petroleum thus played three roles at once: public revenue, industrial and household fuel, and strategic vulnerability. It financed the war, it kept factories and vehicles running, and it exposed the economy to attack and to the vagaries of the world market. Because of this, the Tanker War of 1984–1988, treated in Section 97.17, was not a peripheral episode but a direct assault on the economic foundations of the Iranian war effort, and a threat in turn to the oil exports of Iran's adversaries and their supporters.

The numerical production record illustrates the difference between disruption and sustained economic capacity. The Energy Information Administration's historical compilation, published in April 1998, reports an Iranian annual crude oil average of 1.380 million barrels per day in 1981 and 2.440 million in 1983. These figures are measured on the same basis, excluding condensate. Their difference shows that Iran was able to restore a significant part of production after the initial shock, despite the continuing conflict. The same series reports 2.035 million barrels per day for 1986 and 2.240 million for 1988. In other words, the decline and subsequent partial recovery of production did not follow an uninterrupted downward course. The series cannot independently establish how much petroleum was consumed domestically, how much was exported, or how much foreign exchange the state earned. Those questions require separate evidence on the volume and value of trade.

Industrial history has a similar measurement problem. The capacity of a refinery is not the amount of crude oil actually processed; an installed plant may be idle or function intermittently. Industrial output is also different from the value of investment. A government that directs resources toward repairing machinery can temporarily preserve output without adding new productive capacity. Conversely, a newly constructed facility may take time to enter production. The war's industrial legacy should therefore be assessed through several kinds of evidence: production records, capital expenditure, employment, the availability of intermediate goods, and accounts of maintenance and repair. The disruption of oil refining and petrochemical production had consequences beyond the southern provinces because these industries were connected to national transport and manufacturing. Shortages or delays in one sector could raise costs elsewhere, even in districts not directly affected by military action.

A study of the postrevolutionary economy in Encyclopaedia Iranica reports that government-controlled enterprises generated 73.5 percent of value added in manufacturing establishments with ten or more employees in 1984. This is a measure of the composition of manufacturing, not its total production. The same article reports oil revenues of about six billion US dollars in 1986. Together these observations illuminate the dependence of publicly managed industry on a strained export economy, but they do not establish a single causal relationship between ownership and productivity.

Section 97.11

Agriculture, Food Security, Rationing, and Household Consumption

The war affected agriculture unevenly. In the border provinces the effects were severe: farmland was mined or occupied, irrigation works were damaged, villagers fled, and in Khuzestan, a major agricultural region, production fell sharply in affected areas. Elsewhere, the picture was more mixed. The government gave priority to food self-sufficiency as both a wartime necessity and a revolutionary ideal, offering price supports, credit, fertilizer and seed distribution, and technical help, and Reconstruction Jihad extended services to villages. Some sectors, including wheat and certain other crops, benefited from these policies and recorded gains in production in parts of the 1980s. Others stagnated. Agriculture should therefore not be described as having declined uniformly; it varied by region, crop, and the degree of state support.

Even so, population growth outpaced production for many goods, and Iran remained a major importer of food, particularly wheat, edible oils, and meat products. Imports required foreign exchange, which the war made scarce, and the government managed them through state trading agencies. When oil revenue fell in 1986, the pressure on food imports and on the rial intensified.

The household experience of rationing was among the most widespread civilian memories of the war. The government introduced coupons for basic goods, among them rice, cooking oil, sugar, meat, dairy products, and later other items, distributed through neighborhood cooperatives and designated shops at controlled prices. Queues were common, shortages occurred, and an informal market charged much higher prices for the same goods. Subsidized prices protected poorer families but also created incentives for hoarding and resale. Households adapted by pooling resources, relying on relatives in villages, growing food at home, and trading coupons. Those with connections to cooperatives, workplaces, or revolutionary institutions often had better access than those without.

The war also altered patterns of consumption and expectations. Wartime austerity was presented in official rhetoric as a form of sacrifice, with households urged to economize and to donate to the front. Collections of money, gold, and goods for the war effort were organized through mosques and committees. Whether donations were fully voluntary varied by context. Meanwhile the middle class faced inflation that eroded salaries, and public employees, whose wages were fixed, often took second jobs. The distribution of burdens was unequal, and wartime inequality is part of the history that official narratives of shared sacrifice can obscure.

The revolution's rural policies formed part of the background to wartime agriculture. The debate over land reform, property rights, and state assistance preceded the Iraqi invasion and continued alongside it. Reconstruction Jihad worked on rural infrastructure and service delivery, and government agencies attempted to expand access to agricultural credit and inputs. Yet a policy that improves the availability of one input does not necessarily improve the profitability of every crop. Official purchasing prices, transport costs, water availability, and the supply of fertilizer or machinery all mattered. Producers also responded to the relative attractiveness of different crops and to growing urban demand for agricultural goods. Regional differences were pronounced: a cultivator in an area exposed to displacement or damage faced conditions quite different from those of a farmer in a province able to maintain normal access to markets.

Encyclopaedia Iranica's article on farming reports an estimated average agricultural growth rate of 4.1 percent for 1979–1987. This is an aggregate estimate cited from an Iranian agricultural planning source, rather than a series measuring each crop or province. It does not mean that food insecurity disappeared or that production rose in every area. In particular, an increase in aggregate agricultural output could coexist with population growth, disrupted distribution, or continued demand for imported staples. The article also describes the different incentives associated with controlled cereal prices and stronger demand for other agricultural commodities. That comparison helps correct the assumption that government intervention generated uniform results across agriculture. A careful account should distinguish growth in agricultural output from the changing share of agriculture in GDP; the latter could rise because other sectors performed poorly.

Rationing shifted a portion of the problem of allocating food from ordinary market transactions to public administration. Eligibility, coupon availability, delivery arrangements, and the location of distribution outlets became important features of household welfare. The official price of an allotted product was only one part of the cost of obtaining it. Time spent waiting, travel to a shop, the need to make another purchase when official supplies ran out, and the opportunity cost of unpaid household labor were not adequately captured by the posted price. These burdens could fall disproportionately on people who were responsible for provisioning a family, especially women. For an employed household, obtaining a ration could conflict with working hours; a displaced family might lack the neighborhood connections that helped more established residents locate goods.

Section 97.12

International Procurement, Economic Isolation, and Domestic Adaptation

Obtaining weapons and industrial supplies was a constant problem for both belligerents, but the conditions differed sharply. Iran had inherited large quantities of American-made equipment, including aircraft, armor, and naval vessels, and it lost the supply of parts and support when relations with Washington broke down. The United States, along with several allies, discouraged arms sales to Iran through an informal campaign, and Iran's revolutionary politics made many governments wary. Iran therefore turned to a variety of suppliers, including some in Asia and the Middle East and, through intermediaries and covert channels, others whose public positions were hostile. The most notorious example, the clandestine American arms sales of 1985–1986 revealed as the Iran–Contra affair, showed how public foreign-policy positions and covert transactions could diverge, and it is treated in Section 97.17.

Iraq had a much easier path. Its government obtained large quantities of weapons from the Soviet Union, France, and other suppliers, and it received financial support from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and other Gulf states, in loans and grants whose total is estimated in the tens of billions of dollars, though estimates differ. Iraq could also obtain technology and materials with civilian and military uses from firms in several countries. The difference between the two states' access to credit and supply is one of the more important structural facts of the war. It does not alone explain the war's outcome, since Iran recovered its territory and sustained eight years of fighting, but it shaped the balance of resources over time.

Iranian adaptation took several forms. The government expanded domestic military production and maintenance, using the technical skills of the Artesh, universities, and workshops. It diversified suppliers, using barter and special trading arrangements to avoid foreign-exchange constraints. It sought to economize by conserving ammunition and relying more on manpower. At the same time, procurement consumed a large share of scarce foreign exchange and limited investment in civilian development. The war encouraged domestic industrial capacity in military and related sectors, even as it constrained broader investment, leaving a mixed legacy of improved self-reliance and deferred development.

The Iraqi economy also experienced a severe fiscal and external shock. A study of Iraqi investment policies published by the International Monetary Fund reports that oil revenue declined from slightly above twenty-six billion US dollars in 1980 to 9.7 billion in 1983 and seven billion in 1986. The figures are nominal dollar amounts, not measures of real purchasing power or the Iraqi defense budget. They demonstrate the scale of the reduction in earnings from the country's central export industry. Closure of the Syrian pipeline, disruption of Gulf exports, and changes in international prices all contributed to the difficult financial environment. The contrast with Iran was therefore not between a country with ample petroleum income and another with none. Both faced major constraints, and the availability of external financing and political support to Iraq was particularly important because its own earnings had fallen.

External credit transformed the temporal distribution of costs. A government receiving a loan could continue expenditure in the present while creating a future liability, whereas a government unable or unwilling to rely on foreign debt had to seek other financing or reduce imports. The financial arrangements of Arab Gulf governments with Baghdad were not all identical and are not reducible to one agreed total. Some support was described as loans, other transfers as grants or political assistance; later disputes over repayment showed that these distinctions mattered. Iraq's capacity to obtain assistance was an important condition of its war effort, but it did not abolish the consequences of declining oil revenues, rising debt, or postponed investment. Those financial burdens shaped regional relations after 1988.

For Iran, the costs of isolation and the disruption of trade included industrial expenditures that did not immediately improve civilian production. Maintaining older equipment and continuing essential industrial operations required technical personnel and capital that could otherwise have been employed elsewhere. The experience encouraged some forms of domestic production and institutional self-reliance. It also imposed substantial opportunity costs. For economic history, it is especially important not to assume that a successful example of domestic manufacture proves a broad improvement in national productivity. The relevant question is whether wartime adaptation made particular institutions more durable, and what other projects were delayed in order to achieve that result. An account of the postwar reconstruction must start from both kinds of inheritance: accumulated domestic competence and a stock of unmet civilian investment needs. International trade and finance were thus not background issues but central determinants of the development possibilities of both societies.

Section 97.13

Borderland Communities, Urban Destruction, and Forced Displacement

The war's earliest and most sustained social effects fell on the provinces nearest the frontier. In Khuzestan, the fighting around Khorramshahr, Abadan, Susangerd, Dezful, and Ahvaz forced large numbers of residents to flee. In Ilam and Kermanshah, border towns such as Mehran, Sar-e Pol-e Zahab, and Qasr-e Shirin were shelled, occupied, or contested. In Kurdistan, the war overlapped with a separate armed conflict between Iranian forces and Kurdish political parties, and populations were displaced by both. Many villages were abandoned, farmland was mined, and several towns were largely destroyed. The scale of destruction was uneven, with some areas experiencing repeated attack and others remaining relatively unaffected.

Estimates of internal displacement vary widely and depend on definitions and timing. Figures ranging in the secondary literature are not necessarily comparable; no national count is asserted here, and the number of people displaced at any one time differed from the total who were displaced at some point. These people were internally displaced persons, citizens who had left their homes without crossing an international border, and they differ in legal and practical terms from the smaller number of Iranians who left the country. Iran also hosted large populations of refugees from Afghanistan and Iraq during these years, a related but separate story.

Displaced people moved to relatives' homes, to neighboring cities, and to tents, schools, mosques, and public buildings that served as temporary shelters. Government and revolutionary organizations, including relief committees and the Housing Foundation, provided emergency housing, food, and assistance, though capacity was limited, and many families relied on informal networks. The loss of homes and livelihoods affected employment, as farmers and shopkeepers lost their means of support, and it disrupted schooling, as children changed schools or lost months of instruction. Families were sometimes separated, with men at the front or in work elsewhere. For Arabic-speaking residents of Khuzestan and for Kurdish communities in the west, displacement was bound up with questions of loyalty, security, and identity that governments and local communities interpreted differently.

The consequences were long-lasting. Cities that had been major commercial centers, Khorramshahr above all, took years to rebuild, and some residents never returned. Rural depopulation changed local economies, and displaced families who settled in other cities contributed to urban growth. The unequal geography of suffering, concentrated in borderlands and in particular provinces, left a legacy of regional inequality and of memory that varied sharply between communities directly affected and those largely spared. In later chapters, the reconstruction of the war zones and the treatment of the border provinces form part of the story of the Islamic Republic's postwar development.

Khorramshahr provides a particularly useful demographic case study because the impact of the war can be studied through observations before and after the conflict. Census figures reproduced in Encyclopaedia Iranica indicate 140,490 inhabitants in 1976, 34,750 in 1991, and 105,636 in 1996. The historical series does not report a population count for 1986, when the city was devastated and abandoned; the missing entry is not a recorded population of zero. The figures for 1991 and 1996 are observations made after the ceasefire, and they demonstrate that the restoration of the population was a prolonged process. They cannot establish that every difference between the prewar and postwar counts represents a person killed or an individual permanently displaced. Some former inhabitants moved elsewhere, others returned, and ordinary demographic change continued. Nor can a single city be treated as representative of the entire country. Nevertheless, this evidence provides a measurable example of the spatial concentration of the war's costs.

The consequences of displacement depended in part on the conditions in which people were received. A household that moved into a relative's home might receive immediate support without appearing in the records of a formal shelter. A family dependent on an assistance program might be counted repeatedly if it moved between temporary accommodations. The difference between the number of displaced people at a particular time and the cumulative number displaced during the war is therefore substantial. A third measure, the number of people unable to return after a ceasefire, concerns the long-term effects of the conflict rather than its immediate scale. These distinctions help explain why estimates in secondary literature vary and why a historical narrative should not combine them into one national total without examining definitions.

Section 97.14

Women, Families, Children, and Everyday Life During the War

The war changed the work and expectations of Iranian households, and women were central participants rather than merely supporters of male combatants. Women served as nurses, physicians, and auxiliary medical staff in hospitals near the front and in cities that received the wounded, and many volunteered with relief organizations, sewing clothing, preparing food, collecting donations, and caring for displaced families. In some areas women took part in local civil defense and in Basij women's units, which trained women in first aid, communications, and, in some cases, basic defense. Their participation was shaped by the gender norms of the Islamic Republic, which both limited women's access to combat roles and, paradoxically, mobilized women's public activity in the name of national and religious duty.

Households also bore the economic burden of the war. When fathers, sons, or husbands were at the front, killed, or disabled, women often became the main earners and managers of family resources, balancing work, childcare, and the demands of queues and scarcity. Widows of the dead received pensions and benefits from the Martyrs Foundation and other institutions, along with social recognition as families of martyrs, but also faced social expectations about mourning, remarriage, and public conduct. Mothers of the dead were honored in official ceremonies, and the language of maternal sacrifice became a theme of wartime culture. Published memoirs and oral histories, along with scholarship on Iranian women in the war, show that many women embraced these roles with pride, while others felt grief, resentment, or constraint.

Children experienced the war through displacement, air-raid alarms, school disruption, and the loss of relatives. Schools were used for propaganda and for commemoration, with students writing letters to soldiers, attending funerals, and learning the language of martyrdom. Classrooms in towns near the front were damaged or closed. The effect on education varied: some children lost months or years of instruction, others experienced continuity. (The closure of universities during the Cultural Revolution of 1980–1983 was a separate political event and should not be attributed to the war.) The long absence of fathers and the loss of relatives shaped a generation whose childhood memories of the war later became part of Iranian literature and cinema.

A persistent gap separated official representations from private experience. Official culture honored sacrifice, and it presented bereavement as a source of pride. Many families, while accepting this, also described exhaustion, anxiety, and sorrow that did not fit the heroic frame. Veterans' families, particularly those of the severely disabled, faced long-term care burdens whose costs were borne largely by women. Historians of the period rely on diaries, letters, oral history collections, and later memoirs, recognizing that each reflects the voice of those who chose or were able to speak.

A concrete point of entry into women's wartime history is the published memoir of Seyyedeh Zahra Hoseyni, known in English as One Woman's War: Da (Mother). The Library of Congress catalog identifies the work as her memoir and provides a bibliographic record for the English edition. The account concerns the war's impact in Khorramshahr and offers a way of approaching the conflict through a woman's experience rather than through the reports of military institutions. It is not necessary to reproduce unverified quotations or claim details from pages that have not been examined. Even at the level of source identification, the work raises significant historical questions: whose experiences were selected for preservation, how oral recollection was edited into a published narrative, how translation shaped its accessibility to foreign readers, and how its publication related to the institutions of Sacred Defense literature. Such questions do not make a memoir unreliable by definition. They establish the method by which it should be compared with other evidence.

Mateo Mohammad Farzaneh's Iranian Women and Gender in the Iran-Iraq War provides a complementary scholarly approach. Its subject is women's participation and changing gender roles rather than the single biography of a particular participant. The publisher's description identifies the study as an examination of the varied roles women played in the war and in the Islamic Republic's gender system. The relationship between the two works illustrates a useful distinction between a memoir and a scholarly synthesis. An individual testimony can illuminate experiences that administrative statistics cannot capture; a synthesis can compare experiences across different circumstances. Neither should be substituted for the other. A historian studying wartime work should ask whether the activity was paid, unpaid, formally organized, or undertaken within a family, because these distinctions determine whether it is visible in employment records. Women's contributions to relief, care, and household provisioning often extended beyond the boundaries of formal employment.

Section 97.15

The War of the Cities and the Expansion of Civilian Vulnerability

In its early years the war was concentrated on frontier zones, but attacks on cities beyond the front became a recurring feature. Both sides used aircraft and, later, surface-to-surface missiles to strike population centers, and several distinct phases, most prominently in 1985, 1987, and the spring of 1988, are often grouped as the War of the Cities. Iraqi attacks reached Tehran and other major Iranian cities, including Isfahan, Qom, Shiraz, and Tabriz, as well as towns closer to the frontier, while Iranian missile and artillery fire struck Iraqi cities, including Baghdad and Basra. The attacks killed and wounded civilians, damaged housing and public buildings, and spread fear.

Civilians experienced these attacks through alarms, evacuations, shelters, and the disruption of daily life. During the spring 1988 attacks on Tehran, many residents left the capital temporarily, and offices, schools, and shops operated at reduced capacity. Psychological stress, sleeplessness, and anxiety were widespread. Governments encouraged citizens to treat the attacks as part of a common struggle, and propaganda on each side highlighted the other's attacks on civilians while presenting its own as retaliation or as legitimate military action. The political consequences were mixed. Attacks hardened resolve in some quarters and increased pressure for an end to the war in others, and historians debate how much the attacks of 1988 influenced the Iranian leadership's calculations.

Under international humanitarian law, parties to a conflict are obliged to distinguish between military objectives and civilians and to refrain from attacks directed at the civilian population. The Geneva Conventions and the principles of customary law applied. Attacks on cities with weapons that could not be aimed precisely raised serious questions about compliance, and international organizations and individual governments called repeatedly on both sides to halt them. With United Nations mediation, both sides agreed to limited, temporary moratoriums on attacks on civilian areas, notably in 1984, but these did not hold. Where the evidence permits, historians attribute responsibility for specific violations to the governments that ordered them; they do not treat civilians on either side as collectively responsible.

Civilian suffering was not confined to one side. Iraqi civilians in Basra, Baghdad, and other towns were hit by Iranian fire, and Iraqi families also experienced bereavement, displacement, and hardship. A balanced account recognizes both, and it recognizes that experience differed within each country. Estimates of civilian deaths from city attacks exist but vary and are not given here as precise totals; Section 97.18 discusses why.

The legal distinction between a civilian population and a military objective is essential to historical analysis of attacks affecting cities. The presence of a military installation within or near an urban area does not make the city as a whole a legitimate military objective. International humanitarian law requires parties to distinguish civilians and civilian objects from combatants and military objectives, and prohibits indiscriminate attacks. It also requires consideration of the expected incidental civilian harm in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage and the taking of feasible precautions. Whether an individual incident violated these rules depends on facts that may be absent from wartime communiqués. A government's description of an attack as retaliation does not remove its obligations to protect civilians. Students should distinguish the humanitarian principles expressed in treaty and customary law from the evidentiary requirements for making a finding about a particular incident.

Diplomatic efforts to limit attacks on cities demonstrate how difficult it was to separate humanitarian protection from the wider political conflict. In June 1984 the UN Secretary-General helped obtain commitments from both governments to stop attacks on civilian population centers. The commitments did not bring an end to the war or create a comprehensive enforcement system, and accusations of violations continued. Security Council Resolution 582 of 24 February 1986 later deplored, among other aspects of the escalation, attacks on purely civilian areas and neutral shipping or aircraft, and called for a ceasefire and prisoner exchange. The wording is significant because it addressed the protection of people and objects beyond the immediate military confrontation. But a resolution condemning violations is not the same as an independently verified register of individual incidents. For those, historians need corroboration from records of local authorities, hospitals, international investigators, and eyewitnesses.

Section 97.16

Chemical Warfare, Medical Consequences, and International Humanitarian Law

Iraq's use of chemical weapons against Iranian forces is among the best-documented violations of international law in the war. The 1925 Geneva Protocol, to which both Iraq and Iran were parties, prohibits the use in war of asphyxiating, poisonous, or other gases. Iran made allegations of chemical attack from the early 1980s, and in 1984 a United Nations mission sent at Iran's request examined casualties and munitions and concluded that chemical weapons had been used. Further missions in the following years reached similar conclusions. The 1984 investigation already documented both blister and nerve agents; later inquiries extended the evidentiary record. Attacks on military positions in the southern marshes, the northern mountains, and the Faw area caused many casualties among Iranian soldiers, and Iraq also used chemical weapons in attacks affecting civilians.

Sardasht and Halabja must be distinguished. Sardasht, in Iran’s West Azerbaijan Province near the Iraqi frontier, suffered an Iraqi chemical attack on 28 June 1987. Residents experienced deaths, injuries, and long-term illness; the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons has formally commemorated their suffering. Halabja, in Iraqi Kurdistan, suffered a chemical attack by Iraqi forces on 16 March 1988. It must be understood within the wider persecution of Iraqi Kurds, including the Anfal campaigns, as well as the immediate context of the interstate war. No precise fatality totals for these attacks are asserted here. Their distinct political settings and victim populations must remain visible in historical analysis.

The medical consequences were severe and enduring. Mustard agent causes burns to the skin, eyes, and respiratory system, and survivors suffered chronic respiratory disease, eye damage, skin conditions, and psychological effects. Emergency treatment, evacuation, and decontamination were improvised, and Iran also sent a number of victims abroad for treatment in European hospitals. Medical and veterans’ records document a substantial survivor population requiring continuing care; the precise number of everyone exposed is not established by those registers. Survivors' testimony, medical studies, and the activities of veterans' organizations have kept the issue visible, and survivors and their families have continued to seek recognition and, in some cases, accountability.

The international response was slow and limited. The Security Council in 1984 issued a presidential statement condemning the use of chemical weapons, without naming the user. Resolution 612 of May 1988 and Resolution 620 of August 1988 condemned their use and called for compliance, and the issue contributed to the momentum toward the Chemical Weapons Convention concluded in 1993. Political circumstances, including the strategic interests of states that supplied or supported Iraq, shaped the cautious tone of earlier responses. The documentary record nonetheless allows historians to describe the events with precision, to identify the responsible state, and to avoid either minimizing the attacks or conflating allegations with verified findings. This chapter does not describe weapons production or attack methods, only the human, legal, and political record.

The 1984 investigation was an important development in the international documentation of the conflict. The report transmitted to the Security Council as S/16433 on 26 March represented the findings of specialists appointed by the Secretary-General. It confirmed chemical weapon use in the cases investigated, with medical and technical evidence identifying more than one class of chemical agent. Its findings did not amount to a comprehensive inventory of attacks, an estimate of every person exposed, or a judicial determination of individual responsibility. Later investigations developed the evidentiary record further. This sequence matters because there is a difference between the initial verification that a prohibited means of warfare had been used and the subsequent attribution of the repeated attacks to Iraqi forces. The continued failure to halt such use illustrates the distance between documentation and effective international enforcement.

Medical research has made it possible to examine some long-term consequences with greater precision than was available during the fighting. A 2020 retrospective cohort study in JAMA Network Open used records of 64,190 Iranian survivors exposed to sulfur mustard during the war. The study population was drawn from a medical and veterans' registration system, not a census of every military or civilian person exposed. Registration, survival to the time of examination, diagnostic criteria, and available records influenced which people could be included in particular analyses. The results therefore should not be generalized automatically to all victims. They nevertheless provide a foundation for studying the chronic health problems associated with exposure and the need for continued treatment. These records also help show why counting immediate deaths cannot capture the full humanitarian consequences of chemical warfare. People surviving the initial exposure may require medical assistance many years later.

Section 97.17

The Tanker War, Foreign Intervention, and International Diplomacy

As the land war stagnated, hostilities spread to commercial shipping in the Persian Gulf. Iraq intensified its attacks on shipping associated with Iranian oil exports from 1984; Iran also attacked shipping associated with Gulf states supporting Iraq. The resulting insecurity affected seafarers, commercial insurers, petroleum exporters, and governments dependent on uninterrupted trade. Estimates of attacks differ according to the period and definition used, and no aggregate total is asserted here. The escalation brought international powers more directly into the conflict.

In 1987 Kuwait sought international protection for its tankers. The United States arranged the reflagging and protection of Kuwaiti vessels, while the Soviet Union also provided shipping assistance. American involvement expanded into direct confrontations with Iran during 1987 and 1988. The attack on USS Stark by Iraq in 1987 and the American response demonstrated the complications of Washington’s stated neutrality and its evolving regional policy. The maritime conflict increasingly affected the interests of governments that were not belligerents in the original interstate war.

On 3 July 1988 the USS Vincennes shot down Iran Air Flight 655, a civilian airliner, killing all 290 people aboard. The United States described the event as a tragic mistake made in a combat situation. Iran regarded it as a deliberate or reckless act and an indication of American hostility, and the event influenced the climate in which Iran's leaders considered the war's end, a matter for Chapter 98.

International involvement was complex and contradictory. The United States officially declared neutrality, discouraged arms sales to Iran, and in the mid-1980s gradually tilted toward Iraq, extending agricultural credits and sharing some intelligence. Yet in 1985–1986 officials of the Reagan administration secretly arranged arms sales to Iran, hoping to free American hostages in Lebanon and to open contacts with Iranian figures, and proceeds were diverted to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua. The affair, revealed in November 1986, damaged American credibility and showed that public positions did not describe the whole of policy. The Soviet Union supplied Iraq with weapons, though it also sought good relations with Iran. France, among others, sold equipment to Iraq. Syria and Libya supported Iran, and Syria's closure of the Iraqi pipeline was a significant blow. Gulf Arab governments, fearing revolutionary Iran, financed Iraq.

The diplomatic process unfolded in parallel. The United Nations Secretary-General, the Security Council, and the Organization of the Islamic Conference all attempted mediation, and in July 1987 the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 598, calling for a ceasefire, withdrawal to recognized boundaries, prisoner exchanges, and an impartial inquiry into responsibility for the conflict. Iran's initial response was cautious and conditional, since it objected to the lack of an immediate inquiry into who started the war; Iraq signaled acceptance. Foreign assistance should not be seen as the sole reason the war continued. Both governments had their own reasons for fighting, and international involvement altered the resources and risks available to them rather than determining their decisions.

The international dimension of the war was also an economic history of shipping, insurance, trade, and the protection of commercial interests. A petroleum-exporting state relies not only on production but also on buyers, transportation, and the financial services through which transactions take place. The extension of the conflict to commercial shipping raised the uncertainty surrounding those relationships. Carriers and insurers had to reassess their exposure to a prolonged conflict, and neighboring governments became concerned about the ability to export their own petroleum. International involvement in the Gulf consequently cannot be explained solely as support for one side's military campaign. It also reflected efforts by outside governments to protect commerce, maintain access to energy markets, and preserve regional influence. Those goals did not always align with the interests of either belligerent.

The United Nations record offers a way to separate the changing international diplomatic agenda from the policies of individual great powers. Resolution 552 of 1984 addressed the threats to commercial shipping raised by Gulf governments. Resolution 582 of 1986 expressed concern about multiple forms of escalation, including attacks on neutral shipping and civilian aircraft. Resolution 598, adopted on 20 July 1987, brought together a ceasefire, withdrawal, prisoner repatriation, mediation, and an inquiry into responsibility. The United Nations' retrospective account of the Iran-Iraq Military Observer Group provides a useful chronology for these developments. The Security Council's successive resolutions did not represent immediate implementation; the observer mission was established in August 1988. This distinction is especially important when comparing the foreign-policy decisions discussed here with the negotiations assigned to Chapter 98. The war was increasingly internationalized, yet the governments in Tehran and Baghdad retained responsibility for decisions about its continuation. Neither diplomatic pressure nor foreign assistance alone can explain the course of the conflict.

The humanitarian consequences extended well beyond the ceasefire. Prisoners of war were held in difficult conditions, and the International Committee of the Red Cross sought access to them, registered prisoners, facilitated communication with families, and later assisted repatriation. Access was frequently restricted, and the ICRC reported serious concerns about the treatment of prisoners on both sides. Large exchanges only began in 1990, and prisoners continued to return for years afterward. For families, the long uncertainty about missing relatives was a continuing source of anguish, and the ICRC and national agencies have continued to search for information and to support the identification of remains.

Veterans and their families formed a new social category. Disabled veterans, known in Iran as janbazan, received pensions, medical care, preferential employment, and other forms of support through dedicated institutions, which also conferred status. Chemical warfare survivors faced chronic illness, psychological trauma was widespread, and many veterans struggled with unemployment and adjustment. The Martyrs Foundation and related bodies provided assistance and also became significant institutions in postwar economic and political life. The costs of care, shouldered by families and the state, continued for decades.

A systematic review published in 2013 in Archives of Iranian Medicine, entitled Mortality and Injuries Among Iranians in Iraq-Iran War, provides a traceable range for fatalities in the reviewed literature: 188,015 to 217,489 Iranians killed during the conflict. This is a range reported by a review of existing studies, not the result of a new national census, and it must not be relabeled as a military-only death toll. It also does not represent deaths in both countries combined. The principal value of the review is that it makes it possible to identify a defined research product behind the numerical statement. Estimates collected in a systematic review may be influenced by the quality and coverage of the original studies, differences in definitions, and incomplete registration. A precise-looking figure can therefore be less reliable than a carefully defined range. No comparable Iraqi series is included in the table below, because it would be misleading to combine estimates drawn from different methods and populations without first establishing their comparability.

The history of injuries requires another set of distinctions. A person wounded during the war may recover fully, live with a lasting disability, or die from a later complication. A veteran enrolled in a welfare program may qualify through a particular administrative definition of injury. Medical surveillance may include civilians as well as former military personnel and may record several conditions in the same individual. The number of clinical conditions recorded is therefore not necessarily the number of distinct people treated. The study of chemical warfare survivors described in Section 97.16 gives a concrete example. Its registry-based population is important for public health research but cannot serve as the total of everyone injured by chemical warfare. Similarly, estimates of the number of wounded should not simply be added to counts of disabled veterans or survivors of exposure: these categories may overlap extensively.

The treatment of prisoners of war constitutes a separate humanitarian history governed by the Third Geneva Convention. Among the responsibilities of the International Committee of the Red Cross were efforts to obtain access to prisoners, register them, support contact with their families, and assist with repatriation. Access and cooperation depended on the authorities holding the prisoners. A family that had received no reliable information about a captured relative faced a different problem from a family whose relative had been registered but remained in detention. A ceasefire might halt the immediate fighting without resolving either problem. The ICRC records that the last exchange of prisoners between Iran and Iraq occurred in 2003. In 2008 the two states and the organization agreed on a tripartite mechanism for addressing missing persons. These dates demonstrate why it is inaccurate to treat the war's humanitarian history as complete in August 1988.

Accounting for missing persons requires both documentary and forensic work. A person recorded as missing might have been killed, detained, or separated from relatives without a record of the event. Identification of human remains requires additional evidence and cooperation across institutional and national boundaries. The eventual recovery of remains can bring an end to uncertainty for a family, but it can also renew grief and create further responsibilities for memorialization and burial. These are not merely administrative tasks: they concern the dignity of the dead and the rights and needs of surviving relatives. The continuing work of the ICRC illustrates the distinction between ending a conflict and addressing its consequences. A measure of humanitarian progress should therefore include not only the number of people released or identified but also the extent to which families receive reliable information and appropriate support.

The long-term costs of disability were distributed between the state, medical institutions, and households. Benefits could assist with treatment and income, while unpaid care within families remained essential. Eligibility rules and the recognition of wartime status also created boundaries between those entitled to particular services and those whose injuries were difficult to document. This makes the history of veterans' institutions inseparable from the history of welfare and citizenship. A war that produced a durable category of citizens with specialized medical and social needs also created a continuing political obligation to provide for them. The social history of the war thus extends from the fighting to decades of medical care, administrative decisions, and family experience.

Section 97.19

Wartime Propaganda, Literature, Cinema, and the Construction of Collective Memory

The war was represented in public culture from its first days. Government radio and television broadcast news, sermons, and songs, newspapers carried communiqués and martyrs' biographies, and posters and wall murals filled streets with images of soldiers, martyrs, and revolutionary leaders. Religious ceremonies, funerals for the dead, and Friday sermons tied the war to religious commemoration. Photographers and filmmakers recorded the front, and the state supported documentary production. Morteza Avini's television series Revayat-e Fath ("Chronicle of Victory"), which presented the war in a spiritual and elegiac register, became one of the most influential bodies of visual material, and Avini himself, killed by a landmine in 1993, became a commemorated figure. Wartime mobilization materials aimed to recruit, sustain morale, and shape interpretation, and they should be distinguished from the commemorative works produced afterward.

After the ceasefire, memory was institutionalized. The state established museums, cemeteries, and memorials, including the large martyrs' sections of cemeteries; annual observances, such as the commemoration of the liberation of Khorramshahr and the week devoted to the Sacred Defense in September; and a body of literature and cinema known as Sacred Defense culture. Veterans wrote memoirs, writers produced novels and stories based on wartime experience, and filmmakers made features and documentaries. Women's oral histories have preserved voices of nurses, relief workers, and families. The scholarship of Narges Bajoghli, Amir Moosavi, and others on how war memory has been produced and contested shows that commemoration was organized by institutions with their own interests, among them the Guards and the foundations, while artists and writers also pursued independent and sometimes critical visions.

Official narratives of the Sacred Defense stress unity, sacrifice, and divine support, and they have often marginalized other experiences: those of conscripts who did not want to serve, minorities whose contributions were overlooked, displaced families whose losses were not recognized, and those who questioned the continuation of the war. Alternative representations, including critical novels, films, and personal narratives, have complicated the picture, particularly in more recent decades. Studies of war literature also note a tension between literature that serves commemorative aims and literature that explores trauma, ambivalence, and loss. Official memory and personal memory coexist, sometimes reinforcing and sometimes contradicting one another.

Historians therefore treat cultural materials as evidence of representation and of the institutions that produced them, not as unmediated windows onto experience. A mural can show how the state wished the war to be seen, and a memoir can show how a participant remembers it decades later. Neither can by itself establish what happened. Reading them against documents, statistics, and other testimony provides a fuller picture.

Morteza Avini's documentary series Revayat-e Fath is a particularly useful example of how the medium of representation can shape historical meaning. A documentary combines footage, selection, editing, narration, and sometimes music; the resulting work is neither identical to the events recorded nor wholly detached from them. Its presentation of sacrifice and spirituality can be studied alongside the official vocabulary of the Sacred Defense. A historian might compare the subjects emphasized in a documentary with the matters left outside the frame, such as the long administrative work of maintaining services or the experiences of civilians far from the front. Such comparison does not require an assumption that the filmmaker's convictions were insincere. It instead recognizes that every representation is shaped by choices concerning audience, purpose, and form. Avini's subsequent commemoration also shows how the creators of wartime culture themselves became subjects of collective memory.

The collection Debating the Iran-Iraq War in Contemporary Iran, edited by Narges Bajoghli and Amir Moosavi, is a useful scholarly entry point for examining the ongoing contest over the war's meanings. Its publication long after the fighting is itself a reminder that historical memory changes as new generations encounter the conflict through textbooks, films, public monuments, and family testimony. A child born after 1988 cannot possess personal wartime memories but may inherit a powerful family or institutional account of the conflict. These forms of transmission do not necessarily produce consensus. Disagreement about the continuation of the war, the recognition of different participants, and the treatment of survivors can coexist with widely shared recognition of the importance of defending Iran against invasion. The cultural legacy of the war is therefore a continuing historical process rather than a single completed official narrative.

Section 97.20

The Iran–Iraq War and the Transformation of the Islamic Republic

By the summer of 1988 the Islamic Republic had survived, but it was a different state from the one that entered the war. The war strengthened some institutions and reshaped others. The Revolutionary Guards and the Basij became large, permanent, politically significant organizations. Revolutionary foundations and welfare bodies acquired resources and social roles that outlasted the conflict. The government developed administrative capacity for distribution, mobilization, and control, and the economy had become more centralized and more dependent on state allocation. Domestic defense industries had expanded, and a doctrine of self-reliance, shaped by international isolation and arms embargoes, became part of national security thinking. The war also deeply influenced regional outlook, leaving a long suspicion of Iraq and of the Gulf states that had backed it, and a conviction that Iran could not rely on international institutions for protection.

The costs were equally lasting. Hundreds of thousands of Iranians had been killed or wounded, many more were displaced, and tens of thousands had survived chemical attack or imprisonment. Regional inequality had deepened, with the border provinces bearing disproportionate damage. The economy had been strained by diverted resources, inflation, and the oil-price collapse. International isolation had increased. Legitimacy gained through defense coexisted with weariness, and the leadership's decision to continue the war after 1982 had exposed it to criticism. Collective memory contained both pride and grief, and public discussion of the war's lessons remains contested.

It is important to distinguish institutional survival from military victory, and national defense from the achievement of every declared objective. Iran recovered its territory and preserved its government, but it did not obtain reparations, the removal of Saddam Hussein, or a formal condemnation of Iraq as aggressor at the war's end. By 1988 the Islamic Republic faced military reversals, economic exhaustion, international pressure, and humanitarian burdens, and its leaders confronted the question whether to continue. Security Council Resolution 598, adopted in July 1987, provided the diplomatic framework within which that question would be resolved. The decision to accept it in July 1988, and the ceasefire that took effect on 20 August 1988, are the subject of Chapter 98.

The relationship between national security and domestic politics is one of the war's most consequential legacies. The armed institutions acquired experience and organizational resources while the political leadership developed mechanisms for emergency administration. These capabilities mattered to the survival of the state and later became part of its ordinary governing arrangements. Yet the strengthening of these institutions should not be confused with the strengthening of every civilian institution or the expansion of political participation. The wartime government had to manage competition over scarce resources, and the same administrative capabilities that made relief and rationing possible could also support the exclusion of opponents. Chapter 95 demonstrated that political consolidation and repression were related but distinct. The war deepened both processes while creating new forms of public service and civic participation. This helps explain why the historical legacy of the Islamic Republic's early years cannot be captured by a single measure of success or failure.

The economic inheritance was similarly mixed. Iran's ability to sustain essential production and develop selected domestic capabilities demonstrated resilience, but the economy also carried the accumulated burden of deferred investment, damaged infrastructure, and scarcity of foreign exchange. The location of direct destruction meant that reconstruction costs were particularly concentrated in certain provinces, while shortages and the redistribution of public resources affected households throughout the country. The war's end therefore posed an economic question about how to distribute the costs of recovery, not simply a technical question about how to repair buildings. The policies of the reconstruction era would be shaped by debates over the state's economic role that had developed during the war itself. Those debates receive fuller treatment in Chapter 99.

For families, survivors, and displaced communities, the endpoint was different again. The end of major combat did not establish the fate of all missing people, restore every lost livelihood, or remove the medical consequences of injuries. These continuing burdens required public institutions to maintain relationships with families long after the emergency had passed. The history of collective memory, in turn, shows that the meaning of the war did not become fixed at the ceasefire. It continued to be constructed through institutions, popular culture, and personal testimony. Later generations inherited the benefits and burdens of both the wartime state and the narratives through which it was remembered. A historical assessment of the war must therefore consider not only its territorial outcome but also its distribution of costs over time. It is this combination of state survival, economic strain, humanitarian obligation, and contested memory that provides the context for the political decisions examined in Chapter 98.

Historical Debate

Did the War Strengthen the Islamic Republic While Weakening Iranian Society and Its Economy?

The question: Did eight years of war consolidate the revolutionary state at the expense of social and economic well-being, or is this a misleading contrast?

Interpretation A — The war consolidated the state: The external threat allowed the leadership to rally support, marginalize rivals, expand institutions, and legitimize revolutionary authority, giving the Republic durable structures it might not otherwise have acquired.
Interpretation B — The costs outweighed the consolidation: Scholars emphasizing the human and economic toll argue that the war destroyed lives and resources, deepened inequality, entrenched coercive institutions, and left unresolved problems that later governments inherited. Some add that consolidation was already underway for domestic reasons and that the war was not its necessary cause.

Evidence: Both draw on economic statistics, institutional histories, memoirs, and social data. The disagreement concerns causation (how much consolidation was due to the war), interpretation (what counts as strength), and scale.

Why it matters: The answer shapes judgments about how a state's institutions are formed under crisis, and about whether wartime centralization was a temporary measure or a lasting feature.

Chapter Summary

The liberation of Khorramshahr in May 1982 marked the recovery of most Iranian territory lost to Iraq's invasion, yet the war lasted until August 1988. The chapter's central argument is that this prolonged conflict simultaneously threatened the survival of the Islamic Republic and contributed to its institutional consolidation, while imposing very heavy costs on Iranian society and economy.

Militarily, Iran survived the invasion of 1980 through improvised resistance, the cooperation of regular and revolutionary forces, and political consolidation after mid-1981. Counteroffensives from September 1981 to May 1982 recovered Abadan, northern Khuzestan, and Khorramshahr. The decision to carry the war into Iraq transformed it from a defensive struggle into a war with wider political aims, a choice made by identifiable leaders and debated by historians.

From 1983 the war became attritional. Iranian offensives, including al-Faw in 1986 and the Basra campaigns of 1987, gained little ground at heavy cost, while Iraq strengthened its defenses with foreign finance and arms. In 1988 Iraq regained the initiative, and Iran faced mounting pressure. Neither government achieved its principal political objectives.

The war was sustained by institutions: the Artesh, the Revolutionary Guards, the Basij, Reconstruction Jihad, and welfare bodies such as the Martyrs Foundation. Mobilization drew on professionals, conscripts, and volunteers, motivated by patriotism, religious conviction, social pressure, and obligation. The language of martyrdom and Sacred Defense gave meaning to sacrifice and legitimacy to the political order, though it did not capture every participant's experience. The participation of minors is documented but poorly quantified.

Economically, Iran depended on oil revenues that fell sharply in 1986, financed deficits through reserves and domestic borrowing, and used price controls and rationing to protect households. The revolution, the war, sanctions, and the oil market each contributed to economic strain, and these factors cannot be cleanly separated. Industry suffered damage and shortages but also adapted domestically, and agriculture varied by region and sector.

Socially, the war displaced large numbers of people, concentrated destruction in border provinces, changed the roles of women and families, and brought attacks on cities within reach of civilians. Iraq's use of chemical weapons, documented by United Nations investigators, caused lasting harm, and Sardasht and Halabja, though both associated with chemical attack, must be distinguished in place and context.

The conflict internationalized through the Tanker War, American naval involvement, Gulf financial support for Iraq, covert arms sales to Iran, and diplomatic efforts that produced Resolution 598 in July 1987. The downing of Iran Air Flight 655 in July 1988 occurred as the war neared its end.

The human costs, measured in deaths, wounded, disabled, chemical survivors, prisoners, and missing persons, are large but imprecisely known, and statistical categories must be kept distinct. Veterans' institutions and humanitarian work continued long after the ceasefire.

Culturally, the war produced propaganda, literature, film, memorials, and commemorations that created an official narrative of the Sacred Defense, which coexists with personal and critical memories of loss and hardship.

The Islamic Republic emerged with stronger military and revolutionary institutions, greater state administrative reach, domestic defense industries, and a durable security outlook, but also with economic exhaustion, social loss, and international isolation. Institutional survival was not military victory. The decisions that ended the war belong to Chapter 98.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has argued that the Iran–Iraq War must be understood as a national transformation as well as a sequence of campaigns. Its military story, from near-collapse of the frontier to the recovery of Khorramshahr to years of attrition, cannot be separated from the institutional, economic, social, humanitarian, and cultural changes that accompanied it.

The war shaped what followed. It enlarged the Revolutionary Guards and the foundations, accustomed the state to mobilizing society and managing scarcity, and gave the Republic a language of sacrifice and legitimacy. It also left a generation of veterans, bereaved families, and survivors of chemical attack, and a landscape of damaged cities and unequal regional recovery. These legacies reappear in the chapters on the war's end, reconstruction, the political economy of the Guards, and contemporary Iranian society.

The chapter also illustrates the work of the historian. Statistics must be interrogated, official narratives distinguished from personal memory, and legal and moral judgments grounded in documentation. Students who keep these distinctions in view will be prepared for Chapter 98, which turns from eight years of war to the decisions that ended it and to the political transition that followed.
