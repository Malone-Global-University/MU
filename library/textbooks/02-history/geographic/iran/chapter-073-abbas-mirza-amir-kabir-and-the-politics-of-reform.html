Abbas Mirza, Amir Kabir, and the Politics of Reform

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

On 29 December 1851 a new school opened in Tehran while the man who had conceived it was already out of office and under guard. Within two weeks he would be dead. The school, Dar al-Funun, would outlast him by generations.

According to the best available accounts, Dar al-Funun, a polytechnic college, was formally inaugurated on 29 December 1851. Its founder, Mirza Taqi Khan, known to history as Amir Kabir, had been dismissed as chief minister the previous November. He was confined at Kashan and, on 10 January 1852, put to death at the Fin bathhouse on the orders of the young Naser al-Din Shah. The institution he had planned nevertheless opened its doors, received its first students, and continued to teach. A reformer had been removed from power, but one of his principal creations continued to function.

That sequence poses the central problem of this chapter. Why did Qajar rulers and ministers repeatedly recognize that their state needed institutional change, yet struggle to sustain it from one political generation to the next? The answer cannot be found in the character of a single man. It lies in the circumstances in which reform was attempted: war and its costs, a revenue system that depended on local intermediaries, a monarchy in which ministers served at the ruler's pleasure, and a court whose members had real interests in the existing distribution of offices, stipends, and privileges.

The chapter follows two related but distinct efforts. The first was led by Abbas Mirza (1789–1833), crown prince and governor of Azerbaijan, who from about 1807 used the resources of his provincial court at Tabriz to build a regular infantry and artillery force, to acquire foreign expertise, to send students to Britain, and to promote printing and translation. The second was led by Mirza Taqi Khan, Amir Kabir (c. 1807–1852), who served as chief minister from 1848 to 1851 and tried to extend reform from the army into taxation, state expenditure, provincial supervision, public works, communications, and education. Between them lay a connecting generation of administrators, above all the Farahani family of viziers who had served Abbas Mirza, from whose household Mirza Taqi himself emerged.

Three distinctions will govern the analysis. First, reform was not the same as Westernization. Rulers and ministers adopted European drill, artillery, and technical instruction for specific administrative and military purposes, and doing so did not commit them to European political ideas or to constitutional government. Second, an innovation was not the same as an institution. A regiment or a press could succeed under a patron and fade without him, while a school with its own personnel, routines, and royal backing might endure. Third, resistance to reform was not a simple contest between enlightened modernizers and obstinate reactionaries. Courtiers, officers, clerics, merchants, tax officials, and provincial governors responded according to what reform meant for their income, authority, and security.

The evidence for this history is uneven and shapes what can be said with confidence. Qajar court chronicles, official correspondence, British and Russian diplomatic and military records, the travel writings of Iranian students and envoys, European travelers' accounts, and the official gazette from 1851 all survive. Each reflects the purposes of its author. Court historians praised or blamed ministers according to their patrons; European observers measured Iran against their own institutions; and later Iranian nationalist and Pahlavi-era writers sometimes presented Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir as heroic forerunners of a modern nation. This chapter treats those later portraits as part of the historiography and not as the starting point of analysis.

Boundaries with neighboring chapters matter here. The wars with Russia, their battles, and the treaties of Gulistan and Turkmenchay belong to Chapters 70 and 71, and the broader imperial competition between Russia and Britain belongs to Chapter 72. The Babi movement and its suppression belong to Chapter 74, concessions and the wider political economy to Chapter 75, and the broader intellectual and print culture of the later nineteenth century to Chapter 77. This chapter concentrates on reform initiatives, the government institutions they created or sought to change, the political struggle over their implementation, and their uneven survival, including the immediate institutional history of Dar al-Funun.

Historical Context

The Qajar dynasty emerged from the tribal Qajar confederation of the Caspian southeast. As Chapter 69 showed, Agha Mohammad Khan reunited much of the plateau by coercive consolidation and was assassinated in 1797. His nephew Fath Ali Shah (r. 1797–1834) inherited a monarchy that depended on a balance among tribal military leaders, provincial governors drawn from the royal family, a scribal and financial bureaucracy, urban notables, and the religious scholars (ulama). Authority was personal: the shah's confidence made a minister, and its withdrawal unmade him.

The military system rested on a combination of tribal levies, mounted retainers supplied by provincial governors and chiefs, and garrison troops. Cavalry was the backbone, and the system had real strengths in mobility and in familiarity with terrain. It had weaknesses that early nineteenth-century warfare exposed: forces were assembled seasonally, paid unevenly, commanded through chains of personal loyalty, and difficult to keep in the field for long campaigns. Against the disciplined infantry and field artillery of the Russian army in the Caucasus, those weaknesses became increasingly serious. The campaigns themselves are narrated in Chapter 70; here they serve only as the pressure that made military organization a political question.

Fiscal arrangements compounded the problem. Revenue came largely from land taxes assessed on districts and collected through governors, tax farmers, and local notables, supplemented by customs, crown lands, and the customary gifts presented at New Year. Government expenditure included salaries, pensions, and stipends to a large population of officeholders, courtiers, clerics, and royal relatives, many of whom regarded income from the state as an entitlement. There was no unified budget in the modern sense, and the treasury's information about actual revenue and payments depended on officials whose interests were often bound up with the arrangements under review.

Iran was also drawn into an expanding diplomatic world. After 1800 British, French, and Russian missions competed for influence at Tehran and Tabriz, offering arms, officers, loans, and subsidies. This created opportunities for acquiring military expertise, and it created dependence, because foreign assistance responded to the shifting needs of European powers rather than to Iranian priorities. The Ottoman Empire, itself pursuing military and administrative reorganization under the name of the New Order (Nizam-i Cedid) and later the Tanzimat, was a comparable and, for Iranian officials, increasingly visible neighbor.

Division I — The Origins of Qajar Reform and Abbas Mirza's Military Experiments, 1797–1813

Section 73.1

Military Defeat, State Capacity, and the Origins of Reform

Reform in Qajar Iran began as a response to a practical problem, and the problem was the government's ability to turn resources into effective force. Historians use the phrase state capacity for a government's ability to raise revenue, recruit and pay personnel, communicate orders, and have them carried out. Around 1800 the Qajar monarchy had considerable political resilience but limited capacity of this kind, and the gap was most visible in war.

The standing arrangements for recruitment illustrate it. Troops were typically raised through tribal leaders and provincial governors, who brought their followers to a campaign and who were rewarded with titles, revenue assignments, and gifts. A commander therefore commanded men whose first loyalty was to a chief. Payment was irregular and sometimes in kind or in revenue rights rather than cash. Training was informal and individual, built on skill in riding and shooting rather than on coordinated maneuver. Artillery existed but was small in scale, and its manufacture, maintenance, and transport depended on a limited number of skilled craftsmen. When armies operated far from their bases, as in the Caucasus, supply and reinforcement were hard to guarantee.

These facts did not make Iranian armies inherently ineffective. Mounted forces fought well in suitable terrain, and Qajar commanders understood their opponents. What the Russian encounters demonstrated, as Chapters 70 and 71 explain, was that an adversary with regular infantry, organized artillery, a reliable commissariat, and a more centralized command could sustain pressure over time. Defeat therefore directed attention to organization: how soldiers were recruited, trained, paid, supplied, and commanded, and how those functions were funded.

This is why the term reform in this chapter means more than the purchase of foreign weapons. Its broader meaning was an effort to increase the government's capacity to mobilize resources, coordinate force, train personnel, and make administrative decisions effective. Military reform soon implied fiscal reform, because regular soldiers had to be paid regularly. It implied administrative reform, because payment required records and accountable officials. And it implied educational reform, because new methods required people who knew them. The sequence from drill to taxation to schooling that structures this chapter followed from that logic.

The issue of capacity also had a temporal dimension. A mobile household army might assemble rapidly for a brief incursion but disperse when harvests, grazing demands, or a chief's political calculations required its return. A drilled unit imposed almost the opposite burden: sustained expenditure before the government could expect reliable performance. Training produced its benefits only after weeks or months of instruction, and regular service removed men from other productive work. The effort to change the army therefore forced rulers to make decisions about who should supply recruits, who would compensate their households, and which authorities could settle disputes when levies were imposed. These were questions of governing society, not merely purchasing muskets.

One obstacle was the difference between revenue written in an assessment register and money available to a commander. Provincial officers might promise a sum but collect it in installments; taxes could be paid in produce, claimed in advance by creditors, or retained locally to meet other obligations. Moving funds across a large territory incurred further costs and risks. A state could thus be comparatively prosperous in land and people yet unable to concentrate money and supplies at the place and moment a campaign required. European military practice appealed partly because it offered a disciplined way to use scarce resources, but imitating its units without building the accounting and transport that sustained them could make fiscal problems more acute.

Reform also had a political language. A shah might present new troops as a demonstration of restored kingship, a crown prince might see them as a means to defend his frontier and strengthen his succession claims, and an accountant might see in the same force an obligation for permanent payrolls and verifiable lists. Their interests overlapped without being identical. This plurality explains why reforms could be praised in principle while resisted in practice. A durable army required agreement not only on the desirability of victory but on the distribution of taxes, obligations, and authority necessary to sustain it.

Section 73.2

Abbas Mirza, Tabriz, and the Political Geography of Reform

Abbas Mirza was born in 1789, a son of Fath Ali Shah, and was designated crown prince (vali-ahd) around the turn of the century. Like other princes, he held a provincial governorship. His was Azerbaijan, with its capital at Tabriz, and the choice made him responsible for the northwestern frontier at the very moment it became the principal theater of conflict with Russia. The position gave him a revenue base, a provincial administration, and the means to maintain troops. It also gave him a degree of autonomy, because communication with Tehran was slow and the shah, who preferred to keep his heir busy and his rivals at a distance, allowed considerable latitude.

Tabriz was a good place for experiment. It was a large commercial city on routes connecting Iran to Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Black Sea, with a merchant community, including Armenian merchants, that traded across the frontier. It lay close enough to the Caucasus to feel the war directly and to receive diplomats, officers, and travelers who passed through on their way to Tehran. It had craftsmen who could be trained to produce weapons, and it was the point from which news of European practice reached Iran earliest. The prince's court there became, in effect, a laboratory.

Abbas Mirza did not work alone. His principal administrator was Mirza Bozorg Farahani, who held the title Qa'em-Maqam ("deputy") and managed the court's finances and correspondence until his death in 1822. Mirza Bozorg was succeeded in the post by his son, Mirza Abu'l-Qasem, also known as Qa'em-Maqam, a literary stylist and a major political figure whose career ended in 1835. The two men are often conflated, but they must be kept distinct: the father was the administrator of the early reform, the son a prominent official of its later phase and of the transition to Mohammad Shah. Around them was a circle of secretaries, accountants, and military officers whose practical knowledge made the experiments workable.

The result was that early reform emerged from a provincial court with substantial autonomy, and not from a centrally directed national program. It was sustained by Azerbaijan's revenues, by the prince's personal authority, and by his administrators' skill. That origin explains both its energy and its fragility. It could move quickly because decisions were made close to the problem. It remained exposed because its success depended on the prince's standing with his father, the loyalty of provincial elites, and the finances of a single province.

Azerbaijan's provincial position shaped both the sources and the limits of reform. Its governors had long maintained relationships with rulers and communities across the Aras, and they dealt with frontier customs, seasonal migration, and the consequences of war in ways that court officials in Tehran experienced more indirectly. Consuls and military travelers could be questioned about the cost of weapons, the organization of workshops, and the conduct of European armies. Yet the same networks could be interrupted by changing frontiers, Russian occupation, or diplomatic quarrels. A commercial city provided contacts and skilled labor but not guaranteed access to the imported inputs on which new machinery depended.

The Qa'em-Maqam family illustrates the importance of administrative continuity beneath princely authority. Mirza Bozorg's secretariat supplied correspondence, fiscal estimates, personnel management, and the ability to turn the prince's decisions into instructions for individual districts. His son Mirza Abu'l-Qasem inherited professional relationships, literary and bureaucratic authority, and the delicate task of negotiating between the prince and his father. Their influence was neither a separate modern ministry nor simply household servitude. It belonged to an older professional culture of secretaries whose competence made ambitious provincial governance possible. A reform history restricted to the prince's personal curiosity would obscure this administrative foundation.

Tabriz should therefore be understood as a site of institutional concentration rather than a miraculous origin of modernity. It brought together a governor with unusual military responsibilities, experienced revenue officers, officers and craftsmen, foreign advisers, and merchants familiar with neighboring states. The network could try new practices on a modest scale. Extending those practices across Iran was a different problem: other provinces possessed different revenue arrangements and political patrons, and Tehran was not obligated to finance every experiment initiated by the crown prince. The distinction between local experimentation and kingdom-wide implementation remains central throughout the chapter.

Section 73.3

The Nezam-e Jadid: Infantry, Artillery, Drill, and Military Organization

The core of Abbas Mirza's program was the Nezam-e Jadid, the "New Order." The name echoed the Ottoman Nizam-i Cedid of Sultan Selim III and conveyed a similar idea: a standing, salaried, drilled, uniformed force under direct government command, supplementing or replacing the loosely organized levies on which the state had depended. In Iran it consisted mainly of regular infantry, called sarbaz, equipped with muskets and trained in formation, together with an expanded artillery.

Developing such a force meant solving a series of practical problems. Recruits had to be found, usually through quotas imposed on districts or communities, and then retained. They needed uniforms, weapons, and barracks, since men who were to train together had to live together. They required instructors who could teach drill, the manual of arms, and maneuver. They needed officers, and the officer corps had to be built out of men whose training and loyalties were different from those of tribal chieftains. They also needed pay, because without regular pay a salaried soldier had no reason to remain. Each of these requirements had financial and political costs, and the program grew in stages, beginning with limited battalions around Tabriz and expanding as resources and instructors permitted.

It is important not to treat the older forces as inherently ineffective. Iranian cavalry remained highly capable and was not abandoned; the new infantry and artillery were intended to complement it. The problem was one of balance and organization. Regular infantry could hold ground and sustain fire, and artillery could reduce fortified positions, which irregular horsemen could not do. But infantry units were more expensive and harder to maintain than levies, and their effectiveness depended on training that took time. Meanwhile the tribal and provincial military leaders whose followers made up the traditional army had interests of their own and could regard a rival force, loyal to the crown prince, as a threat to their position.

The new force also relied on mixed personnel. Some soldiers were recruited locally, and some regiments included men from the frontier regions. Later in the period, deserters from foreign armies, including Russian deserters who formed units in Iranian service, supplied additional experience. The result was not a copy of a European army but a hybrid: European methods adapted to the resources, social structure, and political constraints of Iran.

The new infantry depended on more than marching in formation. Soldiers had to learn to load and discharge weapons at a coordinated pace, move while maintaining lines, distinguish orders under noise and smoke, and obey officers whose authority did not rest solely on personal kinship. Artillery crews needed specialized knowledge of powder, shot, range, transport, and the preparation of positions. Instructors could demonstrate these skills, but a large force required Iranian personnel capable of teaching the next group. The transition from imported expertise to domestic instruction was therefore as important as the first successful parade or inspection.

Contemporary figures make the scale of the experiment clearer but should be handled as estimates rather than a census. An Encyclopaedia Iranica synthesis cites approximately 6,000 European-drilled infantry in 1808–1809, 8,000 in 1817, and a reported 12,000 infantry alongside roughly 1,200 artillerymen by 1831. Such numbers derive from observers who counted different kinds of soldiers at different moments. They do not establish how many were present, armed, paid, and ready for battle simultaneously. The contrast between an authorized establishment and actual field strength was precisely the sort of administrative difficulty the new system was supposed to address.

The army also adapted techniques selectively. Abbas Mirza did not attempt to abolish cavalry, which he and his commanders considered a formidable arm. Iranian horsemen could scout, harass an enemy, protect supply lines, and exploit maneuver on terrain unsuitable for compact infantry formations. New infantry and artillery were intended to work alongside these forces, not to make them irrelevant. Friction arose when a temporary levy and a regular battalion operated under different expectations about pay, discipline, and command. A hybrid army could be flexible, but flexibility required coordination among units that did not share the same institutional history.

Historical Vocabulary
Nezam-e Jadid, Sarbaz, Tufangchi

Definition: Nezam-e Jadid ("New Order") designates the regular, drilled, salaried forces that Abbas Mirza organized, modeled in part on European and Ottoman practice. Sarbaz was the usual term for a regular soldier of such units. Tufangchi ("musketeer") denoted a soldier armed with a firearm and was used both for regulars and for irregular infantry.

Historical context: The terms belong to a period in which military vocabulary was shifting. Artillery (topkhaneh, "cannon house") was also a distinct branch with its own personnel. Older tribal and provincial forces continued to exist beside the New Order.

Why it matters: The vocabulary captures the idea of a permanent government-controlled force, which was the central goal of military reform.

Potential misunderstanding: "New Order" does not mean an army replaced the cavalry wholesale, nor that the new units were European in composition. They were an Iranian institution with foreign-trained instructors.

Section 73.4

French and British Military Missions: Expertise, Diplomacy, and Dependence

Abbas Mirza obtained expertise by exploiting European rivalry. After the Treaty of Finkenstein in 1807 between Iran and Napoleonic France, a French mission under General Claude-Mathieu de Gardane reached Iran, and its officers assisted in training troops, surveying, and artillery work. The shift in European alliances that followed that same year reduced France's interest in Iran, and the mission withdrew by 1809. Britain, concerned about French influence and the security of India, then sought Iranian friendship, and a British mission under Harford Jones concluded a preliminary treaty in 1809. A British military mission, which arrived around 1810 under Captain Charles Christie, was followed in 1812 by a further agreement under Sir Gore Ouseley that provided for British officers and a subsidy conditioned on the Russian war.

The British personnel made specific contributions. Christie and Henry Lindsay, together with others such as William Monteith, trained infantry battalions, assisted in casting and repairing guns, and helped to establish workshops and arsenal capacity at Tabriz. Their presence gave the Nezam-e Jadid a more systematic training regime than it had had before. Christie was killed in action in 1812, an event that showed that European officers shared the risks of the field and that the mission's work depended on individuals.

The relationship also had costs. Foreign officers could be recalled, and their governments' priorities changed. When Anglo-Russian cooperation in the Napoleonic wars altered Britain's position, Iranian leaders found that the assistance they had counted on was conditional. The subsidy mechanism meant that military reform was entangled with treaty obligations, and disputes over whether Britain's obligations had been triggered recurred in the following decades, as Chapters 70 and 72 explain. Reform thus acquired a diplomatic vulnerability. An institution that required foreign instructors and arms supplies could be disrupted by events outside the control of its Iranian sponsors.

Iranian agency in this process deserves emphasis. Abbas Mirza and his advisers chose which mission to engage, set the terms of instruction, assigned officers to units, and decided what to retain after instructors left. Their approach was selective and pragmatic. The institutional effect of the missions was to supply skills and equipment, transfer knowledge to Iranian officers and craftsmen, and demonstrate methods, while leaving open whether the Qajar state could finance and organize the results on its own.

The diplomatic context determined what foreign officers were willing and able to do. French advisers arrived in the aftermath of Finkenstein with knowledge of fortification and drill, but France's rapprochement with Russia at Tilsit immediately weakened the political premises of their mission. British instructors then worked in a setting where their government's friendship with Russia constrained its support of Iranian military objectives. Officers could still teach mathematics or gunnery while their diplomats discouraged a military campaign. From Tehran's perspective these were not contradictions easily resolved by choosing the more generous European patron. They revealed a structural gap between a technical service contract and a reliable strategic alliance.

Foreign instructors did not act alone in a workshop. Casting guns required Iranian metalworkers; distributing muskets depended on clerks and transport contractors; translating instructions relied on scribes and multilingual intermediaries. When an instructor departed, the value of his teaching depended on whether the workshop kept tools, records, working examples, and apprentices. The replacement of one specialist with another was not enough if there was no budget for materials or repair. These unglamorous arrangements determine whether a military mission leaves a durable legacy or only an episode of temporary assistance.

The surviving British record is particularly revealing because it provides the perspective of an interested foreign government. The memorandum of 31 May 1810, prepared by David Morier for Sir Harford Jones, reports the crown prince's priorities as heard through an interpreter and British diplomatic circumstances. It is evidence that Abbas Mirza's concerns reached foreign policymakers, not an unmediated transcript of his voice. Read beside Iranian correspondence and the later careers of British officers, the document helps distinguish promises of support, actual delivery of personnel and equipment, and the military results that followed. This is a better measure of the mission than an uncomplicated story of foreign tutelage.

Division II — The Expansion and Limits of Abbas Mirza's Reform Program, 1813–1833

Section 73.5

Financing Reform: Revenue, Recruitment, Military Supply, and Provincial Administration

A regular army is a financial commitment. Levies assembled for a campaign could be dismissed afterward, but salaried regiments had to be fed, clothed, armed, and paid throughout the year. Abbas Mirza's resources came principally from the revenues of Azerbaijan: land taxes collected through district officials and local notables, customs and market dues at Tabriz and the frontier, and assignments on crown lands. Supplementary funds arrived from the central treasury and, for a time, from British subsidy payments. None of these sources was elastic. Land revenue depended on harvests and on the capacity of local officials, and customs revenue was sensitive to the disruption of trade by war.

Military administration therefore became an exercise in fiscal management. Officials had to register troops, issue pay, contract for supplies such as cloth, leather, gunpowder, and iron, and account for arms production. These tasks required clerks and accountants with training in registration and financial record keeping, and the position of military registrar or paymaster developed into a significant bureaucratic role. It was in precisely this field that the young Mirza Taqi later gained his early experience, as Section 73.10 shows.

Recruitment and requisitioning affected the population directly. Districts were required to furnish men, grain, animals, and transport, and the burden fell unevenly: on cultivators who lost labor, on towns that quartered soldiers, and on communities close to the front. Merchants experienced disruptions to trade and extraordinary levies. Provincial elites could resist or negotiate, and their cooperation was essential, since the government lacked the means to collect directly. The more ambitious the military establishment, the greater its dependence on local intermediaries whose own interests it might harm.

Finally, the army's expenses competed with other obligations. The monarchy had to maintain the court, pay stipends and pensions to royal relatives and officeholders, honor religious endowments, and reward the tribal leaders whose goodwill it needed. A prince who spent heavily on regular troops risked appearing to neglect those commitments, and a central treasury that supported both Tabriz and the court faced hard choices. Reform's finance was thus a political issue and not merely a technical one.

Recruitment itself had to be negotiated. A district ordered to supply infantry might furnish young men unevenly, attempt to replace wealthier households' sons with poorer recruits, or seek exemptions through a governor's patronage. During service, men lost access to agricultural and artisanal work at home; their families could become dependent on wages that arrived late. An officer inspecting a battalion could therefore see uniformity of dress and drill while missing large differences in the circumstances under which its soldiers had been enlisted. The army's creation redistributed labor and risk among communities, making local consent, coercion, and compensation inseparable from fiscal policy.

In the prince's administration, troop lists, accounts of cartridges and powder, and payments to officers served related purposes. Each record could be used to estimate readiness, but each also created opportunities for manipulation. Commanders had incentives to overstate the number of men under arms if salaries were allotted by establishment strength. Treasury officials had incentives to delay disbursement when cash was scarce. An arsenal might report weapons completed without sufficient powder or trained gunners to use them. Closer supervision could improve accuracy but could also provoke opposition from men who had previously benefited from a less scrutinized system.

Foreign subsidies did not eliminate these difficulties. Even when a British payment was promised, it could be delayed or made conditional on political circumstances, and it could not permanently replace a domestic tax base. The problem was also one of priorities. The shah needed funds for the court, other governors defended their own provincial obligations, and the northwest suffered the direct damage of war. From an administrator's point of view, funding a regular army meant making commitments across multiple years without knowing the harvest, political stability, or the willingness of foreign partners to cooperate. The financial limitations of reform should thus be understood as systemic rather than as simple reluctance to spend.

Section 73.6

Sending Students Abroad: The Missions of 1811 and 1815

The decision to send Iranian students to Britain addressed a recognized weakness, the dependence on foreigners for skills. Abbas Mirza and his administrators concluded that Iran needed its own people trained in the techniques that European instructors brought. In 1811 two students traveled to Britain, and in 1815 five more followed. The two students of 1811 were Hajji Baba Afshar and Mohammad Kazem, son of the court painter; Hajji Baba studied medicine and chemistry, while Mohammad Kazem died in Britain in 1813. The second group of five included Mirza Saleh Shirazi and Mirza Ja’far Khan Tabrizi.

The students were sent for technical purposes. Their subjects included military engineering, artillery and gunnery, medicine, the manufacture of arms, languages, and printing. They faced obstacles familiar to later students abroad: unfamiliar languages, differences in schooling, the cost of residence, and the challenge of learning practical skills through apprenticeship in an environment very unlike their own. On return, the question became what to do with them. Positions had to be found, their skills had to be matched to government needs, and the conditions of work, including tools, materials, and colleagues, often lacked what they had used in Britain. A specialist who returned to find no workshop could do little.

The most famous of the group was Mirza Saleh Shirazi, who studied languages and other subjects and who observed British life with attention. He later wrote an account of his journey. Its value as evidence lies in what it shows an Iranian observer selecting as important in a foreign society. His interest in printing, in educational institutions, and in government, including Parliament and the monarchy's relation to law, reflects curiosity about the sources of European strength. But exposure to European institutions did not automatically convert him, or the officials who read of them, into advocates of constitutional change. His account reveals curiosity and a willingness to compare. It should not be read as an early program for representative government, which would have to wait for different circumstances and different advocates.

The two students who departed in 1811 can be identified more precisely. Hajji Baba Afshar, son of an officer serving the crown prince, traveled with Mohammad Kazem, a son of Abbas Mirza's court painter, and arrived in London in October that year. Hajji Baba was assigned medicine and chemistry, but his education was delayed by the practical difficulty of arranging teachers and placements. Mohammad Kazem died in Britain in 1813, before he could complete his course. Hajji Baba remained until 1819 and returned to become chief physician to the crown prince. These two biographies illustrate sharply different outcomes: one student died far from home; the other acquired skills that could be employed within an existing court medical office.

Among the five sent in 1815 were Mirza Saleh Shirazi and Mirza Ja'far Khan Tabrizi, later known as Moshir al-Dowleh. Mirza Saleh's experience included printing and the observation of public institutions, while Mirza Ja'far studied engineering and later participated in government service. Mirza Reza Mohandes-bashi, associated with engineering instruction in Britain, subsequently drew the plan for Dar al-Funun's building in Tehran; this provides a direct institutional link between an earlier student mission and a later reform project. The variety of assignments warns against treating the students as a uniform cohort of military cadets.

Studying abroad did not guarantee that a returning student could influence policy, but it made possible a new kind of professional career. A medical practitioner needed patients and institutional trust; an engineer needed employers willing to fund works; a translator required books, commissions, and administrative access. The court could confer offices without creating independent professional associations or broadly accessible schools. A useful measure of the missions, therefore, is not merely how many students departed or returned but whether their new skills were repeatedly used, taught to others, and embodied in texts, workshops, or public institutions. The surviving evidence is stronger for the careers of some individuals than for an aggregate national effect.

Section 73.7

Printing, Translation, Arsenals, and the Transfer of Technical Knowledge

Knowledge moved through people and through texts. Foreign instructors, returning students, diplomats, and artisans carried practical skills. Printed and translated works made some of that knowledge portable. Tabriz became the center of these activities. Translations of European military and technical works, produced with the help of interpreters, supplied manuals for drill, gunnery, fortification, and related fields, and workshops supported by the prince produced weapons and equipment.

Printing requires careful treatment. Printing in Iran did not begin with Abbas Mirza: Armenian printers had operated a press at New Julfa, Isfahan, from the seventeenth century, producing Armenian works. What the Tabriz initiatives represented was a government-supported effort to establish printing in Persian for state purposes. Sources differ on the date and sequence of the earliest presses and publications, with some accounts pointing to activity in Tabriz in the late 1810s, and the careful historian avoids attributing the whole development to one founder or year. Movable-type printing in Tabriz was followed by lithography, which was cheaper and better suited to Persian script and became the dominant technique for most of the century. Attributions of particular early imprints must still be checked against library catalogues, but the distinction between the early typographic press and later lithography is well documented.

The social effect of printing was real but limited. Costs were high, print runs small, and literacy confined to a minority. Printed technical works reached officers, officials, and educated elites rather than the general population, and manuscripts remained the dominant medium for many purposes. Nevertheless, printing helped standardize the transmission of information, supported translation as an administrative skill, and prepared the ground for the official gazette of 1851 and the broader print culture treated in Chapter 77.

Arsenals tell a similar story. The production of cannon, small arms, and ammunition required skilled labor, materials, and consistent supply. The workshops at Tabriz trained craftsmen and demonstrated that some European techniques could be reproduced locally. But output depended on the availability of iron, fuel, and funds, and on the presence of skilled personnel, and these conditions were not always met. Technical transfer was thus partial: it created pockets of competence without producing a self-sustaining industrial base.

The chronology of Persian-language printing is more definite than a general reference to the late 1810s suggests, although individual firsts remain disputed. Specialist bibliographical research places a movable-type press under Abbas Mirza's patronage at Tabriz around 1816–1817, associated with Mirza Zayn al-Abedin and the printing of Persian works. The date should not be confused with the earlier Armenian printing traditions of New Julfa, nor with the subsequent arrival of lithographic techniques. Sources identify Tabriz lithographic printing securely by 1832–1833, with reports of earlier experiments that do not establish continuous operation. This sequence places a typographic initiative, an interval of experiment, and a later lithographic industry into distinct stages.

The choice of printing method mattered. Movable type required casting and arranging letter forms and reproducing a script whose connected letters and calligraphic conventions presented technical challenges. Lithography allowed a scribe's page to be reproduced more directly and could preserve visual qualities valued in Persian manuscripts. Its adoption was therefore not simply a sign of technological backwardness relative to letterpress printing: it addressed local preferences, labor skills, and the economics of small print runs. A military manual, a legal notice, and a literary text had different readerships and different requirements for legibility and production.

Translation was similarly more than replacing words in one language with words from another. A French military term for a formation, a Russian word for an arsenal component, or an Ottoman administrative designation might have no exact equivalent in the vocabulary of Iranian officials. Translators had to choose whether to borrow a word, use an established Persian term, or explain an unfamiliar institution. These decisions affected whether a manual could be used consistently by officers and clerks. The outcome was a developing technical register rather than wholesale replacement of Persian scholarly traditions. Its later usefulness depended on trained readers, institutional continuity, and the survival of workshops and libraries.

Section 73.8

Evaluating Abbas Mirza's Reforms: Achievement, Defeat, and Structural Limits

Assessment must begin by separating the reforms from the wars. Iran's defeat in the Russian wars, which led to the settlements discussed in Chapters 70 and 71, did not by itself prove that every aspect of military reorganization had failed. The Russian army enjoyed advantages in resources, logistics, and numbers, and the strategic context was unfavorable. Regular Iranian forces also achieved successes, most notably in the Ottoman war of 1821–1823, where Iranian forces advanced into Ottoman territory and won significant engagements before an outbreak of cholera and inconclusive diplomacy led to the Treaty of Erzurum in 1823, which restored the earlier frontier. The outcome suggests that the new methods could be effective in some circumstances, and that the effectiveness of regular forces depended on how they were used and on the strength of the opponent.

At the same time the limits were real. Finance was inadequate and uneven, and training varied between units. Foreign instructors were essential and temporary. Recruitment depended on local cooperation that the state could not always enforce. The monarchy was politically fragmented, with princes, governors, and tribal leaders pursuing their own interests, and the regular army could not fully displace the older system. Abbas Mirza's position as crown prince also meant that his project depended on the succession: the army was in effect attached to the prince. When he died at Mashhad in 1833, a year before his father, the question became whether his initiatives had become institutions. His son Mohammad Mirza succeeded to the throne in 1834, and some of the military arrangements continued. But the program as a whole lost its sponsor and much of its coherence.

One measure of success is whether reform changed what commanders could do. Better-drilled infantry could hold a defensive position and work with artillery more effectively than an improvised levy, even if the army as a whole lacked the depth to defeat a great power. Another measure is fiscal: could the crown prince maintain these units during long periods without campaigning? A third is educational: could Iranian instructors, officers, and craftsmen reproduce the system after foreign personnel left? These tests yield different answers. Military historians who emphasize battle outcomes and historians of institutions who emphasize technical transmission are not necessarily evaluating the same achievement.

The Ottoman campaigns reveal both the promise and the complications of comparison. Abbas Mirza's armies could demonstrate disciplined action against Ottoman forces, but an opponent's local weaknesses, the presence of other Iranian commanders, epidemic disease, and European diplomatic intervention all affected the outcome. Success in one campaign did not automatically prove that the new army was prepared to meet Russian artillery and supply systems in the Caucasus. Conversely, a Russian battlefield defeat might reflect command and political decisions as well as equipment. The proper evidence is comparative: how particular units performed, how they were supported, and whether improvements appeared across repeated campaigns.

The legacy extended beyond one army. The prince's experiments helped create an expectation that a government should train specialists, keep more precise records, translate technical texts, and employ officials whose competence was not simply inherited. Such an expectation could survive a patron's death in the working practices of clerks and officers, even without an uninterrupted program of reform. Yet the absence of stable kingdom-wide finance and of independent institutions made progress vulnerable. Abbas Mirza did not create a uniform modern state; his achievement was to demonstrate the organizational possibilities and the recurring obstacles that later reformers confronted at a larger scale.

Historical Debate

Were Abbas Mirza's Reforms Defeated by External Circumstances or by Institutional Limits?

The question: Historians ask whether the limited outcome of Abbas Mirza's program is better explained by the strength of Iran's adversaries and the international situation, or by weaknesses inside the Qajar state.

Interpretation A — external circumstances: Russia's military and economic superiority, the withdrawal of European support, and the unfavorable terms of diplomacy limited what any Iranian reform could have achieved, regardless of institutional quality.
Interpretation B — institutional limits: The shortage of revenue, the dependence on a personal patron, the weak administrative apparatus, and the resistance of established interests prevented the reforms from becoming durable, whatever the international environment.

Evidence: Both draw on Iranian chronicles, British and Russian records, accounts of military campaigns, and financial information where available. The Ottoman campaign is often cited as evidence for the effectiveness of the new methods, while the fiscal and administrative record supports the argument about institutional constraints.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and the relative weight of factors, not the basic facts. Many historians combine the two explanations.

Why it matters: If external factors predominated, reform failed for reasons beyond Iranian control; if internal limits dominated, later reformers faced a structural problem that required institutional change.

Division III — From Abbas Mirza to Amir Kabir: Administrative Experience and Political Authority, 1833–1848

Section 73.9

Reform after Abbas Mirza: Mohammad Shah and the Institutional Inheritance, 1833–1848

Abbas Mirza died in 1833 and Fath Ali Shah in 1834. Abbas Mirza's son Mohammad Mirza, who had been governor in Tabriz and who had the support of Russian and British diplomats and of troops from Azerbaijan, succeeded as Mohammad Shah (r. 1834–1848). The new shah inherited the army his father had built, the administrators who had served in Tabriz, and a government with a long list of unresolved problems. He also had rivals, including relatives who claimed the throne, and early in his reign needed to establish his authority.

His chief minister was initially Mirza Abu'l-Qasem Qa'em-Maqam, the son of Mirza Bozorg and the most prominent administrator of the Tabriz tradition. He was executed in 1835 on the shah's orders, and the circumstances reveal the vulnerability of ministers. The sources emphasize the opposition of court rivals and the shah's suspicion of an overmighty servant, and they do not reduce the event to a single motive. The execution removed an experienced administrator and replaced him with Haji Mirza Aqasi, the shah's former tutor, who held the chief ministry until 1848. Aqasi's reputation in later narratives is poor, and he is often depicted as an eccentric mystic who neglected the state. The historian should treat that portrait cautiously, since it comes largely from his opponents, while recognizing that his administration was widely seen as ineffective and that financial and administrative problems accumulated.

Military life continued under Mohammad Shah. The regular infantry remained part of the army, foreign officers continued to serve, and Russian deserters formed units whose services he used. The Herat campaign of 1837–1838, discussed in Chapter 72, tested these forces in a different region and with a different outcome. Provincial uprisings occurred, notably in Khurasan, where Hasan Khan Salar rebelled beginning in 1846. The government's capacity remained limited, and troops were often unpaid. Yet the institutions of the earlier period did not vanish. Trained officers, craftsmen, translators, and administrators remained in service, and the idea that a state needed regular forces and trained officials had become part of the political vocabulary of the court.

That is the point of continuity. Changes of ruler and minister weakened reform without necessarily destroying every institution, because institutions have more than one source of support. What survived did so where personnel, routines, and interests were sufficiently embedded to continue. What was lost was the coherent sponsorship that had linked military organization, finance, and technical education into a single program. The next reformer would have to rebuild that coherence from the central government, with the experience of the earlier generation.

The ministerial transition in 1835 shows how administrative knowledge could be separated from political authority. Mirza Abu'l-Qasem Qa'em-Maqam possessed experience, contacts, and a commanding literary reputation, but his position depended on the favor of a new ruler who faced claimants to the throne and court factions seeking access to appointments. His execution removed a leading advocate for the Azerbaijan tradition of disciplined administration, while leaving the lower ranks of secretaries, accountants, officers, and provincial agents still in place. An institution may therefore persist in fragments while losing the leadership capable of coordinating those fragments into policy.

Haji Mirza Aqasi should not be treated only as the villain of a reformist narrative. As chief minister and the shah's former tutor he presided over attempts to strengthen the army and managed difficult foreign and provincial crises, but his administration also faced criticism for financial disorganization, favoritism, and limited success in the Herat campaign. Specialists have documented attempts at rearmament and the later dispatch of students to France. A historically balanced account can recognize these initiatives while assessing their limited effects and the difficulties of translating royal projects into sustainable institutions. The most useful comparison with Amir Kabir concerns methods of administration and accountability, not presumed differences in enlightenment.

Some changes widened rather than closed the reform network. Iranian students reached France during the 1840s; European techniques continued to circulate through diplomats and advisers; and officials serving in the provinces gained experience with increasingly elaborate financial and military correspondence. However, the employment of trained individuals did not establish a coherent civil service. Authority still moved through household appointments and patronage. When the 1848 succession approached, a young crown prince depended on officials who understood logistics, security, and finance but who had no guarantee that a change of ruler would preserve their positions. This was the institutional inheritance available to Mirza Taqi Khan.

Section 73.10

Mirza Taqi Khan's Administrative Education: Tabriz, Russia, and Erzurum

Mirza Taqi was born around 1807 in the village of Hazava, near Farahan. His father, Kerbala'i Mohammad Qorban, served as a cook in the household of Mirza Bozorg Qa'em-Maqam, and the boy grew up within that household. The connection was decisive. Through it he obtained an education in reading, writing, and accounting that would otherwise have been unavailable to a man of modest background, and he entered a network of administrators who knew one another's families and careers. Later opponents sometimes mocked his origins, an indication that Qajar political culture valued lineage and that a minister of humble family was exposed to the contempt of established elites.

His early career belonged to the Azerbaijan administration. He worked in the army's financial and registration offices at Tabriz and became familiar with the practical problems of military finance: recording troops, calculating pay, and supervising supplies. After the war with Russia and the murder of the Russian envoy Griboyedov at Tehran in 1829, Iran sent a mission to St. Petersburg under Prince Khosrow Mirza to offer apology, and Mirza Taqi accompanied it. The visit exposed him to Russian administrative practice, to the organization of a large bureaucratic state, and to the realities of the empire that had defeated Iran.

A second formative experience came in the 1840s, when he served as one of the Iranian commissioners on the boundary and dispute-settlement commission meeting at Erzurum in the Ottoman Empire, with Russian and British mediators. There, over several years, he dealt with Ottoman officials, observed the reforms known as the Tanzimat, and encountered diplomacy as sustained negotiation about territory and rights. The experience taught him about fiscal organization, the training of officials, and the instruments of an expanding central government in a neighboring Muslim state.

It would be wrong to portray him as a simple imitator of Russia or the Ottoman Empire. He learned from both, and also from British and Iranian practice, and he evaluated what he saw by the problems of the Iranian state. Observers noted his insistence on state authority, his suspicion of foreign pretensions, and his preference for economy in government. His program combined Iranian administrative traditions, drawn from the Tabriz school of finance, with selected borrowings. The relevant context for him was not Westernization but the challenge of rebuilding a government's capacity to act.

His rise illustrates a distinctive path into the bureaucracy. Mirza Taqi's father, Karbala'i Mohammad Qorban, was employed in Mirza Bozorg's household, and the boy received instruction through proximity to a family of professional administrators rather than through inherited aristocratic status. Before he held high office he learned the work of military registration, eventually becoming responsible for supervising finances and supplies in the Azerbaijan army. These assignments trained him to connect an order from a superior with a register, a payment, a quantity of provisions, and a person accountable for delivery. That habit of administrative verification later informed his distrust of unexamined claims to stipends and provincial revenue.

On the 1829–1830 embassy led by Khosrow Mirza, Amir Kabir served as a scribe while visiting Russian cities and institutions. Scholarly accounts record the mission's exposure to arsenals, educational establishments, factories, financial organizations, and departments of government. It would be misleading to infer from a visit that he immediately embraced any one foreign institutional model: no comprehensive personal Russian travelogue by him survives. But the experience offered a series of practical comparisons concerning the scale of a treasury, the training of administrators, and the existence of specialized bodies able to perform tasks beyond the household of a monarch.

The Erzurum negotiations were equally formative because they lasted far longer than a diplomatic ceremonial visit. For several years Mirza Taqi helped negotiate disputed districts, compensation claims, and the position of Mohammerah with Ottoman representatives and British and Russian intermediaries. He had to determine which instructions from Tehran were workable, assemble historical and geographic arguments, and persist through changes in other delegations' priorities. Ottoman Tanzimat reforms provided a nearby example of centralizing government in a Muslim polity. Their relevance was selective: they suggested methods for supervising officials, but they did not supply a ready-made constitution for Qajar Iran.

Section 73.11

The Succession of 1848 and Amir Kabir's Rise to Power

Mohammad Shah died in September 1848. His heir, Naser al-Din, was then a youth of about seventeen, serving as governor in Tabriz, and the succession was not secure. Other princes had ambitions, provincial unrest continued, and the capital was far away. Mirza Taqi, who had returned from Erzurum and was attached to the crown prince's household, took a decisive role. He organized the young prince's departure for Tehran, assembled money and military support, and helped to ensure that authority passed without a contested succession. The sources praise his energy and loyalty, though they come largely from admirers and later narratives, and details of the journey should be treated cautiously.

On Naser al-Din's accession, Mirza Taqi became chief minister. He received the title Amir-e Nezam, "commander of the army," reflecting the military dimension of his position, and was later styled Amir-e Kabir, "the great commander," and Atabak, a title with the sense of guardian to a young prince. He also married the shah's sister, which tied him to the royal family and signaled the monarch's confidence. These honors were the formal recognition of authority that in practice depended on the continuing trust of a very young ruler.

That trust was precarious from the beginning. Naser al-Din was inexperienced, and his mother Mahd-e Olya, other royal women, courtiers, and officials had interests in influencing him. Amir Kabir's origins, his insistence on exclusive control of the state's business, and the severity of his financial policy made him enemies from the outset. Among them was Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri, an official who would later replace him. The political opposition within the court thus emerged almost at once, and the minister's security rested on a single relationship.

The 1848 transfer of power also had a material timetable. Courtiers could announce a succession in Tabriz, but the new ruler still needed transport, escorts, money for officers, and reliable messengers to prevent rival princes from establishing control in Tehran. Mirza Taqi's ability to assemble these resources mattered more immediately than any abstract declaration of reformist principles. He made himself indispensable at a moment when administrative competence could affect dynastic survival. The connection between his early responsibility for the army's accounts and his later authority as minister was therefore direct: the same skills that kept troops supplied helped make a coronation politically possible.

The titles he received marked stages in the consolidation of his position. After leaving Tabriz, Naser al-Din Shah gave him the rank of Amir-e Nezam, with responsibility for the army. In Tehran in October 1848 he became the kingdom's principal minister and received the honorifics Amir-e Kabir and Atabak. These distinctions mattered because a military commission and the leadership of the civil administration were not identical forms of authority. The young shah's selection of an adviser as tutor-like guardian also created ambiguity: did the minister serve merely as the king's agent, or could he independently decide what the state required? Opponents could exploit that ambiguity as his power grew.

His marriage to the shah's sister Ezzat al-Dowleh on 17 February 1849 further integrated him into the royal household. A dynastic marriage conveyed confidence and access, but it also entangled policy with family resentments and contests over rank. The confrontation was already visible in a March 1849 mutiny by some Azerbaijani troops at Tehran, which threatened the minister. Support from the Friday prayer leader and mobilized bazaar merchants helped end the crisis. This early episode makes clear that Amir Kabir's later dismissal was not the result of one sudden palace quarrel: ministerial authority had been challenged almost from the beginning, and his survival depended on alliances beyond the ruler himself.

Section 73.12

Defining the Reform Agenda: Central Authority, Bureaucracy, and the Limits of Ministerial Power

Amir Kabir's program rested on a view of the central government's role. In his understanding, the state needed a strong central executive, led by a chief minister who could supervise all departments, control expenditure, and discipline officials. The offices he depended on included the court secretaries and financial officers (the mostowfis), who kept the accounts, and the provincial governors, who were responsible for collecting revenue and maintaining order. Existing religious and judicial institutions, including the courts of the ulama and the customary courts, formed another layer of authority that he sought to coordinate with, and in some respects to limit.

His preference was for greater central supervision and the reduction of independent sources of political authority. This did not mean transforming the monarchy. He worked to strengthen it, with the shah at its center and himself as its chief administrator. It is crucial to distinguish this program from later constitutional demands. Constitutionalism, as it emerged in the later nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and as Chapters 77 and 78 explain, sought to limit royal power through law and representative bodies. Amir Kabir sought to increase the efficiency of royal government by disciplining the administration. The two projects might at times converge, as when both opposed arbitrary exactions, but they differed in their conception of political authority.

The logic of his approach shaped the specific measures examined in Division IV. If the central government was to control revenue, it had to supervise the accounts of provincial officials. If it was to pay a regular army, it had to cut other expenses. If it was to administer justice more consistently, it had to curb the power of local magnates. And if it was to have the trained personnel these tasks demanded, it had to educate them. Each measure threatened established interests, and each depended on the minister's ability to remain in office.

His conception of authority operated through specific mechanisms. Appointments were to be reviewed; salary claims were to be documented; reports from provincial governors were to be checked against other information; and civil and military offices were expected to follow instructions issued from the capital. In a monarchy where provincial government often combined an officeholder's personal resources with the state's, such directives did not simply reorganize paperwork. They threatened customary opportunities to distribute favors, obtain private revenue, or protect clients. A more reliable administration required the minister to discipline officials whose cooperation the same government continued to need.

Amir Kabir's administrative program also depended on the distinction between regulating religious institutions and eliminating religion from public life. Shi'i scholars remained central to education, law, charitable foundations, and the moral standing of the monarchy. He sometimes resisted their capacity to grant refuge or intervene in state affairs and was prepared to use coercion to enforce royal decisions. This brought disputes with some clerics without producing a uniform conflict between government and the entire religious establishment. Different scholars had different relationships with the court, and institutional reform could benefit religious constituencies when it checked provincial abuses. The key issue was who would exercise final authority in particular disputes.

Centralization must therefore be treated as a political project, not as a synonym for representative government. An independent legislature was not the minister's objective, nor did his reforms introduce secure rights against the crown. His vision relied on an energetic chief minister acting in the name of a sovereign who could revoke his commission. This arrangement allowed rapid changes when royal confidence was strong and made them acutely vulnerable when it weakened. It also helps explain a paradox: measures meant to protect the ruler's authority could make the person implementing them appear to overshadow that ruler. The problem would recur at the climax of his ministry.

Division IV — Amir Kabir's Administrative, Military, Fiscal, and Social Reforms, 1848–1851

Section 73.13

Fiscal Reconstruction: Tax Assessment, Government Expenditure, and Administrative Discipline

The treasury was the first object of Amir Kabir's attention. The reforms attributed to him included reviewing tax assessments, supervising provincial accounts more closely, regulating salaries and pensions, and reducing payments that he regarded as unnecessary or unjustified. The aim was to reduce waste and to increase the proportion of revenue available to the central government, thereby enabling it to pay the army and fund new institutions. The method was administrative: officials were required to submit accounts, appointments were scrutinized, and customary gifts and allowances were curtailed.

The measures had to be carried out through existing local structures. Governors, revenue officials, and notables controlled the information on which assessment depended and had interests in how it was used. A decree issued in Tehran meant little unless officials in provinces complied, and compliance could be negotiated or avoided. Amir Kabir's response combined supervision with dismissals and punishments, and contemporary accounts stress the severity with which he dealt with officials found guilty of embezzlement or abuse. Whether this produced lasting administrative discipline is more doubtful than the intentions are clear.

The cost of reducing allowances was political. Courtiers, royal relatives, officeholders, and members of influential families lost stipends and privileges they regarded as their due, and their loss became the minister's opponents' gain. Some measures also touched religious figures and others who relied on state payments. A reformer who disciplined spending thus alienated precisely those who had access to the shah.

It is necessary to distinguish what was intended, what is documented, and what endured. Reports credit Amir Kabir with improving government finances in the short term, and some of his procedures were later cited as models. But he did not create a permanent, comprehensive modern budgetary state. Systematic budgeting, centralized treasury control, and professional civil service were not established in his three years, and after his dismissal many of his policies were weakened or abandoned. The significance of his fiscal work lies in the demonstration that a chief minister could attempt sustained supervision, and in the expectations it created.

Reviewing payments was more controversial than making a general promise of economy. Government pensions and allowances could be attached to office, service, family position, or royal favor; their legal and customary bases were not identical. A payment that the minister regarded as unwarranted might be a household's central source of income, a courtier's means of maintaining a retinue, or the basis of an influential family's public status. Reducing such expenditure could improve the treasury without making those affected accept the measure as legitimate. A government that needed elite cooperation faced a recurring trade-off between immediate fiscal savings and the political cost of enforcing them.

Tax supervision encountered another difficulty: an assessment register could contain customary obligations but not the actual harvest or a district's capacity to remit cash. Governors and local fiscal officers possessed practical knowledge of land, irrigation, cultivation, and arrears, and some used that knowledge to protect their communities while others used it to conceal revenues. More centralized accounting promised comparability and reduced opportunities for appropriation, yet it also made Tehran dependent on the honesty and competence of the very officials under scrutiny. Amir Kabir's disciplinary methods may have induced obedience in the short term; they could also encourage concealment or resistance when compliance threatened existing arrangements.

Military finance illustrates why these measures were linked. Reliable pay made it easier to retain soldiers, while more accurate troop registers reduced the possibility that commanders could claim funds for absent men. Funds saved from stipends could support arms, garrisons, and education, but budgetary transfers were not automatic. Expenditure still had to be authorized, physically delivered, and protected against diversion. It is important to distinguish a reported reduction in waste from the establishment of a permanent national audit system. The surviving evidence supports a serious effort to centralize financial supervision and improve regularity, but not a fully integrated modern treasury able to govern every province by standardized accounts.

Section 73.14

Military Administration, Provincial Control, and the Reassertion of Central Government

Military reform under Amir Kabir continued the principles of the Nezam-e Jadid and tried to improve their administration. His measures concerned recruitment, the regular payment of troops, the discipline of officers, the production of weapons, the administration of garrisons, and the security of provinces. Pay mattered most: an army whose soldiers were paid late or not at all was an army that could not be relied on, and the effort to regularize payment was inseparable from the fiscal measures described above. Orders also addressed the conduct of officers and the registration of troops, partly to prevent the common practice of collecting pay for nonexistent soldiers.

Foreign instruction continued, with an important shift. Amir Kabir was wary of the dependence on Britain and Russia that earlier reform had involved, and he looked to other European states for instructors and equipment. This outlook, which also shaped the recruitment of teachers for Dar al-Funun from Central Europe, reflected a diplomatic aim as well as a technical one: to obtain expertise without increasing the leverage of the two powers whose rivalry dominated Iran's foreign relations, a subject treated in Chapter 72.

The relationship between military reform and provincial control was close. In Khurasan, Hasan Khan Salar, a provincial magnate, had raised a revolt in 1846 that continued into the new reign, and its suppression became a test of the central government's reach. The campaign against him, conducted in the early years of Naser al-Din's reign, showed both the persistence of regional resistance and the government's growing ability to mobilize force against it. The suppression of provincial challenges was a central part of Amir Kabir's conception of authority.

It would nonetheless be an overstatement to claim that he created an entirely new national military system. What he did was to improve organization and administration within a structure inherited from earlier decades, and to link it more closely to the central treasury. The military changes of his ministry remained incomplete when he fell, and his successors did not sustain his emphasis on discipline.

Amir Kabir approached the army as a system of offices and obligations rather than merely a collection of regiments. A commander required an identifiable establishment of men; soldiers required wages and equipment; arsenals needed raw materials and maintenance; and the treasury needed records to evaluate competing demands. Plans for more regular inspection and military education were intended to link these functions. Bringing them into one administrative chain was difficult because provincial commanders had long exercised discretion over recruitment and pay and could interpret a request for closer scrutiny as an accusation of disloyalty.

The Khurasan revolt offers a concrete measure of the coercive side of centralization. Hasan Khan Salar had challenged the government from the mid-1840s, and control of Mashhad affected both revenue and the prestige of a monarchy claiming authority over the Imam Reza shrine city. Amir Kabir sent forces to restore control; the second expedition, led by Soltan Morad Mirza, defeated Salar and captured him. Salar, one of his sons, and a brother were executed in 1850. The severity of the punishment demonstrated the minister's determination to make provincial rebellion costly. It also underlines why political order and reform cannot be narrated solely in terms of efficient accounting or progressive education: the ministry used exemplary violence to enforce authority.

Foreign expertise remained useful but posed a dilemma of dependence. British and Russian officers represented powers with interests that often conflicted with Iran's. Seeking instructors from Habsburg and other Central European territories was a way of diversifying sources of military knowledge rather than eliminating all external influence. It did not guarantee political neutrality: foreign teachers still required salaries, translations, supplies, and royal protection. The schools and military administration that grew from these decisions might create Iranian specialists over time, but for the immediate future the state remained vulnerable to shortages of trained personnel and to political changes that interrupted contracts.

Section 73.15

Public Works, Industry, Public Health, and the Protection of Communities

Amir Kabir's concerns extended beyond the court and army. Reports attribute to his ministry efforts to promote domestic manufacturing, including textiles and other goods, to improve roads and water supply, and to encourage the construction and maintenance of public works. Government sponsorship of manufacturing reflected the view that Iran should reduce its dependence on imports and develop skills of its own. The evidence for the scale and durability of these projects is limited, and the effect on the overall economy was modest. Their significance is as evidence of a conception of the state as an active promoter of economic life.

Public health attracted attention as well. Vaccination against smallpox, introduced to Iran earlier in the century through European physicians and Iranian practitioners, was encouraged, and measures were taken against epidemic disease. Medical instruction, which became part of the curriculum of Dar al-Funun, was another form of investment in health. As in other fields, the effects were uneven and depended on the reach of government, which in rural areas was slight.

Policy toward religious communities deserves notice. Amir Kabir reportedly tried to restrain arbitrary taxation and mistreatment of non-Muslim minorities, including Armenians, Assyrians, Zoroastrians, and Jews, as part of a general effort to regularize taxation and limit arbitrary exactions. The motives were mixed: a concern for orderly administration, fiscal calculation, and a view that predictable treatment of taxpayers served the state. His relations with religious authorities were also tense, as he sought to limit certain privileges and the use of sanctuary, though the details vary in the sources. The specific history of the Babi movement, whose leader was executed at Tabriz in 1850 under his government, and the suppression of the movement belong to Chapter 74. Here it is enough to say that the security context of those years affected his policies and that centralization had coercive dimensions.

That last point should not be minimized. State action in this period was not uniformly benevolent. Taxation, conscription, the suppression of rebels, punishments of officials, and the repression of dissent all imposed burdens, and the same ministry that promoted vaccination and public works also used force against opponents. The reforms affected artisans and merchants through economic policy and regulation, soldiers' families through military service and pay, provincial populations through taxation, and the poor through the uneven reach of the state's protection.

Government encouragement of manufactures had practical limits. A workshop could receive an order for cloth, weapons, or equipment, yet still depend on imported tools, unstable supplies of materials, and the purchasing decisions of officials. Promoting domestic production could preserve skilled labor and produce goods useful to the army, but without sustained orders there was little reason for private craftsmen to reorganize their businesses around government initiatives. Imported manufactures also appealed to merchants and consumers for reasons of price or quality; ministerial preference alone could not transform those market conditions. Evidence of successful demonstration projects should therefore not be mistaken for proof of general industrial growth.

Vaccination presents a similar tension between central intention and local implementation. Smallpox inoculation and vaccination had entered Iranian medical practice before Amir Kabir, often through a mixture of European physicians and Iranian practitioners. Ministerial support could give such measures official status, encourage training, and signal that epidemic protection was a responsibility of government. But reaching rural communities required transport, trusted practitioners, storage of materials, and cooperation from local authorities. The survival of a decree is weaker evidence of public-health outcomes than records of vaccinations, expenditures, or changes in mortality, most of which are incomplete for this period.

Predictable treatment of religious minorities could also serve more than one purpose. Limiting arbitrary levies on Armenian or Zoroastrian households might reduce abuse and make obligations easier to calculate; it could simultaneously protect productive merchants and taxpayers whose livelihoods strengthened the treasury. Generalizations about tolerance, however, must be qualified by uneven implementation and the very different conditions faced by communities in individual provinces. The same state that sought orderly fiscal treatment also employed violence in suppressing resistance, including the Babi movement. Historical analysis should hold these policies together without equating their motivations or importing Chapter 74's detailed theological and political narrative into this administrative chapter.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reform in Ordinary Households

For most Iranians, reform was experienced through its practical effects. A household in a district subject to recruitment quotas might lose a son to the army, while a family fortunate enough to receive regular pay from a soldier's service gained a cash income. A village that owed taxes dealt with collectors whose conduct might improve or worsen depending on the vigor of supervision. A parent offered smallpox vaccination for a child confronted a new practice whose benefits were not always understood or trusted. Artisans and merchants in Tehran or Tabriz encountered government regulation, new manufactures, and the competition of imports.

These experiences left few direct records. Most sources are official or written by elites, and historians must infer ordinary effects from administrative orders, travelers' accounts, and later memory. What can be said with confidence is that reform's burdens and benefits were distributed unevenly, that its reach was greatest in cities and near the center, and that the ordinary population had little voice in setting its terms.

Section 73.16

The Government Gazette: Vaqaye-ye Ettefaqiyeh and the Communication of Reform

On 7 February 1851 the first issue of Vaqaye-ye Ettefaqiyeh ("Events of the Day") appeared in Tehran. It was the first regular official government newspaper in Iran, and it served the administration's purposes: publishing royal orders and appointments, reporting provincial news, announcing administrative decisions, and presenting information about developments in science, technology, and public life, including news from abroad.

It was not Iran's first newspaper. Mirza Saleh Shirazi had issued an earlier paper, Kaghaz-e Akhbar, in 1837, which began regular publication in May 1837 and was distinct from a permanent official government gazette. The distinction matters: the 1851 gazette is significant for its regularity and official status and for the continuity of a government publication, which lasted, under changing titles, for decades.

The gazette's reach and effect had clear limits. Literacy was restricted, circulation was concentrated among officials and elites, and the content was supervised by the government. There was a difference between announcing a decree and securing its enforcement, and the publication of an order did not guarantee its implementation. The paper was an instrument of government communication, intended to shape the information available to officials and to project the state's image of itself. It was not an independent political press. The latter would develop later in the century, in Iranian-language papers published abroad and, after 1906, in the constitutional press, themes taken up in Chapter 77.

The history of the gazette begins with a distinction between printing a notice and maintaining an administrative channel. Earlier Iranian newspapers had appeared, and Mirza Saleh's paper commenced regular publication in May 1837 rather than being merely a single isolated experiment. What changed in 1851 was that a continuing official newspaper became part of the government's routine presentation of its affairs. Its first issue appeared on 7 February under the new ministry. The enterprise required a press, paper, paid personnel, decisions about content, and a means of circulating copies to readers who had influence within provincial administrations or the capital.

For officials, the paper offered a shared point of reference. An appointment announced publicly could be compared against later conduct; an account of military developments could shape expectations of government action; a notice of technical instruction could help legitimize expenditure on education. Yet the gazette also selected and framed information to favor the monarchy. Reports of improvement were statements of official priority, not necessarily independent measures of success. A reader evaluating its claims should ask whether a decree was implemented, whether a province acknowledged it, and how government statements compare with travel accounts or correspondence.

The admission notice for Dar al-Funun in issue 43 of November 1851 provides an example of these functions. It made a new institution visible to the class of households from which the state hoped to recruit students; it also communicated expectations about social standing and age before the school opened. The newspaper thus links the administrative, educational, and cultural dimensions of reform. Its history should not be interpreted as an inevitable march toward a free press. It began as a royal instrument of publicity and discipline, while later generations would adapt printing and journalism to purposes that differed substantially from those of its founders.

Division V — Dar al-Funun, Court Resistance, and the Historical Outcomes of Reform, 1850–1852 and After

Section 73.17

Founding Dar al-Funun: The New School and the State's Educational Objectives

The idea of a government school for technical and military instruction grew out of the experience of the preceding decades. Abbas Mirza's student missions and foreign instructors had shown the need for trained personnel, and Amir Kabir, who had seen Russian and Ottoman institutions, planned a school in Tehran that would educate officers and technically qualified officials at home. Planning and construction took place in 1850–1851, with a building erected beside the royal citadel and Iranian officials, architects, and translators involved in the work.

Its initial purpose was practical: to train officers for infantry, cavalry, and artillery, and specialists in engineering, medicine, pharmacy, and mining, for service to the state. Broader scientific and linguistic subjects developed alongside these aims. Because Iran lacked teachers of the relevant disciplines, instructors were recruited in Europe, principally from the Austrian Empire and neighboring Central European lands, a choice consistent with Amir Kabir's wish to avoid increasing British and Russian leverage. The recruited specialists included Jakob Eduard Polak (medicine), Alfred de Gumoëns (infantry), Johann von Nemiro (cavalry), Captain Zatti (engineering), Joseph Czarnotta (mineralogy), and August Kržiž (artillery), whose appointments are documented in the institutional history of Dar al-Funun. Iranian translators were essential to making instruction possible.

The chronology is critical and is easily garbled. Planning and preparation occurred in 1850 and 1851. Amir Kabir was dismissed in November 1851. Dar al-Funun was formally inaugurated on 29 December 1851, after his dismissal and shortly before his execution on 10 January 1852. The instructors arrived at about the time of the dismissal. The school therefore opened under the new ministry, with the shah's support, and that fact is essential to understanding why it survived. The founder was removed, but the monarch had adopted the institution as his own.

Planning began before the school possessed its later name. In instructions issued in August 1850, Amir Kabir directed the Iranian legation in St. Petersburg to seek teachers in technical and military disciplines. Contemporaneous descriptions used several titles for the intended establishment, including a new school and a military academy. The building was laid out near Tehran's royal citadel on a former military exercise ground. Mirza Reza Mohandes-bashi, who had received instruction in Britain after the 1815 student mission, prepared the plan; Mohammad-Taqi Me'mar-bashi supervised construction under the oversight of Bahram Mirza. Its courtyard and teaching rooms incorporated familiar Iranian architectural arrangements into a new institutional purpose.

Recruitment exposed the constraints of founding a school before there was a domestic body of qualified teachers. Contracts for instructors from the Austrian Empire and adjoining Central European regions were negotiated in Vienna and signed in August 1851. The party included Alfred Baron de Gumoëns for infantry, Johann von Nemiro for cavalry, Captain Zatti for engineering, Joseph Czarnotta for mineralogy, August Kržiž for artillery, and Jakob Eduard Polak for medicine, with pharmacy also assigned to Polak. Their individual languages and specializations required translation into an educational program that Iranian students could follow. Not all the specialists initially requested could be obtained.

The teachers reached Tehran on 24 November 1851, just after Amir Kabir had fallen from office, according to a detailed institutional history. This contrast was more than a dramatic coincidence: contracts had already been signed, teaching rooms had been prepared, and the court had committed resources that a successor could continue to authorize. The foreign minister Mirza Mohammad-Ali Khan Shirazi oversaw the opening on 29 December. The departure of the minister who conceived the school did not compel cancellation of the staff's appointments. A functioning institutional project had begun to separate from the political standing of its first patron.

Section 73.18

Inside Dar al-Funun: Students, Teachers, Science, and Institutional Durability

The curriculum reflected the school's technical aims. Courses were organized around infantry, cavalry, and artillery training, engineering, medicine, pharmacy, and mining, with mathematics, natural sciences, and European languages, notably French, taught as supporting subjects. Teaching depended on translation: instructors lectured through interpreters, and textbooks had to be translated into Persian. These arrangements were slow and imperfect. Students arrived with unequal preparation, and some lacked the mathematical foundations the courses assumed. Examinations and the preparation of teaching materials developed gradually.

Several individuals were central. Polak, an Austrian physician, taught medicine and later wrote about Iran. Other European instructors taught military and technical subjects. Iranian translators and officials, among them Rezaqoli Khan Hedayat, who later became a prominent administrator of the school and a historian, supplied the linguistic and organizational work without which instruction could not have proceeded. Their contribution deserves as much emphasis as that of the foreigners, since they converted foreign knowledge into usable form.

The social base was narrow. The school was initially intended for a male population closely associated with government service and with privileged families, and its first students were in large part drawn from this milieu. Actual enrollment, however, appears to have been somewhat more varied than the original recruitment plan, and over time the student body broadened. Women were not admitted, and the school's benefits flowed to a small segment of society. Even so, it produced graduates who entered the army, the administration, medicine, translation, and teaching, and who formed networks that later reform movements drew on.

The school endured after Amir Kabir's removal for specific reasons. It had a building, a staff, a curriculum, and a student body. It had the shah's continuing support, since Naser al-Din regarded it as a royal institution and took pride in it. It was useful to the state, because it trained officers and officials. And it developed its own administrators, routines, and professional ties. Such features gave it more resilience than reforms that depended on a single minister's presence, and its experience exemplifies the contrast between a personal achievement and an institution.

In its initial form Dar al-Funun was not a modern university with faculties open to broad public competition. The formal admission notice in issue 43 of Vaqaye-ye Ettefaqiyeh, dated 28 November 1851, asked for sons of the a'yan and ashraf—elite and notable households—approximately fourteen to fifteen years of age. The authorities first contemplated a comparatively small intake, but scholarship documents about 105 students in its first year from a somewhat wider social range. The divergence between the recruitment plan and actual enrollment is important evidence: institutions adapt to the availability of applicants, and social categories in administrative notices do not perfectly predict who ultimately attends.

Teaching conditions reveal the challenge of translating ambition into daily work. Some students had studied mathematics or traditional learned subjects and advanced rapidly; others required basic work in Persian, Arabic, or calculation before attempting engineering and military science. European instructors needed interpreters. Rezaqoli Khan Hedayat, known as Lala-bashi, was central to administrative supervision, while Iranian translators and teachers such as Mirza Malkom Khan helped bridge language and technical instruction. Books, instruments, and examinations had to be prepared. A program for artillery or medicine could only become a repeatable curriculum if students and teachers agreed on terminology, sequence, and standards of achievement.

The school's budget and supervision also matter to its survival. Naser al-Din Shah visited and approved its activities; subsequent directors and officials handled enrollment, discipline, and the distribution of resources. An account attributed to Lady Sheil gives an early operating estimate of about 7,750 tumans annually, roughly half directed to European teachers. The figure is a contemporary estimate, not a certified budget, but it illustrates how recurrent costs—rather than a single founding expenditure—made the school dependent on continued patronage. Later dedicated revenues and the growth of Iranian teaching personnel reduced some vulnerabilities and created others. Durability was an achievement of repeated financing and administration, not simply of the original inauguration.

Historical Vocabulary
Dar al-Funun, Technical Education, Translation, Professional Training

Definition: Dar al-Funun (Persian, from Arabic), a name conventionally understood as a “polytechnic school,” was the name of the polytechnic school opened in Tehran in 1851. Technical education refers to instruction in applied skills such as engineering, gunnery, medicine, and mining. Professional training prepares individuals for specific occupations, here military and administrative service.

Historical context: The school was a state institution created for state purposes and operated alongside older religious schools (madrasas), which continued to provide religious and legal education.

Why it matters: It introduced a model of state-organized, subject-based instruction with foreign teachers and translation, and trained a cadre of officials.

Potential misunderstanding: Dar al-Funun was not a modern university and did not replace the madrasa system. Its numbers were small and its recruitment selective.

Section 73.19

Reform and Political Resistance: Amir Kabir's Dismissal and Execution, 1851–1852

By 1851 Amir Kabir faced a coalition of interests. Courtiers and officials resented his economies and his control of access to the shah. Royal women, including the queen mother Mahd-e Olya, had their own stakes in influence over the young monarch. Military officers, established administrative families, and religious figures disliked specific measures. Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri, an ambitious official, emerged as the leading rival. These groups did not necessarily share a program, and the conflict should not be reduced to reformers against reactionaries. Many of the minister's opponents acted from personal and factional motives, and others from genuine disagreement with his methods.

The nature of authority in an absolute monarchy was decisive. Amir Kabir held power by royal delegation, and nothing in the political system protected him against the withdrawal of the shah's confidence. The more he concentrated authority, the more he relied on that confidence and the more his opponents sought to undermine it. They worked on the young shah's suspicions, suggesting that the minister was overbearing or that he threatened royal authority. The sources offer varied accounts of the details, and later narratives, shaped by sympathy for him or hostility to his enemies, tend to assign blame simply. Responsible history must acknowledge uncertainty about what exactly was said to the shah and with what effect.

In November 1851 the shah dismissed him. He was sent to Kashan, where he was held at the Fin garden, and he was killed there on 10 January 1852 by order of the shah, in the bathhouse at Fin. The decision to execute him was the shah's, and responsibility for it lies with the monarch and with those who urged it. Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri succeeded as chief minister. The dismissal and execution are separate events, about seven weeks apart, and the opening of Dar al-Funun fell between them.

The opposition to Amir Kabir did not emerge only in his final months. The March 1849 mutiny by Azerbaijani troops had shown how quickly grievances could become a demand for his removal, and the subsequent campaigns against provincial insurgents displayed the coercive consequences of his assertion of central authority. Officials stripped of stipends, courtiers excluded from decision-making, and families whose appointments were questioned could cooperate against him without agreeing on any alternative program. Some welcomed particular reforms yet rejected the concentration of power in one minister. Their complaints cannot be reduced to a simple division between supporters and opponents of modern technology.

The queen mother Mahd-e Olya and Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri occupy prominent places in later narratives of the fall, but evidence for every conversation and motive is uneven. Accounts written under the influence of court rivalries or retrospective admiration for Amir Kabir can overstate the coherence of the opposition. It is historically stronger to identify the institutional vulnerability: ministers had no tenure secure against the ruler, and access to the young shah was itself a source of power. Amir Kabir's close control over royal business made him effective at governing while making him appear, to some, as a barrier between the sovereign and his subjects. The system had no independent institution capable of adjudicating that conflict.

His dismissal, removal to Kashan, and execution demonstrate the distinction between political defeat and institutional extinction. The shah chose to dismiss him and ultimately authorized his death; opposition alone did not make these decisions inevitable. Meanwhile Dar al-Funun opened and officials who had served the minister continued to work. Some fiscal economies were reversed or softened, while technical education found sponsors beyond its founder. Later memorial portrayals made Amir Kabir into an emblem of lost national possibility, but that retrospective image should not erase either his coercive practices or the actual mechanisms by which some of his work survived. The question is not whether he was personally heroic but which forms of authority his policies attempted to establish and why they proved fragile.

Historical Debate

Why Did Amir Kabir Fall from Power?

The question: Was Amir Kabir's downfall chiefly the product of personal court rivalry, or of structural features of his position and program?

Interpretation A — court rivalry: Intrigue by the queen mother, Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri, and others, played on the young shah's insecurities and removed a minister they resented.
Interpretation B — concentration of power: Amir Kabir's exclusive control of government and his intolerance of rivals made enemies and alarmed the shah.
Interpretation C — threatened interests: His fiscal and administrative measures harmed courtiers, officeholders, and others who had a stake in the existing distribution of resources.
Interpretation D — limits of ministerial authority: Under absolute monarchy a minister could not secure himself institutionally; his fall expressed the structural dependence of every minister on royal favor.

Evidence: Court histories, diplomatic reports from British and Russian representatives, and later memoirs. Each source has its own bias.

Points of disagreement: Mostly causation and weight, and some uncertainty over facts about the shah's role and the sequence of events.

Why it matters: The explanation determines whether reform's failure is attributed to individuals, to specific policies, or to the political structure itself.

Student task: The interpretations are not mutually exclusive. Construct an argument showing how they might combine, and identify which evidence would test each.

Section 73.20

Historical Synthesis: Why Reform Survived Unevenly in Qajar Iran

Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir were related but distinct reformers. Abbas Mirza worked from a provincial base to build a military establishment, and his additional initiatives in students, printing, and technical knowledge served that end. Amir Kabir worked from the center to strengthen the whole government, with the army, finance, and education as parts of a unified conception of state authority. The first relied on a prince's personal position and the resources of one province; the second on a minister's delegated authority and the treasury. Both were therefore vulnerable, and in different ways: the first to the succession and financing, the second to royal favor and court opposition.

Comparing the two clarifies what endured. Dar al-Funun survived, with its staff, curriculum, and royal support. Elements of the regular military, trained technical personnel, translators, printing, and administrative knowledge also continued in some form. The broader fiscal and political reorganization was far more limited in durability. Measures that depended on the minister's presence, such as close supervision of accounts and curbs on privilege, did not outlast him. The distinction reveals the conditions of survival: an initiative lasted when it had sufficient personnel, material resources, routines, and political support to continue without its founder.

Later reformers inherited practical methods, trained officials, and new expectations. The graduates of Dar al-Funun, officials who remembered Amir Kabir, and the example of earlier experiments shaped the thinking of administrators and intellectuals in the decades that followed. But this inheritance should not be read teleologically. Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir did not lead inevitably to constitutionalism, which arose from different circumstances, among them commercial concessions, popular mobilization, new political ideas, and the crises treated in Chapters 75 through 79. Their reforms strengthened the state; the later constitutional movement sought to limit it. Both belong to Iranian history, but they were different projects.

The chapter's conclusion is that the most durable achievements of reform often depended less on the qualities of individual ministers than on whether institutions acquired personnel, resources, routines, and political support enough to survive changes in leadership. Recognizing a problem, designing a reform, implementing it, and establishing an institution that outlasts political change are separate tasks, and Qajar reformers accomplished them to very different degrees. That insight, more than any judgment of the men themselves, is what the history of reform offers students.

The contrast between the two reformers should be tested across specific institutions. The new infantry could perform effectively in some circumstances but depended on a reliable fiscal base; translation and printing preserved knowledge but required readers and commissions; a chief minister's austerity could redirect expenditure but depended heavily on royal trust. Dar al-Funun was more durable because successive rulers and administrators had reasons to maintain it even when they disagreed with the man who founded it. The cases illustrate not a single measure of reform success but several distinct tests: operational performance, regular finance, trained personnel, social acceptance, and the ability to endure political succession.

Institutional continuity also changed the social position of expertise. A technical teacher, physician, engineer, or trained military officer could gain authority from an acquired skill, although appointment and advancement still depended on patrons. A printed manual or school syllabus made some knowledge reproducible without relying entirely on one master's memory. These were modest but consequential changes in the ways the government could mobilize expertise. They did not eliminate religious education, manuscript culture, or tribal military organizations; Iranian institutions continued to contain multiple methods of training and governance. The language of total replacement would conceal this coexistence.

Later critics of Qajar governance drew on the prestige of earlier reformers, sometimes reading in their initiatives aspirations the original reformers did not hold. Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir sought a more effective monarchy, not a representative legislature able to constrain it. Their successors could nevertheless inherit trained personnel, printed knowledge, and expectations of administrative accountability and apply them to different political purposes. The chapter's comparative lesson is therefore neither inevitability nor futility. Reform generated capacities and conflicts whose outcomes were contingent upon finance, political sponsorship, social cooperation, and the cumulative work of institutions. The fate of the school founded in 1851 demonstrates how an initiative could outlive its author while changing its own functions over time.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Qajar reform developed from military experiment into a broader effort to strengthen the state, why it encountered political and institutional resistance, and why some innovations survived while others did not. It began with the closing moment of 1851, when Dar al-Funun opened in the absence of its founder, and used that paradox to pose the central question: why was reform hard to sustain across political generations?

The first division showed that reform began as a response to problems of state capacity exposed by war with Russia. Abbas Mirza, as governor of Azerbaijan, used the resources of Tabriz and the skills of administrators such as Mirza Bozorg Qa'em-Maqam to organize the Nezam-e Jadid, drawing on French and British expertise while retaining Iranian direction.

The second division examined the program's expansion and limits. Financing depended on provincial revenue and local intermediaries; recruitment imposed burdens on communities; student missions, printing, and arsenals transferred knowledge only partially. Abbas Mirza's reforms were not simply defeated by Russia, since regular forces achieved successes in the Ottoman war, but the program remained dependent on a patron and constrained by weak institutions.

The third division traced continuity through the reign of Mohammad Shah. The execution of Qa'em-Maqam in 1835 and the ministry of Haji Mirza Aqasi weakened the reform's coherence without erasing its personnel. Mirza Taqi Khan emerged from the Farahani household with experience in military finance, Russia, and the Ottoman boundary commission at Erzurum, and rose to power in the succession of 1848.

The fourth division examined his ministry. His fiscal measures, military administration, support for public works, health, and communities, and the creation of the official gazette expressed a program of centralization that aimed at strengthening royal government, not limiting it. The measures also threatened established interests, and their documented effects were more limited than their intentions.

The fifth division treated the founding and early life of Dar al-Funun, whose chronology of planning, inauguration on 29 December 1851, and development after Amir Kabir's dismissal is essential. It also examined his dismissal in November 1851 and execution on 10 January 1852, explaining them through court politics, concentrated power, threatened interests, and the structural dependence of ministers on the monarch.

The chapter's evidence includes British and Iranian official records, a student's travelogue, the official gazette, and later chronicles and memoirs, each with limits of perspective. Debates about the success of Abbas Mirza's reforms and the cause of Amir Kabir's fall show that interpretation involves causation and weight more than basic facts.

Throughout, reform was distinguished from Westernization. Foreign expertise was adopted selectively for specific state purposes, and exposure to European institutions did not automatically produce constitutional ideas. Social history, though thinly documented, showed that the burdens and benefits of reform fell unevenly on soldiers, taxpayers, merchants, and minorities.

The synthesis argued that durability depended on whether initiatives acquired personnel, resources, routines, and political support. Dar al-Funun survived; much of the fiscal and political reorganization did not. The reformers should not be read as inevitable precursors of constitutionalism, which arose later from different conditions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows reform as a political and institutional process and not simply the arrival of new ideas or equipment. Qajar officials understood their problems and designed responses; what proved difficult was making those responses last.

The experience shaped later developments. Dar al-Funun's graduates, translators, and technical specialists entered government and intellectual life, while the failure of ministerial reform clarified the dependence of every policy on royal favor. Those lessons informed later debates about law, representation, and the limits of royal power.

The chapter also cautions against two simple stories: that reform was doomed by tradition, and that it led inevitably to modern institutions. The evidence supports neither. It suggests a contested, uneven process in which resources, personnel, and politics decided what lasted.

The question the chapter leaves is the one with which it concluded: what determines whether a reform becomes a durable institution rather than a temporary achievement of an individual ruler or minister? The following chapters on the Babi movement, concessions, print culture, and constitutional revolution show other answers to that question.
