Chapter Opening

Between 2005 and 2013 an engineer and former mayor of Tehran, presenting himself as an ordinary servant of the poor, governed an oil-rich Islamic Republic through a program of revolutionary populism, public spending, and assertive sovereignty, and left office amid inflation, a collapsing currency, tightening sanctions, and a divided conservative camp.

In the second round of the presidential election held in late June 2005, Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, then mayor of Tehran, defeated Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, a founding figure of the Islamic Republic and president from 1989 to 1997, by a wide margin. Many observers, inside and outside Iran, had not expected him even to reach the runoff. His victory is often summarized as a conservative triumph over reformists. That summary is incomplete. The reformist movement examined in Chapter 100 had already lost much of its institutional leverage, and the 2005 contest pitted Ahmadinejad against a veteran pragmatist and several rivals, in an electorate whose dissatisfactions were as much economic as ideological.

This chapter studies the eight years that followed. It treats Ahmadinejad's presidency as an attempt to restore the egalitarian language of the revolution, to turn petroleum income into visible benefits for lower-income households and provincial communities, and to defend Iran's nuclear program as a matter of national sovereignty. It treats that attempt as neither a simple success nor a simple failure. Some policies reached large numbers of households; others encountered inflation, administrative limits, and the volatility of oil markets.

The chapter also follows a political story. Ahmadinejad came to office with the support of conservative organizations, revolutionary veterans, and networks associated with the Revolutionary Guards, but these groups never formed a single bloc. By his second term, disputes with parliamentary conservatives, with former allies, and ultimately with the Supreme Leader's circle had exposed deep disagreements over constitutional authority, ideological direction, succession, and access to resources. The contested election of 2009, which receives its full treatment in Chapter 102, deepened these divisions and is examined here chiefly for its consequences for the president's legitimacy.

A third thread concerns the nuclear confrontation. Iran's enrichment activities, the safeguards disputes surrounding them, referral to the United Nations Security Council, successive sanctions resolutions, and unilateral American and European financial measures transformed a technical and legal dispute into the central issue of Iranian foreign and economic policy. The chapter explains these developments historically and diplomatically, without technical detail, and leaves the comprehensive sanctions chronology and the later negotiations to Chapter 103.

Historians working on these years use evidence of very different kinds and reliability: speeches and campaign materials; legislation and budget documents; Central Bank and statistical center publications whose definitions and reliability are themselves debated; electoral records issued by the Interior Ministry; IAEA reports; Security Council resolutions; diplomatic memoirs; press coverage inside and outside Iran; and survey and household data. Rhetoric, policy, and measurable outcome must be kept apart, and contested claims, especially on electoral integrity, weapons-related allegations, and economic statistics, must be attributed to their sources.

The chapter's organizing question is why a presidency committed to revolutionary social justice, redistribution, and national sovereignty encountered increasing economic instability, international confrontation, and political fragmentation. The answer developed here is that these outcomes arose from interactions among presidential decisions, institutional structures, petroleum dependence, international pressure, and the expectations of Iranian society, and not from any single cause or from the personality of one man.

The chapter follows Chapter 100, on Khatami and the limits of reform, and precedes Chapter 102, on the 2009 election and the Green Movement. It overlaps chronologically with that chapter by design. It also anticipates Chapter 103 on sanctions and nuclear diplomacy, Chapter 104 on the Rouhani presidency, and Chapter 105 on the Revolutionary Guards and the political economy of the Islamic Republic.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and economic conditions that enabled Ahmadinejad's election in 2005, without reducing it to a reformist–conservative contest.
  2. Analyze revolutionary populism as a contested concept and describe how it combined egalitarian, religious, and nationalist appeals.
  3. Identify the constituencies of Ahmadinejad's governing coalition and explain why they were not a unified establishment.
  4. Describe how petroleum revenues financed public spending and why oil dependence limited fiscal discipline and diversification.
  5. Distinguish among consumer subsidies, direct cash transfers, directed credit, public housing, provincial expenditure, and public employment as redistribution instruments.
  6. Evaluate the targeted subsidy reform and explain why cash transfers lost real value under inflation.
  7. Assess economic performance using the distinctions between nominal and real income and between expenditure and productive investment.
  8. Trace the expansion of Revolutionary Guards-associated organizations in economic life while distinguishing public, private, and parastatal enterprise.
  9. Explain the consequences of the disputed 2009 election for Ahmadinejad's second-term legitimacy.
  10. Trace the escalation of the nuclear dispute from the IAEA to the Security Council and distinguish UN, unilateral, and secondary sanctions.
  11. Analyze how sanctions, domestic policy, and global markets interacted in the currency crisis of 2011–2013.
  12. Evaluate competing assessments of the Ahmadinejad presidency and the significance of the 2013 transition to Hassan Rouhani.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a presidency committed to revolutionary social justice, redistribution, and national sovereignty encounter increasing economic instability, international confrontation, and political fragmentation?
  2. Why did dissatisfaction with reformist politicians not necessarily mean a rejection of political reform?
  3. In what sense was Ahmadinejad both an outsider to the political establishment and a product of it?

  5. Why can nominal increases in income fail to improve real household welfare?
  6. How did petroleum revenue make redistribution possible while weakening fiscal discipline?
  7. Why did conservative alignment in elections not produce institutional unity?
  8. How should historians treat disputed electoral and nuclear claims for which evidence is incomplete?
  9. How can the effects of sanctions be separated from those of domestic economic policy?
  10. Why did the 2013 election change the direction of the government without changing the constitutional system?

Historical Context

The Islamic Republic emerged from the revolution of 1979 with a mixed constitutional structure: elected presidents and a parliament (the Majles) operating alongside the Supreme Leader, the Guardian Council, the Expediency Council, and other unelected institutions, as examined in Chapter 93. Presidential power was real but bounded, particularly in security, foreign policy, and the judiciary, where the Supreme Leader held ultimate authority. Understanding Ahmadinejad's presidency requires keeping this layered structure in view.

Rafsanjani's reconstruction presidency (Chapter 99) emphasized infrastructure, selective liberalization, and diplomatic pragmatism, and left debts and inflation as legacies. Khatami's presidency (Chapter 100) won large electoral majorities for a program of civil society and rule of law but met sustained resistance from unelected institutions, including closures of newspapers and disqualification of candidates. By 2003–2004 many reform supporters were demobilized. Conservatives made gains in the 2003 municipal elections, with particularly low turnout in Tehran, and in the 2004 parliamentary elections the Guardian Council disqualified large numbers of reformist candidates, producing a conservative majority.

Economic grievances ran alongside these political developments. Unemployment, especially among young people entering a large labor force, remained a persistent concern; housing costs in Tehran and other cities rose faster than many household incomes; and visible wealth, often associated with political connections, made inequality a charged subject. The language of social justice and of the "oppressed" (mostaz'afin) remained deeply embedded in revolutionary political culture, and it was available to politicians willing to use it.

The regional environment had also shifted. The American-led invasions of Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003) removed two hostile neighbors, the Taliban and Saddam Hussein, but placed large foreign forces on Iran's borders and opened new arenas of competition. Iran's leaders perceived both opportunity, particularly in Iraq, where Shi'i parties with long Iranian connections gained power, and threat. President George W. Bush's 2002 characterization of Iran as part of an "axis of evil" had reinforced security concerns.

Finally, the nuclear question had already become internationally significant. In 2002–2003 the disclosure of previously undeclared facilities led to IAEA investigations and to negotiations with Britain, France, and Germany (the "E3"). Under Khatami, Iran accepted a voluntary suspension of enrichment-related activities as a confidence-building measure while negotiations continued. Whether that suspension would survive the change of government was an open question in 2005. This chapter begins from that inheritance.

Section 101.1

Iran on the Eve of the 2005 Presidential Election

The Khatami presidency (Chapter 100) ended with reformist institutions weakened but reformist expectations not extinguished. The Guardian Council's disqualification of many candidates in 2004 and conservative gains in the municipal elections of 2003 and the parliamentary elections of 2004 convinced many voters that elections held under existing screening rules offered limited prospects for change. Turnout fell in those contests. Historians caution that low turnout reflects many motives: disillusionment, boycott, indifference, or satisfaction. It cannot be read as a straightforward rejection of reform.

Economic insecurity formed the second pillar of dissatisfaction. Even with rising oil revenue after 2000, unemployment remained a major concern, particularly for young job seekers; many urban households faced housing costs that outstripped wages; and a public sense that political insiders enjoyed unearned privilege made inequality a politically potent theme. Reform-era leaders had spoken chiefly of civil liberties and legal accountability. Critics argued that they had given less attention to livelihoods, though reformists themselves pointed to the constraints placed on them.

These conditions created an opening for candidates who spoke of economic justice, administrative directness, and renewed revolutionary commitment. Dissatisfaction with reformist politicians did not mean that all former reform voters turned conservative. Some may have abstained, voted for pragmatists, or supported populist alternatives; aggregate returns cannot determine individual motives.

The institutional change preceded the presidential campaign. The second Khatami administration had won a fresh mandate in 2001, yet its efforts to change election law and strengthen presidential supervision of constitutional implementation failed. The dispute over these proposals, known as the twin bills, made visible the difference between ordinary legislation and a redistribution of authority within the state. In the 2004 Majles contest, the screening of prospective candidates deprived reformist organizations of many established representatives. This meant that the political resources available to a candidate in 2005 were very different from those available in 1997. A campaign based on the promise of another confrontation over constitutional interpretation could no longer appeal to voters on the assumption that a victory at the polls would be sufficient.

Municipal government was an especially important intermediary. In the first council elections in 1999, reform-oriented organizations had done well, but disputes over municipal management and factional rivalries damaged the standing of the capital's council. In 2003 a conservative electoral list associated with the Builders of Islamic Iran won control of the Tehran council amid exceptionally low participation in the capital. The council, rather than the electorate directly, selected the mayor. The municipal office gave Ahmadinejad an administrative record, a public platform, and contact with neighborhood-level networks. This trajectory illustrates the importance of elections below the national level: a change in the composition of a local council could help create a presidential contender two years later. It did not mean that every voter who had previously supported reform had become a conservative.

Iran's demographic circumstances also help explain the renewed appeal of redistribution. Expanded schooling and a larger educated population generated expectations of employment in increasingly diverse professions. The transition from education to employment was often difficult. Young adults unable to secure stable work could face delays in marriage and in establishing independent households. Housing was both an asset and a necessary expense, so a rise in real estate prices had opposite implications for existing owners and prospective buyers. Social justice consequently included questions about access to credit, land, and public services as well as wages. The distinction is particularly important in a society where family networks often supplied resources that labor markets and public institutions did not.

The Fourth Five-Year Development Plan began in the Iranian year 1384, corresponding approximately to 2005/06. It was not written as a blank slate for the new president. The government inherited investment projects, state-owned banks, a large public enterprise sector, an oil stabilization mechanism, and policies designed to encourage private investment. The preceding administration had unified official exchange rates in 2002 and attempted to build more predictable economic institutions. A historical comparison should therefore ask which of those policies continued, which were redirected, and which were displaced by the new emphasis on direct presidential intervention. The relevant transition was not from an economy without public spending to one with public spending; the controversy concerned the allocation and management of that spending.

Meanwhile, the collapse of the Taliban government in Afghanistan and of Saddam Hussein's regime in Iraq created conflicting interpretations of security. Some leaders believed the country needed diplomatic accommodation to limit the dangers created by American forces in neighboring countries. Others interpreted those same developments as confirmation that deterrence and independence were indispensable. Neither geopolitical position mapped perfectly onto the domestic distributional debate. A voter could support public housing and also prefer accommodation with Europe, or endorse nuclear sovereignty while opposing administrative patronage. These cross-cutting concerns explain why the 2005 outcome requires a study of campaign choices and institutions rather than a simple two-camp description of Iranian society. [1] [2] [3]

Causal Analysis

From Reformist Setbacks to Populist Mobilization

Long-term causes

Revolutionary egalitarian political culture; oil-financed state with large expectations of public provision; persistent youth unemployment and housing pressure.

Short-term causes

Institutional defeat of the reform agenda; conservative wins in 2003–2004; demobilization of reform voters; fragmentation of reformist candidates in 2005.

Immediate context

A crowded 2005 field in which a candidate with an outsider image and a social-justice message could consolidate support in the runoff.

Effect

A populist, veteran-linked presidency that redirected political emphasis toward distribution and sovereignty.

Historical complexity: Reform failure was necessary context, not sufficient cause; organization, candidate choice, and the runoff structure also mattered.

Section 101.2

Mahmoud Ahmadinejad and the Presidential Election of 2005

Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, born in 1956 in a village near Garmsar in what is now Semnan province and raised in Tehran, trained as an engineer and earned a doctorate in transportation engineering. He took part in revolutionary-era student politics and in wartime service networks, held provincial office in Ardabil, and was appointed mayor of Tehran in 2003 after conservative councils took control of the capital. As mayor he cultivated an image of frugality and attention to ordinary neighborhoods, while reversing some cultural initiatives of his reformist predecessors.

The Guardian Council approved only a handful of the more than one thousand people who registered as candidates in 2005. Among those on the ballot were Rafsanjani, Ahmadinejad, the cleric Mehdi Karroubi, the conservative former police chief Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, Ali Larijani, and the reformist Mostafa Moin. In the first round, held in June 2005, Rafsanjani finished first with roughly a fifth of the vote, Ahmadinejad narrowly secured second place ahead of Karroubi, and no candidate won a majority. Karroubi alleged improper interference on behalf of Ahmadinejad, an accusation contested by officials and not conclusively resolved in public evidence.

In the runoff a week later, Ahmadinejad won by a large margin, with roughly three fifths of the vote. Many reform-inclined voters supported Rafsanjani as the lesser risk, while Ahmadinejad appealed to low-income and provincial voters with a message of honest administration and shared oil wealth. Rafsanjani's long association with elite wealth made him a convenient target. The election thus cannot be read only as reformists against conservatives: the most significant contest was between a pragmatic establishment figure and a populist outsider, and the reformist field had divided.

The historian must separate Ahmadinejad's self-presentation from independently established evidence of who voted for him. Survey data on 2005 voters are limited, and claims about his support among poor or rural voters require careful qualification: constituency-level voting and organized conservative networks do not establish individual voter motives or income.

Ahmadinejad's rise was unusual not because he had no institutional experience but because his experience lay outside the most familiar circles of senior clerical and presidential office. Born in Aradan near Garmsar, he studied at Iran University of Science and Technology, served in local administrative positions, and became governor of the newly constituted Ardabil province during the 1990s. After leaving that post he returned to academic work before becoming mayor of Tehran. Published biographical accounts differ in their descriptions of his precise wartime assignments. It is safer to describe his connections with revolutionary and wartime institutions than to assign an unverified military rank or responsibility to him. His professional training also complicates the contrast he drew with technocrats: his own credentials were technical, but he advocated a different relationship between expertise and political leadership.

The rules of presidential competition mattered as much as the candidates' biographies. The constitutional system required screening before a candidate could stand. In 2005, the initial exclusions of Mostafa Moin and Mohsen Mehralizadeh were reversed following the Supreme Leader's intervention. Eight prospective candidates were ultimately permitted to compete, but Mohsen Rezaei withdrew before voting. Voters faced seven candidates on June 17. Moin campaigned on reform-oriented concerns, Karroubi combined a clerical background with material promises, and Mehralizadeh appealed in part through his regional and administrative background. On the conservative side Qalibaf presented an image of managerial competence, while Ali Larijani had experience in broadcasting and national institutions. Rafsanjani offered a return to experienced pragmatic government. The field therefore contained several alternative approaches to development, rather than a pair of candidates corresponding to the previous decade's main political divisions.

The official first-round results placed Rafsanjani and Ahmadinejad ahead of Karroubi, Qalibaf and Moin. The narrow gap between the second- and third-place candidates mattered because the electoral rule required an outright majority or a runoff between the two leading candidates. Karroubi publicly alleged intervention by organized state-linked networks. The allegations deserve a documented account, not a retrospective assumption that the much larger controversy in 2009 settled what happened four years earlier. The election authorities maintained the official returns. The subsequent runoff created a different strategic decision for voters whose preferred candidates had been eliminated. An endorsement by one reformist figure did not necessarily cause all supporters of that figure to vote for Rafsanjani. The second round was consequently a new political contest, not a simple addition of first-round votes.

The runoff on June 24 made economic inequality a particularly visible point of contrast. Rafsanjani's record as the architect of reconstruction was both an asset and a liability: supporters saw administrative competence, while opponents associated his political networks with privileged access to resources. Ahmadinejad's campaign could depict the contest as one between ordinary citizens and an entrenched elite. That description was effective rhetoric, but it concealed the extent of his own institutional connections. The final chapter's election table therefore presents the Interior Ministry's reported aggregate results as evidence of the declared outcome, not as evidence of each voter's motives. Where nationwide and provincial totals differ, the table identifies the reporting basis rather than silently reconciling the records. [1] [4] [5]

Section 101.3

Revolutionary Populism, Social Justice, and Political Rhetoric

Ahmadinejad's appeal drew on the revolution's own moral vocabulary: dignity for the dispossessed, criticism of privilege, and the claim that oil revenue belonged to the people. His campaign became associated with a promise to share oil wealth directly with ordinary households, and criticized concentrated wealth, bureaucratic indifference, and what he portrayed as technocratic elites insulated from ordinary life. His emphasis on personal simplicity, from modest dress and a simple home to plain speech, reinforced the message.

His populism was also religious and nationalist. Ahmadinejad spoke of the return of the Twelfth Imam and of just government as a religious duty, framing economic fairness as a form of piety. In foreign policy he cast resistance to foreign pressure, above all in the nuclear dispute, as a defense of national dignity and independence, and invoked revolutionary memory of the Iran–Iraq War to bind these themes together. Critics from within the clergy, including some conservative scholars, objected to the prominence of messianic language.

"Populism" is a contested analytical term. Some scholars use it descriptively for movements that oppose "the people" to "the elite"; others use it pejoratively for demagoguery or fiscal irresponsibility. This textbook uses it descriptively, and notes that Ahmadinejad was a populist who governed inside a state whose own institutions, including the Revolutionary Guards and the clerical establishment, were themselves powerful elites. Promises are to be distinguished from measurable outcomes, which later sections examine.

The revolutionary vocabulary Ahmadinejad employed had a longer genealogy than his campaign. The term mostaz'afin, commonly translated as the oppressed or dispossessed, belonged to the political language of the revolution and informed the names and missions of welfare institutions. The constitutional commitment to removing deprivation and securing economic independence gave competing political factions a shared language in which to argue over economic policy. By the 1990s, a developmental interpretation emphasized investment and growth as conditions for raising living standards. Ahmadinejad instead emphasized the immediacy of distribution: citizens should experience the benefits of national resources in ordinary life. These were competing emphases within a common political inheritance, rather than wholly separate economic doctrines.

Populist rhetoric worked through a moral distinction as well as a policy claim. A public official who represented himself as accessible and austere could imply that elaborate offices, privileged consumption, or opaque administrative procedures reflected a failure to serve the public. The provincial visit provided a setting in which this distinction could be dramatized. A petition delivered to the president, an announced road project, or a promised allocation could establish a direct relationship between the executive and a local community. The same practice could generate problems for routine administration: if decisions made during visits became commitments outside a coordinated investment plan, other agencies still had to identify funding, contractors, and implementation schedules. Accessibility and orderly administration were not necessarily opposites, but the balance between them was a central question of the presidency.

The religious dimension also requires careful differentiation. Twelver Shi'ism regards the hidden Imam and his eventual return as matters of religious belief. This is distinct from a political claim about the role of a contemporary administration in preparing society for that return. Ahmadinejad's public emphasis on the Mahdi was neither the origin of this belief nor an expression shared in identical form by all Shi'i clerics. Different religious authorities attached different importance to these themes and objected when an executive politician appeared to claim special religious standing. The resulting disagreements were about authority and interpretation as well as doctrine. The state was constitutionally grounded in an established system of religious leadership; an executive claim to moral or spiritual proximity to ordinary believers did not supersede that system.

National sovereignty joined these themes by representing technological and economic capacity as a collective achievement. A nation able to pursue scientific development despite outside pressure could be portrayed as vindicating the revolution's promise of independence. This narrative was politically useful because it connected an international dispute to domestic dignity. Yet support for scientific capacity was not equivalent to agreement with every negotiating tactic. Reformists, pragmatists, and conservatives could all defend the right to peaceful nuclear technology while disagreeing about safeguards, the costs of sanctions, or the desirability of temporary diplomatic compromises. The historian should disaggregate those positions rather than infer a person's entire political identity from support for one objective.

Finally, populism should be evaluated as a relationship between representation and policy. It can reveal how Ahmadinejad justified direct intervention, but the term alone cannot explain whether credit allocations were effective, whether housing projects were completed, or whether household transfers raised consumption. Those questions require budget, production, and household evidence. The later divisions use these different forms of evidence to test the administration's stated commitments. [2] [6] [7]

Section 101.4

Ahmadinejad's Governing Coalition and the Conservative Establishment

Ahmadinejad's first administration rested on several overlapping constituencies. Conservative organizations and networks, including figures associated with the Alliance of Builders of Islamic Iran, helped provide an organizational base; other conservatives backed rival candidates before the runoff. Revolutionary and war veterans supplied legitimacy and personnel, and many cabinet and provincial appointments went to people with backgrounds in the Revolutionary Guards, the Basij, or associated institutions. Religious conservatives provided ideological support, provincial administrators supplied local networks, economic populists advocated state intervention, and lower-income voters offered a mass base with material expectations.

His early relationship with Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was cooperative. Khamenei publicly endorsed his emphasis on social justice and on nuclear rights, and conservative majorities in the Majles gave the government broad support. Yet the coalition was a convergence of interests rather than a unified establishment. Traditional conservatives, many from clerical and bazaar backgrounds, distrusted the president's outsider style and his willingness to bypass established institutions. Some veterans prized security priorities over economic ones. Parliamentary conservatives guarded their own authority over budgets and appointments. These tensions were already visible in the first term and became more acute in the second.

Iran's conservative political field had developed through a series of organizational changes before 2005. The Builders' electoral victories created opportunities for younger administrators and individuals associated with wartime service. Established conservative clerical and commercial networks retained institutional resources, however, and prominent conservatives competed against Ahmadinejad in the presidential election. Some endorsed him only after the first round. Thus, references to conservative support should identify the institution, organization, or phase of the campaign whenever possible. A shared opposition to reformist leadership could facilitate electoral cooperation without supplying a common fiscal program or a shared preference among conservative candidates.

The composition of the cabinet translated political alliances into administrative responsibilities. Appointment to a ministry could give a former security or military official authority over economic or infrastructure policy, even though the ministry itself remained a civilian institution. Personnel background, institutional affiliation, and chain of command must be distinguished. A former Guards officer serving as a minister was not automatically acting under the command of the Guards in that office. Conversely, a commercial organization with formal corporate autonomy could retain close relations with a security institution. The political importance of these overlaps lay in the circulation of personnel, experience, and networks across government, not in the disappearance of the distinction between all public institutions.

The Majles was another independent source of political leverage. Under the Constitution, ministers require parliamentary confidence, legislators approve budgets and may question ministers or the president, and legislation undergoes Guardian Council review. These powers did not cease to operate merely because a majority of legislators shared a broad political tendency with the executive. Legislators had electoral constituencies and interests in the distribution of funds to their own regions. They could object to cabinet nominees, contest spending commitments, or demand information from executive agencies. In the first term disagreements over budgets and personnel already demonstrated that conservative government did not mean the abolition of legislative conflict.

The Supreme Leader's public support gave Ahmadinejad important political protection, particularly in the disputes that followed the 2009 election. It also imposed limits. Article 110 of the amended Constitution assigns the Leader authority over general policies and major appointments, including military leadership. The president, under Article 113, heads the executive within the limits established by the Constitution. These provisions are not a complete map of how authority actually operated; personal relationships, advisory institutions, and political conventions also mattered. The early cooperative relationship therefore depended on agreement about priorities, not an institutional transfer of the Leader's authority to the president.

The governing coalition's long-term difficulty was that its members could agree on a language of social justice without agreeing on how to distribute resources or who should control the succession. Parliamentary conservatives could support public spending but oppose discretionary executive commitments. Religious authorities could endorse moral regulation but criticize the president's associates. Commercial organizations could welcome contracts yet object to uncertainty in economic management. Voters who supported redistribution might withdraw support when its real benefits declined. Understanding these different interests establishes the foundation for the second-term conflicts in Section 101.12. [2] [4] [8]

Section 101.5

Petroleum Revenues, Public Spending, and the Populist Development Model

Iran's government finances depend heavily on petroleum exports managed by the National Iranian Oil Company. During most of Ahmadinejad's first term, international oil prices rose strongly, peaking in mid-2008 before falling sharply with the global financial crisis. This inflow financed expanded public expenditure, state-directed investment, credit programs, and provincial development projects. It also made the government's room for maneuver dependent on a price it did not control.

The administration pursued a distinctive governing style. The president toured provinces with cabinet members and announced projects and allocations on the spot, linking central funds to visible local benefits. In 2006 the government distributed "justice shares" in state enterprises to lower-income households, a program intended to share public assets. In 2007 the government dissolved the Management and Planning Organization, long the country's central planning body, and placed its functions under the presidency, a change critics said weakened technocratic budget discipline and supporters described as reducing bureaucracy. The government also drew heavily on the Oil Stabilization Fund, which had been designed to save part of oil revenue, and parliament and the president disputed how much of the revenue should be spent.

Oil income created real opportunities for redistribution while weakening incentives for fiscal discipline and diversification. Higher spending expanded demand, but when it is financed by converting foreign exchange into local currency and borrowing from the banking system it can expand the money supply and push up prices. A temporary rise in revenue differs from a sustained increase in productive capacity: houses, factories, and skills last, but windfall revenue does not.

The flow of petroleum revenue into the public economy began before the budget was written. Oil sold abroad generates foreign currency; the state obtains its fiscal share under the applicable revenue arrangements, and the central bank and banking system mediate the conversion of foreign receipts into domestic purchasing power. If the government spends the local-currency counterpart of a foreign receipt, its expenditure may increase domestic liquidity unless the monetary authorities offset that effect. The same external receipt can therefore support investment in imports or construction while adding to domestic demand. Whether it produces a sustained increase in real output depends on what is purchased, the economy's capacity to supply it, and the availability of foreign exchange. This is why the price of a barrel of oil is not an adequate measure of public welfare.

The Oil Stabilization Fund had been established during the Khatami period to cushion the budget against changes in petroleum prices and support selected investment. A stabilization mechanism is most effective when withdrawals follow predictable rules and savings accumulate during favorable market conditions. When expenditure commitments expand in response to a temporary windfall, however, the government can face pressure to use reserves for current expenditure. The IMF's 2008 consultation reported rapidly rising broad money and strong domestic demand even while the external account was in surplus. This combination shows how an oil exporter can have a favorable external balance yet face domestic inflationary pressure. A surplus in one account cannot be substituted for an assessment of the entire economy.

Justice shares represented a different distributive instrument. Instead of paying recipients only out of current revenue, the program offered eligible groups interests in enterprises whose assets had previously been publicly owned. The arrangement was implemented through designated intermediaries and restrictions rather than through the straightforward distribution of immediately tradable shares. A nominal entitlement to enterprise ownership is not identical to a dividend in cash or to the ability to sell the asset. Students should distinguish the announced value of an allocation, dividends actually received, repayment obligations attached to the allocation, and control over corporate decisions. These distinctions are particularly relevant where the policy is described both as privatization and as welfare provision.

The 2007 restructuring of the Management and Planning Organization illustrates a conflict over the method of government rather than only the size of government. The body had served a coordinating function between development plans, annual budgets, and the allocation of resources to sectors and regions. Reassignment of its duties to presidential structures could reduce the number of institutional intermediaries, as the administration argued, but did not eliminate the need for project appraisal, accounting, and coordination. Critics feared that a weakening of the planning organization would reduce the ability of expert officials to scrutinize promises before funds were committed. Distinguishing the abolition or reorganization of an agency from the disappearance of its functions is essential here.

The global downturn in 2008–2009 tested these arrangements. A fall in international prices changed the anticipated inflow of foreign exchange, whereas salaries, contracted construction costs, and other obligations could not be reduced at the same speed. It also affected the value of the budget's underlying assumptions. The government's capacity to continue programs depended not simply on the preceding year's revenue but on accumulated reserves, access to financing, and the ability to prioritize expenditures. The first and second presidential terms must consequently be studied as parts of a common resource cycle. [3] [9] [10] [44] [49]

Section 101.6

Housing, Employment, Credit, and Provincial Development

Housing was among the most visible programs. The Mehr Housing initiative, expanded from 2007 onward, aimed to provide publicly supported residential units to lower-income households, with state land, subsidized financing, and government-directed construction. It produced large numbers of units, and in many cases enabled families who had no prospect of owning a home to acquire one. Yet many projects were located on city peripheries or in new towns lacking transport, schools, clinics, and employment, and builders and ministries faced shortages and delays.

State-directed credit through state-owned banks financed housing, small enterprises, and employment projects, sometimes at below-market rates. Directed credit can expand investment, but when allocation follows administrative priority rather than repayment prospects it can strain bank balance sheets and add to money creation. Provincial spending, tied to the presidential tours, gave regions beyond Tehran greater resources, though administrative capacity and the quality of projects varied widely.

Increasing the number of units did not by itself solve housing affordability. Construction output, access to housing, household purchasing power, and long-term urban development are different measures. Rising land and rental prices in major cities, partly fed by liquidity and inflation, continued to burden households outside the programs. The experiences of metropolitan renters, new-town residents, and provincial families therefore diverged.

Mehr Housing was designed to address a serious supply problem: rapid household formation had increased demand for residential accommodation, especially around the largest cities. The 2014 IMF staff report describes the authorities' assessment of a large housing deficit in 2006 and the need for substantial annual construction. It identifies the project as an effort to serve lower-income families. The program's design separated the land cost from building finance by making public land available on favorable terms, including long leases, and channeling credit through the specialized housing bank, Bank Maskan. This was intended to reduce the initial cost for the buyer. It did not remove the cost of construction, public utilities, transportation, or maintenance.

The time between approval of a project and occupancy was economically important. Residential developments require site preparation, roads, power, water, sanitation, and access to jobs and services. A completed apartment in a peripheral location might remain difficult to occupy if transportation was inadequate or a household could not meet its required contribution. Conversely, a family with an affordable unit and a nearby transport connection could experience a major improvement in living conditions. Government announcements, starts, units structurally completed, units connected to services, and units occupied are different measurements. Treating them as interchangeable would overstate or understate the policy's effects. The later completion of a unit originally commissioned under Ahmadinejad also illustrates why administrative responsibilities extend beyond presidential terms.

The financing mechanism had wider consequences. If a housing bank is required to lend at favorable rates while its normal funding is insufficient, it may obtain credit from the central bank. The IMF identified central bank lending associated with the housing program as a source of pressure on monetary management. This does not imply that providing inexpensive housing necessarily causes inflation. The question is how the program is financed, whether the additional credit is offset elsewhere, and whether construction capacity increases in step with demand. A loan backed by long-term funding has different macroeconomic effects from one that is supported repeatedly by additional central bank credit. The financial arrangement should be evaluated separately from the social purpose of the housing program.

Small-enterprise credit pursued a related objective through a different channel. A borrower who obtains affordable financing for equipment or working capital may expand output and employ additional workers. Yet a business does not become profitable simply because it receives a loan. The quality of project selection, access to inputs, the market for its products, and loan repayment conditions affect the outcome. If a policy emphasizes the number or total value of loans issued instead of business survival and employment retained, its administrative indicators may overstate real success. The same issue applies to provincial projects. Their distributive importance cannot be inferred from a sum of announced allocations alone; the completed assets and local use of those assets must be assessed.

Domestic geography made such evaluation particularly necessary. Peripheral housing developments in the Tehran metropolitan region faced different land, transport, and labor conditions from smaller provincial cities. Oil-producing regions generated national income but had their own environmental and infrastructure needs. Rural communities could benefit more from a connection to a road or electricity network than from a large project designed for a metropolitan housing market. A national commitment to geographical fairness thus required locally differentiated administration. [9] [10]

Section 101.7

Subsidy Reform, Cash Transfers, and the Redistribution of Household Income

For decades Iranian governments had kept the prices of gasoline, electricity, natural gas, water, bread, and other essentials far below market levels. These indirect subsidies were expensive, consuming a large share of the economy's resources, and they benefited households roughly in proportion to consumption: wealthier households with cars and large homes received the largest share. They also encouraged high energy use and imports of refined fuel.

In 2010 the Majles passed, and the government began implementing, the Targeted Subsidies Law. Administered prices of energy and some other goods were increased, in several steps and by large factors, and the revenue was to be shared between households, through direct monthly cash payments, and producers and government. Households were registered, and initially payments were made broadly rather than strictly by need. The cash transfer was a significant addition to income for low-income families, many of whom had previously been far from the benefits of cheap fuel.

The program also illustrates why redistribution encounters problems. Price increases raised production and transport costs. Cash payments were fixed in nominal terms while inflation rose, so their real value declined. Government finances were strained when the number of recipients and expenditure commitments grew while revenue was disrupted. Administrative issues, including registration, banking channels, and political pressure to maintain payments, added difficulty. A reform that initially improved household access to income thus faced inflationary, fiscal, and administrative pressures, especially when sanctions and currency instability followed.

The distinction between an administered price and the economic cost of a resource explains the reform's importance. If domestic gasoline is sold for less than its economic cost, the benefit to a household depends on how much it purchases. A household with several vehicles may receive more implicit assistance than a household without a vehicle. The opportunity cost of that arrangement may include export revenue forgone or the cost of purchasing supplies from abroad. The fiscal cost recorded in an annual budget, however, does not necessarily equal the full economic value of the subsidy. Estimating that value requires an appropriate reference price and assumptions about consumption and exchange rates. Those assumptions must be disclosed when comparisons are made across countries or years.

Iran's parliament approved the Targeted Subsidies Reform Act in January 2010, following extended debate about the economic and political risks of a large price adjustment. Its intended mechanism was to increase administered prices progressively and distribute the proceeds among households, enterprises, and the government. The IMF's 2011 account describes a reform designed to compensate consumers and support changes in production. The main launch took place on December 18–19, 2010, after preparations including household registration, opening or identifying bank accounts, and depositing compensation that would become available when prices changed. The sequencing mattered: imposing the new prices before households could access compensation would have placed the burden of transition on people with little cash on hand.

The authorities initially discussed targeting payments by need, but the practical difficulties of classifying households and the political risks of exclusion led to very broad coverage. According to the IMF's 2014 Selected Issues report, the basic monthly transfer was 445,000 rials per person, equivalent to 44,500 tomans at the conventional conversion of ten rials per toman. Additional payment components and reporting conventions help explain different figures in contemporary accounts. A fixed payment per household member gave a larger proportional income benefit to families with low incomes than to high-income families, even when both were eligible. The change could therefore reduce inequality without restricting eligibility to a narrowly defined poor population. Household size, location, and prior energy consumption also influenced the net effect.

The financing of compensation was not the same as the financing of general government expenditure. Ideally, the receipts generated by higher administered prices would cover payments to households and other authorized purposes. In practice, the volume of receipts depended on consumption, payment collection, the cost of supplying the subsidized products, and whether further price adjustments were implemented. Expenditure commitments could exceed the incremental resources actually available. The IMF's subsequent assessment identified financing and macroeconomic coordination problems, including insufficient support for producers and the influence of other credit programs. Once inflation accelerated, keeping nominal transfers constant reduced their purchasing power; raising payments without matching resources risked intensifying the fiscal problem.

Evidence from household research suggests that the initial distributional benefits should not be dismissed. Djavad Salehi-Isfahani and other economists emphasized that broad cash compensation could reach people who had previously received relatively little benefit from subsidized energy. The durability of the result, however, depended on price stability and sustainable financing. The reform should thus be assessed in phases: preparation and launch, initial changes in household income and consumption, and the erosion of those changes during the subsequent crisis. An assessment based only on the first few months or only on the conditions prevailing in 2013 would miss part of the historical record. [10] [11] [12] [13]

Section 101.8

Inflation, Employment, Economic Growth, and the Limits of Redistribution

Economic indicators for these years must be read cautiously. Official statistics on growth, inflation, and unemployment come from the Central Bank and the Statistical Center of Iran, and definitions, coverage, and reliability are debated. With that caution, the broad pattern is clear: growth was strong in the first years of oil-price increases, slowed after the 2008 price decline, and became weak and sometimes negative in the later years as sanctions tightened; consumer inflation, often in the double digits and higher in some years, ranked among the most important household concerns; and unemployment, particularly among the young and among women, remained high.

Nominal increases in wages, payments, and spending do not necessarily raise real welfare. If prices rise faster than incomes, purchasing power falls. The government raised public-sector wages and made transfers, but in a high-inflation environment, with imports a major source of consumer and industrial inputs, these gains were eroded. Import dependence made the economy sensitive to exchange rates, and weak investment conditions limited industrial output.

Two sets of causes must be distinguished. Domestic policy choices, including money-financed spending, directed credit, administrative price controls, and exchange-rate management, were important sources of inflation before the most severe sanctions. International restrictions then imposed independent constraints on oil exports, banking, and trade. The two interacted: a government facing sanctions had fewer options for financing expansion, and a government with weak fiscal buffers was more exposed to sanctions.

The first presidential term began in relatively favorable external conditions. The IMF's 2008 Article IV consultation estimated real GDP growth at factor cost of 5.7 percent in 2005/06, 6.2 percent in 2006/07, and 6.6 percent in 2007/08. Its contemporary estimates of annual-average inflation for those years were 10.4, 11.9, and 18.4 percent respectively. Growth and accelerating inflation could coexist: productive activity expanded while the demand generated by public spending and credit increased faster than some sectors could accommodate. Rising international commodity prices added to costs. These are historical estimates from one reporting vintage, not immutable numbers that can be combined without qualification with every later national accounts revision.

The downturn of 2008/09 cannot be attributed solely to the nuclear dispute. The global financial crisis sharply reduced the price of petroleum after its 2008 peak, and a severe drought affected agricultural production. The 2011 IMF staff report estimated that growth slowed considerably in that year and then recovered as agriculture and oil prices improved. This pattern helps distinguish cyclical influences from the more severe external restrictions that characterized the end of Ahmadinejad's presidency. It also demonstrates why a single average for 2005–2013 obscures important changes in the economy's external conditions.

The concept of real income permits a more precise assessment of distribution. Consider a household receiving a fixed transfer of 445,000 rials per person each month. If the prices of its consumption basket rise by 30 percent over a year and the payment remains unchanged, the original purchasing power is reduced to about 77 percent of its initial level. The decline is not 30 percent, because the calculation divides the original nominal payment by the new price index. This example uses a hypothetical price change rather than attributing an identical experience to every Iranian household. Actual households purchased different combinations of food, housing, transport, and medical services and therefore experienced different effective rates of price increase.

Employment figures likewise require interpretation. The unemployment rate measures people without employment who meet a survey's criteria for being in the labor force and seeking work; it does not count everyone who would prefer a job. An apparent decline in unemployment could coincide with labor-force withdrawal, increased informal work, or deterioration in the quality of jobs. A complete account would examine employment-to-population ratios, participation by age and sex, hours worked, and income. The high expectations generated by expanded university education made the match between skills and available occupations particularly important. A public program that created short-term construction employment did not necessarily solve the problem of finding stable work for university graduates.

Finally, the comparison between the two terms must account for the changing external constraint. In 2012/13 the IMF estimated a real contraction of 5.8 percent alongside substantially increased inflation. Oil and gas production fell sharply and non-oil output also declined. This pattern is not explained by the same combination of factors as inflation in 2007/08. Early inflation predated the most disruptive oil and banking sanctions, while the late contraction coincided with their intensification. A sound historical interpretation recognizes the contribution of both policy and external pressure, and does not use one to absolve all other actors of responsibility. [3] [9] [10] [14]

Section 101.9

Cultural Policy, Social Restrictions, and the Revolutionary Public Sphere

After 2005 the cultural climate shifted from the relatively open, if constantly contested, press environment of the Khatami years. Newspapers faced closures and pressure; publishing and film were subject to tighter oversight; universities saw administrative changes, restrictions on some student organizations, and measures against dissident students and faculty; and moral regulation, including periodic campaigns about dress and public behavior, intensified in several cities. Internet use expanded rapidly in these years, producing a new domain of communication that authorities sought to filter and monitor.

Civil society did not disappear. Women's rights activists launched the One Million Signatures Campaign in 2006, a grassroots effort to gather support for changing discriminatory laws, and many of its organizers faced arrest and harassment. Labor activists, student groups, and nongovernmental organizations continued to operate under constraint. Conservative constituencies were not culturally uniform: some religious conservatives criticized the government for neglecting morality, others for interfering with religious institutions, and some artists and clerics objected to the state's methods. Women, students, workers, minority communities, and professional groups experienced the period differently, and the contrast with the reformist public sphere of Chapter 100 should not be drawn as total silence versus total freedom, but as a change in the terms and risks of public participation.

The changes in cultural administration took place within institutions already established by the Islamic Republic. The Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance administered licensing and cultural policy; judicial bodies could take action against publications and writers; and broadcasting remained under an institutional structure not controlled directly by the presidency. The consequences of a change in government therefore varied across cultural fields. A ministry could adopt a more restrictive licensing policy, while a court could pursue a case on grounds distinct from the ministry's decision. The political significance of these mechanisms lay not only in the closure of particular publications but in the uncertainty created for publishers deciding whether to invest in new work or publish controversial material.

Women's rights activism illustrates continuity as well as change. The One Million Signatures Campaign grew out of earlier efforts to challenge discrimination in law, including inequalities associated with marriage, divorce, inheritance, and citizenship. Launched in 2006, it sought public discussion and signatures in support of legislative change. Its emphasis on conversation and public education differed from an electoral campaign organized around a presidential candidate. Participants could agree on the need to reform a particular legal provision while differing about religious interpretation or the broader political system. Human Rights Watch's 2012 report on Iranian activists in exile documents arrests, pressure, and restrictions encountered by women's rights advocates. Such sources offer important testimony but should be read with attention to their selection of interviewees and their purpose as rights documentation.

Universities continued to be places of political discussion and social mobility. The rapid increase in educational participation meant that restrictions on university organizations or admission affected more than a small, isolated intellectual community. Student disciplinary procedures, restrictions on associations, and exclusions from study were experienced by individuals as constraints on both political expression and their future occupations. The term star-marked students became associated with controversial administrative practices that impeded enrollment. Reports of exclusion of members of the Baha'i community also illustrate that the problem of educational access was not limited to the contest between reformist and conservative political factions. Institutional procedures need to be examined alongside the stated policy rationale of officials and the experiences of those affected.

Labor activism followed its own trajectory. Workers at large enterprises and in urban transportation sought independent representation, payment of wages, and more predictable working conditions. The Tehran bus workers' union and organizing among workers at the Haft Tappeh sugar complex became prominent examples in this period. The demands of a worker facing unpaid wages did not necessarily coincide with the priorities of a newspaper editor or university association, although all might object to restrictions on independent organization. Repression of labor organizing also exposed a contradiction within an administration presenting itself as committed to ordinary workers. The existence of a redistributive policy did not guarantee that its intended beneficiaries could organize independently to influence it.

Digital communication added another layer. Blogs, websites, text messaging, and emerging social media gave people ways to circulate information and debate issues beyond printed publications. Access was uneven, and government filtering, surveillance, and disruption limited the new possibilities. These technologies should not be treated as automatically liberating or as a sufficient explanation of protest. They operated within networks of people, organizations, and institutions already present in Iranian society. The full role of digital media in 2009 belongs to Chapter 102; here its historical significance is the changing structure of the public sphere. [15] [16] [17]

Section 101.10

The Revolutionary Guards, State Institutions, and the Expansion of Economic Influence

Ahmadinejad's presidency coincided with a rise in the political and economic prominence of the Revolutionary Guards and associated networks. Veterans moved into ministries, governorships, and agencies; the Guards' engineering and construction arms won large state contracts in pipelines, roads, and energy; and, under the general privatization policies implementing Article 44, transfers of state assets frequently went to semi-public bodies and organizations connected with revolutionary institutions rather than to private entrepreneurs. A widely reported 2009 sale of 50 percent plus one share in the Telecommunication Company of Iran to the Etemad Mobin consortium linked to the Guards is one commonly cited example, though details of ownership structures were disputed.

This was not conventional private-sector development. Four categories must be distinguished: formal state ownership; private enterprise operating at arm's length from government; parastatal organizations, including foundations (bonyads) and cooperatives, with public connections but independent management and often limited oversight; and enterprises associated with the Guards, which combined security status, political influence, and commercial activity. Boundaries among these were blurred, and patronage played a role in access to contracts. Their comprehensive history belongs to Chapter 105.

The economic expansion of revolutionary institutions was rooted in the postwar reconstruction era, when engineering organizations connected with the Revolutionary Guards developed experience in major projects. Ahmadinejad inherited these organizations rather than creating them. Their influence grew as the government commissioned infrastructure and energy projects and as international restrictions made the participation of some foreign contractors more difficult. Organizations able to mobilize engineering personnel, equipment, and political relationships had advantages when public agencies sought domestic contractors. Yet the award of a contract must be distinguished from ownership of the underlying resource or project. A company building part of an oil development does not thereby own the petroleum reserves.

The privatization framework also had earlier constitutional origins. Article 44 divided the economy into state, cooperative, and private sectors, with extensive activities assigned to public ownership. General policies issued by the Supreme Leader in 2005 and 2006 provided a framework for widening nonstate participation and transferring many public enterprise assets. Implementation accelerated during Ahmadinejad's administration. The constitutional provision itself was not simply repealed or rewritten. The policy directives and the legislation enacted to implement them altered the practical interpretation of permissible ownership. This distinction helps explain how a transfer could take place under a Constitution that had originally envisaged a dominant public sector.

The sale of a controlling stake in the Telecommunication Company of Iran in 2009 illustrates the issue. A consortium associated with the Revolutionary Guards' cooperative foundation acquired the controlling interest in a transaction widely reported as a major privatization sale. The transaction did reduce direct state shareholding in the enterprise, but it did not necessarily broaden competition in ownership in the manner implied by an uncomplicated use of the term privatization. Contemporary descriptions of the ultimate beneficial ownership of the consortium and its constituent companies varied. The historical point is the intersection of corporate ownership and political affiliation, not a claim that every worker or business in the telecommunications industry belonged to a military organization.

A similar distinction applies to revolutionary foundations. Bonyads administered assets and welfare activities and could operate commercial subsidiaries. Their mandates, legal status, and lines of accountability differed across organizations. They were not merely ministries and were not identical to the Guards' engineering enterprises. The state's public sector therefore contained entities with different degrees of administrative, commercial, and political autonomy. A comparison of published company registers, government procurement records, parliamentary investigations, and audited accounts would be needed to establish the precise allocation of economic resources among them. Estimates of a single percentage of the entire economy controlled by one institution are especially difficult to evaluate without specifying the year and the definition of control.

Personnel changes reinforced these relationships but did not eliminate political disagreement. Veterans could bring experience and networks into civilian administration while retaining different views of presidential policy. The Guards' leadership and the president did not necessarily share identical objectives on every budget, commercial transaction, or political succession. Chapter 105 will examine the institutional history in greater depth. For Chapter 101, the crucial development is the increasing importance of these networks in a government that combined asset transfers, state contracts, and a claim to redistribute public wealth. Section 101.11

The Contested Presidential Election of 2009 and the Crisis of Political Legitimacy

Ahmadinejad ran for reelection in June 2009 against Mir-Hossein Mousavi, a former prime minister, the cleric Mehdi Karroubi, and Mohsen Rezaei, a former Guards commander. The campaign was unusually intense, with televised debates in which candidates attacked the president's economic management and conduct, and with large public rallies. The official result declared Ahmadinejad the winner with roughly two thirds of the vote on high turnout. Opponents alleged fraud and demanded annulment; officials rejected the claims and the Supreme Leader endorsed the result. Mass demonstrations followed and were met with arrests and force. The Green Movement emerged from these events.

The official outcome and the allegations are separate historical items. This chapter does not resolve the dispute by assertion; it records that the opposition claimed irregularities, that official bodies rejected the claims after limited recounts and reviews, and that independent assessments differ. Chapter 102 examines the evidence and the movement fully.

For Ahmadinejad's presidency the consequences were profound. His second term began with a legitimacy deficit among a significant part of the population, his dependence on the Supreme Leader and on security institutions increased, and political elites who had stood aside from earlier disagreements were pushed to take positions. The episode also reinforced a security-minded approach to dissent and a narrowing of the political space.

The electoral campaign of 2009 differed markedly from that of 2005 because Ahmadinejad was now an incumbent defending a record of government. Economic promises could be compared with inflation, employment, housing, and the distribution of petroleum receipts. Mousavi, who had served as prime minister during the Iran–Iraq War, brought his own revolutionary credentials and a reputation for administrative experience. Karroubi criticized restrictions on political rights and presented alternative social and economic proposals. Rezaei offered another conservative alternative. The candidates' televised debates made accusations about administrative misconduct and personal privilege unusually visible. A contest that might otherwise have been described as competition between established factions became a much broader public argument about the conduct of government.

The Ministry of Interior's declared results gave Ahmadinejad approximately 62.6 percent of reported ballots. Those figures are evidence of the outcome announced by the authorities, not a substitute for analysis of the opposition's allegations. Mousavi and Karroubi challenged the results. The Guardian Council reviewed complaints and conducted a partial recount, ultimately certifying the result; the opposition maintained its objections. The lack of access to some underlying records and the contested environment limit what can be independently established from aggregate returns. Statistical anomalies, reports by witnesses, administrative records, and official declarations are distinct forms of evidence and need different methods of assessment. Chapter 102 will examine them in detail, including the arguments made by supporters and opponents of the official outcome.

The political consequences did not depend on every citizen accepting the same interpretation of electoral integrity. A substantial movement rejected the declared result and demanded accountability; state authorities defended it and treated subsequent opposition mobilization as a security challenge. The Supreme Leader's intervention strengthened the institutional defense of the announced outcome. Restrictions on demonstrations, political organizations, journalists, and public expression narrowed the space available for organized opposition. Contemporary rights documentation establishes extensive repression, though particular cases require individual attribution. The resulting conflict damaged relationships among elites who had previously competed within the institutions of the Islamic Republic, including political figures with long records of service to the revolutionary state.

For the presidency, the distinction between formal confirmation in office and broad-based political acceptance was decisive. Ahmadinejad retained executive authority after the official certification and inauguration of his second term. Yet continued disputes over legitimacy affected the environment in which his policies were received. Economic measures, even those conceived before the election, were now implemented amid greater polarization. Officials who supported the official outcome did not thereby commit themselves to every presidential decision. Some defended the political system against the opposition while subsequently challenging Ahmadinejad over appointments or the conduct of economic policy. The alliance formed to protect the constitutional order in 2009 therefore should not be assumed to have permanently resolved disagreements within the conservative establishment.

This section deliberately stops short of a full history of the Green Movement. Its purpose is to explain the altered conditions of government: a presidency sustained by formal institutions and strong allies, but operating amid an unresolved crisis of public confidence and elite relations. Those conditions help explain why the next major conflict emerged within the president's own political camp rather than only between government and opposition. [19] [15] [50]

Section 101.12

Ahmadinejad's Second Term and Conflict Within the Conservative Establishment

The most revealing disagreements of the second term involved the president's closest associate, Esfandiar Rahim Mashaei. In 2009 Ahmadinejad appointed him first vice president, but an objection from the Supreme Leader led to the appointment's reversal; Ahmadinejad later made him chief of staff. Mashaei's statements emphasizing an Iranian national and humanistic identity, and his expressions of openness to other peoples, drew hostile reactions from many clerics and conservatives, who saw them as downplaying Islamic and clerical authority. Opponents labeled the president's circle a "deviant current."

The conflict grew to include constitutional authority. In April 2011 Ahmadinejad dismissed the intelligence minister, Heydar Moslehi. Khamenei reinstated him, and the president withdrew from public duties for approximately eleven days before returning. In 2012 parliament summoned the president for questioning, which was unusual, over economic management, cabinet disputes, and other matters. Allegations of corruption touched some of the president's associates, while the president suggested that opponents, including figures linked to the judiciary and parliament led by the Larijani brothers, were abusing their positions. By 2012–2013 the president was increasingly isolated from his former supporters.

Alignment in an election did not guarantee institutional cooperation. The disagreements concerned several distinct matters: the limits of presidential authority relative to the Leader, parliament, and the judiciary; the ideological direction of the government, especially the relationship between national identity and religious authority; the question of who would shape the political succession; and control of economic resources and appointments. Different actors emphasized different issues, which is why the conservative camp could not reunite despite shared opposition to the Green Movement.

The conflict over Mashaei made the distribution of authority unusually visible. In July 2009, at the beginning of the transition to Ahmadinejad's second term, the president appointed him first vice president. Khamenei directed that he be removed from that post. Mashaei's appointment ended, but he remained close to Ahmadinejad and acquired another important role in the presidential office. His continued political prominence demonstrated the president's determination to maintain control over his advisers even after accepting the Leader's intervention in one appointment. The episode involved a disagreement about the influence of a particular individual, as well as a test of the executive's freedom to organize its own leadership team.

Mashaei's opponents interpreted his public emphasis on Iranian identity and some religious themes as a challenge to the primacy of established clerical authority. The label deviant current, used by political critics, was polemical rather than a neutral designation of a legally constituted party. The administration's critics were themselves diverse. Some were concerned about religion, others about the political ambitions of the presidential circle, and still others about access to government resources. An accusation made by a factional rival is evidence of the conflict and the language through which it was conducted; it is not automatically proof of every allegation contained in it.

In April 2011 the intelligence ministry dispute raised the same question in a particularly sensitive administrative setting. Ahmadinejad sought the departure of Intelligence Minister Heydar Moslehi, but the Leader intervened to retain him in office. Ahmadinejad then withdrew from public engagements and cabinet meetings for approximately eleven days before resuming his duties. The Ministry of Intelligence is formally a ministry of the executive, yet the political practice of national security and the Leader's constitutional role made it a field of particularly close oversight. It would be misleading to say either that the president enjoyed unrestricted ministerial appointment power or that every intelligence institution was directly administered by the Leader. The dispute is important precisely because formal executive responsibility and wider structures of security authority intersected.

Parliamentary opposition further reduced the president's freedom of action. Under Article 88 of the Constitution, legislators can require the president to answer questions under specified conditions. In March 2012 Ahmadinejad appeared before the Majles to respond to a series of questions about his administration, an unprecedented formal questioning of a president under the Islamic Republic. Although this procedure did not itself remove him from office, it showed that parliamentary conservatives were prepared to use institutional powers against a president associated with their own broader political camp. Budgetary disputes, controversial ministerial decisions, and disagreements about the implementation of economic policies contributed to the confrontation.

By the final year of the presidency, competition over the succession intensified these disputes. Ahmadinejad could not immediately seek a third consecutive term, and the political prospects of his associates became a major concern. The Guardian Council's later exclusion of Mashaei from the 2013 presidential ballot demonstrated that support from the incumbent did not guarantee access to the next electoral contest. At the same time, conservative politicians outside his circle were unable to settle on a single effective presidential candidate. The fragmentation of the coalition was therefore both an institutional conflict and an electoral problem. It contributed to the conditions under which Rouhani could assemble a competing coalition in 2013. [2] [20] [21]

Section 101.13

The Nuclear Program and the Breakdown of Earlier Diplomatic Arrangements

The nuclear dispute began under Khatami. After the disclosure in 2002 of undeclared facilities at Natanz and Arak, Iran signed a joint statement with the foreign ministers of Britain, France, and Germany in Tehran in October 2003, agreed to suspend enrichment-related activities voluntarily, and in December 2003 signed the IAEA Additional Protocol, applying it provisionally. The Paris Agreement of November 2004 extended the suspension while negotiations continued. Iranian negotiators, including Hassan Rouhani, then secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, presented these steps as confidence-building measures rather than legal obligations.

On August 1, 2005, during the presidential transition, Iran notified the IAEA of its decision to resume conversion at Isfahan; activity resumed on August 8, shortly after Ahmadinejad entered office. In January–February 2006 it resumed enrichment-related work, and announced in April that it had enriched uranium at Natanz on a research scale. The government presented these steps as the exercise of a sovereign right to peaceful nuclear technology under the Nonproliferation Treaty. Public support for a national nuclear program, including among many critics of the government, gave the issue broad domestic resonance and made concessions politically costly. Other states and the IAEA emphasized concerns about the program's transparency and purposes, given Iran's earlier failure to declare certain activities.

Decisions of the late Khatami period, including the decision to resume conversion, should be distinguished from later decisions under Ahmadinejad, which ended further elements of the suspension and shifted toward a more confrontational posture. The nuclear issue also became linked to domestic legitimacy: resisting pressure served as a claim to independence.

The diplomatic arrangements inherited from the Khatami period attempted to create time for negotiation while the Agency investigated previously unreported activities. In the Paris Agreement of November 2004, Iran and the European governments recognized a suspension as a voluntary confidence-building measure rather than a permanent renunciation of nuclear activity. The distinction mattered to Iranian negotiators because they asserted the rights of a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, while European negotiators sought assurances against proliferation that they regarded as durable. The two sides did not agree on what a satisfactory long-term settlement would require. Thus, the agreement postponed an immediate confrontation without eliminating the underlying dispute over the program's future.

By the summer of 2005 the arrangement had reached an impasse. On August 1 Iran notified the IAEA that it would resume uranium conversion activity at Isfahan, while Khatami's presidency was drawing to a close; conversion activity resumed on August 8. Ahmadinejad was sworn into office during this transition. The distinction between the decision and its implementation corrects a misleading account in which the suspension survived unchanged until the incoming president independently reversed it weeks later. Negotiations had already encountered serious disagreements under Khatami. Ahmadinejad's subsequent policies and rhetoric contributed to the escalation, but they were not the sole origin of the breakdown.

The next steps increased the international dispute. On September 24, 2005, the IAEA Board adopted a resolution finding that Iran's earlier safeguards failures constituted noncompliance within the terms of the Agency's Statute. In early 2006 Iran ended further elements of the suspension and the Board reported the issue to the Security Council. Iran also ceased voluntary implementation of the Additional Protocol in February 2006. These developments involved different commitments: the comprehensive safeguards agreement remained a legal obligation, while the Additional Protocol had been signed but had not been ratified, and the suspension had been undertaken as a negotiated voluntary measure before the Council imposed its own requirements. The chronology matters because ceasing one form of cooperation was not equivalent to withdrawing from the Non-Proliferation Treaty.

For Iranian leaders, the program was an assertion of scientific capacity and equal treatment under the international treaty system. Article IV of the NPT recognizes peaceful nuclear activity in conformity with the treaty's other provisions. Iranian officials emphasized that principle. Other governments argued that the history of incomplete reporting and the remaining verification questions required additional measures. The legal dispute was thus not adequately described either as a blanket prohibition of peaceful science or as a situation in which a state could invoke peaceful intent to set aside safeguards duties. The historical problem was how to reconcile treaty rights, verifiable commitments, and the confidence demanded by other parties.

Domestic politics influenced the way these questions were framed. Ahmadinejad made technological achievement an important element of his public image. Negotiating compromises could be portrayed by opponents as surrendering a national accomplishment, even when the actual issue was the duration or scope of a confidence-building measure. The change in negotiating leadership during 2007 further indicated disagreement about diplomatic strategy. The Supreme Leader and the Supreme National Security Council continued to exercise authority in this policy area; the president's highly visible rhetoric did not mean that he could unilaterally define the country's entire nuclear policy. Section 101.14

The International Atomic Energy Agency and United Nations Sanctions

The IAEA's Board of Governors found Iran in noncompliance with its safeguards obligations in 2005 and in February 2006 reported the matter to the Security Council. Three kinds of action must be kept distinct. IAEA investigations are technical and legal verification processes under safeguards agreements. The Security Council adopts resolutions under the UN Charter; the legal effect of each measure depends on the operative wording and the authority invoked. Unilateral national sanctions are measures taken by individual states or the European Union under their own law. In July 2006 the Council demanded suspension of enrichment-related activity. When Iran did not comply, it adopted sanctions in December 2006 and expanded them in 2007, 2008, and 2010.

The United States, Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and China took part in negotiations over these resolutions, with Russia and China generally favoring more limited measures and more diplomacy. Iran argued that the resolutions were unlawful, that it had a right to enrichment under the Nonproliferation Treaty, and that Western states applied standards selectively. Western governments and many others replied that the Council's authority was binding and that uncertainty about the program's purposes justified restrictions. The IAEA continued to report on cooperation and outstanding questions, and in 2007 a US intelligence assessment judged with high confidence that a specifically defined weapons program had halted in 2003, while the question of current intentions remained disputed. Concerns, documented findings, legal obligations, and allegations must each be identified in the sources.

The International Atomic Energy Agency and the Security Council exercise different kinds of authority. Under a safeguards agreement, the Agency verifies declarations and investigates questions falling within its legal mandate. The Director General reports findings to the Board of Governors, whose decisions are institutional acts of the Agency. The Security Council, by contrast, may act under the United Nations Charter to address threats to international peace and security. A report from the Board does not by itself impose a worldwide trade embargo. The movement of the dispute from Vienna to New York in 2006 was therefore a consequential change in the institutional framework, not merely a change in the location of negotiations.

The Board's September 2005 finding concerned Iran's failures and breaches of its safeguards obligations. In February 2006 the Board decided to report the matter to the Council. Resolution 1696 of July 31, 2006, invoked Article 40 of the Charter and demanded suspension of enrichment-related and reprocessing activities, subject to verification by the Agency. It also indicated the possibility of measures under Article 41 if Iran did not comply. Resolution 1737, adopted on December 23, then acted under Article 41, specifying restrictions and obligations for member states. These measures were not an authorization of military force. The distinction between the Council's demand in July and its imposition of sanctions in December helps explain the successive steps of diplomatic escalation.

The subsequent resolutions broadened the framework. Resolution 1747 of March 24, 2007, extended measures against designated parties and prohibited Iranian arms exports. Resolution 1803 of March 3, 2008, expanded designations and called for heightened vigilance in specified financial and transport dealings. Resolution 1835 of September 27, 2008, reaffirmed earlier demands without establishing a new major sanctions package. Resolution 1929 of June 9, 2010, enlarged restrictions involving arms, designated entities, and other activities connected with the nuclear and missile disputes. The instruments differed in both operative wording and scope. A call for vigilance is not automatically equivalent to a mandatory prohibition, and a targeted freeze does not necessarily apply to all assets of all Iranian citizens. Chapter 103 will develop the full legal architecture.

Political agreement among permanent Council members was possible without agreement on every aspect of policy toward Iran. Russia and China supported successive Council measures, while also maintaining diplomatic and commercial relations with Iran and resisting some proposals for broader pressure. European governments had participated in earlier negotiations; the United States was initially outside the direct European talks but became a central member of the later multilateral process. Their shared support for a particular resolution reflected a negotiated text rather than a permanent common strategy. The votes on Resolution 1929, including opposition from Turkey and Brazil, make visible the limits of consensus.

The public release of the 2007 American National Intelligence Estimate also complicated political interpretation. Its key judgments assessed with high confidence that Iran had halted a nuclear weapons program in autumn 2003, defining that program in a narrower sense than all civilian nuclear activity. It did not conclude that safeguards disputes were resolved or that declared civilian activities had ended. An intelligence assessment, an IAEA finding concerning safeguards, and a Security Council decision address different questions. None should be used as though it established every fact about the others. For the historian, the coexistence of these documents is evidence that security policy involved both uncertain judgments about intentions and concrete disputes over observable commitments. Section 101.15

American and European Pressure, Financial Restrictions, and Economic Isolation

American sanctions long predated Ahmadinejad, including broad restrictions dating from the 1980s and 1990s. During his presidency Washington expanded measures targeting Iranian banks, companies linked to the Revolutionary Guards, and foreign firms investing in Iran's energy sector. Congress passed legislation in 2010 and 2011 that penalized foreign banks dealing with sanctioned Iranian institutions, including, by the end of 2011, the Central Bank. The European Union, previously more reluctant, adopted its own measures after 2010, including an embargo on Iranian crude oil that took effect in mid-2012, and in 2012 Iranian banks designated under EU rules were disconnected from the SWIFT financial messaging system.

Restrictions on financial transactions can affect businesses, households, and government finances even where goods are not prohibited. Banks that fear penalties refuse to process payments; shipping and insurance firms withdraw; exporters cannot easily repatriate revenue; and importers cannot obtain trade finance. Such effects reached medicine suppliers and food importers through payment difficulties, even where humanitarian trade was formally allowed.

The United States already maintained extensive restrictions on Iran before Ahmadinejad's election. American restrictions had developed over many years in response to several disputes, including alleged support for armed organizations, terrorism designations, human rights concerns, and nuclear proliferation. The sanctions associated with the nuclear confrontation did not replace every earlier measure. This historical layering makes it important to identify the issuing authority and policy basis for each restriction. A government may suspend a measure adopted for one purpose while leaving another measure in force. Chapter 103 treats that legal complexity comprehensively; this section examines the economic channels through which pressure increased during 2005–2013.

The Comprehensive Iran Sanctions, Accountability, and Divestment Act of 2010 strengthened American restrictions on specified petroleum-related transactions and access to the financial system. The National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal year 2012, enacted in December 2011, included provisions targeting transactions with Iran's central bank and other designated institutions. The latter approach made access to the American financial system relevant to the decisions of foreign banks that otherwise had no direct dealings with American customers in a particular transaction. These are examples of the significance of secondary sanctions. They differed from a prohibition on an American company directly conducting a transaction with a sanctioned Iranian party.

European policy changed substantially in the same period. On January 23, 2012, the Council of the European Union adopted a new package that prohibited imports of Iranian crude oil and petroleum products subject to transitional arrangements. Existing qualifying oil contracts could continue until July 1. The measures also affected finance, insurance, and other services. Because many transactions depend on several independent institutions, the withdrawal of one participant can prevent a transaction even if another participant is willing to proceed. A foreign importer, for example, might be legally permitted to purchase a product but unable to obtain insurance or a bank willing to process payment under the applicable rules. This is an economic transmission mechanism, not evidence that every type of trade was legally forbidden.

The March 2012 disconnection of designated Iranian financial institutions from SWIFT was particularly important in this respect. SWIFT announced the decision on March 15 and implemented it on March 17. SWIFT supplies secure standardized financial messages; it does not itself hold the balances of the institutions using its network. The restriction affected their ability to exchange messages through that service rather than automatically confiscating all Iranian funds held abroad. It applied to institutions subject to the relevant European sanctions, not indiscriminately to every bank in the country. Other restrictions and the decisions of correspondent banks separately limited the use of international funds.

The distinction between a legal exemption and practical access was particularly important for humanitarian trade. Even where food and medicine were generally exempt or eligible for authorization, importers could encounter bank compliance concerns, a shortage of usable foreign currency, delayed payment, and rising transport costs. Domestic distribution and exchange-rate policies could amplify the problem. The material effect of international financial pressure must therefore be studied through actual commercial relationships and the experience of households rather than inferred only from the text of a prohibition. At the same time, an analysis of that hardship must not assume that sanctions were responsible for every shortage or every policy failure. [32] [33] [34] [35] [10]

Section 101.16

Nuclear Negotiations, the Tehran Declaration, and the Limits of Diplomatic Alternatives

Negotiations continued throughout. The P5+1, comprising the five permanent members of the Security Council and Germany, with the European Union's foreign policy chief as coordinator, became the main negotiating grouping. Saeed Jalili, who succeeded Ali Larijani as the lead Iranian negotiator in 2007, held repeated rounds of talks with them. Russia and China acted both as participants in sanctions and as advocates for diplomacy. The Obama administration, which took office in 2009, spoke of engagement as well as pressure, while Iran's disputed election and the revelation in 2009 of an additional enrichment site near Qom complicated talks.

In October 2009 negotiators in Geneva and Vienna discussed a plan under which Iran would send part of its low-enriched uranium abroad in exchange for fuel for the Tehran Research Reactor, a measure seen by the proposers as reducing Iran's stockpile and building confidence. Iranian officials disagreed internally over the plan, and it was not accepted as drafted. In May 2010 Turkey and Brazil, led by Erdoğan and Lula, concluded a declaration with Iran in Tehran in which Iran agreed in principle to a swap along broadly similar lines. The United States and its partners judged it insufficient, notably because Iran had since begun enriching to a higher level and its stockpile had grown, and the Security Council adopted a further sanctions resolution in June 2010, with Turkey and Brazil voting against.

A diplomatic proposal, a political declaration, a negotiated agreement, and an implemented settlement are different things. The failure of this initiative does not show that no alternatives existed; it shows that the parties disagreed about what the declaration would accomplish and about sequence and trust. Domestic politics constrained all sides, and in Iran, resistance to concessions was strong, while in Washington and other capitals, skepticism of Iranian intentions was strong. Temporary arrangements could not resolve the underlying questions, and the later nuclear agreement is examined in Chapter 103.

The evolution of negotiating formats reflected the growing international significance of the dispute. The European three had initiated negotiations with Tehran in 2003. As the dispute moved into the Security Council, the five permanent members and Germany became the principal group of governments seeking a negotiated arrangement. The European Union's foreign policy representative helped coordinate this process. A negotiating group is not itself identical to the Security Council: Germany participated in the talks even though it was not a permanent member of the Council. The group sought a settlement with Iran, whereas the Council adopted resolutions under its own procedures. This distinction explains why a compromise proposed in diplomatic talks did not automatically change obligations established in Council resolutions.

The personnel conducting Iranian negotiations also changed. Hassan Rouhani had led the earlier talks under Khatami. Ali Larijani succeeded him in 2005 and was replaced as chief negotiator in 2007 by Saeed Jalili. Differences over tactics and the relationship of negotiating officials to the president's political agenda were part of the context for that transition. The change did not create a wholly new constitutional decision-making structure. Senior officials continued to operate within the Supreme National Security Council and under the broader direction of the Supreme Leader. The political importance of a negotiator should therefore be distinguished from the authority of the state institutions whose decisions the negotiator represented.

Obama's arrival in office in January 2009 introduced an explicit effort to combine diplomatic engagement with international pressure. His Nowruz message that year addressed both the Iranian people and the Islamic Republic. The contested June election and subsequent repression made sustained engagement politically more difficult. In September the disclosure of a previously unannounced facility near Qom further complicated the atmosphere of negotiations. The ensuing Geneva and Vienna discussions explored a confidence-building arrangement involving fuel for a research reactor used for medical purposes. The central diplomatic idea was that an exchange could give Iran a practical benefit while providing other parties with a reason to have greater confidence in the program's immediate direction. The parties did not ultimately implement the proposed arrangement.

Turkey and Brazil attempted to revive the diplomatic opportunity in May 2010. Their foreign ministers signed a joint declaration with Iran in Tehran on May 17, in the presence of their national leaders. The declaration set out a conditional framework involving the deposit of Iranian material in Turkey and the provision of research-reactor fuel under a subsequent arrangement with the relevant parties. It reaffirmed peaceful nuclear rights and presented the exchange as a confidence-building step. The declaration's historical significance lay in the participation of governments outside the established permanent-member negotiating framework. It also depended on cooperation by parties that had not themselves signed the Tehran document. A joint declaration between three governments could not by itself obligate all other negotiating parties to supply the promised fuel or lift sanctions.

Washington and other governments argued that the proposed exchange no longer addressed the broader concerns that had developed since the 2009 negotiations, and the Security Council adopted Resolution 1929 in June. Turkey and Brazil opposed it; Lebanon abstained. The failure of the initiative illustrates competing judgments about the sequencing and scope of diplomacy. Later talks at Istanbul, Baghdad, Moscow, and Almaty continued through the final years of the presidency, but no comprehensive settlement was concluded before the transfer of office. The historian should recognize the alternatives that were debated without treating a proposed exchange as an accomplished settlement or assuming that every failure resulted from one side's rejection alone. Section 101.17

Regional Strategy, International Alliances, and Confrontational Diplomacy

Iran's foreign policy in these years extended well beyond the nuclear dispute. In Iraq, where the fall of Saddam Hussein brought Shi'i parties with long Iranian connections to power, Tehran built extensive political and economic ties, and Ahmadinejad made a 2008 visit to Baghdad, the first by an Iranian president since the revolution. Iran supported Hezbollah in Lebanon, which fought a war with Israel in 2006, maintained a close alliance with the Syrian government, and engaged Palestinian organizations including Hamas. Relations with Saudi Arabia and the Gulf monarchies combined commerce and rivalry, with both sides competing for influence in Iraq, Lebanon, Bahrain, and the wider region.

Beyond the Middle East, Iran cultivated Russia and China as diplomatic and commercial partners, with Russia as a nuclear supplier and a veto-holding Council member, and China as the largest purchaser of Iranian oil. Ahmadinejad also developed ties with Venezuela's Hugo Chávez and other Latin American governments critical of American policy. Iran hosted the 2012 summit of the Non-Aligned Movement in Tehran, which it presented as evidence that it was not isolated. The Arab uprisings of 2011 offered Iranian officials a narrative of "Islamic awakening", while the Syrian conflict that followed placed Iran in support of a government confronting a rising armed and popular opposition.

Ahmadinejad drew intense international criticism for statements about Israel and the Holocaust. In October 2005 he quoted a saying attributed to Ayatollah Khomeini about the Israeli regime, and the English rendering "wiped off the map" became widely used, though translators dispute its precise meaning and Iranian officials argued it referred to a regime rather than to a population. He also questioned or denied the Holocaust in several speeches, and a conference on the subject was held in Tehran in 2006. These are statements of rhetoric, to be distinguished from the government's actual policy and from independently established historical facts, and the Holocaust is thoroughly documented by historical evidence. Foreign policy was not the president's alone: the Supreme Leader, the Supreme National Security Council, the Guards, and the foreign ministry shaped decisions, and the Quds Force in particular played a role in regional operations outside the president's control.

Iran's regional policies in Iraq and Afghanistan reflected the removal of governments that had previously threatened it. The Iraqi state created after 2003 contained political parties and leaders with long-standing connections to Iran, including organizations that had spent years in exile during Saddam Hussein's rule. Iran could cultivate relationships through diplomatic, commercial, religious, and security channels. Nevertheless, the Iraqi political field contained actors with competing local and national priorities; shared Shi'i identity was not a guarantee of obedience to Iran. Pilgrimage and commerce between the countries also involved ordinary citizens and businesses, so relations cannot be reduced to the activities of governments and security organizations alone.

The relationship with Syria had developed much earlier, particularly during the Iran–Iraq War. It provided Iran with an important Arab state partner and a connection to Hezbollah in Lebanon. Hezbollah itself occupied several roles, including political participation, social organization, and armed activity. Iran's support for Palestinian groups, including Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, similarly formed part of a regional strategy and ideological commitment. The relationships varied over time and were not simply extensions of one identical alliance. The upheavals after 2011 exposed disagreements among some of these partners, particularly over Syria. The historical analysis must distinguish the policies of the Iranian government from the agency and competing interests of the organizations with which it maintained relations.

Relations with Saudi Arabia combined continuing diplomacy and trade with regional competition. The collapse of Saddam Hussein's government altered the balance in the Gulf, while the 2011 uprisings introduced new disputes, including over Bahrain and Syria. Iranian officials used a language of Islamic awakening to describe events in several Arab countries, but their government's response to the uprising against its Syrian ally followed a different logic. This contrast demonstrates how ideological identification with popular mobilization could conflict with established strategic relationships. Saudi policy, meanwhile, reflected its own security concerns, domestic institutions, and alliances. The conflict was not simply a consequence of theological differences between Sunni and Shi'i communities.

Partnerships with Russia and China were based on a different set of interests. Russia participated in civilian nuclear cooperation, including the Bushehr power station, and maintained substantial diplomatic influence as a permanent member of the Security Council. China was an important purchaser of Iranian petroleum. Neither state was simply an unconditional ally: both participated in multilateral negotiations and voted for Council sanctions. The resulting relationships show why commercial and security interests may coexist with disagreement over a particular international issue. Iran also pursued links with Venezuela and other governments in Latin America, which provided diplomatic visibility and selected commercial opportunities without replacing the importance of the country's neighboring markets.

The 2012 Non-Aligned Movement summit in Tehran provided an opportunity to emphasize the country's diplomatic relationships outside the Western-centered institutions imposing many of the restrictions. Participation in an international meeting, however, was not equivalent to endorsement of all Iranian policies. The president's confrontational rhetoric also generated distinct diplomatic costs, especially his documented denial and distortion of the Holocaust. Historical evidence establishes the Nazi persecution and mass murder of European Jews; its reality is not a matter of competing legitimate historical interpretations. Contemporary criticism of Ahmadinejad's statements came from both outside Iran and Iranian intellectuals. The episode illustrates why presidential rhetoric could damage diplomatic relationships even where the underlying strategic interests of governments remained unchanged. [38] [39] [40]

Section 101.18

Currency Crisis, Sanctions, and the Material Consequences of International Confrontation

The second term's economic crisis combined several pressures. Oil export volumes fell after the 2012 European embargo and tighter financial controls, and payment channels for the remaining exports were disrupted. Foreign-exchange earnings fell relative to demand for imports, and the shortage pushed up the price of dollars in the open market. The rial lost a large share of its value between late 2011 and late 2012, more than halving against the dollar at the lowest points, and the government maintained multiple exchange rates, with an official preferential rate for essential imports alongside a much weaker market rate, a system that created opportunities for arbitrage and allegations of corruption.

The chain from foreign exchange to prices is direct. Imported inputs and finished goods became more expensive when the currency fell; domestic producers dependent on imported components faced higher costs and shortages; consumer prices rose; and households with fixed incomes, including recipients of cash transfers, saw purchasing power fall. Medicines and food were generally subject to exemptions or authorizations under applicable sanctions regimes but were affected by payment difficulties and by the exchange-rate system, producing shortages for some patients. Employment pressures increased as firms cut production; the reported unemployment rate alone does not capture these effects.

Responsibility for the crisis is contested and historians should avoid single explanations. Direct sanctions effects, especially on oil and banking, were severe. Domestic policies, including earlier money-financed spending and the exchange-rate regime, left the economy vulnerable, and administrative failures and uncertainty added to the damage. Critics of the government stress the domestic factors; the government stressed sanctions. Neither factor alone explains the timing or the scale of the outcome. The impact also differed by group: those with access to preferential exchange rates or hard assets fared differently from wage earners and pensioners.

The 2012 crisis involved a sharp reduction in the country's ability to earn and use foreign exchange. The United States Energy Information Administration's contemporary assessment found that crude oil and condensate exports fell steeply in 2012 as oil-sector restrictions intensified. The IMF's 2014 consultation reported that oil and gas export earnings fell from 118.2 billion dollars in 2011/12 to 62.9 billion in 2012/13. These measures capture related but different developments. A physical export series measures the quantity of a specified product sold abroad; a revenue series reflects quantities, prices, and the product categories included. Neither measure alone establishes how much foreign currency was immediately available to importers. Restrictions on banking could make some receipts difficult to use even after a sale was recorded.

When demand for foreign currency exceeds the amount available through official channels, rationing and parallel markets can develop. The government may allocate preferential foreign exchange for designated essential imports in an attempt to limit consumer prices. Such a policy creates a distinction between the official price of a dollar and the price at which a transaction can be completed elsewhere. A business that cannot obtain currency at the official rate must either pay more, delay the transaction, reduce its orders, or find a domestic substitute. If some firms can acquire currency at the preferential rate while others cannot, access to the allocation becomes a valuable economic advantage. This can increase incentives for lobbying, misuse of licenses, and other forms of rent-seeking.

The mathematics of currency depreciation is important for interpreting contemporary reports. Suppose, purely as an illustration, the local-currency price of a dollar rises from 10,000 to 20,000 rials. The price of the dollar has doubled, but the dollar value of a rial has fallen by half. Describing both changes as a 100 percent loss of the rial would be incorrect. Official and parallel exchange rates must also not be mixed in a calculation. The IMF reported that the authorities abandoned their unified managed exchange-rate regime during the 2012 deterioration and again unified official rates in June 2013. The existence of a unified official rate did not mean that the parallel rate was necessarily identical or that access to foreign currency had returned to normal.

The consequences extended beyond imported luxury goods. Industrial firms requiring foreign components, machinery, or intermediate goods faced higher costs and difficulties in obtaining credit and materials. A domestic producer could face a simultaneous decline in consumer demand and increase in production costs, a combination that discouraged investment and employment. Households with wages or pension payments adjusted infrequently were particularly vulnerable to rapidly rising prices. Some households had assets whose nominal values rose with inflation, but that did not mean every asset holder could readily convert those assets into the goods and services needed for daily life. The unequal distribution of assets and foreign-exchange access therefore shaped the social consequences of the crisis.

Medical access illustrates the interaction of external and domestic causes. The legal treatment of medicines differed from that of many restricted products, yet payment disruptions and the depreciation of the rial could still impede supplies. Contemporary medical and human-rights accounts described difficulties obtaining specific imported medicines. The impact was especially serious for people requiring continuous treatment, for whom a delayed purchase could not be offset merely by reducing another category of consumption. Attributing a particular shortage requires examination of purchasing, bank payment, currency allocation, domestic production, and distribution records. The experience is not adequately captured by a single national inflation figure. The later demand for diplomatic and economic adjustment reflected this combination of aggregate contraction and concrete household insecurity. [10] [14] [34] [35] [41] [45]

Section 101.19

The Presidential Election of 2013 and the Search for a New Political Direction

The constitution bars a president from serving more than two consecutive terms, and Ahmadinejad could not run in June 2013. He tried to keep influence through Mashaei, whom he supported for the presidency, but the Guardian Council disqualified Mashaei, along with Rafsanjani, who had registered, from a field of several hundred registrants, leaving eight approved candidates. The conservative field included Saeed Jalili, Ali Akbar Velayati, Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, Mohsen Rezaei, and Gholam-Ali Haddad-Adel (who withdrew), and the conservative vote was divided among several of them.

Hassan Rouhani, a cleric and former nuclear negotiator who had served on the Supreme National Security Council, ran on a platform of "prudence and hope," promising economic recovery, an improved relationship with the world, and greater respect for civil rights. The reformist Mohammad Reza Aref withdrew in his favor, and Khatami and Rafsanjani endorsed him. Voters concerned about inflation and sanctions, and many who had stayed home in earlier elections, turned out in high numbers. Rouhani won an outright majority in the first round, with roughly half the vote on turnout in the low seventies percent, and took office in August 2013.

The election represented a change in political direction without replacing the constitutional system: Rouhani was himself a long-standing member of the establishment, the Supreme Leader retained final authority, and screening had still limited the field. The causes of his victory, including economic dissatisfaction, the fragmentation of the conservative vote, and public expectations on nuclear diplomacy, should be distinguished from his later governing record, which Chapter 104 examines.

The presidential succession in 2013 was governed by the constitutional limitation on consecutive terms. Article 114 permits a president to be reelected for one successive term. Ahmadinejad therefore could not stand again immediately after completing the two terms that began in 2005. The restriction did not prevent him from seeking to influence the choice of a successor. His support for Mashaei reflected the importance of the political networks built through appointments and administrative relationships over the preceding eight years. The Guardian Council's decision not to approve Mashaei's candidacy demonstrated that the incumbent's political preference did not determine the composition of the electoral field.

The exclusion of Rafsanjani was also politically consequential. He had been a founder of the Republic, speaker of parliament, president, and chair of the Expediency Council. His long service did not guarantee eligibility for the new contest. The screening process permitted eight prospective candidates, and two subsequently withdrew. Mohammad Reza Aref, the leading explicitly reform-oriented candidate, withdrew before voting, while Gholam-Ali Haddad-Adel also withdrew from the conservative side. The withdrawals had different political consequences: the first helped concentrate support behind Rouhani, whereas several prominent conservative candidates remained in the race. Six candidates ultimately participated on June 14. [52]

Rouhani's campaign could draw on experience of the nuclear negotiations under Khatami, as well as a record of service in national security institutions. He presented a political course that emphasized more effective economic management and reduction of international tension, rather than abandonment of the Islamic Republic or of its asserted peaceful nuclear rights. Rafsanjani and Khatami endorsed him, making it possible to unite reform-oriented and pragmatic voters behind a candidate who had his own establishment connections. This coalition should not be described as a simple revival of the Second of Khordad movement of 1997. Its candidate, program, and immediate political circumstances were different, although it drew on political networks and expectations developed during the reform period.

The results announced by the Ministry of Interior placed Rouhani ahead of all his rivals with just over half of reported ballots, enough to avoid a second round. Approximately 73 percent of eligible voters participated according to the national figures. The final comparison table differentiates the national summary from a separately compiled provincial series, which contains slightly different vote totals. The fact that participation increased relative to both rounds of 2005 does not establish the motives of every additional voter, but it demonstrates that the political conditions of 2009 had not permanently extinguished the capacity for electoral mobilization. The election provided an institutional route to a change of direction even under continuing candidate screening.

Rouhani assumed the presidency in August 2013. The incoming government confronted the fiscal obligations, credit commitments, exchange-rate distortions, and diplomatic dispute inherited from the outgoing administration. It did not start with a clean institutional or economic slate. The fact of electoral victory should be distinguished from subsequent policy results. Whether the new coalition could translate demands for diplomatic engagement into an agreement, and whether that agreement would produce sustained economic improvement, were questions that remained open at the transfer of power. Chapters 103 and 104 examine these developments rather than treating the subsequent nuclear settlement as inevitable in 2013. [2] [5] [42] [43]

Section 101.20

The Achievements, Contradictions, and Historical Legacy of Ahmadinejad's Presidency

Assessment depends on the criteria used. By the standard of social justice, the period brought expanded transfers, housing, credit, and provincial spending that reached many households, and the subsidy reform shifted support from consumption to income. By the standard of economic performance, the record includes inflation, a collapsing currency, weak investment, and mounting hardship. By revolutionary legitimacy, the presidency revived egalitarian rhetoric but also saw the contested 2009 election and a divided establishment. By national sovereignty, the government defended the nuclear program and widened some partnerships, at the price of sanctions and isolation. By institutional stability, it widened conflicts over authority that remained unresolved.

The contradictions did not arise only from one man. They came from interactions among presidential decisions, institutional structures that limited and redirected his power, oil dependence, international pressure, and the expectations of an Iranian society that wanted both prosperity and dignity. Ahmadinejad's rhetoric and style shaped the way these were experienced, but the Supreme Leader, parliament, the security institutions, and foreign governments all shaped outcomes. By 2013 many Iranians sought a different political and diplomatic direction, which is why the presidency's legacy is often read as a lesson in the limits of populist government.

The presidency's legacy is best evaluated by separating objectives, instruments, implementation, and outcomes. Ahmadinejad emphasized social justice and direct access to national wealth. Cash compensation, public housing, and justice shares were identifiable instruments intended to realize those objectives. They cannot be judged solely by the sincerity of the stated goals or by the aggregate amount of money distributed. The number of people reached, changes in real consumption, the quality and location of completed housing, the actual returns on allocated assets, and the sustainability of financing are different tests of the program. These criteria explain how a policy could accomplish one objective while creating difficulties in another area.

The first term also shows that aggregate growth is not sufficient to establish a durable developmental model. Favorable petroleum prices provided both greater revenue and an opportunity to establish savings, strengthen institutions, and support investment. The economy expanded in important sectors, yet rapid credit and expenditure growth also increased domestic price pressures. When petroleum prices fell in 2008 and international financial restrictions intensified later, the inherited expenditure commitments and vulnerabilities complicated adjustment. A comparison with Rafsanjani and Khatami highlights continuity: all three governments relied on oil income and large public institutions, and all faced difficult choices concerning subsidies, private investment, and the allocation of resources. The distinctive feature of the Ahmadinejad years was the emphasis on direct presidential intervention and visible distribution, not the invention of state economic activity.

The political record similarly requires more than a single verdict. The president's outsider image helped mobilize electoral support, but he governed through institutions and networks associated with the establishment. The conflicts of 2009 changed the conditions of political legitimacy; the disputes of 2011 and 2012 demonstrated that conservative unity was conditional. Neither the expansion of executive patronage nor the support of powerful security institutions freed the presidency from constitutional and political constraints. The 2013 transition then showed that electoral competition remained consequential even in a field limited by screening. These developments are mutually consistent when authority is understood as distributed across institutions rather than held completely by one office.

Internationally, the government presented scientific development and resistance to outside pressure as national achievements. The continuing nuclear negotiations demonstrate that the period was not one of uninterrupted diplomatic refusal, yet the cumulative sanctions and restrictions had severe effects. The contrast between nuclear capability and diplomatic isolation should not be interpreted as a choice that could have been resolved solely by a different presidential speech. International governments had their own objectives and legal positions; Iranian institutions disagreed about strategy; and policy choices were made amid considerable uncertainty. The appropriate historical questions concern what alternatives were available at specific moments and what evidence existed about their likely consequences, not what actors should have known with the benefit of hindsight.

The election of 2013 closed a period of government rather than settling its central disputes. Debates over social justice, institutional accountability, national sovereignty, and economic dependence remained active. The Green Movement's challenge to official electoral legitimacy, which is the subject of the following chapter, also had a historical trajectory of its own that cannot be reduced to an intermediate event in the history of the presidency. The study of Ahmadinejad's administration provides the economic and institutional context for that more focused inquiry while keeping its distinct historical questions intact. Historical Debate

Did Ahmadinejad's Presidency Advance Revolutionary Social Justice or Expose the Limits of Populist Government?

The question: Should the period be judged chiefly by its distributive aims and reach, or by its macroeconomic and political results?

Chapter Summary

Ahmadinejad's victory in 2005 resulted from the demobilization of reform voters after the institutional defeats of the Khatami years, persistent economic insecurity, a divided field, and a campaign that combined revolutionary egalitarian language with an outsider image. It was a change in governing priorities, not a break with the constitutional system. The 2005 runoff also showed the importance of electoral rules and the division of both reformist and conservative forces in the first round. Candidate approval and actual ballot participation were not identical.

His revolutionary populism joined social justice, religious conservatism, and nationalist assertions of sovereignty. It is best treated as a descriptive category, and promises must be kept apart from implemented policy and measurable outcomes. Religious arguments concerning the hidden Imam and nationalist claims about scientific development contributed to the program, but neither theme erased disagreement within the religious establishment.

The governing coalition included conservative organizations, veterans, Guards-linked networks, religious conservatives, provincial administrators, and lower-income voters. It was an alignment of interests, not a unified establishment, and its fault lines widened over time. The ability of the Majles to scrutinize budgets and ministerial appointments meant that common factional identity did not eliminate legislative authority or conflicting institutional interests.

Petroleum revenues financed expanded spending, provincial tours, justice shares, housing, and directed credit. Oil income enabled redistribution but weakened fiscal discipline and diversification, and temporary revenue was not equivalent to productive capacity. The early external surplus coexisted with rapid money growth and rising inflation. Fiscal resources and the availability of foreign currency must be distinguished from sustainable output and institutional capacity.

The Mehr Housing program produced many units but did not resolve affordability, services, or location problems. The 2010 targeted subsidy reform replaced much indirect support with direct cash transfers, whose real value was eroded by inflation and fiscal strain. The shift to broadly distributed cash payments initially had a progressive distributional effect because equal nominal payments were more significant for low-income households; price stability and financing mattered to the durability of that effect.

Economic outcomes combined strong early growth with rising inflation, high youth unemployment, and then sanctions-era hardship. Nominal gains did not always mean real gains, and domestic policy and external pressure interacted. The IMF’s contemporary reports record growth of 6.6 percent in 2007/08 and a contraction of 5.8 percent in 2012/13, estimates from distinct reporting vintages that show the contrast between phases.

Politically, cultural restrictions, the rising role of Guards-associated organizations, and the contested 2009 election weakened the president's legitimacy. In the second term, disputes over Mashaei, the intelligence ministry, parliamentary authority, and succession fragmented the conservative camp. The 2009 official electoral result and the disputes over its integrity must be analyzed separately. The later intelligence-ministry crisis demonstrated the limitations of presidential discretion even within a conservative government.

The nuclear confrontation moved from the end of voluntary suspension to IAEA findings, the Security Council demand in Resolution 1696 and sanctions beginning with Resolution 1737, and American and European financial and oil measures. Alternatives such as the 2010 Tehran Declaration did not end the confrontation. The 2005 decision to resume conversion straddled the presidential transition. The distinction between safeguards, voluntary arrangements and Security Council decisions is essential to interpreting what changed.

Regional policy expanded Iranian influence in Iraq, Lebanon, and Syria and cultivated partners such as Russia, China, and Venezuela, though the president was not the sole author of foreign policy. Controversial rhetoric must be separated from policy and from established historical fact. International relationships differed according to geography, economic interests, security ties and diplomatic cooperation. Participation in the Non-Aligned Movement did not imply support for every Iranian policy.

By 2013, inflation, a depreciated rial, and isolation led voters to elect Rouhani, a change of direction within the system. The presidency's legacy rests on how one weighs social justice, economic performance, legitimacy, sovereignty, and institutional stability. The 2013 contest allowed a new governing coalition to take office within the same constitutional framework. This outcome should not be mistaken for the resolution of the problems confronting the new administration.

Chapter Reflection

Ahmadinejad's presidency matters because it tested, under favorable fiscal conditions at first, whether revolutionary populism could deliver both social justice and sovereignty. The result was mixed and instructive: it reached many households and also revealed the limits of oil-financed redistribution without institutional discipline or diversification. The case invites students to examine the distance between program design and household outcomes. A budgetary allocation can establish that money was committed, but not that it created an effective public service or durable income gain. Such an assessment requires multiple forms of evidence.

It also shows how power in the Islamic Republic is distributed. A president elected by a mass vote could shape rhetoric and spending but not control security institutions, the judiciary, or the Supreme Leader's authority, and conflicts among those centers eventually reshaped the conservative camp. Authority was neither wholly centralized in the executive nor absent from it. Appointments, oversight, legislative review, and informal alliances each influenced the government. A study of one presidency can illuminate the operation of the constitutional system without assuming all institutions shared one political agenda.

The nuclear confrontation of these years connected domestic and international history. Positions that served domestic legitimacy narrowed diplomatic room, while sanctions altered domestic economic life. The relationship between the two ran in both directions. An international document establishes a particular decision or negotiating position. It is important to read it alongside domestic political debates and the evidence of implementation. This distinction prevents a diplomatic proposal from being mistaken for a settlement and a sanctions law from being mistaken for a measure of its actual economic effect.

Continuities extended beyond 2013: dependence on oil, debates over subsidies, the role of Guards-linked enterprises, and the question of how to combine sovereignty with integration into the global economy all carried forward into the chapters that follow. The record also requires chronological discipline: early monetary expansion cannot be explained entirely by later sanctions, and the severe contraction of 2012–2013 cannot be understood without considering external restrictions. The period offers a useful case study in interacting causes rather than a single explanatory variable.

The chapter is a bridge. It connects the failed reform project of Chapter 100 to the crisis of 2009 in Chapter 102, the negotiations of Chapter 103, the Rouhani years of Chapter 104, and the institutional history of Chapter 105. Understanding it helps students see these later developments as linked outcomes of choices and constraints, not as isolated episodes. The next chapter returns to 2009 in detail. The chronological overlap is deliberate: the presidency provides a broad institutional and economic context, while the focused study of the contested election examines distinct questions of participation, evidence, and legitimacy.
