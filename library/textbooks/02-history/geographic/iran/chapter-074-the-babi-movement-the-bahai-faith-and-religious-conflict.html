The Babi Movement, the Baha’i Faith, and Religious Conflict

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1848, a few dozen followers of a young merchant-turned-claimant from Shiraz gathered in the gardens of a hamlet on the Mazandaran–Khurasan road and argued about whether the religion they had joined was a renewal of Islam or the start of something else. The argument they began did not end at Badasht; it helped drive a decade of war, execution, exile, and schism.

The gathering at Badasht is one of the best-known episodes in the history of the Bábí movement, and one of the least securely documented. Our fullest descriptions come from narratives written decades afterward by believers, supplemented by an early Bábí chronicle written within a few years and by fragments of official and diplomatic reporting. What the sources agree on is that, in the early summer of 1848, a group of prominent Bábís, among them Mirza Husayn-Ali Nuri, the future Bahá’u’lláh, the scholar Mulla Muhammad-Ali Barfurushi, known as Quddus, and the poet and theologian Fatemeh Baraghani, known as Qurrat al-Ayn and Tahirih, stayed together for some three weeks and debated what the claims of their leader, Sayyid Ali-Muhammad Shirazi, the Báb, implied for the Islamic religious law they had grown up under. What they decided, and how they decided it, is a matter on which the accounts diverge, and this chapter returns to the problem in Section 74.6.

Badasht matters for the larger history of Qajar Iran because it shows a movement in the act of discovering what it was. The Bábís were not outsiders to Iranian religious society. Most were Twelver Shi‘ites by birth and education, trained in its seminaries, steeped in its devotional calendar, and moved by its longing for the return of the Hidden Imam. Yet within four years of the Báb’s first declaration in 1844, a growing number of them were reading his writings as a new revelation that could supersede received law, and the established clerical institutions that examined the claim, together with a Qajar state that feared disorder, were unwilling to treat it as one more theological opinion. The movement’s trajectory ran from preaching and manuscript circulation to disputation, from disputation to arrest and public punishment, and, in three regions, to armed confrontation with government troops.

The chapter that follows is a history of that transformation and of what came after it. The Báb was executed in Tabriz in 1850. In 1852 a small group of his followers attempted to kill Naser al-Din Shah, and the government responded with a wave of arrests and executions that destroyed much of the original leadership. The survivors, many of them in exile in Ottoman Iraq, reorganized under competing leaders. A majority of those who remained in the movement eventually accepted the claims of Bahá’u’lláh, who declared himself the figure promised by the Báb, and so formed the Bahá’í community; a smaller body continued to follow Mirza Yahya Nuri, called Subh-i-Azal, and became known as Azalí Bábís. Bábí, Bahá’í, and Azalí are historically connected categories, but they are not synonyms, and a central task of the chapter is to keep them distinct.

The chapter also takes up a difficulty that confronts every historian of these movements. Almost all of the surviving evidence was produced by parties with a stake in the outcome. Bábí and Bahá’í chronicles were written to preserve the memory of martyrs and to demonstrate the truth of a revelation. Qajar court histories and clerical polemics were written to justify repression and to represent the Bábís as heretics and rebels. European diplomats and travelers wrote from outside, often through intermediaries and rumor, and sometimes with their own agendas. Modern academic scholarship, which this chapter relies upon, tries to read all of these genres critically. It neither accepts devotional accounts as neutral reporting nor dismisses religious claims as mere political cover, and it does not treat state or clerical hostility as an objective description of the movement.

The history told here also sits inside a larger Qajar story. Part XII has followed the dynasty from Agha Mohammad Khan through Fath Ali Shah’s defeats by Russia, the contest between Russia and Britain over Iranian territory and routes, and the reform efforts of Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir, which Chapter 73 examined. The Bábí crisis unfolded in the same years as Amir Kabir’s ministry and the accession of Naser al-Din Shah in 1848, and it intersects with that reform story at one point of great consequence, the decision to execute the Báb. But its causes lay mainly in Iran’s own religious life. Nineteenth-century Iranian society was contested not only by European commercial and military pressure and by administrative reform but also by new claims to revelation that arose from inside its own religious culture.

Readers should note several conventions. The chapter uses “Bábí” for adherents of the movement during the Báb’s lifetime and for those who continued to identify with the Báb’s teaching after the schism, “Bahá’í” for followers of Bahá’u’lláh, and “Azalí” for followers of Subh-i-Azal. It presents religious claims as claims, saying what believers understood and taught, and it neither affirms nor denies them. And it keeps a firm line between the state, which was represented by a monarch, ministers, provincial governors, and troops, and the Shi‘ite scholarly class, which was never a single institution. Chapter 75 turns from religious conflict to royal finance, foreign concessions, and the economic restructuring that accompanied Qajar political change.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the Twelver Shi‘ite concepts of occultation, the Qa’im, and clerical authority, and how Shaykhism and Usuli–Akhbari debates shaped the intellectual setting of the Bábí movement.
  2. Trace the life of Sayyid Ali-Muhammad Shirazi and the development of his claims from the declaration of 1844 through the Persian Bayan.
  3. Describe how preaching, correspondence, study circles, and merchant networks spread Bábí communities across Iran and Iraq.
  4. Analyze the role of Tahirih and the gathering at Badasht in the emergence of Bábí religious identity, with attention to the limits of retrospective evidence.
  5. Distinguish clerical polemic, state repression, local violence, and official protection in the responses to the Bábís.
  6. Compare the conflicts at Shaykh Tabarsi, Zanjan, and Nayriz in causes, organization, and outcomes.
  7. Assess the execution of the Báb in 1850 and the assassination attempt and repression of 1852 as events with separate causes and consequences.
  8. Explain how the Bahá’í and Azalí communities emerged from the Bábí crisis, and why the division is not reducible to a single factor.
  9. Evaluate three kinds of source on this history: devotional, polemical, and academic, including their perspectives and limitations.
  10. Interpret excerpt-based and paraphrase-based evidence from the Báb’s and Bahá’u’lláh’s writings as the words of religious claimants.
  11. Describe the social composition of the communities and the everyday constraints of believers, including women and religious minorities.
  12. Connect the history of Bábí, Bahá’í, and Azalí communities to wider Qajar questions of authority, legitimacy, and social change.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a movement rooted in Shi‘ite expectations about divine guidance and the return of the Hidden Imam become the origin of a new religious tradition?
  2. Why did claims about revelation and religious authority produce such intense conflict in Qajar Iran, and not simply a theological dispute among scholars?
  3. In what ways were the Qajar state and the Shi‘ite clerical class distinct actors in the history of the persecution, and where did their interests converge?
  4. What distinguishes religious dissent from armed rebellion, and how should historians assess the conduct and intentions of the Bábís at Tabarsi, Zanjan, and Nayriz?
  5. How do historians reconstruct events such as Badasht, the Tabriz examination, and the execution of the Báb when sources are devotional, hostile, or foreign and secondhand?
  6. Why did the movement fracture after 1850, and what do Azalí and Bahá’í understandings of succession reveal about competing ideas of authority?
  7. How did exile in Ottoman territory change the character of the Bábí and Bahá’í communities?
  8. What roles did women, merchants, artisans, and rural households play in the movement, and how did persecution shape their choices?
  9. What does the history of these movements reveal about the relationship among religious innovation, institutional authority, state power, and social change in nineteenth-century Iran?

Historical Context

Chapter 58 traced the institutionalization of Twelver Shi‘ism under the Safavids, and Chapter 65 followed the growth of clerical authority, shrine economies, and endowments. Several features of that history are essential here. Twelver Shi‘ites hold that after the eleventh Imam, a twelfth Imam went into a concealment, or occultation, in the ninth and tenth centuries CE, and that he will return as the Mahdi or Qa’im to establish justice. In his absence, who may speak for him? Safavid and later Iranian practice gave increasing weight to jurists, the mujtahids, who derived rulings from scripture and tradition and whom ordinary believers followed. By the early nineteenth century the Usuli school, which defended this juristic reasoning, had come to dominate, and prominent mujtahids, many of them based in the Iraqi shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala as well as in Isfahan, Tehran, and Tabriz, held a recognized public role in legal, charitable, and dispute-settling life.

The Qajar state was an uneasy partner of this clerical class. After the defeats by Russia described in Chapters 70 and 71, and amid the strategic pressures of Chapter 72, the dynasty needed both revenue and legitimacy. It courted clerical support, particularly when it sought to present its wars as defense of the faith, yet it also sought to retain authority over appointments, courts, and land. Provincial governors, many of them princes of the royal house, ruled with limited resources and often relied on local notables and religious figures for order. This created a political setting in which a religious controversy could quickly become a matter of public order, and in which officials could choose among protection, indifference, and repression depending on their own interests.

The decades before 1844 were also a period of religious ferment. Shaykhism, a school of Shi‘ite thought associated with Shaykh Ahmad al-Ahsa’i and Sayyid Kazim Rashti, had attracted followers by offering a distinctive reading of the Imams’ role and the afterlife, and had been denounced as unsound by prominent Usuli scholars. Expectation of the Qa’im’s appearance was widely felt, in part because of the approach of the year 1260 of the Islamic calendar, corresponding to 1844 CE, a date a thousand lunar years after the occultation as many Shi‘ites reckoned it. The period was also marked by plague and cholera, famine, and the financial strain that followed war, and by growing European commercial and diplomatic presence. Historians debate how much weight to assign to these social stresses. This chapter treats them as part of the environment in which religious appeals found audiences, not as a mechanical explanation for belief.

The evidence that survives is unevenly distributed. For the period before 1850 we depend heavily on the Báb’s own writings, on Bábí and Bahá’í narratives written later, on Qajar chronicles, and on scattered diplomatic reports. For 1850–1892 the record includes Bahá’u’lláh’s extensive correspondence, Ottoman and Iranian official documents, European accounts, and the continuing polemics of Shi‘ite scholars and Azalí writers. Every genre has strengths and limits, and Section 74.4 and the Source Windows give students practice in using them.

Division I · c. 1800–1846

Shi‘ite Messianism and the Emergence of the Bábí Movement

Section 74.1

Twelver Shi‘ism, the Hidden Imam, and the Crisis of Religious Authority

Twelver Shi‘ism rests on the belief that after the Prophet Muhammad, divinely guided Imams from the Prophet’s family, beginning with Ali, were the rightful interpreters of revelation. The twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is held to have entered a state of concealment, called the occultation. Tradition distinguishes a lesser occultation, during which he communicated through agents, from a greater occultation, which began in the tenth century and continues. His return as the Mahdi, or Qa’im (“he who arises”), is expected to bring justice to a world filled with oppression. This hope was not a marginal doctrine. It shaped Shi‘ite devotion, the mourning rituals for the Imam Husayn at Karbala, and attitudes toward worldly rulers, whose authority many Shi‘ite scholars regarded as at best provisional.

The occultation left a question that every generation of scholars had to answer: who speaks with authority in the Imam’s absence? The answer that prevailed in Iran by the early nineteenth century was the Usuli position. It held that qualified jurists, the mujtahids, could use reasoned methods (ijtihad) to derive law from the Qur’an and the reports of the Imams, and that lay believers were obliged to follow a living mujtahid in matters of practice. The Akhbari school, which had been strong in the eighteenth century, rejected this reliance on juristic reasoning and insisted on the primacy of the transmitted reports themselves. By the Qajar period the Usulis had largely won, and the leading mujtahids of Najaf, Karbala, Isfahan, and elsewhere commanded substantial followings, collected religious taxes, and settled disputes. Their authority, though real, was not formally institutionalized and was open to contest.

Shaykhism emerged within this setting. Shaykh Ahmad al-Ahsa’i (1753–1826), born in eastern Arabia, taught in the Iraqi shrine cities and in Iran, and was received with respect by Fath Ali Shah’s court and many scholars. His writings offered a distinctive account of the Imams’ cosmic role, of the intermediate world between the material and the spiritual, and of bodily resurrection, which some of his critics considered inconsistent with consensus doctrine. His successor, Sayyid Kazim Rashti (1793–1843), led the school from Karbala. Both attracted students from Iran and Iraq, and both were criticized by leading Usuli scholars, some of whom declared them unbelievers. The Shaykhis thus formed a community with its own scholarly lineage, its own network of correspondents, and a history of being attacked by the Usuli mainstream.

Shaykhism is not a synonym for Bábism, and the distinction matters. Many Shaykhis rejected the Báb’s claims and continued as a separate school, which remained a recognized current in Iranian Shi‘ism long afterward. Shaykhism did, however, offer features that made a messianic claim intelligible to some of its adherents. Rashti taught that the Qa’im’s appearance was close, and he is reported to have told his students to look for him and not to remain in Karbala after his own death. Hearers took the teaching in different ways, and after Rashti’s death in late 1843 his followers divided over whether to recognize a successor from among the senior Shaykhi scholars or to seek a new figure. The Báb’s first disciples came largely from this milieu, and Shaykhi study circles became one channel through which Bábí claims were first carried.

A general point follows. The Qajar religious landscape was not a settled orthodoxy awaiting a challenge from outside. It was an arena in which the authority of jurists, the memory of the Imams, the expectation of the Qa’im, and the legitimacy of the state were already argued. The Bábí claims fell within a recognizable tradition of Shi‘ite messianic expectation and also threatened the established principle that the Imam’s representatives were jurists, which is why they could be heard sympathetically by some and condemned decisively by others.

The religious influence of a mujtahid was also practical rather than merely theoretical. A scholar's authority might be exercised through students who copied opinions, agents who collected contributions, families who endowed schools or shrines, and litigants who brought questions to him for arbitration. Such networks did not eliminate competition among scholars: two eminent jurists could offer different rulings, and urban laypeople might respond according to family affiliation, patronage, or local reputation. In this setting the Báb's appeal challenged not a centralized church but dispersed claims to interpret sacred law. The absence of a single clerical hierarchy helps explain why condemnations varied by place and why a few officials or educated believers could initially protect or investigate a claimant whom other authorities considered intolerable.

Section 74.2

Sayyid Ali-Muhammad Shirazi: The Báb’s Early Life and Declaration of 1844

Sayyid Ali-Muhammad was born in Shiraz in October 1819 into a family of merchants who claimed descent from the Prophet, a status shown by the title sayyid. His father died while he was young, and he was raised by a maternal uncle, a Shiraz merchant, who is generally reported to have given him a basic Persian and Arabic education. As a young man he worked in the family trade in Bushehr, the Persian Gulf port through which much of southern Iran’s commerce passed. Around 1841–1842 he spent time in Karbala, where he is reported to have attended Sayyid Kazim Rashti’s lectures, though not as a formal student of high standing. He married in 1842 and returned to Shiraz. Accounts of these years come largely from Bábí and Bahá’í sources and from his own later writings, and historians generally accept the outline while treating details of his supposed precocity with caution.

On the evening of 22–23 May 1844, according to Bábí tradition, he declared his mission to Mulla Husayn Bushru’i, a Shaykhi scholar from Khurasan who had traveled to Shiraz in search of the successor to Rashti. The traditional narrative of that night, reported in later Bábí histories, describes a long conversation that ended in Mulla Husayn’s conviction. Historians cannot reconstruct the conversation, but they accept that the declaration took place in Shiraz in May 1844 and that the Báb began the commentary on the Sura of Joseph, the Qayyum al-Asma’, around that time, a text we examine below.

The title Báb, meaning “gate,” had a precise range of associations. In Shi‘ite tradition the Imams were sometimes described as “gates” to divine knowledge, and during the lesser occultation, the Hidden Imam’s four agents had been known as his gates (abwab). A person claiming to be a bab thus claimed to be in communication with the Hidden Imam. This was an exceptional assertion, since Twelver doctrine held that no such direct channel existed after the lesser occultation ended. It was, however, a claim that could be conceived within the existing categories of the faith, and the early reception shows people grasping it in different ways: some as a claim to be a spokesman of the Imam, some as a claim to be the Imam himself, and some as the first stage of a larger message.

It would be a mistake to assume that the later doctrines were fully developed in 1844. The early writings contain claims that scholars read in different ways, and the Báb’s declarations expanded over the next several years. By around 1847–1848 he was writing in the voice of the Qa’im, and the Bayan went further, setting out laws and describing a new dispensation. The movement’s first years are therefore best understood as a period of unfolding claims and interpretations, in which believers, opponents, and the Báb himself were all working out what the declaration meant. Historians commonly describe the sequence as a progression from claiming to be a channel of the Imam, to claiming to be the Qa’im, to claiming to be the founder of a new revelation. The evidence for the dating of each stage is debated, and the chapter returns to the problem in the Continuity and Change feature at Section 74.8.

After his declaration, the Báb performed the pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina in 1844–1845, traveling by way of Bushehr, and is reported to have made declarations of his mission there, though the details are poorly attested. He returned to Bushehr in 1845 and then to Shiraz, where his growing following brought him to the attention of the authorities.

Historical Vocabulary
Occultation, Qa’im, and Báb

Definition: The occultation (ghayba) is the Twelver doctrine that the twelfth Imam has been hidden from public view since the ninth–tenth century CE. The Qa’im (“one who arises”) or Mahdi is the expected return of that Imam. The Báb (“gate”) was the title the movement’s founder adopted in 1844.

Historical context: Shi‘ite scholars wrote extensively about what signs would precede the Qa’im’s return and about who could speak for him in his absence. “Báb” evoked the Imams’ designation as gateways to knowledge and the title of the four agents during the lesser occultation.

Why it matters: The terms show how the Bábí claims were intelligible inside Shi‘ite categories even as they challenged them.

Potential misunderstanding: “Báb” is a title, not a surname, and the movement’s claims were not fixed at the moment of the title’s first use. Students should also avoid assuming that Shi‘ite messianic hope was necessarily revolutionary; most scholars interpreted it as a matter of devotion and waiting.

Section 74.3

The Letters of the Living and the Early Expansion of Bábí Communities

Within weeks of the declaration, the Báb gathered around him a first circle, which Bábí tradition counts as eighteen persons, who with him made nineteen, a number of symbolic significance in the movement. They were called the Letters of the Living (huruf al-hayy). Mulla Husayn was reckoned the first. Others included Mulla Muhammad-Ali Barfurushi, who received the title Quddus, “the Most Holy,” and became the Báb’s principal representative in Mazandaran; Fatemeh Baraghani, the Qazvin scholar later called Tahirih, “the Pure,” who accepted the Báb’s claims from a distance through correspondence; and Mulla Ali Bastami, among the first to propagate the faith in Iraq. Most were scholars from Shaykhi or Usuli seminary backgrounds, many in their twenties and thirties.

The Báb dispatched the Letters in different directions to teach, and their journeys created the movement’s first network. Mulla Husayn went to Isfahan, Kashan, Tehran, and Khurasan, where he attracted numerous followers, including scholars and merchants, in Mashhad and surrounding towns. Quddus traveled between Shiraz, Mazandaran, and other places; Mulla Ali Bastami carried the message to the shrine cities of Iraq, where he was arrested and tried by Ottoman and Shi‘ite authorities in 1845 and sent into exile, an early sign of cross-border legal attention. In Karbala, Tahirih lectured among Shaykhi women and men; her teaching drew opposition and, in 1847, forced her departure from Iraq. Within a few years the movement was a visible presence in Shiraz, Isfahan, Qazvin, Tehran, Kashan, Mashhad, and parts of Mazandaran, Azerbaijan, and Yazd.

The mechanisms of expansion were mostly conventional. Teaching occurred in homes, mosques, and bazaar shops, where merchants and artisans met to hear scholars. Letters and copied texts circulated along commercial and pilgrimage routes. Personal ties, including kinship, discipleship, and the relationships of students who had studied together, carried the message from one town to the next. Merchant networks were especially important: they moved goods, news, and books between cities, and the Báb’s own commercial background gave him ties to Shiraz and Bushehr merchants. Pilgrimage to Karbala and Mashhad put believers in contact with fellow travelers. These were the ordinary channels of Qajar religious and commercial life.

Who joined? Early adherents included scholars and seminary students, who were the movement’s principal teachers and, in places, its military leaders. Merchants and shopkeepers, craftsmen, and some landowners appear in the lists, as do rural villagers, especially in regions where a local leader converted. Women appear as participants and, in Tahirih’s case, as theological leaders. Historians do not have reliable membership figures, and estimates of the number of Bábís in the late 1840s, which range from tens of thousands upward in some accounts, are not supported by anything like a census. The movement should not be described as the project of a single class: it included clerics, bazaar merchants, craftsmen, and villagers, whose motives varied.

The geography of recruitment mattered. The Iraqi shrine cities linked Iranian scholars and pilgrims, the Persian Gulf connected Shiraz and Bushehr to merchant travel, and the overland routes through Tehran, Qazvin, Isfahan, and Khurasan brought teachers to urban and village audiences. A letter might be read aloud to several listeners and copied for others, so a movement could spread without producing a large printed literature. But the survival of a manuscript in a later collection does not prove that a whole province had read or accepted its contents. Historians must distinguish the circulation of people from the circulation of written claims, and both from actual conversion. Converts could remain embedded in the same kinship, commercial, and scholarly institutions through which they had learned about the Báb, producing local communities with different religious emphases and degrees of public visibility.

Section 74.4

Early Bábí Writings: Qur’anic Interpretation, Revelation, and Religious Legitimacy

The earliest and most important text of the movement was the Qayyum al-Asma’, a commentary on the Sura of Joseph (the twelfth chapter of the Qur’an), which the Báb began in 1844 and which tradition says he composed in the days after the declaration. It is a lengthy work in Arabic, organized into 111 chapters that comment, verse by verse, on the Qur’anic sura. Tradition holds that Mulla Husayn recognized the Báb partly because the text was written with extraordinary speed and in an unusual voice. Historians treat this account as devotional narrative, but they agree that the work was an early and central statement of the Báb’s claims and that it circulated among his first followers.

Qur’anic commentary (tafsir) was an established scholarly genre, and in writing a commentary the Báb placed himself within a recognized tradition. What distinguished the text was its voice. It did not simply explain the verses; it spoke at points in a voice that its readers took as divine speech addressed to or issued through the Báb, who is identified in the text as the “Remembrance” (dhikr). Shi‘ite and Shaykhi esoteric traditions had long read the story of Joseph, the beloved son sold by his brothers and later raised to power, as an allegory of the sufferings and eventual vindication of the family of the Prophet, and the Báb’s text drew on that tradition, identifying the Imam Husayn with Joseph and associating himself with the same line. Passages in the text, according to published selections and scholarly studies, make claims for obedience to the Báb, call on the community and rulers to heed him, and anticipate a time of trial.

Here was a text that functioned at once as scriptural commentary and as a claim to a new source of authority. A reader could take it as a pious Shi‘ite exegesis by a gifted layman. A reader could take it as the utterance of the Imam’s representative. A reader could take it as new revelation. The text itself does not settle which reading its author intended in 1844, and modern scholars have debated how explicit the early claims were. What is clear is that readers responded variously: believers, including a number of scholars, found its language and ideas compelling, while Usuli scholars and many Shaykhis regarded it as presumptuous or dangerous, since a layman who produced new Qur’anic discourse in a divine voice was claiming a role that only the Prophet and Imams were believed to have held.

Historians must distinguish three questions here. The first is what the text says, which can be studied through the Arabic manuscripts and published selections. The second is what the author intended, which can be inferred but is not directly recoverable. The third is how the text circulated and was understood in 1844–1846, which depends on scattered evidence in letters, polemics, and later narratives. The surviving manuscripts, produced mostly by copyists within the movement, show that the text was read and valued; they do not give us the full range of early readers’ responses. Such evidence must be combined with accounts by opponents, who recorded their objections, and by believers, who recorded their conversions.

The physical forms of the early writings illuminate their authority. Verses and commentaries circulated as handwritten copies, sometimes made rapidly by enthusiasts, and their Arabic idiom invoked the language of Qur'anic revelation even when a particular passage was not a conventional commentary. Such texts were difficult for an audience without specialist training to assess independently. Teachers explained them in Persian, and a listener's understanding often depended on the interpretation of a locally respected believer. This distance between author and audience is important: the Báb's expanding claims did not automatically reach every follower at the same moment. What appeared in a manuscript produced under confinement could be understood differently in Shiraz, Karbala, or Khurasan, and the resulting ambiguity sharpened the later disputes over what Bábí fidelity required.

Section 74.5

From Shiraz to Isfahan, Maku, and Chihriq: Arrest, Protection, and Imprisonment

After his return from the pilgrimage in 1845, the Báb faced growing hostility in Shiraz. Local clerics objected to his teaching, and the governor of Fars, Husayn Khan, took action. According to the sources, the Báb was arrested, brought to a public hearing, and beaten with the bastinado. A report that he publicly disavowed certain claims in a Shiraz mosque exists in the sources, and scholars disagree about the wording, the circumstances, and what it implied; some accounts treat it as a tactical clarification and others as a more direct retreat. Whatever occurred, the Báb remained under restriction in Shiraz for some time, during which an epidemic struck the city in 1846.

In September–October 1846 he left for Isfahan, where he was received by Manuchehr Khan, the governor, known by the title Mo’tamed al-Dawla. Manuchehr Khan, a Georgian-born convert to Islam and long-serving official, took a favorable interest in the Báb, and arranged for him to stay in his own residence. The governor organized a gathering of scholars to hear the Báb, and the Báb is reported to have composed a treatise on the Prophet’s mission at the governor’s request. Isfahan’s leading clerics, including the Imam-Jum‘a, objected, however, and issued condemnations. Manuchehr Khan’s protection, though sincere in the view of most accounts, was an individual act of an official and not a policy of the state, and it ended with his death in February 1847.

The central government then took the decision. The court of Muhammad Shah, in which the grand vizier Haji Mirza Aqasi was dominant, ordered that the Báb be moved, and after a time in a village near Tehran he was sent in 1847 to the fortress of Maku in the far northwest of Azerbaijan, near the Russian and Ottoman borders. The location kept him far from the capital and from the Bábí communities of the center and south. The warden and local population, according to accounts, treated him with unexpected respect, and visitors came in large numbers, prompting officials to move him in 1848 to the more remote fortress of Chihriq, where he remained until 1850 under stricter watch.

The government’s motives mixed two concerns that the evidence does not neatly separate. The court and governors cared about public order and the unrest that a popular religious claimant might cause. The clerics cared about the doctrinal claims. The two overlapped: clerical denunciations gave the state a religious justification for restriction, and the state’s ability to detain the Báb gave the clerics a remedy they could not provide for themselves. Yet they were not identical. Manuchehr Khan’s protection shows that officials could weigh the matter differently, and some clerics objected to the use of force.

Imprisonment limited the Báb’s ability to lead directly but did not silence him. In Maku and Chihriq he wrote prolifically, including the major texts of his later period, dictated to secretaries and sent to followers through trusted visitors and couriers. His isolation also changed his role: with the leader out of reach, initiative passed in practice to disciples in the field, men and women who interpreted his writings and acted on their own understanding. That shift is essential to understanding the events of 1848.

Confinement also shifted the balance between personal leadership and delegated authority. A political prisoner could produce texts, send selective messages, and receive visitors, but correspondence depended on intermediaries who faced surveillance, delays, and danger. Provincial teachers consequently acquired influence by interpreting writings and making decisions that the Báb could not settle immediately. The contrast between Isfahan, where the governor's patronage made temporary protection possible, and the northern fortresses, where contact was constrained, illustrates the dependence of religious dissent on local political circumstances. It also cautions against describing every stage as an uninterrupted and centrally directed campaign: at times believers operated on partial information and interpreted news of imprisonment as evidence of either divine testing or growing official hostility.

Section 74.6

Tahirih, Badasht, and the Break with Established Religious Law, 1848

Fatemeh Baraghani, later known as Qurrat al-Ayn (“Solace of the Eyes”) and Tahirih, was born in Qazvin around 1814–1817 into a prominent clerical family. Her father and uncles were well-known scholars, and she received an unusual education for a woman of her time, studying Islamic jurisprudence, theology, and Arabic and Persian poetry. She married a cousin, the son of her uncle, and had children, but the marriage ended in separation. Drawn to Shaykhi teaching, she corresponded with Sayyid Kazim Rashti and, after the Báb’s declaration, accepted his claims by letter and joined the Letters of the Living, though she never met him. In Karbala she taught publicly, and in Qazvin she was drawn into a bitter family dispute: her uncle Mulla Muhammad Taqi, a leading Usuli opponent of the Shaykhis, was murdered in 1847, and a number of Bábís were arrested in connection with the killing. Tahirih was held under house confinement and later escaped with help from Bahá’u’lláh, according to Bahá’í sources.

In the summer of 1848, Bábí leaders gathered at Badasht, a place on the border of Mazandaran and Khurasan, in gardens rented by Bahá’u’lláh. The accounts agree that about eighty persons, perhaps more, were present, that the gathering lasted a few weeks, and that Tahirih and Quddus were central figures. They also agree that the participants took up the question of the movement’s relation to Islamic law. Beyond that, the stories vary. A common narrative holds that Tahirih appeared before the assembly unveiled, a gesture that scandalized many present and that she explained as a sign that the old law had ended. Others report that the Bábí leaders adopted new names, and that Quddus, who was cautious about a sudden break with the sharia, disagreed with Tahirih, while Bahá’u’lláh mediated.

Historians face difficulties. The most detailed accounts of Badasht were written decades later, some by believers who had not been present. An early Bábí chronicle, Nuqtat al-Kaf, completed in or around 1852 and traditionally attributed to Haji Mirza Jani of Kashan, gives a different picture. The attribution of the entire surviving text to him is disputed, however: manuscript comparison indicates a complicated textual history, and scholars have identified the possibility of multiple contributors or redactions. Details such as who spoke, in what order, and what Tahirih said differ. Even the unveiling, widely reported, is described in different ways, and some scholars suggest that its significance was theological, meaning that it was a symbolic declaration that the era of the old law had ended, not a statement about gender norms in themselves.

This last point deserves emphasis. Tahirih has deservedly become a symbol in modern discussions of women’s history in Iran, but the historian must first ask what her actions meant to her contemporaries. For the Bábís at Badasht, the central question was religious. Did the Báb’s claims mean that the Islamic law, including its norms of dress and conduct, remained binding, or had a new dispensation begun? Tahirih’s answer, that it had, was radical not because it concerned women but because it asserted that the sharia’s authority had come to an end. That is how her opponents understood it, and that is why her action was perceived as a challenge to the clerical order. Her significance as a theologian and leader, the author of poems and treatises, should not be reduced to a single gesture.

The assembly also had practical consequences. After Badasht, the participants dispersed, and several were attacked by local villagers, with some loss of property and injuries. The incident exposed a growing gulf between Bábís who saw themselves as bearers of a new religion and a society whose normative order remained that of Shi‘ite law. The result, in the following months, was a series of confrontations in which that gulf took violent form.

The problem of evidence is inseparable from Tahirih's standing. A later narrator who describes the unveiling as the decisive moment may preserve an authentic recollection, but also may select a scene that turns a complicated discussion of law into a memorable founding drama. A clerical critic could describe the same episode as scandal without recording the theological arguments that participants believed they were making. Tahirih's own writings and the earlier disputes surrounding her teaching help restore a longer chronology: her challenge to inherited interpretation had developed before Badasht, especially in her preaching in Iraq. Her intellectual leadership and the reactions to it are therefore better understood as a sustained contest over who could explain revelation than as a single theatrical protest.

Section 74.7

The Persian Bayan: New Revelation, Religious Law, and the Future Manifestation

The Persian Bayan was written during the Báb’s confinement, mainly in Maku in 1847–1848, and is generally regarded as the principal statement of his mature teaching. It was left unfinished: it is organized in “unities” (vahids) of nineteen chapters each, and the work as it survives breaks off before reaching its planned length. The Arabic Bayan, shorter and structured similarly, was also composed in this period. Together they present a systematic statement of what the Báb described as a new religious dispensation.

By the time of the Bayan, the Báb’s claims were explicit. He presented himself as the Qa’im whose appearance had been anticipated, and he understood the resurrection and the day of judgment, which Islamic tradition projected into the future, as events occurring in his own time through the appearance of a new revelation. The Bayan repeatedly describes itself as the divine book of a new dispensation and claims for the Báb a status above that of the community’s existing legal authorities. It sets out laws, among them regulations for worship and personal conduct, rules for the community’s relationship to books and objects it regarded as pertaining to the old dispensation, and a distinctive calendar of nineteen months of nineteen days. Scholars note that some of its harsher provisions, such as those directed at non-believers or at property, were framed as ideal norms conditional on a Bábí political order that did not come into being and were not carried out in practice. The distinction between a text’s prescriptions and the community’s actual practice is essential.

One feature of the Bayan has a particular importance for later history. The Báb repeatedly refers to a figure he calls “He Whom God Shall Make Manifest” (man yuzhiruhu’llah), a future bearer of revelation to whom the Bayan itself is in some sense subordinate, as the Báb’s own dispensation was subordinate to what would follow. The text warns believers not to be veiled by the Bayan from the one whose appearance it foretells. The identity and the timing of this figure were left open. Because of this, believers after 1850 had a scriptural framework within which a new claimant could be recognized, and that same openness made it possible for rival claims to arise. Bahá’ís understand Bahá’u’lláh to be the promised figure; Azalís hold that the promised manifestation had not yet appeared and that the Bayan remained the binding book; and academic historians treat the passages as the Báb’s own statements, whose referent is a matter of faith and not historical demonstration.

Students must therefore distinguish the Báb’s texts from later interpretations. Bahá’í, Azalí, and academic readers differ not only in conclusion but in method. The Bayan’s legal provisions have been interpreted, restated, and in places superseded by Bahá’u’lláh’s later laws; Azalí writers sought to preserve the Bayan’s law; and academic scholars have attempted to understand it as a text of its own time. The Library of Congress holds a manuscript fragment of the Persian Bayan, a reminder that the text came down to us in copied manuscripts whose transmission is itself a subject of study.

The Bayan also shows the distance between a law's announcement and its daily implementation. Its provisions encompass ritual, interpersonal conduct, material objects, and the ordering of a community whose institutions were still fragile. Some prescriptions were exacting or exclusionary, but imprisonment and persecution restricted the opportunity to establish a comprehensive legal order. It is therefore unsafe to infer from the law-book that believers universally practiced each injunction. The work's vision of future revelation created a further tension: the Báb both legislated for a new dispensation and taught that the manifestation to come would possess authority beyond the Bayan. Later Bahá’í and Azalí readings made different use of those strands, and the historian must first distinguish what the text says from what subsequent communities claimed it meant.

Section 74.8

Shi‘ite Clerics, Qajar Officials, and the Political Meaning of Religious Dissent

Responses to the Báb’s claims ranged across the spectrum of Qajar religious and political actors. Many leading mujtahids wrote against him, and by the late 1840s a number had issued rulings that treated his followers as unbelievers, which in Shi‘ite jurisprudence could carry the death penalty for apostasy, though practice varied. The Shaykhi leaders, concerned to distance themselves, were among his sharper critics. Provincial governors acted according to local calculation, as the case of Manuchehr Khan shows. Local populations varied too: in some towns crowds attacked Bábí preachers, while in others sympathetic notables sheltered them.

The state’s approach evolved. During Muhammad Shah’s last years, the court under Haji Mirza Aqasi favored confinement. When the king died in September 1848 and the young Naser al-Din succeeded, the real power rested with the chief minister Amir Kabir, whose priority was to restore order and finances. As Chapter 73 showed, he was a reformer who valued central authority, and he saw a movement defying clerical and state control as a threat. In this setting, in July 1848, the Báb was brought from Chihriq to Tabriz for an examination before the heir apparent, Naser al-Din Mirza, and Tabriz’s leading scholars. The accounts of the proceedings differ widely. They agree that the Báb was questioned about his claims, that he affirmed himself to be the Qa’im, and that the scholars condemned him, with some recommending punishment. Details such as who asked which questions, the Báb’s precise answers, and the penalty inflicted, perhaps a beating, are reported differently by Bábí, hostile, and European sources.

The key point is that religious dissent was, in this setting, rarely only religious. The Qajar monarchy’s claim to legitimacy was bound up with the religious order; the ulama’s position depended on a system of authority the Báb’s claims undermined; and the provincial order depended on a fragile compromise among governors, notables, and clerics. A person who claimed to speak for the Hidden Imam, and whose followers claimed that the old law had ended, challenged all three at once. Yet nothing in this made violence inevitable. The earlier years had shown alternatives: protection by a governor, confinement without execution, and efforts by some clerics to dispute rather than punish. The confrontations of 1848–1853 were the outcome of contingent decisions by the Bábís, the state, and local actors at a moment of political transition.

The Tabriz examination illustrates the intersection of these powers with particular clarity. The heir apparent's presence gave the inquiry political weight, but the questioning drew upon the vocabulary of Shi‘ite theology and jurisprudence. A ruler could order confinement or execution, yet a religious opinion might provide justification and public legitimacy, especially before audiences disposed to see the Báb's claims as heresy. Conversely, a jurist's condemnation did not by itself command an army. The record cannot be reduced to a single chain of command from clerics to state or from state to clerics. It is more accurate to trace the decisions of identifiable people and institutions, asking at each stage whether they were protecting religious orthodoxy, defending property and status, averting disorder, or pursuing several objectives simultaneously.

The accession crisis of 1848 added uncertainty. News of Muhammad Shah's death reached provinces unevenly, and succession politics placed greater pressure on governors to demonstrate obedience to the new court. The Bábí gatherings and armed encounters unfolded in a climate in which officials could interpret a mobile group of believers as a threat to a still-consolidating monarchy. This does not establish that the Bábís intended to overthrow the Qajar dynasty. It explains why the same movement could be assessed differently before and after the change of ruler, and why state responses intensified even while the theological issues continued to be debated.

  • From commentary within Islamic genres to law-giving in the Bayan

When and how: Change was uneven. Historians disagree on how early the more expansive claims appear and how deliberately they were staged. Those closest to the Báb in 1848 experienced the shift as rapid; many ordinary believers experienced it as continuity with their existing devotion until they were forced to choose. Why it matters: The pattern explains why different contemporaries could see the movement as renewal, heresy, or a new religion.

Division III · 1848–1853

Armed Confrontation, Execution, and State Repression

Section 74.9

Shaykh Tabarsi: Armed Conflict and Messianic Expectations, 1848–1849

The first of the armed confrontations began in the autumn of 1848. After Mulla Husayn Bushru’i had spent time in Mashhad, where he gathered a large following, he set out toward Mazandaran with a company of followers, reportedly flying a black banner. The black banner carried eschatological associations in Islamic tradition, and the symbolism of the march, with its references to Karbala, martyrdom, and the Qa’im, was understood by his supporters and opponents alike as messianic. Accounts differ about his intentions. Bábí narratives say that he was heading to join Quddus in Mazandaran and, according to some versions, intended to proceed on to the Báb. Hostile accounts say that he intended revolt. His followers carried arms, as many travelers did, and in the villages along the route there were hostile encounters, shaped by the opposition of local clerics and notables to the Bábís.

At Barfurush, Quddus’s home town in Mazandaran, local opponents, led by a prominent cleric called Sa’id al-Ulama, incited a crowd against the Bábís. Fighting broke out. The Bábís withdrew to the shrine of Shaykh Tabarsi, a pilgrimage site in the forest nearby, where they were joined by Quddus and by others, perhaps three to six hundred people, including scholars, merchants, craftsmen, and villagers. There they built improvised earthworks and fortifications. The government dispatched forces, first local levies and then regular troops under princes of the royal family, to dislodge them. The besieged Bábís repelled several attacks. Mulla Husayn was killed in one of the sorties, in early 1849.

The siege lasted through the winter and into the spring. By its end the defenders were starving, reduced to eating their animals and leather. In May 1849, the commander of the royal forces, Prince Mahdi-Quli Mirza, offered terms, and the Bábís, according to both Bábí and external accounts, agreed to surrender after an oath on the Qur’an that they would be spared. Instead, once they had left the fort, many were killed or enslaved by soldiers and local people. Quddus was taken to Barfurush, where he was killed by a mob. Government accounts, Bábí narratives, and European reports differ about numbers of killed, the sequence of events, and the degree of official culpability for the breach of the truce; historians agree that the killing of those who had surrendered occurred and that it was a significant event in the memory of the movement.

What were the Bábís doing at Shaykh Tabarsi? Historians distinguish the intentions of participants from the military circumstances that developed. For many participants, the march and the defense had religious significance within a Shi‘ite narrative of the faithful besieged by the oppressors, the Karbala paradigm. Some seem to have expected the Qa’im’s appearance in a way that would transform politics. Others fought because they were attacked and could not retreat. The leaders’ decisions, the timing of the Báb’s own writings, and the extent of communication between the Báb in Chihriq and Mulla Husayn in Mazandaran are matters on which the evidence is thin and interpretations differ. The Báb is reported, in Bábí accounts, to have supported the Bábís at Tabarsi; whether he had planned or sanctioned the specific march is debated.

The consequences were serious. The defeat removed Mulla Husayn and Quddus, two of the most capable early leaders, and tens or hundreds of other believers. It also hardened state and clerical views. Official reports and later chronicles represented the incident as proof that the Bábís were rebels, a representation that Bábí accounts rejected as a distortion of a defensive stand. The event was also a precedent: it showed that a religious movement could withstand regular troops for months, and that government forces could be defeated or deceived by its leaders, lessons that officials and Bábís drew in different ways.

The material circumstances of the siege deserve as much attention as its symbols. The defenders were not an army supplied by an established state. Their capacity depended on weapons carried into the region, provisions gathered or purchased earlier, the labor required to strengthen the shrine, and the willingness of nearby people to assist or oppose them. Government forces likewise faced difficulties operating through the forested coastal lowlands, where seasonal weather and the cooperation of local leaders affected campaigning. These conditions help explain why a comparatively small fortified position could withstand repeated attacks. They also help explain the deterioration of the defenders' situation once food supplies failed. The narrative of heroic endurance preserved by later believers must be read alongside a history of hunger, exhaustion, disease risk, and the choices available to civilians around the shrine.

Tabarsi also reveals the limitations of counting combatants and victims from partisan sources. Figures supplied by one account may include followers who arrived only briefly, while another may count those who remained at the fort through its final surrender. The fate of survivors can be obscured by reports that combine deaths in fighting with executions and later attacks. A cautious reconstruction does not make the violence less consequential; it separates the sufficiently documented defeat and killing after promises of safety from the precision of particular casualty totals. The subsequent use of the episode in Bábí and Bahá’í remembrance endowed a military catastrophe with the significance of a test of faith. Historians must explain that memory while retaining the perspective of regional opponents who feared an armed messianic assembly.

Section 74.10

Zanjan and Nayriz: Regional Revolts, Community Conflict, and Government Suppression

In 1850, two further conflicts occurred, in places far apart. The first, at Nayriz in Fars, began in the spring. Sayyid Yahya Darabi, called Vahid, a respected scholar from a family of religious notables who had been sent by the Muhammad Shah’s court to investigate the Báb, and who instead became a believer, traveled through Yazd, Nayriz, and neighboring towns teaching the new faith. At Nayriz, tension grew between his supporters and the local governor, Zayn al-Abidin Khan, and in the aftermath of a clash the Bábís took refuge in a fort. They were besieged by government and local forces. Vahid was killed in June 1850, and after the fort fell, the Bábís who remained were attacked. Many of the defenders were killed, and women, children, and prisoners were taken, mistreated, and paraded in humiliating fashion. A second outbreak at Nayriz in 1853, after the Báb’s execution and the 1852 repression, ended in another defeat and further killings.

The conflict at Zanjan, in the northwest, was larger and lasted longer, from May 1850 to January 1851. Its leader was Mulla Muhammad-Ali Zanjani, called Hujjat, a scholar who had a long record of quarrels with the local ulama and the governor and who attracted a large following in a town with deep communal divisions. Local disputes between Hujjat’s supporters, many of them artisans and townspeople, and the governor and the ulama escalated into fighting. The Bábís barricaded themselves in quarters of the city, and despite repeated assaults held out for roughly seven months, even as the government sent forces and artillery. Hujjat died of wounds in late 1850; the remaining defenders fell in January 1851. Women took part in the defense, and later accounts honor their roles. After the surrender, many survivors were killed, and women and children were detained and, according to accounts, held in miserable conditions.

The three confrontations, Tabarsi, Nayriz, and Zanjan, had different origins and character. Tabarsi began as a march by a charismatic group with messianic symbolism, ended as a siege in a forest, and involved participants from many places. Nayriz grew out of a regional teacher’s preaching in an area with local rivalries over governance, and its violence was tied to the governor’s hostility and a local struggle for control. Zanjan was a town conflict, in which an existing quarrel between a prominent cleric and the ulama and governor was transformed by Bábí identification, and where the Bábís controlled substantial districts for months. The differences matter because collective labels such as “Bábí rebellions” obscure them. In each case, the questions of who initiated violence, whether participants expected an apocalyptic transformation, and whether they were responding to attack are answered differently by different sources.

Civilian consequences were severe in all three places. Fighting displaced households; defeated Bábís, women, and children were killed, imprisoned, or enslaved by victors; property was seized. Government officials and soldiers participated, as did local civilians. Such outcomes were not unique to the Bábís; Qajar suppression of local revolts often involved plunder and reprisals. But the religious element, in which Bábís were treated as unbelievers outside protection, intensified them.

Zanjan's urban setting gave the conflict an especially destructive character. The city was not simply a fortress held by outsiders: religious allegiance, neighborhood ties, and personal loyalty divided people who had lived together before fighting began. Barricades, control of streets, access to wells and food, and the movement of civilians through contested quarters became central to the struggle. Because homes and shops stood inside the fighting area, the distinction between military and civilian loss was often impossible for residents themselves. The Bábí leadership could draw on existing urban solidarities, while the governor and hostile clerics mobilized their own. To describe the fighting only as a confrontation between a persecuted minority and an external army would miss the transformation of a local political quarrel into prolonged communal war.

Nayriz, by contrast, involved the movement of a preacher with a reputation gained outside the town and the response of local authorities to a growing circle of adherents. The first episode in 1850 and the renewed violence in 1853 should be separated, since the second occurred after the execution of the Báb and the great repression in Tehran. Later narratives sometimes fold the two together to emphasize a continuous pattern of martyrdom. In both episodes, the fate of noncombatants deserves specific scrutiny: kinship with an armed supporter could expose a woman or child to punishment, displacement, or loss of property regardless of individual conduct. The uncertainty of casualty totals requires caution, but does not erase the documented pattern of reprisals that followed the defeat of the Bábí defenders.

Section 74.11

The Execution of the Báb at Tabriz, July 1850

The decision to execute the Báb was taken against the background of these events. By the middle of 1850 the Tabarsi siege was over, Nayriz and Zanjan were in revolt, and the movement was identified in the minds of the government with armed uprising. The chief minister, Amir Kabir, determined that the Báb should be put to death, and he ordered him brought from Chihriq to Tabriz. Amir Kabir’s motives, according to historians, were primarily political: he wanted to end what he saw as a source of disorder by removing its leader. Religious authorities in Tabriz, including prominent clerics, issued opinions condemning the Báb, in some accounts after a second hearing; the sources differ on the details. Chapter 73 treated Amir Kabir’s reform program, and his role in the Báb’s death is one of its darker episodes, one that his admirers and critics assess differently.

The Báb was shot by a firing squad in Tabriz on 9 July 1850 (some sources give 8 July), together with a young follower, Mirza Muhammad-Ali Zunuzi, in the barracks square. The regiment was commanded by an Armenian officer, Sam Khan, in the Azerbaijani service. Bábí and Bahá’í accounts report that, after the first volley, the Báb could not be found and that his companion was found unhurt, and that a second regiment then carried out the execution. Hostile and external accounts differ, with some saying that the ropes were severed by the bullets and that this was an accident. Historians treat the events that can be documented, the decision to execute, the place, the approximate date, the firing squad, as well established, and the extraordinary details as devotional narrative or contested report. The bodies were exposed and, according to Bábí accounts, recovered by believers and concealed. Accounts of their successive hiding places must be treated as traditions rather than an uninterrupted documentary record. The remains identified as the Báb’s were transferred from Iran to Ottoman Palestine in 1899; they were interred in the shrine on Mount Carmel in 1909, almost sixty years after the execution.

The immediate effects were severe. The Báb’s death ended his direct leadership and deprived the community of its central source of authority. It also raised acute questions about continuity, since the Báb had named successors or deputies in various ways, and the loss of leaders at Tabarsi and the imminent defeat at Zanjan reduced the pool of capable heirs. Morale among followers varied: some were devastated, others read the execution within the Karbala paradigm of martyrdom, and many in Iran went underground. Yet the movement did not collapse. Communities survived in many places, their identity partly strengthened by persecution.

Evidence about the execution must be weighed by kind and date. The occurrence of the shooting, the role of the chief minister, and the location in Tabriz can be established without accepting every detail of the later narratives. Accounts of the first volley and the intervention of a second firing party vary, and subsequent religious retellings attached extraordinary significance to the disruption of the initial attempt. A historian need not decide whether a miracle occurred in order to explain how that memory functioned: it strengthened a community's understanding of martyrdom and helped distinguish the founder's death from an ordinary state execution. Nor should apparently precise reproductions of conversations be treated as transcripts when the sources do not support verbatim reconstruction.

The decision also had effects beyond the immediate community. The authorities could present it as a warning against further challenges to the religious and political order, but the execution removed the person who might have clarified increasingly divergent interpretations among his followers. Surviving believers had to decide how to relate to deputies, writings, and local leaders when a common source of direct instruction was gone. This was not merely a succession problem of names: it was a question of whether revelation, law, or practical leadership would supply the movement's future unity. The memory of the bodies' treatment and eventual reinterment became an enduring devotional theme, although the later shrine history must remain chronologically distinct from the event in 1850.

Historical Debate

Defensive Resistance, Political Rebellion, or a Changing Mixture?

The question: How should historians characterize the Bábí confrontations of 1848–1853? Were they defensive actions by persecuted believers, revolts against the Qajar order, or something that changed with place and time?

Interpretation A — Defensive resistance: Bábí and Bahá’í narratives, and some scholars, emphasize that the believers at Tabarsi, Nayriz, and Zanjan were attacked and fought to defend their lives, families, and faith. They point to local opposition, clerical incitement, and the breach of the Tabarsi truce.
Interpretation B — Political or messianic rebellion: Qajar chronicles and hostile writers described the Bábís as rebels seeking to overthrow the state. Some modern scholars argue that for at least part of the movement, millenarian expectation of a new order shaped decisions to take up arms, and that the black banner and the march show more than a defensive posture.
Interpretation C — A combination that varied by place: Many historians argue that the evidence supports different explanations in different cases: messianic expectation was stronger at Tabarsi; local power struggles were central at Zanjan and Nayriz; and in each, intentions changed as sieges developed.

Evidence: The principal evidence consists of Bábí and Bahá’í narratives, Qajar official and chronicle accounts, the writings of the Báb, and diplomatic reports. Each has an interest in presenting the events in a particular light.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ over interpretation, over the relation between the Báb’s writings and the actions of his followers, and over how much weight to give later narratives.

Why it matters: The question bears on how the state’s repression is understood, whether the Báb bore responsibility, and how later Bahá’í teaching on nonviolence relates to this history.

Section 74.12

The Attempt on Naser al-Din Shah and the Repression of 1852

After the executions of 1850 and the defeat at Zanjan, the movement’s remaining leaders were scattered, and some Bábís, in grief and anger, believed that vengeance was called for. On 15 August 1852, as the Shah rode out near his summer palace at Niyavaran, outside Tehran, a small group of men, usually numbered at three, attacked him with pistols loaded with small shot. The Shah was wounded but not seriously injured, and the attackers were quickly seized; one was killed at once. Investigation revealed a plot involving a handful of Bábís in Tehran, some of whom had been inspired by anger at the Báb’s death. Both Bábí and Bahá’í tradition and modern scholars describe the act as an action of a few individuals, who had acted without the sanction of the movement’s leaders, though the degree of connection among the participants and others is debated.

The government’s response was swift and broad. Bábís and suspected Bábís were arrested in Tehran and elsewhere, and a number of prominent prisoners were put to death in the capital, in public and with great cruelty. Contemporary reports, including those of European diplomats, describe executions in which officials, courtiers, and members of various guilds were assigned individual victims, a practice that the sources describe as a way of sharing responsibility. Property was confiscated, and the families of victims suffered. Tahirih, who had been held in Tehran, was put to death in 1852, reportedly strangled and her body thrown into a well; the date is variously given. Haji Mirza Jani of Kashan, the merchant traditionally associated with the early chronicle Nuqtat al-Kaf, was among the victims; the attribution of the complete surviving chronicle to his sole authorship remains disputed. Numbers of those killed in the aftermath differ greatly, from dozens in the capital to hundreds nationwide, and the reliability of the figures is poor. The grand vizier at the time, Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri, who had succeeded Amir Kabir after the latter’s dismissal in 1851 and killing in January 1852, directed much of the response.

Among those arrested was Mirza Husayn-Ali Nuri, later Bahá’u’lláh, who had been in the vicinity of the Shah’s camp when the attack occurred. Though he had no role in the attempt, he was arrested and imprisoned in the Siyah-Chal, the “Black Pit,” a subterranean dungeon in Tehran, for roughly four months, in conditions of great hardship. The government’s investigation found no evidence of his participation, and in early 1853, with the Russian minister reportedly intervening on behalf of his family’s standing, he was released and exiled. Bahá’í tradition says that in the dungeon he experienced the first intimations of his later mission; this belief is the foundation of Bahá’í understanding and cannot be verified historically.

A crucial control: the attempt was the act of a small group and cannot be ascribed automatically to all Bábís. The state’s response, which punished people not shown to have taken part, was collective punishment. The repression also involved clerics, who issued rulings and urged action, and local populations, some of whom looted or informed. Yet officials, again, were not uniform: reports exist of those who tried to limit the violence or to protect individuals. The distinction between documented acts and exaggerated or partisan accounts is especially important here, because both Bábí narratives and hostile sources have reasons to heighten the events.

Responsibility for the attempt and responsibility for its aftermath must therefore be analyzed separately. A few conspirators possessed weapons and planned an assault, but the subsequent arrests reached people for whom no participation in the attack has been demonstrated. The Qajar state's investigative methods, limited procedural safeguards, and use of exemplary punishment enabled suspicion of affiliation to substitute for evidence of individual guilt. Public participation by courtiers, guild representatives, or officials in the executions was a political performance as well as an act of killing: it displayed allegiance to the Shah and implicated prominent social groups in punishment. Accounts disagree on the precise distribution of victims, yet they consistently show the monarchy seeking to demonstrate that an assault on its person would be answered collectively and publicly.

For surviving families, the consequence was not confined to the named martyrs. Arrest could disrupt a household's source of income; confiscation might remove its capital, documents, or tools; flight could separate children from relatives and religious teachers. Survivors had reason to conceal their affiliation, but concealment made later histories less complete because ordinary believers left fewer identifiable records. This asymmetry helps explain why the repression is often told through the final words and deaths of prominent figures. Their experiences are historically significant, but they should not crowd out the less visible social transformations that the wave of 1852 inflicted on neighborhoods and provincial networks.

Cause and Effect

The Assassination Attempt of 1852 and the Repression

Long-term causes

Clerical condemnation of Bábí claims; state concern about public order; the experience of persecution and defeat from 1848 to 1851.

Short-term causes

Execution of the Báb; deaths at Zanjan and Nayriz; the leadership vacuum; grief and anger among some followers.

Immediate trigger

A small group’s decision to attack the Shah on 15 August 1852 near Niyavaran.

Immediate effects

Mass arrests, public executions, confiscation of property, and the death of Tahirih and other prominent Bábís.

Long-term consequences

Destruction of much of the original Bábí leadership in Iran; exile of survivors to Iraq; deeper suspicion of Bábí-derived communities by officials and clerics; reinforcement of Bábí and later Bahá’í memory of martyrdom.

Historical complexity

The repression drew on existing hostility; the attempt gave it a pretext and scale. Individual agency, state policy, clerical encouragement, and local opportunism combined.

Note: The attempt did not cause persecution from nothing. It transformed the scale and character of an existing policy.

Section 74.13

Survival after Persecution: Bábí Exiles, Subh-i-Azal, and the Baghdad Community

The repression of 1852 left the Bábí community in Iran without most of its original leaders, but it did not eliminate it. Believers survived in towns and villages, often by concealing their identity, a practice that echoed the Shi‘ite tradition of prudential dissimulation (taqiyya). Many Bábís who could do so left for Ottoman Iraq, where the Shi‘ite shrine cities and Baghdad offered both refuge and proximity to Iran. Baghdad, under Ottoman rule, had a large Iranian population and was a point of exchange for Iranian merchants and pilgrims, and so it became the movement’s new center.

In the vacuum, attention turned to Mirza Yahya Nuri (1831–1912), a young half-brother of Mirza Husayn-Ali. Yahya, later called Subh-i-Azal (“Dawn of Eternity”), had been designated by the Báb to a prominent position, according to Azalí and some Bábí accounts that cite letters from the Báb in the final years. The significance of that designation is contested. Azalí writers regard it as a nomination of a successor; Bahá’í writers see it as a nominal role for a young man in a time of danger and note that real guidance came from Mirza Husayn-Ali. Historians treat the evidence as supporting at least a formal leadership role for Yahya in the community after 1850, while recognizing that Mirza Husayn-Ali was widely respected and was in practice a principal organizer.

Mirza Husayn-Ali, after his release from the Siyah-Chal, was exiled from Iran and arrived in Baghdad in the spring of 1853. Born in Tehran in 1817 to a family of high-ranking officials from the province of Nur in Mazandaran, he had joined the Bábís in 1844, had been present at Badasht, and had used his family’s standing to help Bábí prisoners. In Baghdad he and Yahya, who had been in hiding in Iran and then fled, joined a community of exiles. The group included relatives, scholars, and others who had survived the repression. They corresponded with believers in Iran and received visitors from there, so that Baghdad became a hub from which news, instruction, and funds circulated.

The community’s problems were serious. It had lost much of its leadership; some believers had been drawn into disputes; and some, in the absence of clear guidance, engaged in conduct that other Bábís regarded as damaging. Subh-i-Azal, who lived in seclusion and communicated mostly by writing, did not supply the energetic direction many needed, and in the following years Mirza Husayn-Ali’s role in guiding the Bábís expanded. Relations between the two men were at first cordial but, as time passed, tension developed over who should exercise leadership and how the Báb’s expectations were to be understood. It would be mistaken to read the later Bahá’í–Azalí division back into 1853. In those years the exiles described themselves as Bábís, and the community’s separation into rival groups was a gradual development.

Baghdad provided possibilities that the exiles did not control. Ottoman administration separated them from the immediate jurisdiction of the Qajar court, but Iranian diplomats could still complain, gather information, or lobby for restrictions. Meanwhile pilgrims moving between Iran and Najaf or Karbala could transmit news without necessarily declaring themselves members of the community. The result was a network neither fully underground nor publicly secure. A letter sent to an Iranian town might offer theological guidance, but it might also help resolve a quarrel over funds, marriages, or the credibility of a traveling preacher. Recovering these practical concerns prevents the years after 1853 from appearing merely as a pause between the Báb's death and the eventual division of the movement.

Section 74.14

Bahá’u’lláh in Baghdad: Spiritual Writings and the Reinterpretation of Bábí Expectations

In 1854 Mirza Husayn-Ali left Baghdad without notice and spent two years in the mountains of Kurdistan near Sulaymaniyah, living among Sufi dervishes. Bahá’í sources attribute his withdrawal to distress over the community’s disarray; he himself later gave reasons connected with the dissension in the community. When he returned to Baghdad in 1856, at the request of his family and others, his stature among the Bábís had grown. He resumed teaching and writing and became the principal religious figure of the Baghdad community, with a circle of visitors including Muslim scholars, Sufis, and officials.

Three works of the Baghdad period are especially important. The Hidden Words, a collection of short ethical and mystical aphorisms in Arabic and Persian, was composed around 1858. The Seven Valleys, a Persian treatise in the Sufi tradition describing stages of spiritual progress, was written in response to a question from a Sufi scholar. The Kitab-i-Iqan (“Book of Certitude”), composed in 1861–1862, was written for the Báb’s maternal uncle, who had asked how the Báb’s claims could be reconciled with Islamic expectations. The Iqan argues that the messengers of God, from earlier prophets to Muhammad and the Báb, are spiritually one, that the prophecies concerning the Qa’im should be understood symbolically and not literally, and that previous generations had repeatedly rejected messengers because they insisted on literal readings and defended their own authority.

Taken together, these works emphasize spiritual transformation, the unity of religious revelation, and the possibility that divine guidance continues. They draw on Islamic scripture and on the Bábí and Sufi traditions. They also begin to reframe Bábí expectation: the Iqan’s discussion of the Qa’im and of a future manifestation does not name a new claimant, but its framework, in which divine messengers appear successively and are rejected by religious authorities, prepared readers to consider that further revelation might follow the Báb. Historians therefore read these texts as stages in a development that culminated in Bahá’u’lláh’s later claims, while recognizing that interpretations differ over how early he saw himself in this role.

Careful dating is essential. A text’s composition date, its circulation among followers, and its author’s public statement of a new religious status are three distinct moments. The Iqan, for example, was written and circulated years before Bahá’u’lláh’s claims were made public, and the full significance of such works was often recognized in retrospect. Baghdad was also the setting for growing interaction with Ottoman officials, Iranian consular officials, and the Shi‘ite clerics of Karbala and Najaf. Iranian authorities, concerned about the exiles’ proximity to the border, repeatedly asked the Ottoman government to remove them, and in 1863 the Ottomans agreed.

The readership of these writings was itself changing. A Persian treatise in a Sufi idiom could speak to educated Muslims familiar with mystical poetry, whereas an Arabic scripture-based argument addressed readers trained to recognize Qur'anic allusion and hadith. Copyists and travelers bridged those audiences, and disciples debated which language, genre, and argument best conveyed the author's authority. The Íqán is especially revealing because it answers a family member's questions about the Báb rather than announcing a separate religion in its title. It illustrates a transition through interpretation: scriptural history was reread so that rejection of a messenger became a recurring human response rather than proof that a claimant was false. The later Bahá'í community could thus understand continuity with earlier revelation as grounds for religious innovation.

Section 74.15

The Declaration of 1863 and the Division between Bahá’í and Azalí Bábism

In April 1863, as the exiles were summoned to Constantinople, Mirza Husayn-Ali moved to a garden outside Baghdad, called by the Bahá’ís the Ridvan Garden, where he spent about twelve days (21 April–2 May, in the tradition) before departing. According to Bahá’í accounts, it was there that he privately declared to close followers that he was the one promised by the Báb. The declaration was, in the first instance, a private communication to a small number; its dating to 1863 is accepted by specialists, but they emphasize that its public effect developed over the next several years. Bahá’ís celebrate the twelve days as the Festival of Ridvan.

The party traveled to Constantinople in the summer of 1863, then, after a few months, was sent to Edirne (Adrianople) in European Turkey, arriving in December. In Edirne the situation developed rapidly. Bahá’u’lláh’s claims became more public, as he addressed letters to believers and, later, to rulers; supporters multiplied, and Subh-i-Azal, who also lived in Edirne, rejected the claim and asserted that he retained the leadership the Báb had given him. Through 1866 the two groups drew apart, with mutual accusations, among them charges of plots and, in some accounts, of attempted poisoning, whose truth historians cannot determine. Bahá’u’lláh and Subh-i-Azal ceased to communicate, and the community divided. The great majority of Bábís who had been in contact with the Edirne exiles, including many in Iran, came to accept Bahá’u’lláh, though the speed and extent of acceptance varied by place and by the strength of local leaders. Subh-i-Azal retained a smaller following, concentrated in some Iranian towns, and Azalí Bábism continued to exist into the twentieth century.

Why did many Bábís accept Bahá’u’lláh’s claim, while others did not? Bahá’í accounts stress the persuasive force of his writings and the recognition of him as the promised one. Scholars point also to his role in the community since Baghdad, his organizational ability, his access to followers, and the character of the Bayan’s own teaching on a future manifestation. Azalís held that the Bayan had to be upheld, that the Báb had named Yahya, and that no new manifestation had been promised so soon. These positions reflected different readings of the same texts and different loyalties. The division should not be pictured as the instantaneous conversion of an entire movement; it was a process that played out among scattered communities over years.

The geographical dispersal of believers made the division uneven. A person in Baghdad or Edirne could witness the leaders' dispute, whereas a believer in an Iranian village might learn of it from a copied tablet carried by someone whose trustworthiness rested on family or scholarly ties. Local communities might divide according to their teachers, or postpone choosing a side until rival explanations reached them. The terminology of distinct Bahá'í and Azalí groups became clearer as these choices accumulated. This slow consolidation matters because later memoirs frequently narrate the schism from the standpoint of communities whose identity was already settled. Such narratives are indispensable evidence of retrospective allegiance, but they may compress the years in which many participants still considered themselves simply followers of the Báb.

Historical Debate

Succession Dispute, Disagreement over Revelation, or Emergence of a New Religion?

The question: How should historians characterize the division between Bahá’ís and Azalís? Was it chiefly a dispute over who should lead after the Báb, a disagreement over the meaning of the Báb’s revelation, or the emergence of a distinct religious tradition?

Interpretation A — A succession dispute: The Báb’s death and the loss of other leaders created a vacuum that two figures claimed to fill; Azalí writers, in particular, understand the division as a contest over a legitimate line of authority designated by the Báb.
Interpretation B — A disagreement over the meaning of the Báb’s revelation: The Bayan’s teaching of a future manifestation meant that the division centered on whether that manifestation had appeared, a theological question and not only a question of office.
Interpretation C — The emergence of a distinct tradition: Bahá’í writers, and many academic scholars, argue that Bahá’u’lláh’s claim and teachings produced a new religion, with its own scriptures, law, and institutions, not merely a branch of Bábism.

Evidence: Letters and writings of the Báb, Subh-i-Azal, and Bahá’u’lláh; Ottoman records; the accounts of followers on both sides, which are written by partisans; and European observers.

Points of disagreement: The authenticity and meaning of the Báb’s alleged designation of Yahya; the interpretation of the Bayan; and the weight of later developments in assessing what was “really” at issue in the 1860s.

Why it matters: The categories chosen affect whether Bahá’í history is told as continuation, fulfillment, or rupture, and they shape how Azalí history is treated.

Section 74.16

From Edirne to Acre: Bahá’u’lláh’s Exile, Proclamations, and Religious Legislation

The dispute between the two Bábí leaders drew the attention of the Ottoman authorities, who were already uneasy about the exiles’ presence and their contacts with Iranian affairs. In 1868 the government decided to separate the parties. Bahá’u’lláh, his family, and his followers were sent to Acre (‘Akka), a fortified town on the Mediterranean coast of Ottoman Syria, where convicts were held; Subh-i-Azal and a smaller group were sent to Famagusta in Cyprus. The Acre exiles arrived in August 1868 and endured harsh conditions at first, with confinement in a barracks, illness, and the death of some members. After some years conditions eased, and Bahá’u’lláh eventually lived in houses in and near Acre.

From Edirne and then Acre, Bahá’u’lláh wrote letters to rulers, among them Sultan Abdulaziz, Napoleon III, Queen Victoria, Tsar Alexander II, and Naser al-Din Shah, calling on them to examine his message and to deal justly. The letter to the Shah, known as the Lawh-i-Sultan, was composed in 1868 during the final weeks of the Edirne exile, before Bahá’u’lláh’s transfer to Acre. Its composition must be distinguished from its later delivery in Iran in 1869 by the young believer Badí‘, who was arrested and killed after delivering it. The introduction to the 2002 edition of The Summons of the Lord of Hosts explicitly places the letter’s revelation in the weeks before the final banishment. Such letters were both an assertion of Bahá’u’lláh’s claims in public and an appeal for toleration on behalf of the persecuted.

Around 1873 Bahá’u’lláh composed the Kitab-i-Aqdas, the “Most Holy Book,” in Arabic, which sets out the laws of the Bahá’í dispensation. It addresses worship, fasting, marriage, inheritance, and the conduct expected of believers, and it replaces the legal system of the Bayan, which it declares no longer binding. It also gives guidance on community life and on the leadership of the faith after his death. The Aqdas was not widely printed until the 1890s, and its provisions were implemented progressively. Its composition marks the formation of a distinct religious law, a main reason historians date the emergence of a separate Bahá’í tradition from this period.

Three kinds of text must be distinguished: appeals to rulers, addressed to political authorities; teaching directed to followers; and the legislation of the Aqdas, which structured the community. Each served a different function, and together they show a figure acting as religious claimant, teacher, and lawgiver. The exile in Acre meanwhile transformed the movement’s geography: Iran remained the home of most believers, but the leader and the core of its correspondence were in Ottoman Syria, a position that shaped how the community related to the Qajar state.

The Ottoman setting shaped these developments. Acre was within an imperial system concerned with exiles, security, and the political consequences of allowing controversial religious figures to correspond internationally. Regulations and restrictions were not constant: the early years of harsh confinement gave way to greater freedom of movement and access to visitors, without the formal rescission of exile. Messages to rulers therefore belonged to a world of inter-imperial diplomacy as well as religious teaching. A letter addressed to the Shah did not establish that he heard and accepted its argument; the episode's evidentiary value lies in the author's appeal, the attempts to deliver it, and the response recorded by other witnesses.

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas should also be understood as a foundational text whose communal realization was gradual. A prescriptive statement about worship or inheritance is not evidence that every family implemented it in the 1870s, especially where fear of prosecution encouraged secrecy and local custom remained powerful. Some provisions were explained and elaborated in later questions and answers; others became operative through institutions developed after Bahá'u'lláh's lifetime. The historian must hold together the text's claim to a comprehensive religious order and the modest, dispersed circumstances in which its earliest readers lived.

Section 74.17

The Bahá’í Faith: Religious Teachings, Community Authority, and Institutional Development

Bahá’u’lláh’s teaching centers on the claim that God has guided humanity through a succession of divine messengers, among them Abraham, Moses, Zoroaster, Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, and the Báb, whose teachings are adapted to the needs of their times and form stages in a single process. Bahá’ís call this progressive revelation. A related principle is the unity of religion, and the Bahá’í writings call for the unity of humankind, the removal of religious prejudice, and the reconciliation of religion and science. The teaching also emphasizes the moral and spiritual transformation of the individual and community, and it directs believers toward service, justice, and obedience to the laws of the land in which they live, a principle that shaped the community’s approach to a hostile state.

The relationship to the Báb is crucial. Bahá’ís regard the Báb as a divine messenger in his own right and as the forerunner who announced the manifestation of Bahá’u’lláh. They understand the Bayan’s laws as belonging to a preparatory dispensation, superseded by the Aqdas. This marks a point of difference with Azalís, who held that the Bayan remained valid, and with the earlier Bábí movement in which the question had not been posed. Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings also differ in tone and emphasis from the Báb’s legislation: the Bayan’s severe provisions toward non-believers do not appear in the Bahá’í writings, which stress toleration, and Bahá’u’lláh’s writings moderate or abrogate many of them.

The community’s religious life centered on prayer, reading of scripture, fasting, and gatherings, with teachers and local leaders guiding believers. Bahá’u’lláh’s tablets, circulated by hand-copying and carried by couriers, provided guidance, and pilgrims traveled to Acre to see him. In Iran, teachers (some of them former Bábí scholars) traveled among towns, and local assemblies of believers met discreetly. The institutions were still developing. The Kitab-i-Aqdas mentions local bodies and, in Bahá’í understanding, anticipates later institutions, and after Bahá’u’lláh’s death his son ‘Abdu’l-Bahá became the interpreter of his writings and head of the community, according to Bahá’u’lláh’s will. The elaborate administrative order that characterizes the twentieth-century Bahá’í community, including national and international elected councils, did not exist in the 1870s and should not be projected back onto that decade.

Distinctions remain across the community. Believers in Iran were largely Shi‘ite converts, and in some places converts from Jewish and Zoroastrian backgrounds joined in the later nineteenth century; those in Ottoman territory included Iranian exiles and local Arabs and Turks. The community’s growth was uneven, and persecution constrained public activity. Its leaders, in exile, communicated with it mainly through letters, which gave written texts a central role in its development.

These teachings did not produce a complete social transformation overnight. Believers continued to trade in the same markets, speak the same regional languages, and negotiate family expectations inherited from Muslim, Jewish, or Zoroastrian backgrounds. In some places religious gatherings were essentially domestic; in others visiting teachers could sustain a more elaborate network. A call for education or unity was a prescriptive ideal, and its effects must be studied through letters, community recollections, marriage practices, and later institutional records. Likewise, the principle of avoiding violent political rebellion should be distinguished from unconditional approval of Qajar government: writers could demand justice from rulers while discouraging followers from attacking them.

Section 74.18

Continuing Persecution: Religious Opposition, Provincial Violence, and Qajar State Policy

Persecution did not end with the executions of 1852. During the long reign of Naser al-Din Shah, which lasted until 1896, Bábís, Bahá’ís, and Azalís were periodically harassed, arrested, imprisoned, and killed, with episodes recorded in Isfahan, Yazd, Kashan, Najafabad, Tehran, and many smaller places. The pattern varied. Some years passed with little violence; at other times, local movements swelled into serious outbreaks. In Isfahan in 1879, two prominent merchant brothers, whom Bahá’ís call the King of Martyrs and the Beloved of Martyrs, were put to death on the initiative of a leading cleric, the Imam-Jum‘a, with the governor’s complicity, and their property was seized; historians note that financial motives were mixed with religious ones. In Yazd in 1891, a wave of violence killed a number of Bahá’ís, an episode connected to local rivalries and the actions of a governor.

Who was responsible? Historians distinguish three levels. Central state policy, under Naser al-Din Shah and his ministers, was generally hostile but inconsistent: the Shah’s experience of the 1852 attempt left a legacy of suspicion, but he was not always eager to authorize local killings, and at times officials, including those under foreign diplomatic pressure, intervened to restrain violence. Provincial governors had broad discretion and, depending on character and incentive, acted to protect, to extort, or to persecute. Local clerics played an important role, issuing rulings, preaching, and mobilizing crowds, though some scholars also objected to violence. Local populations were drawn into persecution through personal rivalries, economic competition, or the belief that action against heresy was a duty. In other words, centrally directed policy, local initiative, and individual opportunism all appear in the record.

Several factors reinforced one another. Accusations of religious deviation could be used against personal enemies. Communal hostility existed in places where Bábí or Bahá’í conversions disturbed local balances of power. Political suspicion, following 1852, made officials alert to the possibility of subversion. And property was often a motive: the confiscation of a victim’s goods gave officials and neighbors an incentive. The combination meant that a pious justification could cover a variety of interests.

The communities responded in several ways. Many practiced concealment, worshipping privately, maintaining ties through kinship and correspondence, and teaching children at home. Some left for other towns or for the Ottoman Empire, India, or the Caucasus and Central Asia, where small communities formed. Others sought protection from officials or foreign consuls, with limited success. These strategies preserved community identity under pressure and shaped the later character of Bahá’í life in Iran, in which privacy, mutual assistance, and a sense of shared suffering were prominent.

The Yazd violence of 1891 provides a useful caution about chronology. An account preserved under the title History of the Seven Martyrs of Yazd concerns that year's persecution and the role attributed to the provincial ruler and his attendants. It is a different episode from the killing of seven Bahá'ís in Yazd in 1901 and the larger violence there in 1903. Separating these events prevents later evidence from being projected backwards and shows that successive outbreaks could arise in the same locality under changing political conditions. The testimony of an individual connected with the governor's household may reveal how an episode was remembered, but it still requires comparison with independent records before motives or the precise sequence of deaths can be stated conclusively.

Economic life made religious hostility more than a dispute over doctrines. A creditor could be vulnerable if his debtor possessed clerical or administrative influence, while an artisan who lost access to suppliers or customers might be ruined without formal judicial action. Religious identity could become a pretext for seizing assets or reversing obligations. At the same time, not every accusation concealed an economic motive, and not every official acted alike. The case of the Isfahan merchants in 1879 illustrates why historians should investigate particular debts, patrons, local officials, and religious denunciations together rather than classifying each persecution as either purely theological or purely opportunistic.

Section 74.19

Everyday Life, Women, Conversion, and the Social Organization of Belief

The preceding sections have concentrated on leaders and events. Ordinary believers, who were the great majority, lived within households, workplaces, and neighborhoods that shaped what belief meant. Merchants used their commercial networks to carry texts and news and relied on trust relationships that overlapped with religious ones. Artisans and shopkeepers, whose livelihoods depended on reputation in the bazaar, faced the economic danger of public identification. Villagers were affected when a landowner or local leader converted or when a cleric denounced the community. Converts came overwhelmingly from Shi‘ite Muslim backgrounds in the first decades; later, in the 1870s–1890s, some Jews and Zoroastrians in cities such as Hamadan, Kashan, Yazd, and Tehran joined the Bahá’í community, drawn by the teaching’s inclusion of their prophets and, perhaps, by its prospect of a more welcoming community.

Women had a significant role in the early history. Tahirih’s leadership was exceptional, but other women taught, hosted gatherings, and joined the movement alongside male relatives, and women were among the defenders at Zanjan and among the victims of the persecutions. In the Bahá’í community, women participated in religious life through teaching and household education, and the Bahá’í writings emphasized the education of girls, though their access to institutional positions was limited, and the actual social positions of women in the nineteenth-century community remained shaped by prevailing norms. Historians caution against both overstating and understating women’s roles: the sources, written largely by men, record the exceptional more than the typical.

Family structure mattered. Conversions often followed lines of kinship and marriage, so households were frequently the unit of belief, and divisions could occur within them. Marriage within the community offered protection and continuity; mixed marriages posed risks. Children were educated at home in the faith, and elders taught prayer and the writings. Imprisonment, exile, and property loss imposed heavy burdens, falling often on women who managed households in the absence of men. The dangers of public identification meant that many believers lived dual lives, outwardly conforming while privately practicing, a pattern with costs for psychological and social life.

Evidence of ordinary practice is scattered but revealing. A local gathering required a host and time away from work; an itinerant teacher needed lodging, paper, transport, and introductions to trustworthy households; handwritten books had to be protected from confiscation or damage. Members who could read Arabic or Persian might interpret texts for those with less schooling, giving literate household members influence beyond formal office. Women could sustain communication networks through visits, teaching in homes, childcare, and maintaining contact with absent relatives. These contributions do not always appear in chronicles organized around male leaders and public confrontations, yet they were necessary for a minority community to reproduce itself over generations.

Conversions from Jewish and Zoroastrian backgrounds raise another question: what did affiliation mean for communities already exposed to legal and social disabilities? Some converts found in Bahá'í teaching a language of religious continuity and human unity, but they did not simply shed their previous relationships or enter a social setting free of prejudice. In some households religious change divided generations, altered marriage prospects, or complicated relations with communal authorities. Surviving testimonies are not a representative survey. The historian should therefore compare individual life stories with evidence about property, language, neighborhood, and family rather than assume that a doctrinal invitation necessarily produced social equality.

Section 74.20

Historical Synthesis: Revelation, Authority, Violence, and Religious Transformation in Qajar Iran

Comparing the Bábí movement of the 1840s with the Bahá’í and Azalí communities of the 1870s and after shows how much changed and how much did not. The Bábí movement was a messianic movement led by a charismatic claimant, driven by expectation of the Qa’im, and heavily influenced by Shaykhi and Shi‘ite scholarly circles. The Bahá’í community, by contrast, developed under a leader who lived in exile, emphasized spiritual and ethical teaching, and produced a body of law and a pattern of community life that were distinct from both Islam and the Bayan. The Azalí community preserved the Bayan and the memory of the Báb’s dispensation in a smaller and less institutionalized form. All three were shaped by persecution, but they interpreted its meaning differently.

The history also illuminates religious authority. In the Shi‘ite order, authority resided in a scholarly class and in the memory of the Imams. The Bábí claims introduced a competing form, charismatic and scriptural authority resting on a claimant’s own revelation. The later Bahá’í community redefined authority again by placing it in the writings of its founder and, after his death, in his appointed interpreter. The state, meanwhile, responded to a challenge that touched its own legitimacy. The responses of state and clergy were distinct: they cooperated in repression but did not share identical interests, and their actions varied across time and place.

Violence was central, and it has to be understood carefully. There were confrontations in which believers took up arms, and there was an assassination attempt by a small group; there was also a far larger body of violence directed at Bábís, including the killing of surrendered fighters, the execution of the Báb, mass executions in 1852, and the killing of civilians. The history must not reduce all believers to the conduct of a few or excuse state brutality by reference to the attempt on the Shah. It must also not treat Bábí actions as merely a response, ignoring the role of messianic expectation. The long-term effects of persecution included a strong memory of martyrdom, a tradition of nonviolence and obedience to government in Bahá’í teaching, the dispersion of believers, and the lasting suspicion with which many Iranian officials and clerics viewed Bahá’í and Azalí communities.

Bahá’u’lláh died on 29 May 1892 at Bahji, near Acre, where he had lived for some years under relaxed confinement. In his will, the Kitab-i-‘Ahdi (Book of My Covenant), he appointed his eldest son, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (Abbas Effendi), as the center of the community and interpreter of his teachings, though a dispute with other family members followed. This marks a transition into a later phase of Bahá’í history that is beyond this chapter’s scope. Azalí Bábism continued under Subh-i-Azal in Cyprus until his death in 1912. Some prominent Iranians of Azalí background later took part in constitutional politics, a matter for Chapters 78 and 79.

By 1892 the movement first proclaimed in Shiraz in 1844 had undergone profound transformation. Its founder had been executed, its early leaders had died in conflict or persecution, survivors had divided over succession, and a distinct Bahá’í community had formed under a leader outside Iran. Yet the histories of Bábí, Bahá’í, and Azalí communities remained tied to the religious, political, and social conditions of Qajar Iran. Their story shows that nineteenth-century Iranian religious change was intellectually creative, socially diverse, and politically consequential, and that the religious order of the Qajar state was challenged not only by European pressure and administrative reform but also by new claims to revelation originating within Iranian religious society itself. Chapter 76 turns to another episode of popular mobilization that occurred in the same last decade of Bahá’u’lláh’s life, the Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892.

One final distinction binds the history together: the transmission of a religious text, the acceptance of a religious claim, and the creation of a durable community are separate processes. The Báb's writings could circulate more widely than his immediate circle, yet only some readers accepted his authority; Bahá'u'lláh could articulate a new vision in exile, yet its practices developed differently among scattered families in Iran. State repression often weakened public institutions while reinforcing private solidarity, and its consequences varied over time. Seen at this level, the chapter is not an inevitable march from Shaykhism to an established world religion. It is a history of decisions made under constrained circumstances, recovered from incomplete and frequently partisan evidence.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 74 has traced the history of a religious movement that emerged from Twelver Shi‘ism in the 1840s, confronted the Qajar state and the clerical establishment, and gave rise to the Bahá’í Faith and to Azalí Bábism. Its central argument is that the Bábí movement arose within the intellectual and devotional world of Shi‘ite expectation about the Hidden Imam, developed claims to revelation and authority that challenged clerical institutions and the political order, and, after the destruction of its original leadership, divided into communities that understood the Báb’s legacy differently.

The chapter began with the religious setting: the occultation of the Twelfth Imam, the question of who speaks in his absence, the dominance of the Usuli school, and the emergence of Shaykhism. It stressed that Shaykhism was not an early name for Bábism and that many Shaykhis rejected the Báb. Sayyid Ali-Muhammad Shirazi, a merchant of Shiraz, declared himself the Báb in May 1844 to Mulla Husayn Bushru’i, and the first circle of believers, the Letters of the Living, carried the message through personal networks, study circles, and merchant routes.

The Báb’s writings, from the Qayyum al-Asma’ to the Persian Bayan, expanded his claims, from a channel to the Hidden Imam to the Qa’im to the author of a new revelation. The chapter emphasized that these claims developed over time and that historians cannot assume the later doctrines were present in 1844. Confinement in Maku and Chihriq limited his direct leadership but not his writing, and initiative shifted to disciples. Tahirih and the gathering at Badasht in 1848 illustrated the movement’s discovery of its own meaning, with the question of whether the sharia remained binding at the center.

Clerical and official reactions varied. Many mujtahids condemned the movement; governors acted according to local interests; the court moved from confinement to execution. The chapter insisted on the distinction between state and clergy and among kinds of response: formal repression, clerical polemic, local violence, and individual protection. It also emphasized that violence was not inevitable.

The armed confrontations of 1848–1853 at Shaykh Tabarsi, Nayriz, and Zanjan were examined separately. They differed in origin, organization, and outcome, and historians debate whether to see them as defensive resistance, messianic or political rebellion, or a combination that varied by place. Each ended in defeat and in heavy civilian suffering. The execution of the Báb in Tabriz in July 1850 removed the central figure of the movement, and the assassination attempt of August 1852, an act of a small group, triggered a broad repression that was in effect collective punishment.

After 1852, the movement’s center shifted to Ottoman Iraq, where Subh-i-Azal and Bahá’u’lláh were the leading figures. Bahá’u’lláh’s writings in Baghdad, including the Hidden Words, the Seven Valleys, and the Kitab-i-Iqan, reframed Bábí expectations in terms of spiritual transformation and the unity of divine messengers. His private declaration in 1863 and the subsequent separation from Subh-i-Azal in Edirne produced two communities, a process that unfolded over years and not in a single moment. The Ottoman exile of 1868 divided them geographically, and Bahá’u’lláh’s letters to rulers and the Kitab-i-Aqdas established the Bahá’í Faith as a distinct tradition with its own law.

Division V examined the teachings and institutions of the Bahá’í community, the continuing persecution under Naser al-Din Shah, and the social life of believers. Persecution was uneven, shaped by central policy, provincial governors, local clerics, and communal and economic motives. The experiences of merchants, rural households, and women showed that belief was lived within families and networks and constrained by the risks of public identification.

Throughout, the chapter has asked students to distinguish kinds of evidence: the writings of the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh as claimants’ texts, devotional histories written by believers, polemical works by opponents, court chronicles, and foreign diplomatic reports, and the academic scholarship that reads them critically. It has also asked them to separate documented events from legend and to treat religious claims as claims.

The chapter’s synthesis is that the Bábí–Bahá’í story reveals the relationship among religious innovation, institutional authority, state power, and social change in nineteenth-century Iran. The Qajar religious order was contested from within as well as from without. The communities that emerged from this crisis, Bahá’í and Azalí, carried its memory into later periods, and the questions it raised about revelation, authority, and toleration did not disappear.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Qajar Iran’s religious life was not a static background to political change. New claims to revelation arose from within Shi‘ite society, and their reception shaped the relations among scholars, rulers, and ordinary people. The confrontations of 1848–1853 and the repression that followed were among the most consequential events of the Qajar century for the people who lived through them.

The chapter also shows the importance of source criticism. Almost every account of these events was produced by a party with a stake in it, and the historian’s task is to read each critically, to distinguish documented events from devotional or polemical narrative, and to say what is unknown.

Later developments drew on this history. The persecution of Bahá’ís continued in later periods of Iranian history, and the memory of 1844–1892 became part of the identity of communities in Iran and abroad. At the same time, the history of religious pluralism, toleration, and dissent that this chapter opens is part of the broader story of how Iranians argued about authority, a story that continues through the constitutional struggles and the revolutions of later chapters.
